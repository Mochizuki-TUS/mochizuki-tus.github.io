Chapter 5The 230 Space Groups and How to Read the International Notation

In Chapter 4 we enumerated the 32 crystallographic point groups. A point group collected only the “symmetry operations that contain no translation.” A crystal, however, is a crystal precisely because it has translational symmetry. What happens to the classification of symmetry when we take translation fully into account? That is the subject of this chapter, and the answer is the 230 space groups.

To state the breakdown of the number 230 in advance, simply combining the $32$ point groups with the $14$ Bravais lattices is not enough. $32 \times 14$ is $448$, which is far too large to begin with (many combinations do not match the crystal system). The number of groups that can actually be built as “a point group $\times$ a compatible Bravais lattice” is $73$, and these are called symmorphic space groups. The remaining $157$ contain new kinds of operations that point groups do not have—screw axes and glide planes. These are operations in which a rotation and a translation, or a reflection and a translation, are “mixed together.”

Another goal of this chapter, and probably the most important one in practice, is to be able to read the international notation (Hermann–Mauguin symbols) character by character. $Pm\bar{3}m$, $Fd\bar{3}m$, $P6_3mc$, $Pnma$, $P2_1/c$—these symbols appear as a matter of course in papers, in the Materials Project, and in CIF files. And these symbols are a code in which the direction that each character refers to is strictly fixed. The first $m$ and the last $m$ of $Pm\bar{3}m$ mean mirror planes in completely different directions. Once you have this rule in your bones, the symmetry elements of a crystal will come to mind just from looking at the symbol.

Finally, we deal with Wyckoff positions. When we describe a crystal structure, we write not “the coordinates of all atoms” but “only the representative points of high symmetry.” Here we will see why the structure of $\mathrm{SrTiO_3}$ can be written in just three lines ($1a$, $1b$, $3c$).

5.1 What Is a Space Group? — Writing It with the Seitz Symbol

5.1.1 Definition

Definition: Space group

The group formed by the whole set of operations that bring an infinitely extended ideal crystal into coincidence with itself (that is, the crystal before and after the operation cannot be distinguished) is called the space group of that crystal.

Unlike a point group, a space group contains translation operations. Hence a space group is an infinite group with infinitely many elements. The space groups possible for three-dimensional crystals are classified into 230 types up to isomorphism and a suitable equivalence relation.

Why can we handle it even though it is an “infinite group”?

The space group $G$ is an infinite group. The operations $\{E|\TT\}$ alone ($\TT$ is an arbitrary lattice translation) already number infinitely many. There is nothing to fear, however. As we will see in Section 5.1.4, the set $T$ of lattice translations is a normal subgroup of $G$, and the quotient group $G/T$ is a finite group—in fact one of the 32 point groups.

In other words, a space group can be decomposed into an “infinite but simple part (translations)” and a “finite but complicated part (the point group).” This decomposition plays a decisive role when we derive Bloch's theorem group-theoretically in Chapter 11. The story is that the wave vector $\kk$ appears as a quantum number from the irreducible representations of the infinite translation part, while the degeneracy of the bands is determined by the finite point-group part.

5.1.2 The Seitz symbol

Definition: Seitz symbol

The operation “first apply an orthogonal transformation $R$ about the origin, then translate by the vector $\tvec$” is written

$$ \begin{equation} \{R|\tvec\}\,\rr = R\rr + \tvec \label{eq:5-seitz-def} \end{equation} $$

and $\{R|\tvec\}$ is called the Seitz symbol. Here $R$ is a $3\times3$ orthogonal matrix of the kind treated in Chapter 4 (rotation, reflection, inversion, rotoinversion), and $\tvec$ is an arbitrary vector.

  • $\{E|\TT\}$: a pure translation ($\TT$ is a lattice translation vector)
  • $\{R|\bm{0}\}$: a pure point-group operation (it does not move the origin)
  • $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}$ ($\bm{\tau}$ is not a lattice translation): a screw axis or a glide plane. This is what makes a space group richer than a point group

Notation caution: Which one is applied first

In this book we adopt, as in Eq. \eqref{eq:5-seitz-def}, the convention that “the rotation comes first and the translation afterward.” Some textbooks define $\{R|\tvec\}\rr = R(\rr+\tvec)$ instead, and then the product rule changes. When you compare formulas with another book, first check this convention.

There is also a convention issue in how $\tvec$ is written. In this book we regard $\tvec$ as a vector in Cartesian coordinates, but in practice (International Tables) it is written in fractional coordinates with the lattice vectors $\aa,\bb,\cc$ as the basis. For example, $\tfrac12\cc$ is written as $(0,0,\tfrac12)$. In the calculation of the systematic absences in Section 5.5, fractional coordinates are overwhelmingly easier, so we switch to them there.

5.1.3 Deriving the product rule

Derivation: The product of Seitz symbols

What do we get if we apply two operations in succession? Following the definition faithfully, we act on an arbitrary point $\rr$ and trace it. First, applying $\{R_1|\tvec_1\}$ gives

$$ \rr \;\longmapsto\; \rr' = R_1\rr + \tvec_1 $$

Next we apply $\{R_2|\tvec_2\}$ to this $\rr'$. Substituting $\rr'$ for $\rr$ in the definition \eqref{eq:5-seitz-def}, we obtain

$$ \rr' \;\longmapsto\; \rr'' = R_2\rr' + \tvec_2 = R_2\left(R_1\rr + \tvec_1\right) + \tvec_2 $$

Here $R_2$ is a linear transformation (a matrix), so we may distribute it inside the parentheses:

$$ \rr'' = R_2R_1\rr + R_2\tvec_1 + \tvec_2 $$

Rearranging the right-hand side into the form “$($ matrix $)\rr + ($ vector $)$,” the matrix part is $R_2R_1$ and the vector part is $R_2\tvec_1+\tvec_2$. That is,

$$ \begin{equation} \{R_2|\tvec_2\}\{R_1|\tvec_1\} = \{\,R_2R_1 \;\big|\; R_2\tvec_1 + \tvec_2\,\} \label{eq:5-seitz-product} \end{equation} $$

∎ Be sure to derive this equation by hand once yourself. From here on, almost every calculation in this chapter is an application of this single equation.

Caution: Do not forget that $R_2$ acts on $\tvec_1$

The easiest mistake to make in Eq. \eqref{eq:5-seitz-product} is to forget that the rotation $R_2$ on the left acts on the translation $\tvec_1$ on the right (the one applied first). The result is not $\tvec_1 + \tvec_2$.

Intuitively this is obvious: we first “shifted by $\tvec_1$” and then “rotated by $R_2$,” so the shift itself is rotated along with everything else. Conversely, $\tvec_2$ is applied last, so nothing acts on it.

Because of this asymmetry, the product in a space group is in general not commutative: $\{R_2|\tvec_2\}\{R_1|\tvec_1\} \ne \{R_1|\tvec_1\}\{R_2|\tvec_2\}$.

Derivation: The inverse and the identity

Identity. $\{E|\bm{0}\}$ is the identity element. Indeed, setting $R_2=E$, $\tvec_2=\bm{0}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:5-seitz-product}, we get

$$ \{E|\bm{0}\}\{R_1|\tvec_1\} = \{ER_1 \,|\, E\tvec_1 + \bm{0}\} = \{R_1|\tvec_1\} $$

Multiplying from the other side, with $R_1=E$, $\tvec_1=\bm{0}$, we get

$$ \{R_2|\tvec_2\}\{E|\bm{0}\} = \{R_2E \,|\, R_2\bm{0} + \tvec_2\} = \{R_2|\tvec_2\} $$

It acts as the identity from both sides. ✓

Inverse. Let us write the inverse of $\{R|\tvec\}$ as $\{S|\bm{u}\}$ and find it as the unknown. The condition is

$$ \{S|\bm{u}\}\{R|\tvec\} = \{SR \,|\, S\tvec + \bm{u}\} = \{E|\bm{0}\} $$

For this to hold, it suffices that the matrix parts and the vector parts agree separately. From the matrix part,

$$ SR = E \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S = R^{-1} $$

and from the vector part,

$$ S\tvec + \bm{u} = \bm{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bm{u} = -S\tvec = -R^{-1}\tvec $$

Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \{R|\tvec\}^{-1} = \{\,R^{-1} \;\big|\; -R^{-1}\tvec\,\} \label{eq:5-seitz-inverse} \end{equation} $$

To be safe, let us also check from the other side:

$$ \{R|\tvec\}\{R^{-1}|-R^{-1}\tvec\} = \{RR^{-1} \,|\, R(-R^{-1}\tvec) + \tvec\} = \{E \,|\, -\tvec + \tvec\} = \{E|\bm{0}\}\ \checkmark $$

Associativity. Since $\{R|\tvec\}$ is defined as a “map acting on the point $\rr$,” associativity follows automatically from the associativity of the composition of maps. To be safe, let us also verify it componentwise:

$$ \left(\{R_3|\tvec_3\}\{R_2|\tvec_2\}\right)\{R_1|\tvec_1\} = \{R_3R_2 \,|\, R_3\tvec_2+\tvec_3\}\{R_1|\tvec_1\} = \{R_3R_2R_1 \,|\, R_3R_2\tvec_1 + R_3\tvec_2 + \tvec_3\} $$ $$ \{R_3|\tvec_3\}\left(\{R_2|\tvec_2\}\{R_1|\tvec_1\}\right) = \{R_3|\tvec_3\}\{R_2R_1 \,|\, R_2\tvec_1+\tvec_2\} = \{R_3R_2R_1 \,|\, R_3(R_2\tvec_1+\tvec_2) + \tvec_3\} $$

Expanding the last parenthesis in the second line gives $R_3R_2\tvec_1 + R_3\tvec_2 + \tvec_3$, which agrees with the first line. ✓

∎ We have thus confirmed that the set of Seitz symbols satisfies the four group axioms (closure, associativity, identity, inverse). Closure is shown by the fact that the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:5-seitz-product} is again of the form of a Seitz symbol.

Example 5.1: Turning a $4_2$ screw axis four times

In a tetragonal crystal, consider the operation $A = \{C_4(z)\,|\,\tfrac12\cc\}$, which is the $90^\circ$ rotation $C_4(z)$ about the $z$ axis (= the $\cc$ axis) accompanied by a translation of $\tfrac12\cc$. This is a $4_2$ screw axis (Section 5.2). Find $A^2, A^3, A^4$.

Solution. First, one remark. Since $C_4(z)$ is a rotation about the $z$ axis, it does not move the vector $\cc$ along the $z$ axis:

$$ C_4(z)\,\cc = \cc $$

Using this makes the calculation very easy. We simply apply Eq. \eqref{eq:5-seitz-product} mechanically.

$A^2$:

$$ A^2 = \{C_4|\tfrac12\cc\}\{C_4|\tfrac12\cc\} = \{\,C_4C_4 \;\big|\; C_4\left(\tfrac12\cc\right) + \tfrac12\cc\,\} = \{\,C_4^2 \;\big|\; \tfrac12\cc + \tfrac12\cc\,\} = \{C_2(z)\,|\,\cc\} $$

Since $\cc$ is a lattice translation vector, $A^2$ is a “twofold rotation plus one lattice translation.” If we ignore the lattice translation, this looks like a plain twofold rotation.

$A^3$:

$$ A^3 = A\cdot A^2 = \{C_4|\tfrac12\cc\}\{C_2|\cc\} = \{\,C_4C_2 \;\big|\; C_4\cc + \tfrac12\cc\,\} = \{\,C_4^3 \;\big|\; \cc + \tfrac12\cc\,\} = \{C_4^3\,|\,\tfrac32\cc\} $$

$A^4$:

$$ A^4 = A\cdot A^3 = \{C_4|\tfrac12\cc\}\{C_4^3|\tfrac32\cc\} = \{\,C_4^4 \;\big|\; C_4\left(\tfrac32\cc\right) + \tfrac12\cc\,\} = \{\,E \;\big|\; \tfrac32\cc + \tfrac12\cc\,\} = \{E\,|\,2\cc\} $$

∎ After four applications, the rotation part has become the identity and the translation part is $2\cc$—that is, a pure lattice translation. This is the simplest check of the general rule (Section 5.2) that “turning a screw axis $n$ times returns a lattice translation.”

What is important here is that $A$ itself cannot be rewritten in the form $\{R|\bm{0}\}$. No matter where we shift the origin, $\tfrac12\cc$ cannot be removed (the $\cc$ direction is the rotation-axis direction, so shifting the origin cannot cancel it by a rotation). This is why a space group containing $4_2$ is nonsymmorphic.

5.1.4 The translation subgroup is a normal subgroup

Derivation: $T \triangleleft G$ and $G/T \cong$ point group

In the space group $G$, let the set consisting only of the pure lattice translations be

$$ T = \left\{\, \{E|\TT\} \;:\; \TT = n_1\aa_1 + n_2\aa_2 + n_3\aa_3,\ n_i \in \mathbb{Z} \,\right\} $$

That $T$ is a subgroup is obvious (it is closed, since $\{E|\TT_1\}\{E|\TT_2\} = \{E|\TT_1+\TT_2\}$, and the inverse is $\{E|-\TT\}$).

We show normality. Let us conjugate an element of $T$ by an arbitrary element $\{R|\vv\}$ of $G$. We compute step by step. First the inner two factors:

$$ \{E|\TT\}\{R|\vv\}^{-1} = \{E|\TT\}\{R^{-1}|-R^{-1}\vv\} = \{\,E R^{-1} \;\big|\; E\left(-R^{-1}\vv\right) + \TT\,\} = \{\,R^{-1} \;\big|\; \TT - R^{-1}\vv\,\} $$

Multiplying this from the left by $\{R|\vv\}$:

$$ \{R|\vv\}\{R^{-1}\,|\,\TT - R^{-1}\vv\} = \{\,RR^{-1} \;\big|\; R\left(\TT - R^{-1}\vv\right) + \vv\,\} = \{\,E \;\big|\; R\TT - \vv + \vv\,\} $$

That is,

$$ \begin{equation} \{R|\vv\}\,\{E|\TT\}\,\{R|\vv\}^{-1} = \{E\,|\,R\TT\} \label{eq:5-normal} \end{equation} $$

Now $R$ is an element of the crystal's point group, so it maps the lattice onto the lattice. Hence $R\TT$ is again a lattice translation vector, and $\{E|R\TT\} \in T$. Conjugation does not take us out of $T$, so $T$ is a normal subgroup of $G$. ∎

Quotient group. Given a normal subgroup, we can form the quotient group $G/T$. Dividing an element $\{R|\vv\}$ of $G$ by $T$ means “ignoring” the translation part $\vv$ up to lattice translations. All that remains is the matrix part $R$. Therefore

$$ G/T \;\cong\; \mathcal{P} \qquad (\mathcal{P} = \text{crystallographic point group}) $$

This $\mathcal{P}$ is called the point group of that space group, or its crystal class. It is one of the 32 point groups.

Physical meaning: Viewing a space group as “a point group plus an assignment of a translation to each element”

The discussion of the quotient group makes the structure of a space group come out clearly. To each element $R_i$ of the point group $\mathcal{P} = \{R_1, R_2, \ldots, R_h\}$ we assign one translation $\bm{\tau}_i$. Then the elements of the space group can be written, exhaustively and without repetition, as

$$ \{R_i \,|\, \bm{\tau}_i + \TT\}, \qquad \TT \in \text{lattice} $$

Since $\bm{\tau}_i$ has an indeterminacy of “an added lattice translation,” the convention is to take it so that it lies within the unit cell. This $\bm{\tau}_i$ is called the nonprimitive translation (also called a fractional translation).

And then,

  • if there exists an origin at which all the $\bm{\tau}_i$ can be made zero $\Rightarrow$ symmorphic (73 groups)
  • if some $\bm{\tau}_i$ remains however the origin is chosen $\Rightarrow$ nonsymmorphic (157 groups)

This is the classification (Section 5.3). In a nonsymmorphic space group, the point group $\mathcal{P}$ is not a subgroup of the space group—this is the source of the difficulty when we construct the “representations of the little co-group” in Chapter 11.

5.2 Symmetry Operations Containing Translations — Screw Axes and Glide Planes

There are two kinds of operations that belong only to space groups and not to point groups: the screw axis, which combines a rotation with a translation, and the glide plane, which combines a reflection with a translation. Neither one, taken alone, brings the crystal back onto itself, but as a single combined operation each one does bring the crystal back onto itself.

5.2.1 Screw axes

Definition: Screw axis $n_m$

The operation that rotates by $360^\circ/n$ about an axis and then translates along that axis by $m/n$ times the lattice translation $\cc$ is called an $n_m$ screw axis ($m = 1, 2, \ldots, n-1$). In terms of the Seitz symbol,

$$ \begin{equation} n_m \;=\; \left\{\,C_n \;\Big|\; \frac{m}{n}\cc \,\right\} \label{eq:5-screw} \end{equation} $$

where $\cc$ is the shortest lattice translation vector along the screw axis. By the crystallographic restriction theorem (Theorem 4.1), only $n = 2,3,4,6$ is possible, so the possible screw axes are

$$ 2_1;\quad 3_1,\ 3_2;\quad 4_1,\ 4_2,\ 4_3;\quad 6_1,\ 6_2,\ 6_3,\ 6_4,\ 6_5 $$

and there are only $1+2+3+5 = 11$ kinds.

Derivation: Applying a screw axis $n$ times gives a lattice translation

First we check the simplest case, $2_1$. Let $\cc$ be the lattice translation along the twofold axis; then $C_2$ does not move $\cc$ ($C_2\cc = \cc$). From Eq. \eqref{eq:5-seitz-product},

$$ \left\{C_2\Big|\tfrac12\cc\right\}^2 = \left\{\,C_2 C_2 \;\Big|\; C_2\!\left(\tfrac12\cc\right) + \tfrac12\cc \,\right\} = \left\{\,E \;\Big|\; \tfrac12\cc + \tfrac12\cc \,\right\} = \{E \,|\, \cc\} $$

Applying $2_1$ twice gives the pure lattice translation $\cc$. In other words, a $2_1$ axis is an operation like “the square root of $\cc$.”

The same holds for a general $n_m$. Let $A = \{C_n | \frac{m}{n}\cc\}$. Since $C_n$ does not move a vector along the axis, $C_n\cc = \cc$, and therefore $C_n(\lambda\cc)=\lambda\cc$ for any real number $\lambda$. Using this, we find the form of $A^k$ by mathematical induction.

Case $k=1$: $A^1 = \{C_n | \frac{m}{n}\cc\}$, and this is exactly the case $k=1$ of the claim $A^k = \{C_n^k | k\frac{m}{n}\cc\}$.

Assume the claim holds for $k$, and compute $k+1$:

$$ A^{k+1} = A\cdot A^{k} = \left\{C_n\Big|\tfrac{m}{n}\cc\right\}\left\{C_n^k\Big|k\tfrac{m}{n}\cc\right\} = \left\{\,C_n^{k+1} \;\Big|\; C_n\!\left(k\tfrac{m}{n}\cc\right) + \tfrac{m}{n}\cc\,\right\} $$

Since $C_n(k\frac{m}{n}\cc) = k\frac{m}{n}\cc$ here,

$$ A^{k+1} = \left\{\,C_n^{k+1} \;\Big|\; k\tfrac{m}{n}\cc + \tfrac{m}{n}\cc\,\right\} = \left\{\,C_n^{k+1} \;\Big|\; (k+1)\tfrac{m}{n}\cc\,\right\} $$

This is the case $k+1$ of the claim. Hence the claim holds for all $k$. In particular, setting $k=n$ and using $C_n^n = E$, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \left(n_m\right)^n = \left\{\,E \;\Big|\; n\cdot\frac{m}{n}\cc\,\right\} = \{E \,|\, m\,\cc\} \label{eq:5-screw-n} \end{equation} $$

∎ That is, applying $n_m$ $n$ times gives $m$ lattice translations. For $4_2$ we have $\left(4_2\right)^4 = \{E|2\cc\}$, which agrees with the result of Example 5.1.

Caution: $4_2$ is not a “twofold axis”

$4_2$ is a $90^\circ$ rotation plus a translation of $\cc/2$. Looking at Table 5.1, two applications give $\{C_2|\cc\}$, that is, a twofold rotation once the lattice translation is removed. Many people jump to the conclusion that “$4_2$ is, in essence, just a twofold axis,” but that is wrong.

The operation obtained by applying $4_2$ once is itself a symmetry operation. A crystal that contains this operation maps onto itself when the atomic arrangement is rotated by $90^\circ$ and raised by $\cc/2$. In a crystal that has only a twofold axis, this does not happen.

The fact that $4_2$ becomes $4$ when we pass to the point group (Section 5.2.3) and the claim that $4_2$ is the same as a twofold axis are entirely different matters. It is thanks to this $4_2$ that rutile-type $\mathrm{TiO_2}$ ($P4_2/mnm$) is tetragonal and not monoclinic.

5.2.2 Glide planes

Definition: Glide plane

The operation that reflects in a plane and then translates in a direction within that plane by half of a lattice translation (or by 1/4) is called a glide plane. In terms of the Seitz symbol it is $\{\sigma\,|\,\bm{\tau}\}$, where $\bm{\tau}$ is a vector parallel to the mirror plane.

Derivation: Applying a glide plane twice gives a lattice translation

Take the normal of the $c$ glide plane along $\bb$ (the $y$ axis). The corresponding reflection is $\sigma_y : (x,y,z)\mapsto(x,-y,z)$. The translation is $\bm{\tau} = \tfrac12\cc$ (in the $z$ direction).

First we confirm an important fact. Since $\sigma_y$ changes only the sign of the $y$ component, it does nothing to the vector $\cc$ in the $z$ direction:

$$ \sigma_y\,\cc = \cc $$

This is a consequence of the fact that the translation of a glide always lies in the plane: a vector lying in the plane is invariant under the reflection in that plane. Hence, from Eq. \eqref{eq:5-seitz-product},

$$ \left\{\sigma_y\Big|\tfrac12\cc\right\}^2 = \left\{\,\sigma_y\sigma_y \;\Big|\; \sigma_y\!\left(\tfrac12\cc\right) + \tfrac12\cc\,\right\} = \left\{\,E \;\Big|\; \tfrac12\cc + \tfrac12\cc\,\right\} = \{E\,|\,\cc\} $$

∎ The result is a pure lattice translation. The glide plane is also the “square root of the lattice translation $\cc$.”

The same holds for an $n$ glide ($\bm{\tau} = \frac12(\aa+\cc)$, normal $\bb$); since $\sigma_y(\aa+\cc) = \aa+\cc$,

$$ \left\{\sigma_y\Big|\tfrac12(\aa+\cc)\right\}^2 = \{E\,|\,\aa+\cc\} $$

which is again a lattice translation. For a $d$ glide ($\bm{\tau}=\frac14(\aa+\cc)$) we obtain

$$ \left\{\sigma_y\Big|\tfrac14(\aa+\cc)\right\}^2 = \left\{E\,\Big|\,\tfrac12(\aa+\cc)\right\} $$

which is half of $\aa+\cc$. For this to be a lattice translation, $\frac12(\aa+\cc)$ itself must be a lattice translation. That is, the lattice must be face-centered. The situation is the same for a body-centered lattice: the translation then takes a form such as $\bm{\tau}=\frac14(\aa\pm\bb+\cc)$ (again an in-plane component), and applying it twice gives $\frac12(\aa\pm\bb+\cc)$—which, after adding lattice vectors, equals the body-centering translation $\frac12(\aa+\bb+\cc)$. In either case, the reason why the $d$ glide appears only in $F$-centered and $I$-centered lattices is contained entirely in this one-line calculation. The $F$ and the $d$ in the diamond structure $Fd\bar{3}m$ thus always appear as a set.

Example 5.2: Must the translation of a glide really lie in the plane?

Let the normal of the plane be $\bb$, and suppose that the translation contains a component $\beta\bb$ perpendicular to the plane. What happens when $\{\sigma_y \,|\, \bm{\tau}_\parallel + \beta\bb\}$ is applied twice? Also show that such an operation is not a “new” symmetry operation.

Solution. Noting that $\sigma_y \bm{\tau}_\parallel = \bm{\tau}_\parallel$ and $\sigma_y(\beta\bb) = -\beta\bb$, we have

$$ \{\sigma_y|\bm{\tau}_\parallel+\beta\bb\}^2 = \{\,E \;|\; \sigma_y(\bm{\tau}_\parallel+\beta\bb) + \bm{\tau}_\parallel + \beta\bb\,\} = \{\,E \;|\; (\bm{\tau}_\parallel - \beta\bb) + (\bm{\tau}_\parallel + \beta\bb)\,\} = \{E\,|\,2\bm{\tau}_\parallel\} $$

The perpendicular component $\beta\bb$ cancels out cleanly. That is, $\beta$ does not appear in the result of applying the operation twice.

Then what is $\beta$ doing? The answer is that it “merely shifts the position of the mirror plane.” Consider a coordinate change that shifts the origin by $\bm{o} = \frac{\beta}{2}\bb$. When we rewrite in the new coordinates $\rr' = \rr - \bm{o}$,

$$ \{\sigma_y \,|\, \bm{\tau}_\parallel + \beta\bb\} \;\longrightarrow\; \{\sigma_y \,|\, \bm{\tau}_\parallel\} $$

(in general, shifting the origin by $\bm{o}$ gives $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}\to\{R|\bm{\tau} + R\bm{o} - \bm{o}\}$, and here we have chosen $\bm{o}$ so that $R\bm{o}-\bm{o} = -\beta\bb$). ∎

Therefore, a translation component perpendicular to the plane can be eliminated by the choice of origin, so it is not essential. Only the in-plane component is meaningful as the translation of a glide. The same argument applies to rotation axes: a translation component perpendicular to the axis can be eliminated by shifting the origin, so only the component along the axis is essential for the translation of a screw axis.

5.3 Symmorphic and Nonsymmorphic Space Groups

Definition: Symmorphic space group

When a space group $G$ contains its point group $\mathcal{P}$ as a subgroup, $G$ is called a symmorphic space group. Equivalently,

  • there exists an origin for which all the nonprimitive translations $\bm{\tau}_i$ can be made zero
  • neither a screw-axis symbol (such as $2_1$) nor a glide symbol ($a,b,c,n,d,e$) appears in the international notation

Space groups that are not symmorphic are called nonsymmorphic.

Of the 230 space groups, 73 are symmorphic and 157 are nonsymmorphic.

Derivation: The breakdown of the number 73 — why it is not “$32 \times$ something”

A naive argument suggests that the symmorphic space groups can be built as “point group $\times$ Bravais lattice.” Then the count would be $32 \times 14 = 448$ — but that is not the case. There are two reasons.

Reason 1: The Bravais types available differ from one crystal system to another. For example, the base-centered monoclinic lattice $C$ (mC) cannot be combined with the tetragonal point group $4/mmm$, because a monoclinic lattice does not have the fourfold symmetry that $4/mmm$ requires. The only lattices that have the symmetry required by the point group $4/mmm$ are the tetragonal $P$ and $I$. Hence the combinations are closed within each crystal system, and we must count as

$$ 73 \;\stackrel{?}{=}\; \sum_{\text{crystal system}} (\text{number of point groups in that system}) \times (\text{number of Bravais types in that system}) $$

Carrying out this naive calculation gives the column “Naive product” of the following table.

Reason 2: Even for the same point group and the same lattice, there are cases in which the orientation can be chosen in two ways. For example, for the tetragonal point group $\bar{4}2m$, after placing $\bar{4}$ along $[001]$, placing the $2$ along $\langle100\rangle$ or along $\langle110\rangle$ gives $P\bar{4}2m$ and $P\bar{4}m2$, which are different space groups. Where the naive product counts one, we must actually count two.

Caution: Even a symmorphic space group “has” glide operations

$Fm\bar{3}m$ (NaCl) is symmorphic, because its symbol contains no glide letter. However, if we write out all the symmetry operations of this space group, glide planes are certainly included among them.

Why? The $F$-centered lattice has centering translations such as $\frac12(\aa+\bb)$, and multiplying one of these by the reflection $\{\sigma|\bm{0}\}$ gives

$$ \left\{E\Big|\tfrac12(\aa+\bb)\right\}\{\sigma|\bm{0}\} = \left\{\sigma\Big|\tfrac12(\aa+\bb)\right\} $$

which is a perfectly good glide operation. Even so, $Fm\bar{3}m$ is symmorphic because every element of the point group $m\bar{3}m$ also appears in the form $\{R|\bm{0}\}$. The definition asks whether the group “contains the point group as a subgroup,” not whether “there is not a single glide operation.”

In practice, it suffices to judge by “whether the symbol contains a screw or glide letter.” $Fm\bar{3}m$ has none, so it is symmorphic; $Fd\bar{3}m$ has a $d$, so it is nonsymmorphic.

Physical Meaning: Nonsymmorphy affects the band structure

In a nonsymmorphic space group, there are cases in which bands are necessarily twofold (or more) degenerate at particular points on the Brillouin-zone boundary. This is called stickiness (sticking together of bands). The reason is that on the boundary the nonprimitive translation $\bm{\tau}$ acts as a phase factor $e^{-i\kk\cdot\bm{\tau}}$, so the representation of the point group becomes a “projective representation,” and only irreducible representations of higher dimension are allowed.

The “nonsymmorphic-symmetry-protected Dirac semimetal” and the “hourglass-shaped band dispersion (hourglass fermion),” which have attracted attention in recent years, are precisely consequences of this nonsymmorphy. We treat them again in Chapter 11. Considering that $157/230 \approx 68\%$ of the space groups are nonsymmorphic, this is not an exceptional circumstance at all; rather, it is the ordinary situation.

5.4 How to Read the International Notation — The Heart of This Chapter

From here on we come to the heart of this chapter. The international (Hermann–Mauguin) notation is not a cipher. Each single character corresponds to a direction and an operation. Once you have this correspondence table in your head, the four characters $Pm\bar{3}m$ immediately call up a concrete picture: three fourfold axes, four threefold rotoinversion axes, six twofold axes, and nine mirror planes.

Also, the third position of the hexagonal system, $\langle1\bar{1}0\rangle$, consists concretely of the three directions $[1\bar{1}0], [120], [\bar{2}\bar{1}0]$. Some textbooks write $[210]$ as the representative, but $[210] = -[\bar{2}\bar{1}0]$, so it is the same axis (to avoid confusion when you compare with the table in Chapter 4).

5.4.4 Short and full forms — why $4/m$ shrinks to $m$

International Tables lists, alongside $Pm\bar{3}m$, the full symbol $P\,\dfrac{4}{m}\,\bar{3}\,\dfrac{2}{m}$. The $Pm\bar{3}m$ we use in everyday practice is the short symbol. Why may we omit the $4$ and the $2$?

Derivation: The product of two mirror planes is a rotation

Consider a mirror plane containing the $z$ axis. Let $\phi$ be the angle that the line of intersection of this plane with the $xy$ plane makes with the $x$ axis. Then the matrix representing this reflection is

$$ \sigma(\phi) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos2\phi & \sin2\phi & 0\\ \sin2\phi & -\cos2\phi & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} $$

Let us check this to be safe. For $\phi=0$ (plane = $xz$ plane, normal along the $y$ direction) we get

$$ \sigma(0) = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix} \;:\; (x,y,z)\mapsto(x,-y,z)\ \checkmark $$

For $\phi=90^\circ$ (plane = $yz$ plane, normal along the $x$ direction, that is, the $(100)$ plane), $\cos180^\circ=-1,\ \sin180^\circ=0$ gives

$$ \sigma(90^\circ) = \begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix} \;:\;(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,y,z)\ \checkmark $$

Now apply two reflections in succession. Writing only the $2\times2$ block, we have

$$ \sigma(\phi_2)\sigma(\phi_1) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos2\phi_2 & \sin2\phi_2\\ \sin2\phi_2 & -\cos2\phi_2\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\cos2\phi_1 & \sin2\phi_1\\ \sin2\phi_1 & -\cos2\phi_1\end{pmatrix} $$

We compute the components one by one. The $(1,1)$ component is $\cos2\phi_2\cos2\phi_1 + \sin2\phi_2\sin2\phi_1 = \cos2(\phi_2-\phi_1)$ (addition theorem). The $(1,2)$ component is $\cos2\phi_2\sin2\phi_1 - \sin2\phi_2\cos2\phi_1 = -\sin2(\phi_2-\phi_1)$. The $(2,1)$ component is $\sin2\phi_2\cos2\phi_1 - \cos2\phi_2\sin2\phi_1 = \sin2(\phi_2-\phi_1)$. The $(2,2)$ component is $\sin2\phi_2\sin2\phi_1+\cos2\phi_2\cos2\phi_1 = \cos2(\phi_2-\phi_1)$. Summarizing,

$$ \begin{equation} \sigma(\phi_2)\,\sigma(\phi_1) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\Theta & -\sin\Theta\\ \sin\Theta & \cos\Theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Theta = 2(\phi_2-\phi_1) \label{eq:5-two-mirrors} \end{equation} $$

∎ That is, the product of two mirror planes is a rotation about their line of intersection, and the rotation angle is twice the angle between the two planes.

Why $4/m \to m$ and $2/m \to m$?

$Pm\bar{3}m$ contains both a $(100)$ plane ($\phi_1 = 90^\circ$) and a $(110)$ plane (its normal is $[110]$, so the line of intersection of the planes is along $[1\bar{1}0]$, and $\phi_2 = -45^\circ$). Applying Eq. \eqref{eq:5-two-mirrors}, we obtain

$$ \Theta = 2\left(-45^\circ - 90^\circ\right) = -270^\circ \equiv +90^\circ $$

In other words, the product of these two mirror planes is a $90^\circ$ rotation about $[001]$, that is, the fourfold axis itself. Let us verify this with matrices:

$$ \sigma(-45^\circ)\sigma(90^\circ) =\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} \;:\;(x,y)\mapsto(-y,x) $$

This is the $+90^\circ$ rotation $C_4(z)$. ✓

Similarly, the product of the reflection in the $(001)$ plane and the reflection in the $(1\bar{1}0)$ plane (which are perpendicular to each other) is a $180^\circ$ rotation about their line of intersection $[110]$, that is, it gives the twofold axis of the third position.

Therefore, if we write three $m$'s, the $4$ and the $2$ are generated automatically. Since there is no need to write them, they are omitted from the short symbol. This is the content of the abbreviation $P\,\frac{4}{m}\,\bar{3}\,\frac{2}{m} \to Pm\bar{3}m$.

Conversely, $\bar{3}$ cannot be omitted. If we drop $\bar{3}$ and write something like $Pmm$, the threefold rotoinversion axis along the body diagonal is lost, and with it the very essence of being cubic. This is why either $3$ or $\bar{3}$ always appears in the second position of the space-group symbol of a cubic crystal. It is fine to remember this as “if a 3 stands in the second position, the crystal is cubic.”

5.4.5 Reading the space groups of real materials one character at a time

Example 5.4: $Fm\bar{3}m$ (NaCl, MgO, Cu, Al)

Full symbol $F\,\frac{4}{m}\,\bar{3}\,\frac{2}{m}$. Cubic.

  • $F$: face-centered cubic lattice. It has the centering translations $\frac12(\aa+\bb),\frac12(\bb+\cc),\frac12(\cc+\aa)$. The unit cell contains four lattice points.
  • $m$ (first position, $\langle100\rangle$): fourfold axes along $[100],[010],[001]$ (three axes), and mirror planes on $(100),(010),(001)$ (three planes).
  • $\bar{3}$ (second position, $\langle111\rangle$): threefold rotoinversion axes along $[111],[\bar{1}11],[1\bar{1}1],[11\bar{1}]$ (four axes).
  • $m$ (third position, $\langle110\rangle$): twofold axes along the six directions of the $[110]$ type (six axes), and six diagonal mirror planes of the $(110)$ type.

The only difference from $Pm\bar{3}m$ is $P \to F$, that is, only the lattice centering. The point group is $m\bar{3}m$ in both cases. Therefore the peak positions of the diffraction pattern differ (the systematic absences of $F$ centering apply; Chapter 3), but the types and orientations of the symmetry elements are exactly the same.

Example 5.5: $Fd\bar{3}m$ (diamond, Si, Ge, spinel $\mathrm{MgAl_2O_4}$)

Full symbol $F\,\frac{4_1}{d}\,\bar{3}\,\frac{2}{m}$. Cubic. Two places change relative to $Fm\bar{3}m$.

  • $F$: face-centered cubic lattice.
  • $d$ (first position, $\langle100\rangle$): a diamond glide plane perpendicular to $\langle100\rangle$. The translation is $\frac14(\bb\pm\cc)$ and the like, that is, $1/4$ of a lattice translation. At the same position a $4_1$ screw axis runs along the $\langle100\rangle$ direction (it is not a fourfold axis!).
  • $\bar{3}$ (second position, $\langle111\rangle$): four threefold rotoinversion axes along the body diagonals. This is the same as in $Fm\bar{3}m$.
  • $m$ (third position, $\langle110\rangle$): six twofold axes along the face diagonals, and six mirror planes perpendicular to them.

Because of the $d$ and the $4_1$, the group is nonsymmorphic. As shown in Section 5.2.2, the $d$ glide has a translation of $\frac14$, so applying it twice gives $\frac12(\bb+\cc)$—and for this to be a lattice translation the lattice must be $F$-centered. $F$ and $d$ always appear as a set.

With $d\to m$ and $4_1\to4$, the point group is $m\bar{3}m$. Diamond is centrosymmetric and therefore not piezoelectric. On the other hand, even with the same diamond-type framework, the zinc-blende type (ZnS, GaAs), in which two kinds of atoms occupy the sites alternately, has the space group $F\bar{4}3m$ and the point group $\bar{4}3m$; it loses centrosymmetry and becomes piezoelectric.

Example 5.6: $P6_3mc$ (wurtzite-type ZnO, GaN, AlN)

Hexagonal. We use the hexagonal row of Table 5.5.

  • $P$: simple hexagonal lattice.
  • $6_3$ (first position, $[001]$): a $6_3$ screw axis parallel to the $c$ axis. The operation is a $60^\circ$ rotation followed by a lift of $\cc/2$. It is not a sixfold rotation axis.
  • $m$ (second position, $\langle100\rangle$): mirror planes perpendicular to the three directions $[100],[010],[\bar{1}\bar{1}0]$ (three planes).
  • $c$ (third position, $\langle1\bar{1}0\rangle$): $c$ glide planes perpendicular to the three directions $[1\bar{1}0],[120],[\bar{2}\bar{1}0]$ (three planes). The translation is $\cc/2$.

With $6_3\to6$ and $c\to m$, the point group is $6mm$—a polar point group. The polar axis is $[001]$. Therefore ZnO and GaN are both pyroelectric and have a spontaneous polarization along the $c$ axis. The well-known problem in blue-LED GaN-based devices, that the “polarization field” lowers the emission efficiency of quantum wells (the quantum-confined Stark effect), is already written into the point group $6mm$ of this symbol.

Note that $P6_3cm$ (No. 185), in which the positions of $m$ and $c$ are interchanged, is a different space group ($\mathrm{YMnO_3}$ and others are of this type). It is a good example of the fact that the order of the positions carries meaning.

Example 5.7: $P4mm$ and $P4/mmm$ (two types of tetragonal perovskite)

$P4mm$ (No. 99). The room-temperature phase of $\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$ (the tetragonal ferroelectric phase, $278\,\mathrm{K}<T<393\,\mathrm{K}$).

  • $P$: simple tetragonal lattice.
  • $4$ (first position, $[001]$): a fourfold rotation axis along the $c$ axis (one axis).
  • $m$ (second position, $\langle100\rangle$): mirror planes perpendicular to $[100],[010]$ (two planes).
  • $m$ (third position, $\langle110\rangle$): mirror planes perpendicular to $[110],[1\bar{1}0]$ (two planes).

The order is $4\times2=8$, and the point group $4mm$ is polar. There is neither an inversion center nor a mirror plane perpendicular to the $c$ axis. This is why a structure in which the Ti ions are displaced along the $c$ axis is allowed and a spontaneous polarization arises.

$P4/mmm$ (No. 123). Full symbol $P\,\frac{4}{m}\,\frac{2}{m}\,\frac{2}{m}$. This is the space group of highest symmetry among the tetragonal ones.

  • $4/m$ (first position, $[001]$): a fourfold axis along the $c$ axis and a mirror plane on the $(001)$ plane.
  • $m$ (second position, $\langle100\rangle$): the full form is $2/m$. Twofold axes along $[100],[010]$, with mirror planes perpendicular to them.
  • $m$ (third position, $\langle110\rangle$): the full form is $2/m$. Twofold axes along $[110],[1\bar{1}0]$, with mirror planes perpendicular to them.

The order is 16, the point group is $4/mmm$, and the group is centrosymmetric. The only difference between $P4mm$ and $P4/mmm$ is the presence or absence of the $m$ in the first position (= the $(001)$ mirror plane). When this single plane is added, “up” and “down” along the $c$ axis become equivalent, and polarization is forbidden. A ferroelectric phase transition can be described as the disappearance of the $(001)$ mirror plane and the inversion center from a $P4/mmm$-like symmetry, leaving $P4mm$ (the actual high-temperature phase of $\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$ is cubic, $Pm\bar{3}m$, and $P4mm$ is a subgroup of it).

Example 5.8: $Pnma$ ($\mathrm{GdFeO_3}$-type perovskite, $\mathrm{CaTiO_3}$, $\mathrm{MgSiO_3}$, many $ABO_3$)

Full symbol $P\,\frac{2_1}{n}\,\frac{2_1}{m}\,\frac{2_1}{a}$. Since the crystal is orthorhombic, the three positions refer to the individual axes $[100],[010],[001]$ (not to sets of equivalent directions).

  • $P$: simple orthorhombic lattice.
  • $n$ (first position, $[100]$): an $n$ glide plane perpendicular to $[100]$ (= lying in the $(100)$ plane). The translation is $\frac12(\bb+\cc)$. At the same position a $2_1$ screw axis runs along the $[100]$ direction.
  • $m$ (second position, $[010]$): the $(010)$ plane is a mirror plane. A $2_1$ screw axis runs along the $[010]$ direction.
  • $a$ (third position, $[001]$): the $(001)$ plane is an $a$ glide plane. The translation is $\aa/2$. A $2_1$ screw axis runs along the $[001]$ direction.

The point group is $mmm$ (order 8, centrosymmetric). When the $BO_6$ octahedra of an $\mathrm{ABO_3}$ perovskite tilt (octahedral tilting), the cubic $Pm\bar{3}m$ structure descends to this $Pnma$. $\mathrm{CaTiO_3}$, $\mathrm{LaFeO_3}$, $\mathrm{GdFeO_3}$, and bridgmanite $\mathrm{MgSiO_3}$, the main mineral of the lower mantle—all are of this type. It is one of the space groups that occur most frequently among inorganic crystals.

Note on the setting. For orthorhombic crystals there are six ways of relabeling the axes (permuting $a,b,c$), and the same structure is written as $Pnma$, $Pbnm$, $Pmcn$, and so on. If you see $Pbnm$ in a paper, simply read it as “an alternative setting of $Pnma$.” The space-group number, No. 62, is the same for all of them.

Example 5.9: $R\bar{3}c$ (calcite $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$, corundum $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$, $\mathrm{LaAlO_3}$)

Full symbol $R\,\bar{3}\,\frac{2}{c}$. Trigonal (shown in hexagonal axes). For the $R$ lattice the third position is not used, so there are only two positions.

  • $R$: rhombohedral lattice. In hexagonal axes it has the centering translations $\left(\frac23,\frac13,\frac13\right)$ and $\left(\frac13,\frac23,\frac23\right)$, and the unit cell contains three lattice points.
  • $\bar{3}$ (first position, $[001]$): a threefold rotoinversion axis along the $c$ axis. It contains both the threefold rotation and the inversion center.
  • $c$ (second position, $\langle100\rangle$): the full form is $\frac{2}{c}$. There are twofold rotation axes along the three directions of the $[100]$ type, and $c$ glide planes perpendicular to them (translation $\cc/2$).

With $c\to m$, the point group is $\bar{3}m$ (order 12, centrosymmetric). Because of the $c$ glide, the group is nonsymmorphic.

$\mathrm{LaAlO_3}$ becomes $R\bar{3}c$ as a result of the rotation of the $\mathrm{AlO_6}$ octahedra of the $Pm\bar{3}m$ perovskite about the $[111]$ axis (an $a^-a^-a^-$ tilt). Together with the fact that $Pnma$ of Example 5.8 corresponds to another tilt pattern ($a^-a^-c^+$), this gives the outlook that the space group of a structural polymorph of the perovskite is determined by “about which axes, and how, the octahedra tilt.”

Example 5.10: $P2_1/c$ (the most frequent space group among organic crystals)

Full symbol $P\,1\,\frac{2_1}{c}\,1$. Monoclinic ($b$-axis setting). There is effectively only one position.

  • $P$: simple monoclinic lattice.
  • $2_1/c$ (the only symmetry direction $[010]$): this is the only content.
    • $2_1$: a $2_1$ screw axis parallel to the $b$ axis ($[010]$): rotate by $180^\circ$ and then translate by $\bb/2$.
    • $c$: a $c$ glide plane perpendicular to the $b$ axis (the $(010)$ plane). The translation is $\cc/2$.
  • The $1$'s before and after in the full symbol mean “there is nothing along $[100]$ and $[001]$.” They are usually omitted, and the symbol is written $P2_1/c$.

In the standard setting with the inversion center at the origin, there are only four symmetry operations in all: $\{E|\bm{0}\}$, $\{C_2(y)|\frac12\bb+\frac12\cc\}$, $\{i|\bm{0}\}$, and $\{\sigma_y|\frac12\bb+\frac12\cc\}$ (together with their products with lattice translations). The point group is $2/m$, which is centrosymmetric, of order 4.

You may wonder why both $\frac12\bb$ and $\frac12\cc$ appear in the translational parts. The essential translation of the $2_1$ screw axis is $\bb/2$, parallel to the axis, and the essential translation of the $c$ glide plane is $\cc/2$, within the plane. The other component added to each—the $\frac12\cc$ on the $2_1$ side and the $\frac12\bb$ on the glide side—is a nonessential component that expresses a shift of the position of the axis or plane by $1/4$, and, as we saw in Example 5.2, it is fixed by the choice of origin. Since we have fixed the origin at an inversion center, these components cannot be removed.

This space group is the overwhelmingly most frequent one: roughly one third of organic molecular crystals belong to it. The reason is intuitive and comes from the demands of packing: (i) with an inversion center, molecules can be packed efficiently “face to face,” and (ii) $2_1$ screw axes and glide planes are well suited to interlocking bumpy molecules without gaps. Space groups of too high symmetry (such as the cubic ones) are too cramped for low-symmetry molecules and are therefore hard to realize. This is a fact worth remembering: “the higher the symmetry, the more likely it occurs” is not true.

5.5 Determining the Space Group and the Systematic Absences

In Chapter 3 we saw that the lattice centering, $I$ centering and $F$ centering, eliminates certain reflections. In fact, screw axes and glide planes eliminate reflections in the same way. Moreover, the way they do so differs from one symmetry element to another, so by reading the pattern of the missing reflections, we can tell which screw axes and glide planes are present. This is the core of the procedure for determining the space group from experiment.

5.5.1 General theory — why reflections vanish

Derivation: The condition that a symmetry operation $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}$ imposes on the structure factor

In Chapter 3 the structure factor was given as

$$ S_{\GG} = \sum_j f_j\, e^{-i\GG\cdot\rr_j} $$

Below we write it in fractional coordinates. Let $\rr_j = x_j\aa + y_j\bb + z_j\cc$ and $\GG = h\bm{b}_1+k\bm{b}_2+l\bm{b}_3$. Since $\bm{b}_i\cdot\aa_j = 2\pi\delta_{ij}$, we have

$$ \GG\cdot\rr_j = 2\pi\left(hx_j + ky_j + lz_j\right) $$

.

A word about notation. The $\bm{b}_1,\bm{b}_2,\bm{b}_3$ carrying subscripts here are the primitive reciprocal lattice vectors of Chapter 3, and are distinct from the primitive vector $\bb$ of the real-space lattice that appears many times in this chapter. The corresponding real-space lattice basis is written with numbers as $\aa_1=\aa$, $\aa_2=\bb$, $\aa_3=\cc$.

Suppose the crystal has a symmetry operation $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}$. Then the set of atoms is mapped exactly onto itself by this operation. Therefore the value of the sum does not change if we change the way of taking the sum from “the $j$th atom” to “the image of the $j$th atom under $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}$”:

$$ S_{\GG} = \sum_j f_j\, e^{-i\GG\cdot\left(R\rr_j + \bm{\tau}\right)} $$

We split the exponent. Since $e^{-i\GG\cdot\bm{\tau}}$ does not depend on $j$, it can be taken outside the sum:

$$ S_{\GG} = e^{-i\GG\cdot\bm{\tau}}\sum_j f_j\, e^{-i\GG\cdot R\rr_j} $$

Now we use a property of the inner product. Since $R$ is an orthogonal matrix ($R^{\mathsf T} = R^{-1}$),

$$ \GG\cdot(R\rr_j) = \GG^{\mathsf T}R\rr_j = \left(R^{\mathsf T}\GG\right)^{\mathsf T}\rr_j = \left(R^{-1}\GG\right)\cdot\rr_j $$

Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} S_{\GG} = e^{-i\GG\cdot\bm{\tau}}\; S_{R^{-1}\GG} \label{eq:5-sf-symmetry} \end{equation} $$

Now let us focus on a special $\GG$ such that $R^{-1}\GG = \GG$. Then Eq. \eqref{eq:5-sf-symmetry} becomes

$$ S_{\GG} = e^{-i\GG\cdot\bm{\tau}}\,S_{\GG} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S_{\GG}\left(1 - e^{-i\GG\cdot\bm{\tau}}\right) = 0 $$

Therefore, if $e^{-i\GG\cdot\bm{\tau}} \ne 1$, we must have $S_{\GG}=0$. To summarize,

$$ \begin{equation} R^{-1}\GG = \GG \ \text{and}\ \GG\cdot\bm{\tau} \notin 2\pi\mathbb{Z} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_{\GG} = 0 \label{eq:5-extinction} \end{equation} $$

∎ This is the general form of the systematic-absence rule. If $\bm{\tau}=\bm{0}$ (a pure point-group operation), nothing vanishes. The only operations that cause reflections to vanish are those with a nonprimitive translation $\bm{\tau}$, that is, screw axes and glide planes.

The condition $R^{-1}\GG=\GG$ has a clear geometric meaning. If $R$ is a rotation about an axis $\uu$, then $\GG \parallel \uu$; if $R$ is a reflection in a plane with normal $\bm{n}$, then $\GG \perp \bm{n}$ ($\GG$ lies in the plane). The former corresponds to a single line in the reciprocal lattice (such as $h00$ or $00l$), and the latter to a single plane (such as $hk0$ or $h0l$). Hence,

  • screw axis $\Rightarrow$ reflections on a line vanish (serial condition)
  • glide plane $\Rightarrow$ reflections in a plane vanish (zonal condition)
  • lattice centering $\Rightarrow$ a condition is imposed on all reflections (integral condition, Chapter 3)

and we obtain a three-level hierarchy.

Example 5.11: Systematic absences for a $2_1$ screw axis ($\parallel\bb$)

In a crystal with a $2_1$ screw axis along the $b$ axis, which reflections vanish?

Solution. The operation is $\{C_2(y)\,|\,\frac12\bb\}$, and in fractional coordinates $\bm{\tau} = (0,\frac12,0)$.

Step 1: Find the $\GG$ with $R^{-1}\GG=\GG$. Since $C_2(y)$ is $(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,y,-z)$, in the reciprocal lattice it is also $(h,k,l)\mapsto(-h,k,-l)$. This equals $(h,k,l)$ when

$$ -h = h,\quad -l = l \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h = 0,\ l = 0 $$

that is, $\GG = (0,k,0)$, the reflections of type $0k0$.

Step 2: Compute the phase.

$$ \GG\cdot\bm{\tau} = 2\pi\left(h\cdot0 + k\cdot\tfrac12 + l\cdot0\right) = 2\pi\cdot\frac{k}{2} = \pi k $$

Step 3: Write the condition. $e^{-i\pi k}=1$, that is, $\pi k \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, holds when $k$ is even. Therefore

$$ \boxed{\;0k0 \ \text{appears only when}\ k = 2n \ \text{holds}\;} $$

∎ The reflections $010, 030, 050,\ldots$ vanish, and only $020, 040, 060,\ldots$ remain.

Intuitive restatement. When a $2_1$ axis is present, viewed along the $b$ axis the atoms appear every $b/2$ (though in different orientations). The period along the $b$ axis is apparently $b/2$, so the period in reciprocal space is doubled and the odd-order reflections vanish; this is the physical picture.

Example 5.12: Systematic absences for a $c$ glide plane ($\perp\bb$)

In a crystal with a $c$ glide plane perpendicular to the $b$ axis, which reflections vanish?

Solution. The operation is $\{\sigma_y\,|\,\frac12\cc\}$, with $\bm{\tau}=(0,0,\frac12)$.

Step 1. $\sigma_y : (h,k,l)\mapsto(h,-k,l)$. From $-k=k$ we get $k=0$, that is, the reflections of type $h0l$.

Step 2. $\GG\cdot\bm{\tau} = 2\pi\left(h\cdot0+0\cdot0+l\cdot\frac12\right) = \pi l$.

Step 3. From $\pi l\in2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, $l$ is even.

$$ \boxed{\;h0l \ \text{appears only when}\ l = 2n \ \text{holds}\;} $$

∎ In the same way, for an $a$ glide ($\bm{\tau}=(\frac12,0,0)$) we get $h=2n$ for $h0l$; for an $n$ glide ($\bm{\tau}=(\frac12,0,\frac12)$), since $\GG\cdot\bm{\tau}=\pi(h+l)$, we get $h+l=2n$ for $h0l$; and for a $d$ glide ($\bm{\tau}=(\frac14,0,\frac14)$), since $\GG\cdot\bm{\tau}=\frac{\pi}{2}(h+l)$, we get $h+l=4n$ for $h0l$.

(b) $P2_1/c$. The only symmetry elements are the $2_1\parallel[010]$ axis and the $c$ glide $\perp[010]$. Therefore

$$ 0k0:\ k=2n, \qquad h0l:\ l=2n $$

∎ That is all. Conversely, if a monoclinic single crystal shows the conditions “$0k0$ only for even $k$, and $h0l$ only for even $l$,” the space group is (almost) certainly $P2_1/c$, because among the monoclinic space groups, No. 14 is the only one that gives this pair of conditions.

Caution: The systematic absences alone do not determine the space group uniquely

The systematic absences see only “symmetry operations that include a translation.” Therefore, the presence or absence of symmetry elements that include no translation is not reflected in the systematic absences.

Typical examples are $P2_1$ (No. 4) and $P2_1/m$ (No. 11). In both, the only systematic absence is “$0k0: k=2n$,” and they cannot be distinguished. The difference is the presence or absence of an inversion center, which is hard to see in ordinary X-ray diffraction because of Friedel's law, discussed in Chapter 4.

In practice, the space group is determined by cross-checking several kinds of information such as the following.

  1. Systematic absences → the candidates are narrowed to a few (check against the “Reflection conditions” column of the International Tables)
  2. Laue group (the symmetry of the diffraction intensities) → the crystal system and the candidate groups are narrowed further
  3. Intensity statistics (the distribution of $\abs{S}^2$) → gives a rough indication of whether the crystal is centrosymmetric
  4. Second-harmonic generation (SHG), piezoelectric response → direct test of centrosymmetry
  5. Actually carrying out structure refinement and seeing whether the $R$ factor decreases

This is why “determining the space group” is not mere measurement but inference.

5.6 Wyckoff Positions — The Art of Writing a Structure in Three Lines

The unit cell of $\mathrm{SrTiO_3}$ contains five atoms. Yet papers and CIF files usually list only three lines. Why do we not need to write them all? The answer is the Wyckoff position.

Definition: general position, special position, and site symmetry

Consider a point $\rr$ in a crystal with space group $G$.

  • Site symmetry group (point symmetry group, site symmetry): the set of all elements of $G$ that leave $\rr$ fixed, $$ G_{\rr} = \left\{\, g \in G \;:\; g\rr = \rr \,\right\} $$ This is always one of the point groups (a translation always moves a point, so $G_\rr$ contains no pure translation).
  • General position: a point for which $G_{\rr}$ consists of the identity element only (site symmetry $= 1$). Most points are of this kind.
  • Special position: a point for which $G_{\rr}$ is larger than the identity element. Points on a rotation axis, on a mirror plane, or at an inversion center are of this kind.
  • Multiplicity: the number of equivalent points in the unit cell obtained by applying all elements of $G$ to $\rr$.
  • Wyckoff symbol: a symbol that pairs the multiplicity with a letter of the alphabet $a, b, c, \ldots$ (assigned starting from $a$ in decreasing order of symmetry). It is written like $1a$, $3c$, $48n$.

Theorem 5.1 (orbit–stabilizer theorem)

Let $M$ be the multiplicity in the unit cell, $\abs{G_\rr}$ the order of the site symmetry group of that position, and $M_{\text{gen}}$ the multiplicity of the general position. Then

$$ \begin{equation} M \times \abs{G_{\rr}} = M_{\text{gen}} = (\text{order of the point group}) \times (\text{number of lattice points in the unit cell}) \label{eq:5-orbit-stabilizer} \end{equation} $$

Meaning. An atom at a place of higher symmetry (larger $\abs{G_\rr}$) has fewer equivalent partners (smaller $M$). The product is always constant.

Reason. Each of the $h$ operations of the point group maps $\rr$ somewhere. If $\rr$ is left fixed by $q = \abs{G_\rr}$ operations, then the $h$ operations give the same destination in groups of $q$, so there are $h/q$ distinct destinations. Multiplying by the number of lattice points gives the number of equivalent points in the unit cell.

Example 5.15: The $8a$ position of diamond

In diamond $Fd\bar{3}m$ (No. 227, origin choice 2), carbon sits at Wyckoff $8a$, coordinates $(\frac18,\frac18,\frac18)$, with site symmetry $\bar{4}3m$ (order 24). Verify Theorem 5.1.

Solution. The point group is $m\bar{3}m$ (order 48), and the lattice is $F$ (four lattice points per unit cell). Therefore

$$ M_{\text{gen}} = 48\times4 = 192 $$

Indeed, the general position of No. 227 is $192i$. For $8a$,

$$ M\times\abs{G_\rr} = 8\times24 = 192\ \checkmark $$

∎ They agree. That the site symmetry is $\bar{4}3m$ ($T_d$) says the same thing as the fact that the carbon atoms in diamond are tetrahedrally coordinated. It is $\bar{4}3m$, not $m\bar{3}m$; that is, there is no inversion center at the position of a carbon atom. Even so, the crystal as a whole is centrosymmetric, with $Fd\bar{3}m$ (the inversion centers lie at places where there is no carbon atom, at the midpoints between two carbons).

This is an important distinction. Even if a crystal is centrosymmetric, the individual atomic sites are not necessarily centrosymmetric. The symmetry of an atomic site (the site symmetry) is the quantity that directly determines how the $d$ orbitals of that atom split (Chapter 10), which vibrational modes are allowed, and what is seen in NMR and Mössbauer spectroscopy.

Math Note: Bieberbach's theorem and the number of space groups by dimension

To the question “why does the list end with a finite number?” L. Bieberbach gave an answer in 1911–1912 (as part of Hilbert's 18th problem).

  • Bieberbach's first theorem: An $n$-dimensional crystallographic group always contains a translation subgroup of rank $n$ as a normal subgroup, and its quotient group is a finite group.
  • Bieberbach's second theorem: Two $n$-dimensional crystallographic groups that are isomorphic as abstract groups are mapped onto each other by an affine transformation.
  • Bieberbach's third theorem: In each dimension $n$, there are only finitely many isomorphism classes of crystallographic groups.

The third theorem is what guarantees that the count “stops at 230.” We do not give the proof, since it goes beyond the scope of this book, but its essence lies in the first theorem: every crystallographic group can be decomposed into “translations (infinite but simple) + a finite point group,” and the point-group part is kept finite by the crystallographic restriction theorem. 5.8 Summary and Exercises

5.8.1 Summary

  • Seitz symbol $\{R|\tvec\}\rr = R\rr+\tvec$. The product is $\{R_2|\tvec_2\}\{R_1|\tvec_1\}=\{R_2R_1|R_2\tvec_1+\tvec_2\}$, and the inverse element is $\{R|\tvec\}^{-1}=\{R^{-1}|-R^{-1}\tvec\}$. Do not forget that the rotation on the left acts on the translation on the right.
  • The translation subgroup $T$ is a normal subgroup (since $\{R|\vv\}\{E|\TT\}\{R|\vv\}^{-1}=\{E|R\TT\}$), and the quotient group $G/T$ is the point group (the crystal class). It is useful to regard a space group as “a point group in which a translation is assigned to each element.”
  • A screw axis $n_m$ is a rotation by $360^\circ/n$ combined with a translation by $\frac{m}{n}\cc$. Applying it $n$ times gives $\{E|m\cc\}$. There are 11 kinds, of which 4 pairs are enantiomorphic pairs. A glide plane is a reflection combined with a translation by half of a length in the plane (1/4 for $d$). The $d$ glide appears only in $F$-centered and $I$-centered lattices.
  • 73 symmorphic space groups (the symbol contains no screw or glide letters) and 157 nonsymmorphic ones. The number 73 is the number of “point groups $\times$ compatible Bravais types” plus 7 duplicates arising from the choice of orientation.
  • The international notation is “Bravais symbol + three positions.” The direction that each position indicates is fixed for each crystal system (Table 5.5). For the cubic system, the first position is $\langle100\rangle$, the second position is $\langle111\rangle$, and the third position is $\langle110\rangle$. A number means an axis parallel to that direction, and $m$ or $a,b,c,n,d,e$ means a plane perpendicular to that direction.
  • The full symbol of $Pm\bar{3}m$ is $P\,\frac{4}{m}\,\bar{3}\,\frac{2}{m}$. The first $m$ stands for the three mirror planes of type $(100)$, $\bar{3}$ for the four threefold rotoinversion axes along the body diagonals, and the last $m$ for the six mirror planes of type $(110)$. Together they reproduce the 48 operations of $O_h$. The $4$ and the $2$ can be omitted because the product of two mirror planes is a rotation by “twice the angle between the two planes” ($45^\circ\times2=90^\circ$).
  • Systematic absences: If $R^{-1}\GG=\GG$ and $\GG\cdot\bm{\tau}\notin2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, then $S_\GG=0$. A screw axis eliminates the reflections along a line, and a glide plane eliminates the reflections in a plane. For $2_1\parallel\bb$, $0k0: k=2n$; for a $c$ glide $\perp\bb$, $h0l: l=2n$.
  • Wyckoff position: multiplicity $\times$ order of the site symmetry = multiplicity of the general position (= order of the point group $\times$ number of lattice points). $\mathrm{SrTiO_3}$ can be written in three rows, $1a$ (Sr), $1b$ (Ti), and $3c$ (O), and has no free parameters.
  • 230 = 219 (types of abstract groups) + 11 (enantiomorphic pairs). That the list ends with a finite number is guaranteed by Bieberbach's theorems.

5.8.2 Exercises

Exercise 5.1: Constructing the multiplication table of $P2_1/c$

The four symmetry operations of $P2_1/c$ (No. 14) can be written in fractional coordinates as follows.

$$ g_1 = (x,y,z),\quad g_2 = \left(-x,\ y+\tfrac12,\ -z+\tfrac12\right),\quad g_3 = (-x,-y,-z),\quad g_4 = \left(x,\ -y+\tfrac12,\ z+\tfrac12\right) $$

(1) Rewrite $g_2, g_3, g_4$ in the form of the Seitz symbol $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}$.

(2) Compute $g_2^2$ and verify that it is a lattice translation.

(3) Compute $g_3 g_2$ and verify that it equals $g_4$ (up to a lattice translation). That is, show that if a $2_1$ axis and an inversion center are present, a $c$ glide arises automatically.

(4) Construct the multiplication table and verify that the four operations form a group (modulo lattice translations).

Hint: (1) $g_2=\{C_2(y)|(0,\frac12,\frac12)\}$, $g_3=\{i|\bm{0}\}$, $g_4=\{\sigma_y|(0,\frac12,\frac12)\}$. (2) Noting that $C_2(y)(0,\frac12,\frac12)=(0,\frac12,-\frac12)$, we get $g_2^2=\{E|(0,1,0)\}=\{E|\bb\}$. (3) $g_3g_2 = \{i\,C_2(y)\,|\,i(0,\frac12,\frac12)\} = \{\sigma_y|(0,-\frac12,-\frac12)\}$, which equals $g_4$ multiplied by $\{E|-\bb-\cc\}$.

Exercise 5.2: Read $P4_2/mnm$ (rutile-type $\mathrm{TiO_2}$) one character at a time

The full symbol is $P\,\frac{4_2}{m}\,\frac{2_1}{n}\,\frac{2}{m}$.

(1) State the crystal system, and write out from Table 5.5 the directions that the three positions indicate.

(2) For each of $P$, $4_2$, the first $m$, $n$, and the last $m$, state one by one “what kind of symmetry element it is, and in which direction.”

(3) What is the point group of this space group? Is it centrosymmetric? Can rutile-type $\mathrm{TiO_2}$ be a piezoelectric material?

(4) Is it symmorphic or nonsymmorphic? Give the reason as well.

(5) Using Table 5.6, state what systematic absences appear for $00l$ and $hk0$.

Hint: (1) Tetragonal. The first position is $[001]$, the second position is $\langle100\rangle$, and the third position is $\langle110\rangle$. (2) $P$ = simple tetragonal lattice. $4_2$ = a $4_2$ screw axis parallel to $[001]$ (rotate by $90^\circ$ and raise by $\cc/2$). First $m$ = the $(001)$ mirror plane perpendicular to $[001]$. $n$ = an $n$ glide plane perpendicular to $\langle100\rangle$ (with a translation such as $\frac12(\bb+\cc)$), together with a $2_1$ screw axis in the same direction. Last $m$ = two mirror planes perpendicular to $\langle110\rangle$, and two twofold axes parallel to $\langle110\rangle$. (3) $4/mmm$, centrosymmetric, no piezoelectricity. (4) Nonsymmorphic, because it contains $4_2$ and $n$. (5) From $4_2\parallel[001]$, $00l: l=2n$. For $hk0$ there is no condition, because the $(001)$ plane is a pure mirror plane. For $0kl$, the $n$ glide gives $k+l=2n$.

Exercise 5.3: Read $I4_1/amd$ (anatase-type $\mathrm{TiO_2}$, white tin $\beta$-Sn)

The full symbol is $I\,\frac{4_1}{a}\,\frac{2}{m}\,\frac{2}{d}$.

(1) Read each of $I$, $4_1$, $a$, $m$, and $d$, together with its direction.

(2) What must the lattice be like for a $d$ glide to appear? Does this symbol satisfy that condition?

(3) State the point group.

(4) What systematic absence appears for $00l$? Combining it with the absences due to $I$ centering, write the condition for $hkl$ as a whole.

(5) What happens when $4_1$ is applied four times? Calculate it using Eq. \eqref{eq:5-screw-n}.

Hint: (1) $I$ = body-centered tetragonal lattice. $4_1$ = a right-handed $4_1$ screw axis parallel to $[001]$. $a$ = an $a$ glide on the $(001)$ plane perpendicular to $[001]$ (translation $\aa/2$). $m$ = a mirror plane perpendicular to $\langle100\rangle$ (and a twofold axis parallel to $\langle100\rangle$). $d$ = a $d$ glide perpendicular to $\langle110\rangle$ (and a twofold axis parallel to $\langle110\rangle$). (2) An $F$-centered or $I$-centered lattice. Since the lattice is $I$, the condition is satisfied. (3) $4/mmm$. (4) From $4_1$, $00l: l=4n$; from $I$ centering, $hkl: h+k+l=2n$. (5) $\left(4_1\right)^4=\{E|\cc\}$.

Exercise 5.4: Narrowing down the space group from the systematic absences

For a certain orthorhombic single crystal, the following reflection conditions were observed.

$$ hkl:\ \text{no condition},\qquad 0kl:\ k=2n,\qquad h0l:\ l=2n,\qquad hk0:\ \text{no condition} $$

(1) Which of $P, C, I, F$ is the lattice? Give the reason.

(2) The condition on $0kl$ comes from what kind of glide plane, perpendicular to which direction?

(3) The condition on $h0l$ comes from what kind of glide plane, perpendicular to which direction?

(4) What can be expected to occupy the third position? List the candidate space groups.

(5) Suppose that $0k0: k=2n$ and $00l: l=2n$ were also observed. Does this provide new information for narrowing down the candidates in (4)?

Hint: (1) There is no condition on $hkl$, so the lattice is $P$. (2) A $b$ glide perpendicular to $[100]$. (3) A $c$ glide perpendicular to $[010]$. (4) The form is $Pbc?$. If $? = m$, it is $Pbcm$ (No. 57); if $? = 2_1$, it is $Pbc2_1$ (an alternative setting of $Pca2_1$, No. 29). The case $? = n$ ($Pbcn$, No. 60) is excluded at this point: an $n$ glide $\perp[001]$ in the third position would require $hk0: h+k=2n$ by Table 5.6, which contradicts the given datum “$hk0$: no condition.” (5) No. The condition $0k0: k=2n$ is just the condition $0kl: k=2n$ of (2) with $l=0$, and $00l: l=2n$ is just the condition $h0l: l=2n$ of (3) with $h=0$; both are restatements of information that has already been used. In fact, $Pbcm$ and $Pbc2_1$ have exactly the same systematic absences ($0kl: k=2n$, $h0l: l=2n$, $0k0: k=2n$, $00l: l=2n$), so diffraction systematic absences alone can never distinguish between them. Since the difference between the two is the presence or absence of an inversion center, the decision must be made from whether SHG (second-harmonic generation) appears, or from the statistical distribution of diffraction intensities.

Exercise 5.5: Wyckoff positions and multiplicity

(1) Rock-salt-type NaCl has space group $Fm\bar{3}m$, with Na at $4a\ (0,0,0)$ and Cl at $4b\ (\frac12,\frac12,\frac12)$. Find the multiplicity $M_{\text{gen}}$ of the general position from Eq. \eqref{eq:5-orbit-stabilizer}, and state the order of the site symmetry of $4a$.

(2) In rutile-type $\mathrm{TiO_2}$ ($P4_2/mnm$, point group $4/mmm$, $P$ lattice), Ti is at $2a\ (0,0,0)$ and O is at $4f\ (x,x,0)$ ($x\approx0.305$). Find $M_{\text{gen}}$ and the orders of the site symmetries of $2a$ and $4f$. What is the number $Z$ of formula units in the unit cell?

(3) In (2), how many free parameters are needed in total to determine the structure (counting the lattice constants as well)? Compare with $\mathrm{SrTiO_3}$ (Example 5.14).

Hint: (1) $M_{\text{gen}} = 48\times4 = 192$. For $4a$, $192/4 = 48$, that is, the site symmetry is $m\bar{3}m$. (2) $M_{\text{gen}} = 16\times1 = 16$. For $2a$, $16/2=8$ ($mmm$); for $4f$, $16/4=4$ ($m.2m$). With 2 Ti and 4 O, $Z=2$. (3) The lattice constants $a$ and $c$ and the internal coordinate $x$ make a total of three. For $\mathrm{SrTiO_3}$ there was only one, $a$.

Exercise 5.6: Symmorphicity and the breakdown of the 230

(1) Determine whether each of the following space groups is symmorphic or nonsymmorphic.
$Pm\bar{3}m$, $Fd\bar{3}m$, $I4/mmm$, $P6_3/mmc$, $R\bar{3}m$, $Cmcm$, $P2_1 2_1 2_1$, $F\bar{4}3m$

(2) There are 16 symmorphic space groups in the tetragonal system (Table 5.3). Write out the 16 explicitly from the seven tetragonal point groups $4,\bar{4},4/m,422,4mm,\bar{4}2m,4/mmm$ and the two lattices $P, I$.

(3) $P4_12_12$ and $P4_32_12$ are isomorphic as abstract groups but are counted as different space groups. Explain the reason. Also consider how these two could be distinguished experimentally.

Hint: (1) The symmorphic ones are the four $Pm\bar{3}m$, $I4/mmm$, $R\bar{3}m$, and $F\bar{4}3m$. The rest contain a screw-axis or glide symbol and are therefore nonsymmorphic. (2) The 16 are $P4, I4, P\bar{4}, I\bar{4}, P4/m, I4/m, P422, I422, P4mm, I4mm, P\bar{4}2m, P\bar{4}m2, I\bar{4}m2, I\bar{4}2m, P4/mmm, I4/mmm$. Note that only $\bar{4}2m$ can be oriented in two ways. (3) One has a right-handed $4_1$ screw axis and the other a left-handed $4_3$ screw axis, and they are related as mirror images. To distinguish them, one can use measurement of optical rotation, circular dichroism, or determination of the absolute structure using anomalous dispersion (the Flack parameter).
