Introduction to Solid-State Physics— From Crystal Symmetry to Physical Properties —

The foundation of solid-state physics is crystallography. This book was written as teaching material for the Mochizuki Research Group, Department of Materials Science and Technology, Faculty of Advanced Engineering, Tokyo University of Science. It starts from the description of crystal structures, builds up the tool of group theory, and on that basis is organized so that the reader can trace the path to the properties of solids—lattice vibrations, electronic bands, semiconductors, dielectricity, and magnetism—as a single thread. In Part IV, we pile the physics of semiconductor devices on top of that—conduction and scattering, high-field effects, junctions and interfaces, light emission, and nonlinear optics. In Part V, we use the idea of the quasiparticle to read what happens when the electrons and phonons that Part III treated as “mutually independent particles” interact—electron–electron interaction and Mott insulators, electron–phonon interaction and Cooper pairs, phonon–phonon interaction and thermal expansion and heat conduction, excitons and polarons, LO–TO splitting, optical excitation and polaritons, infrared and Raman spectroscopy, one-dimensional electron systems, random systems, and finally superconductivity. The intended readers are third-year undergraduates who have studied calculus, linear algebra, and elementary quantum mechanics (to the extent of having solved the Schrödinger equation). Part V is also written so that it can be read by anyone who has finished Part III, and it does not assume linear response theory or second quantization.

This book has two clear principles. The first is not to let group theory end as abstract theory. If one lines up only definitions and theorems, group theory becomes terribly abstract and without any tangible feel. But if we keep one concrete point group at hand—for example $C_{3v}$—fill in its multiplication table ourselves, divide it into classes ourselves, and construct its character table ourselves, the picture becomes surprisingly clear. In this book, whenever a theorem is stated, it is applied to a concrete example on the spot, in the form of an example in which the reader works things out by hand.

The second is not to omit the algebraic manipulations. Standard textbooks of solid-state physics often show only the starting equation and the final equation and leave everything in between to the reader. In this book, we write out all of that “in between.” The mathematics needed to fill in the gaps is collected in a self-contained form in the “Mathematical Notes” of Appendix A.

How to read this book
  • Read straight through: read from Chapter 1 in order. Part I (crystallography) and Part II (group theory) support each other, so we recommend reading them without skipping.
  • If you only want crystallography: Chapter 1 → Chapter 2 → Chapter 3 → Chapter 4 → Chapter 5 → Appendix B
  • If you only want group theory: Chapter 4 → Chapter 6 → Chapter 7 → Chapter 8 → Chapter 9 → Chapter 10
  • If you want to start from the properties of solids: Chapter 3 (reciprocal lattice) → Chapter 13 → Chapter 15 → Chapter 16. When a discussion of symmetry comes up, return to Part II.
  • If you want to study semiconductor devices: Chapter 15 → Chapter 16 → Chapter 17 → Part IV (from Chapter 21 through Chapter 26 in order). Follow the physics of devices as a single thread, through conduction and scattering, high-field effects, junctions and interfaces, light emission, and nonlinear optics. Optical absorption and photovoltage (Chapter 33), one-dimensional electron systems (Chapter 35), and random systems (Chapter 36) are treated in Part V. Light emission (Chapter 25) and optical absorption (Chapter 33) are processes inverse to each other, so it is good to read them together.
  • Moving on to Part V (Advanced Topics): once you have finished Part III, first read Chapter 27 (Introduction to Quasiparticles). The vocabulary used in every chapter of Part V—creation and annihilation operators, how to read Feynman diagrams, and the self-energy (energy shift and lifetime)—is prepared there.
  • If you want to study quasiparticles and interactions: Chapter 27 → Chapter 28 (electron–electron interaction) → Chapter 29 (electron–phonon interaction) → Chapter 30 (phonon–phonon interaction). Follow the three kinds of interactions in solids in turn.
  • If you want to study light and the lattice: Chapter 27 → Chapter 29, Section 29.2 → Chapter 31 (excitons and polarons) → Chapter 32 (LO–TO splitting) → Chapter 33 (optical excitation phenomena and polaritons) → Chapter 34 (infrared and Raman spectroscopy). Chapter 31 uses Chapter 33, Section 33.7 (exciton absorption), and Chapter 34 uses Chapter 10 (selection rules for molecular vibrations), so go back to them as needed.
  • If you want to study superconductivity: Chapter 15 → Chapter 27 → Chapter 29 (especially Sections 29.6–29.8 on the electron–electron attraction and Cooper pairs) → Chapter 37. An equation of the same form as the BCS transition temperature appears in Chapter 35, Section 35.1 (Peierls transition), so reading them together gives a clearer view.
  • If you want to study the boundary between metals and insulators: Chapter 16 → Chapter 28 (Mott insulators) → Chapter 36 (Anderson localization and the Mott transition in semiconductors) → Chapter 35 (Peierls transition).
  • If you treat phonons by first-principles calculation: Chapter 13 → Chapter 29, Section 29.3 (phonons with imaginary frequencies) → Chapter 30 (quasi-harmonic approximation, linewidth, thermal conductivity) → Chapter 32 (nonanalytic-term correction) → Chapter 34, Section 34.8 (comparing calculation with experiment).
  • If you are uneasy about the mathematics: look over Appendix A (Mathematical Notes) first. Each chapter has links to the sections you need.
  • If you cannot tell what the horizontal axis of a band diagram ($\Gamma$–$X$–$M$…) means: read Appendix C (Bloch States) first. It is written so that it can be read even if you skip the group theory of Chapter 11.
