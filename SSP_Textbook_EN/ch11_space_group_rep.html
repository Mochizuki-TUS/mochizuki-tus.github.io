Chapter 11Representations of Space Groups and Bloch's Theorem

In Chapters 6 through 10 we assembled the full toolkit of group theory: multiplication tables, classes, irreducible representations, character tables, projection operators, direct products, and selection rules. Yet everything we treated there was a point group with a finite number of elements. A crystal is more than that. A crystal has translations. The totality of symmetry operations including translations is the space group (Chapter 5), and in this chapter we construct the representations of that space group.

Let us state the conclusion first. Bloch's theorem is a statement about the irreducible representations of the translation group. The wave vector $\kk$ is neither momentum divided by $\hbar$ nor a mere label of convenience. $\kk$ is the very name attached to an irreducible representation of the translation group. That is why $\kk$ works as a quantum number, why $\kk$ has meaning only up to a reciprocal lattice vector $\GG$, and why it suffices to look at the first Brillouin zone alone. All of these flow from a single fact: the translation group is an abelian group, and all of its irreducible representations are one-dimensional.

Once we have this viewpoint, the way we read a band diagram changes completely. A band is threefold degenerate at the $\Gamma$ point, splits into two and one along the $\Delta$ line, and sticks together again at the $X$ point. Such a seemingly complicated picture can be explained by one single principle: the dimension of the irreducible representation of the group of the wave vector at that $\kk$. Why is the top of the valence band of Si threefold degenerate? Why, in the diamond structure, do bands necessarily stick together in pairs at the zone-boundary $X$ point? Why can we assert that the Raman peak of Si at $520\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ is due to an optical phonon? All of these are answered in this chapter.

This chapter concludes Part II and is at the same time the entrance to Part III. The concept of $\kk$ built here flows directly into the phonons of Chapter 13, the free electrons of Chapter 15, the energy bands of Chapter 16, and the Fermi surfaces of Chapter 18. It is the chapter that turns group theory from “abstract mathematics” into “a tool for band calculations.”

11.1 The Translation Group $\mathcal{T}$ — The Most Important Subgroup of a Space Group

11.1.1 Review of the Seitz symbol and the product rule

Recall the Seitz symbol $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}$ introduced in Chapter 5. It denotes the operation “first apply the point-group operation $R$ about the origin, then translate by $\bm{\tau}$.” Its action on a position vector $\rr$ is

$$ \begin{equation} \{R|\bm{\tau}\}\,\rr = R\rr + \bm{\tau} \label{eq:11-seitz} \end{equation} $$

From this we derive the product rule. To get used to it, we first write out the calculation without skipping any steps.

Derivation: Product and inverse of Seitz symbols

We apply two operations in succession. The convention is that the one on the right, $\{R_2|\bm{\tau}_2\}$, acts first.

$$ \{R_1|\bm{\tau}_1\}\Bigl(\{R_2|\bm{\tau}_2\}\rr\Bigr) = \{R_1|\bm{\tau}_1\}\bigl(R_2\rr + \bm{\tau}_2\bigr) $$

Now we regard the contents of the parentheses as a single position vector and apply Eq. \eqref{eq:11-seitz}:

$$ = R_1\bigl(R_2\rr + \bm{\tau}_2\bigr) + \bm{\tau}_1 = (R_1R_2)\rr + \bigl(R_1\bm{\tau}_2 + \bm{\tau}_1\bigr) $$

Comparing this with the form of Eq. \eqref{eq:11-seitz}, we see that the point-group part is $R_1R_2$ and the translational part is $R_1\bm{\tau}_2+\bm{\tau}_1$. That is,

$$ \begin{equation} \{R_1|\bm{\tau}_1\}\{R_2|\bm{\tau}_2\} = \{R_1R_2 \,\bigm|\, R_1\bm{\tau}_2 + \bm{\tau}_1\} \label{eq:11-product} \end{equation} $$

The key point is that $R_1$ acts on $\bm{\tau}_2$. The rotational part of the left operation rotates the translation on the right. Getting this wrong would throw off everything that follows, so be sure to derive it yourself at least once.

The identity element is $\{E|0\}$. Let us find the inverse element $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}^{-1} = \{S|\bm{\sigma}\}$. By Eq. \eqref{eq:11-product},

$$ \{R|\bm{\tau}\}\{S|\bm{\sigma}\} = \{RS \,|\, R\bm{\sigma}+\bm{\tau}\} \overset{!}{=} \{E|0\} $$

From the point-group part, $RS = E$, that is, $S = R^{-1}$. From the translational part, $R\bm{\sigma}+\bm{\tau} = 0$; multiplying both sides by $R^{-1}$ from the left gives $\bm{\sigma} = -R^{-1}\bm{\tau}$. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \{R|\bm{\tau}\}^{-1} = \{R^{-1} \,\bigm|\, -R^{-1}\bm{\tau}\} \label{eq:11-inverse} \end{equation} $$

∎ Note that it is not simply $\{R^{-1}|-\bm{\tau}\}$.

11.1.2 Definition of the translation group

Definition: Translation group

The set of all elements $\{E|\RR_n\}$ of a space group $G$ whose point-group part is the identity operation $E$ and whose translational part is a lattice translation vector

$$ \RR_n = n_1\aa_1 + n_2\aa_2 + n_3\aa_3 \qquad (n_1,n_2,n_3 \in \mathbb{Z}) $$

is called the translation group $\mathcal{T}$. Here $\aa_1,\aa_2,\aa_3$ are the primitive translation vectors of the primitive cell (Chapter 1).

Caution: The $\RR_n$ in $\{E|\RR_n\}$ is a primitive lattice vector

When we describe an fcc lattice with the conventional cell (a cube), the cube edge $a\hat{x}$ is also a translational symmetry, but in fcc the primitive translation vectors are shorter ones such as $\frac{a}{2}(0,1,1)$. When constructing the translation group, always use the primitive vectors of the primitive cell. If we use the edges of the conventional cell, we drop the translations to the face centers, and the entire argument below goes wrong.

This point was emphasized in Section 1.3.2 of Chapter 1, “There are infinitely many choices, but the volume is unique.” It is also exactly the reason why band-structure calculation programs require a primitive cell.

11.1.3 $\mathcal{T}$ is a normal subgroup

The translation group occupies a special position within the space group. The following calculation shows this.

Derivation: $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}\{E|\RR\}\{R|\bm{\tau}\}^{-1} = \{E|R\RR\}$

First we multiply the two left factors using Eq. \eqref{eq:11-product}. With $R_1=R$, $\bm{\tau}_1=\bm{\tau}$, $R_2=E$, $\bm{\tau}_2=\RR$,

$$ \{R|\bm{\tau}\}\{E|\RR\} = \{RE \,|\, R\RR + \bm{\tau}\} = \{R \,|\, R\RR+\bm{\tau}\} $$

Next we multiply from the right by $\{R^{-1}|-R^{-1}\bm{\tau}\}$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:11-inverse}. Using Eq. \eqref{eq:11-product} again with $R_1=R$, $\bm{\tau}_1 = R\RR+\bm{\tau}$, $R_2=R^{-1}$, $\bm{\tau}_2 = -R^{-1}\bm{\tau}$,

$$ \{R \,|\, R\RR+\bm{\tau}\}\{R^{-1}\,|\,-R^{-1}\bm{\tau}\} = \{RR^{-1} \,\bigm|\, R(-R^{-1}\bm{\tau}) + R\RR + \bm{\tau}\} $$

The point-group part is $RR^{-1}=E$. The translational part is $-RR^{-1}\bm{\tau} + R\RR + \bm{\tau} = -\bm{\tau}+R\RR+\bm{\tau} = R\RR$. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \{R|\bm{\tau}\}\{E|\RR\}\{R|\bm{\tau}\}^{-1} = \{E \,|\, R\RR\} \label{eq:11-normal} \end{equation} $$

∎ Since $R$ is a point-group operation of the crystal, it maps the lattice onto itself. Hence $R\RR$ is also a lattice translation vector, and the right-hand side is an element of $\mathcal{T}$.

Physical Meaning: What it means for $\mathcal{T}$ to be a normal subgroup

Equation \eqref{eq:11-normal} says that “no matter which element of $\mathcal{T}$ we conjugate by which element of the space group, the result stays inside $\mathcal{T}$.” This is the definition of a normal subgroup (Section 6.7.1 of Chapter 6).

If a subgroup is normal, its cosets form a group. This quotient group

$$ G/\mathcal{T} \cong P $$

is isomorphic to the point group $P$ of the crystal. In other words, a space group can be organized as “a point-group structure sitting on top of the foundation of the translation group.” For a symmorphic space group, $G$ is the semidirect product of $\mathcal{T}$ and $P$; for a nonsymmorphic one, it cannot be written this way (Section 5.3 of Chapter 5). This difference appears in Section 11.8 as the sticking together of bands.

Furthermore, the existence of a normal subgroup makes a powerful technique of representation theory available (induced representations). The irreducible representations of a space group are built starting from those of $\mathcal{T}$. That starting point is Section 11.2 of this chapter.

11.1.4 $\mathcal{T}$ is an abelian group — hence every irreducible representation is one-dimensional

Derivation: $\mathcal{T}$ is commutative

Setting $R_1=R_2=E$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:11-product}, we obtain

$$ \{E|\RR_1\}\{E|\RR_2\} = \{E\cdot E \,|\, E\RR_2 + \RR_1\} = \{E \,|\, \RR_1+\RR_2\} $$

Since $E$ is the identity matrix, $E\RR_2 = \RR_2$. Even if we interchange the order,

$$ \{E|\RR_2\}\{E|\RR_1\} = \{E \,|\, \RR_2+\RR_1\} = \{E \,|\, \RR_1+\RR_2\} $$

Because vector addition is commutative, the two are equal. Hence

$$ \{E|\RR_1\}\{E|\RR_2\} = \{E|\RR_2\}\{E|\RR_1\} $$

∎ The translation group is an abelian group.

Theorem 11.1: Every irreducible representation of an abelian group is one-dimensional

An abelian group of order $h$ has $h$ irreducible representations, and all of them are one-dimensional.

Proof: Just combine two theorems of Chapter 8

Step 1: In an abelian group, each element forms a class by itself.

The definition of a class is as follows: $g$ and $g'$ are conjugate, that is, there exists an $x$ such that $g' = xgx^{-1}$ (Section 6.6 of Chapter 6). In an abelian group $xg = gx$, so

$$ g' = xgx^{-1} = gxx^{-1} = g $$

and the only element conjugate to $g$ is $g$ itself. Hence the number of classes equals the number of elements, namely $h$.

Step 2: The number of irreducible representations equals the number of classes (Section 8.3 of Chapter 8). Therefore the number of irreducible representations is also $h$. Let their dimensions be $\ell_1,\ell_2,\dots,\ell_h$.

Step 3: The sum of the squares of the dimensions equals the order (Chapter 7, corollary of the great orthogonality theorem).

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{h} \ell_i^2 = h $$

Each $\ell_i$ is an integer greater than or equal to 1, and the only way for $h$ such numbers to add up (after squaring) to $h$ is for all of them to equal $1$. Indeed, if even one of them satisfied $\ell_j \ge 2$, then $\sum \ell_i^2 \ge 4 + (h-1) = h+3 > h$, a contradiction.

$$ \therefore \quad \ell_1 = \ell_2 = \cdots = \ell_h = 1 $$

∎

Why? This theorem determines everything in this chapter

That an irreducible representation is one-dimensional means that its representation matrices are just complex numbers ($1\times1$ matrices). Each irreducible representation is then nothing but “a function that returns a complex number $D(\RR)$ for each translation $\RR$.” Since $D$ is a representation, it satisfies $D(\RR_1)D(\RR_2) = D(\RR_1+\RR_2)$. The only complex-valued functions satisfying this functional equation are exponential functions—and this is how $e^{-i\kk\cdot\RR}$ appears inevitably. We examine this carefully in the next section.

And there is one more thing. That an irreducible representation is one-dimensional also means that translational symmetry alone does not produce degeneracy. All band degeneracies originate from symmetries other than translation—namely the group of the wave vector $G_\kk$. This is the subject of Section 11.6.

11.2 Periodic Boundary Conditions and the Quantization of $\kk$

This is the heart of this chapter. If we treat a crystal that extends to infinity as it is, the translation group becomes an infinite group, and the theorem of the previous section (the theorem for a finite group of order $h$) cannot be used. We therefore impose periodic boundary conditions (also called Born–von Kármán boundary conditions) to make it a finite group.

11.2.1 First in one dimension — an atomic chain closed into a ring

Consider a one-dimensional crystal with lattice constant $a$. Suppose that $N$ atoms are lined up, and agree that the atom after the $N$th is the 0th again. That is, we bend the chain into a ring. In equation form,

$$ \begin{equation} \{E|Na\} = \{E|0\} \qquad\text{(periodic boundary condition)} \label{eq:11-pbc1d} \end{equation} $$

Under this condition, the translation group becomes

$$ \mathcal{T}_1 = \bigl\{\, T_0=\{E|0\},\ T_1=\{E|a\},\ T_2=\{E|2a\},\ \dots,\ T_{N-1}=\{E|(N-1)a\} \,\bigr\} $$

a finite group of order $N$. The multiplication rule is

$$ T_n T_m = \{E|na\}\{E|ma\} = \{E|(n+m)a\} = T_{(n+m)\bmod N} $$

That is, when the index exceeds $N$, we subtract $N$. This is nothing other than the cyclic group $C_N$. The single element $T_1$ generates everything: $T_n = (T_1)^n$.

Derivation: Finding all irreducible representations of the one-dimensional translation group

By Theorem 11.1, there are $N$ irreducible representations, and all of them are one-dimensional. In a one-dimensional representation the representation matrix is a complex number, so we write the representation of the generator $T_1$ as

$$ D(T_1) = c \qquad (c \in \mathbb{C}) $$

Once $c$ is fixed, the homomorphism property of the representation, $D(gg')=D(g)D(g')$, determines everything:

$$ D(T_n) = D\bigl((T_1)^n\bigr) = \bigl[D(T_1)\bigr]^n = c^n $$

What fixes $c$ is the periodic boundary condition. Eq. \eqref{eq:11-pbc1d} means $(T_1)^N = T_0 = E$, so taking the representation of both sides gives

$$ c^N = D(E) = 1 $$

That is, $c$ must be an $N$th root of unity. Putting $c = e^{i\theta}$, we have $e^{iN\theta}=1$, hence $N\theta = 2\pi\times(\text{integer})$, and therefore

$$ c = \exp\!\left(-\,\frac{2\pi i\, m}{N}\right), \qquad m = 0, 1, 2, \dots, N-1 $$

(The sign was taken to be $-$ for later convenience; since $m$ runs from $0$ to $N-1$, the set of values is the same.)

Now all $N$ irreducible representations have been found. This is exactly the number predicted by Theorem 11.1.

Here we introduce $\kk$. We want to put the exponent in the form $-i\times(\text{something})\times a$. So we define

$$ \frac{2\pi m}{N} = k\,a \quad\text{so that}\quad \boxed{\,k \equiv \frac{2\pi m}{Na}\,} $$

Here $Na = L$ is the length of the whole crystal. Then

$$ c = e^{-ika}, \qquad D^{(k)}(T_n) = c^n = e^{-ikna} $$

Since $na$ is the position $R_n$ of the $n$th lattice point, we finally obtain

$$ \begin{equation} D^{(k)}\bigl(\{E|R_n\}\bigr) = e^{-i k R_n}, \qquad k = \frac{2\pi m}{Na} \label{eq:11-irrep1d} \end{equation} $$

∎ From “one-dimensional representation plus periodic boundary condition,” the exponential function and the quantization of $k$ have emerged by themselves.

Supplement: How to choose the range of $k$ values

If we take $m=0,1,\dots,N-1$, then $k$ runs from $0$ up to just below $2\pi/a$. However, $m$ and $m+N$ give the same $c$ ($e^{-2\pi i(m+N)/N} = e^{-2\pi i m/N}e^{-2\pi i}=e^{-2\pi i m/N}$), so the representatives of $m$ may be chosen in any way, as long as they are $N$ consecutive integers.

Usually we take them symmetric about the origin:

$$ m = -\frac{N}{2}+1,\ \dots,\ -1,\ 0,\ 1,\ \dots,\ \frac{N}{2} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad -\frac{\pi}{a} < k \le \frac{\pi}{a} $$

This is the first Brillouin zone. This is the reason why the first Brillouin zone is “natural as the range of $k$.” In Section 11.3 we will state this once more, properly, in three dimensions.

11.2.2 Extension to three dimensions

Derivation: Periodic boundary conditions in three dimensions and the quantization of $\kk$

We impose a periodic boundary condition in each of the three directions. Suppose the crystal closes on itself after $N_1$ cells in the $\aa_1$ direction, $N_2$ cells in the $\aa_2$ direction, and $N_3$ cells in the $\aa_3$ direction:

$$ \begin{equation} \{E|N_i\aa_i\} = \{E|0\} \qquad (i=1,2,3) \label{eq:11-pbc3d} \end{equation} $$

The order of the translation group $\mathcal{T}$ is $N = N_1N_2N_3$, which is the number of primitive cells contained in the crystal.

Step 1: An irreducible representation is determined by three numbers.

$\mathcal{T}$ is generated by the three generators $\{E|\aa_1\}, \{E|\aa_2\}, \{E|\aa_3\}$. We write the values of the one-dimensional representation for them as

$$ D(\{E|\aa_1\}) = c_1, \quad D(\{E|\aa_2\}) = c_2, \quad D(\{E|\aa_3\}) = c_3 $$

A general lattice translation $\RR_n = n_1\aa_1+n_2\aa_2+n_3\aa_3$ can be decomposed as

$$ \{E|\RR_n\} = \{E|\aa_1\}^{n_1}\{E|\aa_2\}^{n_2}\{E|\aa_3\}^{n_3} $$

(since the group is abelian, we need not worry about the order). Therefore

$$ D(\{E|\RR_n\}) = c_1^{\,n_1}\,c_2^{\,n_2}\,c_3^{\,n_3} $$

Step 2: Determine $c_i$ from the periodic boundary conditions.

From Eq. \eqref{eq:11-pbc3d}, $\{E|\aa_i\}^{N_i} = \{E|0\}$; taking the representation of both sides gives

$$ c_i^{N_i} = 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad c_i = \exp\!\left(-\frac{2\pi i\, m_i}{N_i}\right), \quad m_i = 0,1,\dots,N_i-1 $$

The number of triples $(m_1,m_2,m_3)$ is $N_1N_2N_3 = N$, which matches exactly the number of irreducible representations $=$ the order of the group $=N$.

Step 3: Combine $(m_1,m_2,m_3)$ into a vector $\kk$.

Here we bring in the reciprocal lattice vectors $\bb_1,\bb_2,\bb_3$. Their defining equation, derived in Chapter 3, was

$$ \aa_i \cdot \bb_j = 2\pi\,\delta_{ij} $$

So let us define

$$ \begin{equation} \kk \;=\; \frac{m_1}{N_1}\bb_1 + \frac{m_2}{N_2}\bb_2 + \frac{m_3}{N_3}\bb_3 \label{eq:11-kquant} \end{equation} $$

Computing the inner product of this $\kk$ with $\aa_i$, we obtain

$$ \kk\cdot\aa_i = \sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{m_j}{N_j}\,\bb_j\cdot\aa_i = \sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{m_j}{N_j}\,2\pi\delta_{ij} = \frac{2\pi m_i}{N_i} $$

This is exactly the quantity in the exponent of $c_i$. Therefore we can write

$$ c_i = e^{-i\kk\cdot\aa_i} $$

Using this to assemble the representation for a general $\RR_n$, we obtain

$$ D^{(\kk)}(\{E|\RR_n\}) = c_1^{n_1}c_2^{n_2}c_3^{n_3} = e^{-i\kk\cdot(n_1\aa_1)}\,e^{-i\kk\cdot(n_2\aa_2)}\,e^{-i\kk\cdot(n_3\aa_3)} = e^{-i\kk\cdot(n_1\aa_1+n_2\aa_2+n_3\aa_3)} $$

that is,

$$ \begin{equation} D^{(\kk)}\bigl(\{E|\RR_n\}\bigr) = e^{-i\kk\cdot\RR_n} \label{eq:11-irrep3d} \end{equation} $$

∎ This is the complete list of the irreducible representations of the translation group. The number of representations is $N_1N_2N_3$, and $\kk$ is quantized in the form of Eq. \eqref{eq:11-kquant}.

Example 11.1: Density of $\kk$ points and the number of states

Find the volume in reciprocal space occupied per point by the $\kk$ points allowed by Eq. \eqref{eq:11-kquant}. Also show how it is related to the volume $V$ of the crystal.

Solution. The $\kk$ points form a lattice of tiny parallelepipeds with edges $\bb_1/N_1$, $\bb_2/N_2$, $\bb_3/N_3$. The volume per point is

$$ \Delta V_k = \frac{\bb_1}{N_1}\cdot\left(\frac{\bb_2}{N_2}\times\frac{\bb_3}{N_3}\right) = \frac{1}{N_1N_2N_3}\,\bb_1\cdot(\bb_2\times\bb_3) = \frac{1}{N}\,\Omega_{\rm BZ} $$

Here $\Omega_{\rm BZ} = \bb_1\cdot(\bb_2\times\bb_3)$ is the volume of the first Brillouin zone. Substituting the relation derived in Chapter 3,

$$ \Omega_{\rm BZ} = \frac{(2\pi)^3}{V_{\rm cell}} $$

($V_{\rm cell}=\aa_1\cdot(\aa_2\times\aa_3)$ is the volume of the primitive cell), we obtain

$$ \Delta V_k = \frac{1}{N}\cdot\frac{(2\pi)^3}{V_{\rm cell}} = \frac{(2\pi)^3}{N V_{\rm cell}} = \frac{(2\pi)^3}{V} $$

In the last step we used $V = N V_{\rm cell}$ (the volume of the whole crystal). ∎

In other words, there is one state per volume $(2\pi)^3/V$ of $\kk$ space. The routine operation used in the Debye model of Chapter 14 and in the counting of states in the Fermi sphere of Chapter 15, “multiply by $V/(2\pi)^3$ and integrate over $\dd^3k$,” is precisely the quantization of $\kk$ that we have just derived. Here group theory connects with the practice of band-structure calculations.

Supplement: The $k$-point mesh in band-structure calculations

The Monkhorst–Pack mesh $N_1\times N_2\times N_3$ that one specifies in VASP or Quantum ESPRESSO is precisely a specification of how many values of $(m_1,m_2,m_3)$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:11-kquant} to take. An $8\times8\times8$ mesh is equivalent to “imposing periodic boundary conditions on a superlattice with $N_1=N_2=N_3=8$, that is, consisting of $512$ primitive cells.”

In group-theoretical terms, increasing the number of $k$ points in a convergence test means “raising the order of the translation group and sampling the label $\kk$ of the irreducible representations more finely.” Metals demand many $k$ points because the sharp structure in $\kk$ space called the Fermi surface must be resolved (Chapter 18).

11.3 $\kk$ Is a Label of an Irreducible Representation

11.3.1 $\kk$ as a quantum number

Physical Meaning: Why $\kk$ is a quantum number

In quantum mechanics, the eigenvalues of operators that commute with the Hamiltonian become quantum numbers. More precisely, the labels of the irreducible representations of the symmetry group become the quantum numbers. Let us list some examples.

Table 11.1: Correspondence between symmetries and quantum numbers
SymmetryGroupLabel of irreducible representation = quantum numberDegeneracy
Spherically symmetric (isolated atom)Rotation group $SO(3)$$\ell = 0,1,2,\dots$$2\ell+1$
Regular octahedron (crystal field)Point group $O_h$$A_{1g}, E_g, T_{2g}, \dots$$1, 2, 3, \dots$
Lattice translationTranslation group $\mathcal{T}$$\kk$$1$

If the system is spherically symmetric, the azimuthal quantum number $\ell$ is a good quantum number. When the atom is placed in an octahedral crystal field, $SO(3)$ is reduced to $O_h$, and labels of irreducible representations such as $E_g, T_{2g}$ become the quantum numbers in place of $\ell$ (the crystal-field splitting of Chapter 10). In the same way, under the translational symmetry of a crystal, $\kk$ becomes the quantum number.

The explanation “the wavenumber is the momentum divided by $\hbar$” is correct only for free electrons. In a periodic potential, $\hbar\kk$ is not the true momentum ($\Ham$ does not commute with $\hat{\bm p}$); it is something different, called the crystal momentum. Nevertheless, $\kk$ remains a quantum number. This is not because $\kk$ is a relative of momentum, but because it is a label of an irreducible representation of the translation group. This one sentence is the key point of this section.

11.3.2 $\kk$ and $\kk+\GG$ give the same irreducible representation

Derivation: $\GG\cdot\RR_n$ is always an integer multiple of $2\pi$

Let the reciprocal lattice vector be $\GG = h\bb_1+k\bb_2+l\bb_3$ ($h,k,l$ are integers; Chapter 3), and let the lattice translation vector be $\RR_n = n_1\aa_1+n_2\aa_2+n_3\aa_3$. We expand the inner product.

$$ \GG\cdot\RR_n = (h\bb_1+k\bb_2+l\bb_3)\cdot(n_1\aa_1+n_2\aa_2+n_3\aa_3) $$

Nine terms appear, but since $\bb_i\cdot\aa_j = 2\pi\delta_{ij}$, only the three terms with $i=j$ survive:

$$ \GG\cdot\RR_n = h n_1 (2\pi) + k n_2 (2\pi) + l n_3 (2\pi) = 2\pi\,(hn_1 + kn_2 + ln_3) $$

Since $h,k,l,n_1,n_2,n_3$ are all integers, $hn_1+kn_2+ln_3$ is an integer. Hence

$$ \begin{equation} e^{-i\GG\cdot\RR_n} = e^{-2\pi i\,(\text{integer})} = 1 \label{eq:11-GR} \end{equation} $$

Using this, the representation obtained when $\kk$ is replaced by $\kk+\GG$ is

$$ D^{(\kk+\GG)}(\{E|\RR_n\}) = e^{-i(\kk+\GG)\cdot\RR_n} = e^{-i\kk\cdot\RR_n}\,\underbrace{e^{-i\GG\cdot\RR_n}}_{=1} = e^{-i\kk\cdot\RR_n} = D^{(\kk)}(\{E|\RR_n\}) $$

∎ The values of the representation agree for every group element. That is, $\kk$ and $\kk+\GG$ are the same irreducible representation. Only the label attached to it differs; the content is identical.

Why? Why we may restrict to the first Brillouin zone

We know from Theorem 11.1 that there are exactly $N$ irreducible representations. However, if we extend $\kk$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:11-kquant} to “all of reciprocal space” and count, arbitrarily many $\kk$ appear. This is not a contradiction. Values of $\kk$ that differ only by a $\GG$ refer to the same representation, so we are merely counting redundantly.

To avoid the redundancy, it suffices to choose one representative from each set of $\kk$ values that are carried into one another by differences of $\GG$. The way of collecting representatives that chooses those closest to the origin of reciprocal space gives the first Brillouin zone. The first Brillouin zone is the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice (Chapter 3, Section 3.7); its volume is $\Omega_{\rm BZ}=(2\pi)^3/V_{\rm cell}$, and it contains exactly $N$ $\kk$ points. This agrees with the number of irreducible representations.

The folding back of the monatomic-chain dispersion relation into the first Brillouin zone in Chapter 13, the use of the reduced-zone scheme in Chapter 16, and the Harrison construction in Chapter 18 all have their root in this single point: $\kk$ and $\kk+\GG$ are physically the same label.

Example 11.2: Checking concretely for fcc

Let the primitive translation vectors of the fcc lattice be $\aa_1=\frac{a}{2}(0,1,1)$, $\aa_2=\frac{a}{2}(1,0,1)$, $\aa_3=\frac{a}{2}(1,1,0)$. Then the reciprocal lattice vectors are (from the example in Chapter 3)

$$ \bb_1=\frac{2\pi}{a}(-1,1,1),\quad \bb_2=\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,-1,1),\quad \bb_3=\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,1,-1) $$

Find $\GG=\bb_2+\bb_3$ and verify directly that $e^{-i\GG\cdot\aa_1}=1$.

Solution. First we take the sum.

$$ \GG = \bb_2+\bb_3 = \frac{2\pi}{a}\bigl[(1,-1,1)+(1,1,-1)\bigr] = \frac{2\pi}{a}(2,0,0) $$

This is a reciprocal lattice vector along a cube edge. Next we take the inner product.

$$ \GG\cdot\aa_1 = \frac{2\pi}{a}(2,0,0)\cdot\frac{a}{2}(0,1,1) = \frac{2\pi}{a}\cdot\frac{a}{2}\bigl[2\cdot0 + 0\cdot1 + 0\cdot1\bigr] = 0 $$

Since $0$ is $0$ times $2\pi$, the condition is satisfied, and $e^{-i\GG\cdot\aa_1}=e^0=1$.

As a side check, we verify it with $\aa_2$ as well.

$$ \GG\cdot\aa_2 = \frac{2\pi}{a}\cdot\frac{a}{2}(2,0,0)\cdot(1,0,1) = \pi\cdot 2 = 2\pi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{-2\pi i}=1 $$

∎ We can see that the definition $\bb_i\cdot\aa_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$ is at work. Incidentally, a reciprocal lattice vector of this same type will appear in Section 11.5 when we treat the $X$ point. For $X=\frac{2\pi}{a}(0,0,1)$, the fact that $2X = \frac{2\pi}{a}(0,0,2) = \bb_1+\bb_2$ is a reciprocal lattice vector is what allows us to identify $X$ with $-X$.

11.4 Group-Theoretical Derivation of Bloch's Theorem

11.4.1 Action of symmetry operations on functions

We define the operator $\hat{P}_{\{R|\bm{\tau}\}}$ that lets a symmetry operation act on a wave function, with the same convention as in Chapters 6 and 9.

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{P}_{\{R|\bm{\tau}\}}\,f(\rr) \;=\; f\bigl(\{R|\bm{\tau}\}^{-1}\rr\bigr) \label{eq:11-Pop} \end{equation} $$

The inverse element appears so that the direction in which the function is moved agrees with the direction in which the coordinates are moved (Chapter 9, Section 9.1). For a pure translation, setting $R=E$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:11-inverse} gives $\{E|\RR\}^{-1}=\{E|-\RR\}$, so that

$$ \hat{P}_{\{E|\RR\}}\,f(\rr) = f(\rr-\RR) $$

holds. This is the operation that translates the graph of the function by $+\RR$.

Derivation: $[\Ham,\ \hat{P}_{\{E|\RR\}}] = 0$

Consider the one-electron Hamiltonian in a periodic potential,

$$ \Ham = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V(\rr), \qquad V(\rr+\RR)=V(\rr)\ \ (\forall \RR\in\text{lattice}) $$

For an arbitrary function $\psi$, we compute $\hat{P}_{\{E|\RR\}}\bigl(\Ham\psi\bigr)$. By the definition in Eq. \eqref{eq:11-Pop}, this is the function $\Ham\psi$ with its argument replaced by $\rr\to\rr-\RR$:

$$ \hat{P}_{\{E|\RR\}}\bigl(\Ham\psi\bigr)(\rr) = \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(\nabla^2\psi) + V\psi\right]_{\rr\to\rr-\RR} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(\nabla^2\psi)(\rr-\RR) + V(\rr-\RR)\,\psi(\rr-\RR) $$

We rewrite the first term. Since $\nabla^2$ is a differential operator that is invariant under translation of the coordinates, we have $(\nabla^2\psi)(\rr-\RR) = \nabla_\rr^2\bigl[\psi(\rr-\RR)\bigr]$ (by the chain rule, $\partial(\rr-\RR)/\partial\rr = 1$, and $\RR$ is a constant vector). In the second term we use the periodicity $V(\rr-\RR)=V(\rr)$. Then

$$ = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\bigl[\psi(\rr-\RR)\bigr] + V(\rr)\,\psi(\rr-\RR) = \Ham\,\bigl[\hat{P}_{\{E|\RR\}}\psi\bigr](\rr) $$

Since $\psi$ was arbitrary, as an operator identity we obtain

$$ \hat{P}_{\{E|\RR\}}\Ham = \Ham\,\hat{P}_{\{E|\RR\}} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \bigl[\Ham,\ \hat{P}_{\{E|\RR\}}\bigr]=0 $$

∎ We used only the assumption that “$V$ is periodic.”

11.4.2 Bloch's theorem

Theorem 11.2 (Bloch's theorem)

The eigenfunctions of the Schrödinger equation in a periodic potential $V(\rr+\RR)=V(\rr)$ can be classified by the label $\kk$ of an irreducible representation of the translation group, and can be written in the form

$$ \psi_{n\kk}(\rr) = e^{i\kk\cdot\rr}\,u_{n\kk}(\rr), \qquad u_{n\kk}(\rr+\RR) = u_{n\kk}(\rr) $$

Here $n$ is the band index.

Proof: Group-theoretical derivation (three steps)

Step 1: The eigenfunctions can be taken as a basis of an irreducible representation.

As shown in Chapter 6, Section 6.8, when there is a group of symmetry operations that commute with $\Ham$, the set of eigenfunctions of $\Ham$ forms a representation space of the symmetry group, and by taking suitable linear combinations it can be made into a basis of an irreducible representation. The symmetry group here is the translation group $\mathcal{T}$.

Step 2: Because the irreducible representations are one-dimensional, an eigenfunction is simply multiplied by a phase factor.

By Theorem 11.1, all irreducible representations of $\mathcal{T}$ are one-dimensional. The basis of a one-dimensional representation is a single function, and applying a symmetry operation to that function merely multiplies it by the representation matrix (that is, a complex number). Using Eq. \eqref{eq:11-irrep3d}, the eigenfunction $\psi_\kk$ belonging to the irreducible representation $\kk$ satisfies

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{P}_{\{E|\RR\}}\,\psi_\kk(\rr) \;=\; D^{(\kk)}(\{E|\RR\})\,\psi_\kk(\rr) \;=\; e^{-i\kk\cdot\RR}\,\psi_\kk(\rr) \label{eq:11-blochcond} \end{equation} $$

By definition the left-hand side is $\psi_\kk(\rr-\RR)$, so this can be written as

$$ \psi_\kk(\rr-\RR) = e^{-i\kk\cdot\RR}\psi_\kk(\rr) $$

If we replace $\RR\to-\RR$ (since $-\RR$ is also a lattice vector), we obtain the more familiar form

$$ \psi_\kk(\rr+\RR) = e^{i\kk\cdot\RR}\,\psi_\kk(\rr) $$

This is already the content of Bloch's theorem itself. “Shifting by one lattice translation merely multiplies the function by the phase factor $e^{i\kk\cdot\RR}$.”

Step 3: We show that $u_\kk$ is periodic.

Now, anew, we

$$ u_\kk(\rr) \;\equiv\; e^{-i\kk\cdot\rr}\,\psi_\kk(\rr) $$

define (this is just a rewriting and assumes nothing). Let us shift the argument of this $u_\kk$ by $\RR$.

$$ u_\kk(\rr-\RR) = e^{-i\kk\cdot(\rr-\RR)}\,\psi_\kk(\rr-\RR) $$

We split the exponential and substitute Eq. \eqref{eq:11-blochcond} for $\psi_\kk(\rr-\RR)$:

$$ = e^{-i\kk\cdot\rr}\,e^{+i\kk\cdot\RR}\cdot e^{-i\kk\cdot\RR}\,\psi_\kk(\rr) $$

The two exponential factors in the middle cancel, since $e^{+i\kk\cdot\RR}e^{-i\kk\cdot\RR}=1$. What remains is

$$ = e^{-i\kk\cdot\rr}\psi_\kk(\rr) = u_\kk(\rr) $$

That is, $u_\kk(\rr-\RR)=u_\kk(\rr)$, and $u_\kk$ has the lattice periodicity. Solving the defining equation for $\psi$, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \psi_\kk(\rr) = e^{i\kk\cdot\rr}\,u_\kk(\rr), \qquad u_\kk(\rr+\RR)=u_\kk(\rr) \label{eq:11-bloch} \end{equation} $$

∎ The form of the Bloch function, “plane wave $\times$ lattice-periodic function,” has emerged.

Where does the band index $n$ come from? A single irreducible representation $\kk$ appears many times in the infinite-dimensional function space. That is, there are many independent eigenfunctions carrying the same label $\kk$. The number that distinguishes them is $n$, and the energy becomes $E_n(\kk)$. Fixing $n$ and varying $\kk$ traces out a single band. Group theory guarantees that “$\kk$ is a good quantum number,” but it does not tell us the values of $E_n(\kk)$ themselves. The values become known only by solving the Schrödinger equation (Chapter 16).

Caution: Comparison with the conventional proof

In Kittel and many other textbooks (in this book, Chapter 16), Bloch's theorem is proved as follows. One defines the translation operator $\hat{T}_\RR$ by $\hat{T}_\RR f(\rr)=f(\rr+\RR)$, and, since $[\Ham,\hat{T}_\RR]=0$, takes simultaneous eigenfunctions; the eigenvalue $C$ in $\hat{T}_\RR\psi = C(\RR)\psi$ is then fixed to be $C=e^{i\kk\cdot\RR}$ by $C(\RR_1)C(\RR_2)=C(\RR_1+\RR_2)$ and the periodic boundary conditions $C(N\aa)=1$. Here $\hat{T}_\RR$ has been defined in the direction of “shifting by $+\RR$,” so the sign of the phase is opposite to that of $\hat{P}_{\{E|\RR\}}f(\rr)=f(\rr-\RR)$ in this chapter (whose representation is $e^{-i\kk\cdot\RR}$). The two say the same thing, and indeed the sign agrees with the $\psi_\kk(\rr+\RR)=e^{i\kk\cdot\RR}\psi_\kk(\rr)$ we have just derived.

The content of the calculation is exactly the same. What differs is how it looks.

11.5 The Group of the Wave Vector and the Little Co-Group — High-Symmetry Points of fcc

So far we have looked only at the translation group. A space group also has a point-group part. However, when we apply $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}$ to $\psi_\kk$, in general $\kk$ is changed into $R\kk$, and we jump to a different irreducible representation. We therefore collect only the operations that “do not change $\kk$.”

Definition: The group of the wave vector and the little co-group

Among the elements of a space group $G$, the set of all $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}$ satisfying

$$ R\kk = \kk + \GG \qquad \text{($\GG$ is a reciprocal lattice vector; $\GG=0$ is allowed)} $$

is called the group of the wave vector $G_\kk$ of $\kk$. Its point-group part $P_\kk$ is called the little co-group of $\kk$ (also called the point group of the wave vector).

A word on terminology. In English, the “little group” usually means $G_\kk$ itself, a subgroup of the space group (the “group of the wave vector” of this book), while its point-group part $P_\kk$ is distinguished by the name “little co-group.” In this book we give priority to readability and call $P_\kk$ the “little co-group,” but what determines the degeneracy is, after all, the irreducible representations of $G_\kk$. The two may be read interchangeably as $P_\kk$ only when the factor system is trivial (at the $\Gamma$ point, at interior points of symmetry lines, and throughout the whole zone for symmorphic space groups); at the zone boundary of a nonsymmorphic space group this breaks down. We will see a concrete example in Section 11.8.

The reason we allow a difference by $\GG$ is that, as we saw in Section 11.3, $\kk$ and $\kk+\GG$ belong to the same irreducible representation.

In addition, the set of $\kk$ points obtained by moving $\kk$ with each element of the crystallographic point group $P$, excluding those connected to one another by a $\GG$, is called the star of $\kk$.

Theorem 11.3: $|P_\kk| \times (\text{size of the star}) = |P|$

The product of the order of the little co-group and the size of the star equals the order of the crystallographic point group.

This is precisely Lagrange's theorem of Chapter 6 (the orbit–stabilizer theorem). Decomposing $P$ into cosets with respect to $P_\kk$, there are $|P|/|P_\kk|$ cosets, and each coset corresponds to one element of the star.

Example 11.3: Checking that the little co-group of the $X$ point is $D_{4h}$

Count the operations of $O_h$ that keep $\kk_X = \frac{2\pi}{a}(0,0,1)$ invariant up to a difference by $\GG$.

Solution. $\kk_X$ is a vector on the $z$ axis.

(i) The rotations about the $z$ axis, $E, C_{4z}, C_{4z}^{3}, C_{2z}$, keep $\kk_X$ exactly as it is. There are four.

(ii) The rotations about twofold axes perpendicular to the $z$ axis ($C_{2x}, C_{2y}$ and $C_2'$ about the two $\langle110\rangle$ axes) send $\kk_X \to -\kk_X = \frac{2\pi}{a}(0,0,-1)$. Here

$$ \kk_X - (-\kk_X) = \frac{2\pi}{a}(0,0,2) = \bb_1+\bb_2 $$

(as we checked in Example 11.2, this is a reciprocal lattice vector of fcc). Hence $-\kk_X = \kk_X - \GG$, and the condition $R\kk=\kk+\GG$ is satisfied. They are allowed. There are four.

(iii) The mirror planes $\sigma_v$ containing the $z$ axis (two of $\{100\}$ type) and $\sigma_d$ (two of $\{110\}$ type) keep $\kk_X$. There are four.

(iv) The inversion $i$, $\sigma_h$, and the $S_4$ family. $i\kk_X = -\kk_X$ is allowed for the same reason as in (ii). $\sigma_h$ (the $xy$ plane) sends $\kk_X\to-\kk_X$ and is allowed. $S_{4z}=\sigma_h C_{4z}$ and $S_{4z}^3$ also send $\kk_X\to-\kk_X$, but these too are allowed for the same reason as in (ii) ($2\kk_X=\bb_1+\bb_2$ is a reciprocal lattice vector). There are four in total. That is, all four operations in (iv) are of the type $\kk_X\to-\kk_X$.

The total is $4+4+4+4 = 16$. This agrees with the order of $D_{4h}$ ($4/mmm$, order 16). Indeed, the set of remaining operations is precisely “$D_{4h}$ with $z$ as the principal axis.”

On the other hand, $C_3$ about $\langle111\rangle$ sends $\kk_X$ to $\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,0,0)$. This is not connected to $\kk_X$ by a reciprocal lattice vector (the difference $\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,0,-1)$ is not a reciprocal lattice vector), so $C_3$ does not belong to $P_{\kk_X}$. ∎

The size of the star is $48/16=3$. Indeed, there are six $X$ points in the Brillouin zone, but the $\pm$ pairs are identified by $\GG$, so only three are independent. Counting the six square faces in Figure 11.2(b), with opposite faces being the same, gives the same result.

Physical Meaning: The irreducible wedge and first-principles calculations

All elements of a star give the same energy: $E_n(R\kk)=E_n(\kk)$. This is because $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}$ commutes with $\Ham$ and sends $\psi_\kk$ to $\psi_{R\kk}$.

Hence a band calculation does not need to cover the whole first Brillouin zone. It suffices to compute only the irreducible wedge (also called the irreducible Brillouin zone, IBZ), which is smaller than the whole zone by a factor of $48$ in the case of fcc, and to copy the rest by symmetry. For example, a mesh of $8\times8\times8=512$ points is reduced to $29$ points when symmetry is used. The computation time changes by more than an order of magnitude.

Also, the reason a fixed path $L\!-\!\Gamma\!-\!X\!-\!W\!-\!K\!-\!\Gamma$ is used for the horizontal axis of a band diagram is that this path traces the edges (high-symmetry lines) of the irreducible wedge. You can think of it as walking over Figure 11.2.

11.6 Band Degeneracy Is Determined by the Dimension of an Irreducible Representation of the Group of the Wave Vector

Theorem 11.4: Degeneracy of a band

The degeneracy of the energy level $E_n(\kk)$ at wave vector $\kk$ is equal to the dimension of the irreducible representation of the group of the wave vector $G_\kk$ to which the level belongs (accidental degeneracies excluded).

The reason is the same as in Section 6.8 (Chapter 6). Every element of $G_\kk$ commutes with $\Ham$ and leaves $\kk$ unchanged. Therefore the operations mix only eigenfunctions with the same $\kk$. The $\ell$ functions that form a basis of an irreducible representation are carried into one another, so they must have the same energy. Conversely, if the irreducible representations differ, there is no reason for the energies to coincide.

Here $G_\kk$ is a subgroup of the space group, not the point group $P_\kk$ itself. However, when the factor system is trivial—at the $\Gamma$ point, at interior points of symmetry lines, and throughout the whole zone for symmorphic space groups—the irreducible representations of $G_\kk$ correspond one to one with those of $P_\kk$ as they stand. This is why we may use the character tables of $P_\kk$ directly in what follows. When this correspondence breaks down, that is, at the zone boundary of a nonsymmorphic space group, it can happen that the bands are necessarily degenerate even though $P_\kk$ has one-dimensional representations. That is the subject of Section 11.8.

Physical Meaning: Large degeneracy at the $\Gamma$ point, splitting away from it

Looking at Table 11.3, the little co-group at the $\Gamma$ point is $O_h$ (order 48), the largest; moving out along the $\Delta$ line it drops to $C_{4v}$ (order 8), and at a general point it becomes $C_1$ (order 1). The smaller the group, the smaller the dimensions of its irreducible representations.

Some of the correspondences:

$$ \Gamma_1 = A_{1g},\quad \Gamma_2 = A_{2g},\quad \Gamma_{12}=E_g,\quad \Gamma_{15}=T_{1u},\quad \Gamma_{25'}=T_{2g},\quad \Gamma_{2'}=A_{2u} $$

The way primes are attached differs by convention from one paper to another, so when reading a paper, always consult that paper's own character table (or the table of the Bilbao Crystallographic Server). The dimensions and the content of the decomposition do not depend on the convention, so if in doubt you can simply recompute from the characters, as in this example.

Caution: Including the spin-orbit interaction changes the story (double groups)

So far we have ignored spin. When spin is included, the wave function changes sign even under a rotation by $2\pi$ (a spinor). To handle this we need the double group, an extension of the group.

For the top of the valence band of Si, the sixfold degeneracy obtained by multiplying $\Gamma_{25'}$ (threefold) by the spin doublet is split by the spin-orbit interaction into $\Gamma_8^+$ (fourfold; heavy and light holes) and $\Gamma_7^+$ (twofold; the split-off band). The splitting is about $44\,\mathrm{meV}$ in Si, about $0.29\,\mathrm{eV}$ in Ge, and about $0.34\,\mathrm{eV}$ in GaAs (larger for heavier atoms). The fact that the effective mass of holes in Chapter 17 comes in two kinds, “heavy” and “light,” is the content of this $\Gamma_8^+$. The details of double groups are beyond the scope of this book, but the principle is the same: the degeneracy is the dimension of an irreducible representation.

11.7 Compatibility Relations — How the Lines of a Band Diagram Connect

Definition: Compatibility relation

When we move from a high-symmetry point $\kk_0$ to a symmetry line $\kk$, the little co-group becomes smaller: $P_{\kk} \subset P_{\kk_0}$. If we restrict an irreducible representation of $P_{\kk_0}$ to the subgroup $P_\kk$, it is in general reducible. Its reduction into irreducible representations is called the compatibility relation.

Since $E_n(\kk)$ is a continuous function of $\kk$, a level that belonged to $\kk_0$ must, away from $\kk_0$, split into the irreducible representations specified by the compatibility relation. How the lines of a band diagram connect is completely determined by the compatibility relations.

Why? Lines with the same label cannot cross (the non-crossing rule)

Suppose that on a single symmetry line two bands carry the label of the same irreducible representation. If they try to cross, then near the crossing point the secular equation of the two-level problem

$$ \det\begin{pmatrix} E_1-E & H_{12} \\ H_{12}^* & E_2-E\end{pmatrix}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_\pm = \frac{E_1+E_2}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{E_1-E_2}{2}\right)^2 + |H_{12}|^2} $$

comes into play. If the two states belong to the same irreducible representation, then $H_{12}=\bra{1}\Ham\ket{2} \ne 0$ is in general allowed (the selection rule of Chapter 10: the matrix element between states of the same irreducible representation need not vanish), so only $|H_{12}|^2>0$ remains under the square root, and $E_+\ne E_-$: the bands cannot cross. They repel and avoid each other (anticrossing).

However, what the selection rule guarantees is only that “$H_{12}$ need not vanish”; it does not say that it “never vanishes.” In the generic situation in which only the single parameter $\kk$ is varied, one does not accidentally hit a point that satisfies $E_1=E_2$ and $H_{12}=0$ simultaneously, so repulsion occurs; this is the precise way to say it. Conversely, if there is a hidden symmetry beyond the symmetry used in the main text, or if the parameters of the model are chosen specially, lines with the same label can cross.

Conversely, if the labels differ, the selection rule of Chapter 10 guarantees $H_{12}=0$, and the lines can cross freely. When you see lines crossing in a band diagram, you can read it as “these belong to different irreducible representations.” This is the group-theoretical version of the von Neumann–Wigner non-crossing rule.

Example 11.5: Why is the conduction-band minimum of Si on the $\Delta$ line rather than at the $X$ point?

Si is an indirect-gap semiconductor, and the lowest point of the conduction band lies on the $\Delta$ line running from $\Gamma$ to $X$, at about $85\%$ of the way to $X$. Explain group-theoretically why the minimum lies at this “halfway” location.

Solution. The lowest conduction band running toward $X$ along the $\Delta$ line belongs to the $\Delta_1$ representation. On reaching the $X$ point, as will be shown in Section 11.8 (because the diamond structure is nonsymmorphic), the bands necessarily stick together in pairs and become degenerate. The two bands that stick together necessarily carry different labels. The reason is that all irreducible representations of the group of the wave vector at the $X$ point are two-dimensional, and when they are restricted to the group of the wave vector on the $\Delta$ line (whose point-group part is $C_{4v}$), if they split into two one-dimensional representations, those two are necessarily inequivalent to each other. If the same label appeared twice, that one-dimensional representation would be carried up to the $X$ point, so a one-dimensional representation would exist at the $X$ point, a contradiction. In the diamond structure, $\Delta_1$ and $\Delta_{2'}$ form a pair, and these two bands merge at the $X$ point into a single twofold degenerate level $X_1$ (the originally two-dimensional $\Delta_5$ does not split and corresponds directly to a two-dimensional representation at the $X$ point).

That the two bands are necessarily degenerate at the $X$ point means that, in terms of the displacement from $X$, $\qq=\kk-\kk_X$ (let its magnitude be $q$), their energies may split to first order in $q$, as

$$ E_\pm(q) \simeq E_X + \alpha q^2 \pm \beta q \qquad (\alpha,\beta>0) $$

The coincidence of $E(\kk_X+\qq)$ and $E(\kk_X-\qq)$ (time reversal) is preserved by the interchange of the two branches $\pm$, so the first-order term is not forbidden. If $\beta\ne0$, the minimum of the lower branch $E_-$ lies not at $q=0$ but at

$$ q_{\min} = \frac{\beta}{2\alpha} > 0 $$

That is, the minimum lies not at the $X$ point itself but short of it. In Si it is near $(0,0,0.85)\times 2\pi/a$. This shape, in which minima form on both sides of the $X$ point and the $X$ point itself becomes a small hill, is called a “camel's back.”

∎ The conduction band of Si has six equivalent minima (in the six directions $\pm x, \pm y, \pm z$), and this is the origin of the valley degeneracy $=6$ of the Si conduction band. The same holds for GaP; in Ge the minima are at the $L$ points at the ends of the $\Lambda$ lines, and the valley degeneracy is $8/2=4$. In the calculation of the effective mass and the density of states in Chapter 17, this number enters directly.

11.8 Nonsymmorphic Space Groups and the Sticking Together of Bands

In Chapter 5 we divided the space groups into two kinds. Those for which a suitable choice of origin makes the translational part of every operation a lattice vector are symmorphic; those in which a screw axis or glide plane remains no matter how the origin is chosen are nonsymmorphic. Of the 230 space groups, 73 are symmorphic and 157 are nonsymmorphic.

This difference has a dramatic effect on the bands at the Brillouin-zone boundary.

11.8.1 The setting of the diamond structure

The structure of Si, Ge, and diamond has the space group $Fd\bar{3}m$ ($O_h^7$). An fcc lattice carries two atoms as its basis; if the origin is placed on one atom, the other is at

$$ \bm{\tau} = \frac{a}{4}(1,1,1) $$

The elements of the space group then fall into the following two kinds (for the idea of assigning a translation to each element of the point group, see Section 5.1.4 of Chapter 5; for the criterion of whether that translation can be removed, see Section 5.3 of Chapter 5).

  • Those whose point-group operation $R$ belongs to $T_d$ (order 24): the translational part is zero, $\{R|0\}$
  • Those whose $R$ belongs to $O_h\setminus T_d = \{i, 8S_6, 3\sigma_h, 6C_4, 6C_2'\}$ (order 24): they necessarily carry $\bm{\tau}$, $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}$

$\bm{\tau}=\frac{a}{4}(1,1,1)$ is not a lattice translation vector (the lattice vectors of fcc are in units of $\frac{a}{2}$). Moreover, this fractional translation cannot be removed wherever the origin is moved. Hence $Fd\bar{3}m$ is nonsymmorphic.

11.8.2 Necessarily twofold degenerate at the $X$ point

Theorem 11.5: Sticking together of bands at the $X$ point of the diamond structure

At the $X$ point $\kk_X=\frac{2\pi}{a}(0,0,1)$ of the diamond structure, every irreducible representation of the group of the wave vector $G_{\kk_X}$ has dimension two or higher. Therefore every band is degenerate at least twofold (sticking together of bands). In fact, the four irreducible representations at the $X$ point are all exactly two-dimensional, so the bands stick together exactly two by two.

Proof: Finding two operations that anticommute

Preparation. In an irreducible representation of $G_\kk$ belonging to $\kk$, the translational part is necessarily

$$ D\bigl(\{E|\RR\}\bigr) = e^{-i\kk\cdot\RR}\,\mathbf{1} $$

that is, “identity matrix $\times$ phase factor.” This is because $\mathcal{T}$ is a commutative normal subgroup of $G_\kk$ and all the basis functions belong to the same $\kk$ (Section 11.2).

Step 1: Choose two operations.

$$ S_1 = \{\sigma_x \,|\, \bm{\tau}\}, \qquad S_2 = \{C_{2y} \,|\, 0\} $$

Here $\sigma_x$ is the reflection $x\to-x$ (it is of the $3\sigma_h$ type, so it belongs to $O_h\setminus T_d$ and carries $\bm{\tau}$), and $C_{2y}$ is the twofold rotation about the $y$ axis (it is of the $3C_2$ type, so it belongs to $T_d$ and has zero translational part).

We check that both belong to $G_{\kk_X}$. $\sigma_x\kk_X = \frac{2\pi}{a}(0,0,1)=\kk_X$ ✓. $C_{2y}\kk_X = \frac{2\pi}{a}(0,0,-1) = \kk_X - \GG$ (as in Example 11.3, $\GG=\frac{2\pi}{a}(0,0,2)$ is a reciprocal lattice vector) ✓.

Step 2: Compute the products in both orders. We use Eq. \eqref{eq:11-product}.

$$ S_1S_2 = \{\sigma_x|\bm{\tau}\}\{C_{2y}|0\} = \{\sigma_x C_{2y} \,\bigm|\, \sigma_x\cdot 0 + \bm{\tau}\} = \{\sigma_z \,|\, \bm{\tau}\} $$

(The point-group part is $\sigma_x C_{2y}$: $(x,y,z)\xrightarrow{C_{2y}}(-x,y,-z)\xrightarrow{\sigma_x}(x,y,-z)$, that is, $\sigma_z$.) In the reverse order,

$$ S_2S_1 = \{C_{2y}|0\}\{\sigma_x|\bm{\tau}\} = \{C_{2y}\sigma_x \,\bigm|\, C_{2y}\bm{\tau} + 0\} = \Bigl\{\sigma_z \,\Bigm|\, \tfrac{a}{4}(-1,1,-1)\Bigr\} $$

($C_{2y}$ sends $x\to-x$ and $z\to-z$, so $C_{2y}\bm{\tau}=\frac{a}{4}(-1,1,-1)$. The point-group part is the same $\sigma_z$.)

Step 3: The discrepancy is a lattice vector. Suppose we can write $S_1S_2 = \{E|\bm{v}\}S_2S_1$. Comparing the translational parts gives

$$ \bm{v} = \bm{\tau} - \frac{a}{4}(-1,1,-1) = \frac{a}{4}(1-(-1),\ 1-1,\ 1-(-1)) = \frac{a}{4}(2,0,2) = \frac{a}{2}(1,0,1) = \aa_2 $$

This is precisely one of the primitive translation vectors of fcc. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} S_1S_2 = \{E|\aa_2\}\,S_2S_1 \label{eq:11-stick} \end{equation} $$

Step 4: Compute the phase factor. At the $X$ point,

$$ \kk_X\cdot\aa_2 = \frac{2\pi}{a}(0,0,1)\cdot\frac{a}{2}(1,0,1) = \frac{2\pi}{a}\cdot\frac{a}{2}\cdot 1 = \pi $$

so

$$ D\bigl(\{E|\aa_2\}\bigr) = e^{-i\pi}\,\mathbf{1} = -\mathbf{1} $$

Transferring both sides of Eq. \eqref{eq:11-stick} to the representation gives

$$ \begin{equation} D(S_1)D(S_2) = -\,D(S_2)D(S_1) \label{eq:11-anticomm} \end{equation} $$

That is, $D(S_1)$ and $D(S_2)$ anticommute.

Step 5: A one-dimensional representation cannot exist. If the representation were one-dimensional, $D(S_1), D(S_2)$ would be mere complex numbers. Complex numbers always commute, so $D(S_1)D(S_2)=D(S_2)D(S_1)$. Substituting this into Eq. \eqref{eq:11-anticomm} gives

$$ D(S_1)D(S_2) = -D(S_1)D(S_2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2D(S_1)D(S_2)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad D(S_1)D(S_2)=0 $$

However, a representation matrix must be analytic (nonsingular; the representation of the inverse element must exist), so it cannot be $0$. This is a contradiction.

∎ Hence every irreducible representation of $G_{\kk_X}$ has dimension two or higher. Every band at the $X$ point is degenerate at least twofold.

Physical Meaning: No sticking together in the symmorphic case

What was decisive in this proof was that $S_1$ carries the fractional translation $\bm{\tau}$, and that the difference between $\bm{\tau}$ and $C_{2y}\bm{\tau}$ was a lattice vector “whose inner product with $\kk_X$ is $\pi$ (odd $\times\pi$).”

In a symmorphic crystal, a choice of origin makes every $\bm{\tau}$ equal to $0$, so $\bm{v}=0$ and the phase factor is $+1$. Anticommutation does not occur, and a one-dimensional representation may exist. The bands do not stick together.

It can be confirmed experimentally. Si (diamond type, nonsymmorphic) and GaAs (zinc-blende type $F\bar{4}3m$, symmorphic) have almost the same lattice; the atoms are merely replaced by Ga and As. In GaAs, however, the only symmetry is $T_d$ and inversion is lost, and as a result the degeneracy at the $X$ point is lifted. What corresponds to the $X_1$ level of Si (twofold degenerate) splits into $X_1$ and $X_3$ in GaAs. The splitting reaches several $\mathrm{eV}$. This is a typical example of how “lowering the symmetry by one step breaks the degeneracy at the zone boundary.”

The same idea extends to other nonsymmorphic crystals having $2_1$ screw axes or glide planes. For example, in perovskites of space group $Pnma$ (such as $\mathrm{CaTiO_3}$) and in molecular crystals of $P2_1/c$, the bands are also twofold degenerate on the zone-boundary planes corresponding to the screw axes and glide planes. The mechanism, however, is not the same. At a general point on the zone-boundary plane of these groups, the point-group part of the group of the wave vector is only $C_s$ of order 2, so the “pair of anticommuting unitary elements” that we have just used cannot be constructed at all. What is effective is an antiunitary operation $A$ that combines a screw-axis operation with time reversal; from $A^2=\{E|\bm{a}\}$ and $e^{-i\kk\cdot\bm{a}}=-1$, a Kramers-type twofold degeneracy follows (Herring, 1937). This is where it differs from the $X$ point of diamond, where unitary operations alone determine the degeneracy. The recent discussions of “nonsymmorphic topological materials” also have their roots in such elementary calculations.

However, one must not generalize to “if the group is nonsymmorphic, bands stick together everywhere on the zone boundary.” The effect is limited to those $\kk$ at which a pair of anticommuting elements can actually be constructed within the group of the wave vector $G_\kk$, as we have just seen, that is, to particular planes, lines, and points where the phase factor generated by $\bm{\tau}$ does not become trivial. At which $\kk$ this happens can only be determined by constructing $G_\kk$ each time. Moreover, the above argument ignores spin; when the spin-orbit interaction is included, one must recount using the double group, and the conclusion may change depending on the presence or absence of inversion symmetry and time reversal.

11.9 Irreducible Representations of Phonons

So far we have had the wave functions of electrons in mind, but the group-theoretical argument can be used in exactly the same form for lattice vibrations. Translational symmetry makes the wavenumber $\qq$ appear as a quantum number (the phonon version of Bloch's theorem), and the degeneracy at $\qq$ is determined by the dimensions of the irreducible representations of the group of the wave vector $G_\qq$. As preparation for Chapters 13 and 14, let us classify the vibrational modes at the $\Gamma$ point ($\qq=0$) by group theory.

Derivation: The lattice-vibration representation at the $\Gamma$ point and its reduction (the case of Si)

When the primitive cell contains $s$ atoms, the displacement vectors at the $\Gamma$ point have $3s$ degrees of freedom. The $3s$-dimensional representation spanned by them is called $\Gamma^{\rm lat}$. As with the molecular vibrations in Chapter 10, its character is

$$ \begin{equation} \chi^{\rm lat}(R) = N_R \times \chi^{\rm vec}(R) \label{eq:11-lat} \end{equation} $$

Here $N_R$ is “the number of atoms that are not moved by the operation $R$ (apart from lattice translations),” and $\chi^{\rm vec}(R)$ is the character of the representation of the three-dimensional vector $(x,y,z)$ ($T_{1u}$ in $O_h$).

Step 1: Find $N_R$. Si has two atoms in the primitive cell (A at the origin and B at $\bm{\tau}$). We use the classification of Section 11.8.1.

  • $R \in T_d$ ($E,\ 8C_3,\ 3C_2,\ 6S_4,\ 6\sigma_d$, with zero translational part): A is at the origin, so it does not move. B does not move either. For example, under $C_{2z}$ we have $\bm{\tau}\to\frac{a}{4}(-1,-1,1)$, and the difference is $\frac{a}{4}(-2,-2,0)=-\aa_3$ (a lattice vector), so we return to the same atom. Hence $N_R = 2$.
  • $R \in O_h\setminus T_d$ (of the form $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}$): A$(0)\to\bm{\tau}=$ B, B$(\bm{\tau})\to R\bm{\tau}+\bm{\tau}$. For example, for $\{i|\bm{\tau}\}$ we get $-\bm{\tau}+\bm{\tau}=0=$ A. A and B interchange, so $N_R = 0$.

Step 2: Write down the characters. $\chi^{\rm vec}$ is the character of $T_{1u}$. We only need to pick up the classes on the $T_d$ side (the rest vanish because $N_R=0$).

We have $\chi^{\rm lat}(E)=6=3s$ ($s=2$), so the number of degrees of freedom is consistent.

Step 3: Reduce into irreducible representations. $h=|O_h|=48$. First, for $T_{1u}$ (characters $E{:}3$, $6C_4{:}1$, $3C_2{:}-1$, $6C_2'{:}-1$, $8C_3{:}0$, $i{:}-3$, $6S_4{:}-1$, $8S_6{:}0$, $3\sigma_h{:}1$, $6\sigma_d{:}1$), we add up only the classes in which $\chi^{\rm lat}$ is nonzero:

$$ n_{T_{1u}} = \frac{1}{48}\Bigl[\underbrace{1\cdot 6\cdot 3}_{E} + \underbrace{8\cdot 0\cdot 0}_{8C_3} + \underbrace{3\cdot(-2)\cdot(-1)}_{3C_2} + \underbrace{6\cdot(-2)\cdot(-1)}_{6S_4} + \underbrace{6\cdot 2\cdot 1}_{6\sigma_d}\Bigr] $$ $$ = \frac{1}{48}\bigl[18 + 0 + 6 + 12 + 12\bigr] = \frac{48}{48} = 1 $$

Next, for $T_{2g}$ ($E{:}3$, $8C_3{:}0$, $3C_2{:}-1$, $6S_4{:}-1$, $6\sigma_d{:}1$, …), the characters in the same classes as above happen to coincide, so

$$ n_{T_{2g}} = \frac{1}{48}\bigl[18+0+6+12+12\bigr] = 1 $$

All the other irreducible representations give $0$. For example, for $A_{1g}$ ($\chi=1$ in all five relevant classes),

$$ n_{A_{1g}} = \frac{1}{48}\bigl[6 + 0 + 3(-2)(1) + 6(-2)(1) + 6(2)(1)\bigr] = \frac{1}{48}[6-6-12+12] = 0 $$

In the end,

$$ \begin{equation} \Gamma^{\rm lat}(\mathrm{Si}) = T_{1u} \oplus T_{2g} = \Gamma_{15} \oplus \Gamma_{25'} \label{eq:11-siphonon} \end{equation} $$

∎ The dimensions add up: $3+3=6$ ✓.

Physical Meaning: The acoustic modes always have the same irreducible representation as $\rr$

An acoustic mode at $\qq=0$ is a displacement in which “the whole crystal translates rigidly.” The translation vector $(\delta x,\delta y,\delta z)$ has exactly the same transformation properties as the position vector $\rr$. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \Gamma^{\rm acoustic} = \Gamma^{\rm vec}\ (=\ \rr\ \text{has the same irreducible representation}) \label{eq:11-acoustic} \end{equation} $$

holds. In $O_h$, $\rr$ belongs to $T_{1u}=\Gamma_{15}$ ($x,y,z$ change sign under inversion, hence $u$). The $T_{1u}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:11-siphonon} gives the three acoustic modes, and the remaining $T_{2g}=\Gamma_{25'}$ gives the three optical modes. That there is just one threefold-degenerate optical phonon is the picture that the phonon dispersion of Si shows at the $\Gamma$ point (check against the figure in Chapter 13).

This rule is universal. In any crystal, of the $3s$ modes at the $\Gamma$ point, three always belong to the same irreducible representation as $\rr$, and these are the acoustic modes. The frequency goes to $\omega\to0$ because a rigid translation requires no energy (Goldstone mode).

Example 11.6: The $520\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ Raman peak of Si

Show by group theory that the optical phonon $\Gamma_{25'}$ of Si is Raman-active and infrared-inactive.

Solution. We use the criteria of Section 10.7 in Chapter 10.

Infrared-active: the irreducible representation of the mode must be the same as that of one of $x,y,z$ (= the dipole moment). In $O_h$, $x,y,z$ belong to $T_{1u}$. The optical mode is $T_{2g}$, so they do not coincide. It is infrared-inactive. More simply, $T_{2g}$ is even ($g$) under inversion whereas $x,y,z$ are odd ($u$), and in a centrosymmetric crystal the two are incompatible because of the mutual exclusion rule.

Raman-active: the irreducible representation of the mode must be the same as that of one of the quadratic forms $x^2, y^2, z^2, xy, yz, zx$ (= the polarizability tensor). The reduction of the quadratic forms in $O_h$ is

$$ \{x^2+y^2+z^2\} \to A_{1g}, \quad \{2z^2-x^2-y^2,\ x^2-y^2\} \to E_g, \quad \{xy, yz, zx\} \to T_{2g} $$

The optical mode $T_{2g}$ coincides with the last set. It is Raman-active.

∎ This is why a sharp single peak at $520.5\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ appears in the Raman spectrum of Si. The measurement routinely used for quality evaluation of Si wafers, stress measurement, and distinguishing amorphous from crystalline material rests on this group-theoretical conclusion. Conversely, in Si this phonon cannot be seen directly in infrared absorption (it is forbidden in one-phonon processes).

For comparison, consider the NaCl type ($Fm\bar{3}m$, symmorphic, two atoms). The two atoms are not interchanged by any operation, so $N_R=2$ for all operations and $\Gamma^{\rm lat}=2\,T_{1u}$. With an acoustic $T_{1u}$ plus an optical $T_{1u}$, the optical mode is infrared-active and Raman-inactive. This is why the reststrahlen band of NaCl is seen in the infrared. Even for the same “two atoms, cubic” case, the selection rules are reversed if the structure differs.

Supplement: Phonons with $\qq\ne0$, and on to Chapters 13 and 14

The story is the same for $\qq\ne0$: the irreducible representations of the little co-group of $\qq$ classify the modes. On the $\Delta$ line the little co-group is $C_{4v}$, so we can simply apply the compatibility relations of Table 11.6, $\Gamma_{15}\to\Delta_1\oplus\Delta_5$ and $\Gamma_{25'}\to\Delta_{2'}\oplus\Delta_5$. Since $\Gamma^{\rm lat}=\Gamma_{15}\oplus\Gamma_{25'}$, the six modes of Si split into $\Delta_1\oplus\Delta_{2'}\oplus2\Delta_5$ ($1+1+2+2=6$). The longitudinal acoustic (LA) mode is $\Delta_1$, the longitudinal optical (LO) mode is $\Delta_{2'}$, and the transverse TA and TO modes are each the twofold-degenerate $\Delta_5$. Precisely because LA and LO carry different labels, the sticking together of bands described in Section 11.8 allows the two to join at the $X$ point. That the phonon dispersion diagram shows TA and TO as double lines and LA and LO as single lines is determined by group theory.

Moreover, the reason why only phonons with $\qq\simeq0$ are seen in optical absorption and Raman scattering is the conservation of crystal momentum, $\kk'=\kk+\qq+\GG$ (the conservation of $\kk$ described in Section 11.3), together with the fact that the wavenumber of light is smaller than the size of the $\mathrm{BZ}$ by orders of magnitude. Inelastic neutron scattering can measure the whole dispersion because the neutron wavenumber is comparable to the $\mathrm{BZ}$ (Chapter 13). A process involving $\GG$ is an Umklapp process, and it makes the thermal conductivity finite (Chapter 14). All of this follows from the quantization of $\kk$.

11.10 Summary and Exercises

11.10.1 Summary

  • The product of Seitz symbols is $\{R_1|\bm{\tau}_1\}\{R_2|\bm{\tau}_2\}=\{R_1R_2|R_1\bm{\tau}_2+\bm{\tau}_1\}$. The rotation on the left acts on the translation on the right. The inverse element is $\{R^{-1}|-R^{-1}\bm{\tau}\}$.
  • The translation group $\mathcal{T}$ is a normal subgroup of the space group, and it is an abelian group. $\{R|\bm{\tau}\}\{E|\RR\}\{R|\bm{\tau}\}^{-1}=\{E|R\RR\}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:11-normal}). The quotient group $G/\mathcal{T}$ is the crystallographic point group.
  • Every irreducible representation of an abelian group is one-dimensional (Theorem 11.1). The argument is a three-step syllogism: “each element forms its own class → the number of irreducible representations equals the order → from $\sum\ell_i^2=h$, all of them are one-dimensional.”
  • Imposing the periodic boundary conditions $\{E|N_i\aa_i\}=\{E|0\}$ restricts the irreducible representations to $D^{(\kk)}(\{E|\RR_n\})=e^{-i\kk\cdot\RR_n}$, and $\kk=\frac{m_1}{N_1}\bb_1+\frac{m_2}{N_2}\bb_2+\frac{m_3}{N_3}\bb_3$ is quantized into $N_1N_2N_3$ values (Eqs. \eqref{eq:11-kquant} and \eqref{eq:11-irrep3d}). The volume of reciprocal space per $\kk$ point is $(2\pi)^3/V$.
  • $\kk$ is the label of an irreducible representation of the translation group. Hence $\kk$ is a quantum number, and since $\GG\cdot\RR=2\pi\times$ integer, $\kk$ and $\kk+\GG$ are the same irreducible representation. This is why it suffices to look only at the first Brillouin zone.
  • Bloch's theorem is a consequence of the irreducible representations being one-dimensional. $[\Ham,\hat{P}_{\{E|\RR\}}]=0$ → the eigenfunctions are bases of irreducible representations → $\hat{P}_{\{E|\RR\}}\psi_\kk=e^{-i\kk\cdot\RR}\psi_\kk$ → $\psi_\kk=e^{i\kk\cdot\rr}u_\kk$, $u_\kk(\rr+\RR)=u_\kk(\rr)$. The band index $n$ corresponds to “the same irreducible representation appearing many times.”
  • The group of the wave vector $G_\kk$ and the little co-group $P_\kk$: the set of operations satisfying $R\kk=\kk+\GG$. $|P_\kk|\times(\text{size of the star})=|P|$. In fcc, $\Gamma{:}\,O_h$, $X{:}\,D_{4h}$, $L{:}\,D_{3d}$, $W{:}\,D_{2d}$, $\Delta{:}\,C_{4v}$, $\Lambda{:}\,C_{3v}$, $\Sigma{:}\,C_{2v}$. This is also the reason why it suffices to calculate only the irreducible wedge.
  • The degeneracy of a band equals the dimension of an irreducible representation of the group of the wave vector $G_\kk$. Where the factor system is trivial (the $\Gamma$ point, interior points of symmetry lines, symmorphic space groups), it may be read in terms of the irreducible representations of the little co-group $P_\kk$. The $\Gamma$ point is at most threefold degenerate, the $\Delta$ and $\Lambda$ lines are at most twofold degenerate, and the $\Sigma$ line has no degeneracy. The top of the valence band of Si, $\Gamma_{25'}$ ($T_{2g}$), is threefold degenerate, and when it goes out along the $\Delta$ line it splits into $\Delta_{2'}\oplus\Delta_5$.
  • The compatibility relations completely determine how the lines of a band diagram connect. Lines carrying the same irreducible-representation label cannot cross in the general situation in which $\kk$ is varied, and they repel each other (the non-crossing rule). The conduction-band minimum of Si lies short of the $X$ point because the two bands that necessarily stick together at the $X$ point ($\Delta_1$ and $\Delta_{2'}$) split, near $X$, to first order in $q=|\kk-\kk_X|$.
  • In a nonsymmorphic space group, bands stick together at the zone boundary. At the $X$ point of the diamond structure, the anticommutation relation $D(S_1)D(S_2)=-D(S_2)D(S_1)$ holds, and since no one-dimensional representation can exist, all bands are twofold degenerate. In symmorphic GaAs they split.
  • Phonons fit into the same framework. $\chi^{\rm lat}(R)=N_R\,\chi^{\rm vec}(R)$. The acoustic modes at the $\Gamma$ point are always the same irreducible representation as $\rr$. For Si, $\Gamma^{\rm lat}=\Gamma_{15}\oplus\Gamma_{25'}$, and the optical mode $\Gamma_{25'}(T_{2g})$ is Raman-active and infrared-inactive—this is the origin of the peak at $520\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$.

Exercise 11.1: Constructing the character table of the one-dimensional translation group for $N=4$

Consider a one-dimensional crystal with lattice constant $a$ and $N=4$.

(1) Write the multiplication table of the translation group $\mathcal{T}_1=\{T_0,T_1,T_2,T_3\}$.

(2) Find all the irreducible representations and summarize them in the form of a character table.

(3) Write down the allowed values of $k$ within the first Brillouin zone.

(4) Verify that $\sum_i \ell_i^2 = h$ and “the number of irreducible representations equals the number of classes” hold.

Hint: (1) $T_nT_m=T_{(n+m)\bmod 4}$. (2) $D^{(m)}(T_n)=e^{-2\pi i mn/4}=(-i)^{mn}$. For $m=0$ all the characters are $1$; for $m=1$ they are $(1,-i,-1,i)$; for $m=2$ they are $(1,-1,1,-1)$; for $m=3$ they are $(1,i,-1,-i)$. (3) $k=2\pi m/4a$, and taking $m=-1,0,1,2$ gives the four values $k=-\pi/2a,\ 0,\ \pi/2a,\ \pi/a$. (4) Since the group is abelian, the four elements form four classes, and there are also four irreducible representations. $1^2\times4=4=h$.

Exercise 11.2: The little co-group and the size of the star

For fcc ($O_h$, order 48), verify the following.

(1) Count the operations that leave the $L$ point $\frac{2\pi}{a}(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12)$ invariant, and show that the little co-group is $D_{3d}$ (order 12).

(2) Show that the little co-group of the $\Lambda$ line $\frac{2\pi}{a}(\xi,\xi,\xi)$ ($0<\xi<\frac12$) is $C_{3v}$ (order 6), and explain why the order doubles at the $L$ point.

(3) Verify that $|P_\kk|\times(\text{size of the star})=48$ holds in every row of Table 11.3.

Hint: (1) The $C_3, C_3^2$ about $[111]$ and the three $\sigma_d$ planes containing $[111]$ give $C_{3v}$ (order 6). Moreover, since $2L=\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,1,1)=\bb_1+\bb_2+\bb_3$ is a reciprocal lattice vector, operations that take $L\to -L$ (such as $i$) are also allowed, and the order doubles to 12. (2) For a point on the $\Lambda$ line, $2\kk$ is not a reciprocal lattice vector, so $\kk\to-\kk$ cannot be used, and the group stops at $C_{3v}$. (3) $48/12=4$ ($L$), $48/6=8$ ($\Lambda$), and so on.

Exercise 11.3: Deriving compatibility relations by character calculations

Using the same procedure as in Example 11.4, derive the following compatibility relations (a check of Table 11.6).

(2) Restrict the same $E_g$ to the $C_{3v}$ of the $\Lambda$ line and decompose it.

(3) Restrict $\Gamma_{15}=T_{1u}$ to $C_{4v}$ and to $C_{3v}$ and decompose it.

(4) Using the results of (1)–(3), find how many lines in total a band derived from $d$ orbitals ($E_g+T_{2g}$, Chapter 10) gives rise to when it goes out from $\Gamma$ along the $\Delta$ line.

Hint: (1) The characters of $E_g$ on $C_{4v}$ are $(2,0,2,2,0)$. Substituting into the formula, $n_{A_1}=\frac18[2+0+2+4+0]=1$, $n_{B_1}=\frac18[2+0+2+4+0]=1$, and the others are 0. Hence $\Delta_1\oplus\Delta_2$. (2) The characters on $C_{3v}$ are $(2,-1,0)$, and $n_E=\frac16[4+2+0]=1$, so only $\Lambda_3$ appears. (3) The characters of $T_{1u}$ on $C_{4v}$ are $(3,1,-1,1,1)$, giving $\Delta_1\oplus\Delta_5$; on $C_{3v}$ they are $(3,0,1)$, giving $\Lambda_1\oplus\Lambda_3$. Exercise 11.4: Checking the sticking together of bands in the diamond structure with a different pair of operations

Repeat the proof of Theorem 11.5 with the pair $S_1=\{\sigma_y|\bm{\tau}\}$ (a reflection $y\to-y$) and $S_2=\{C_{2x}|0\}$ (a twofold rotation about the $x$ axis).

(1) Verify that both belong to $G_{\kk_X}$ ($\kk_X=\frac{2\pi}{a}(0,0,1)$).

(2) Compute $S_1S_2$ and $S_2S_1$ in Seitz symbols, and find the $\bm{v}$ in $S_1S_2=\{E|\bm{v}\}S_2S_1$.

(3) Show that $e^{-i\kk_X\cdot\bm{v}}=-1$, and conclude that no one-dimensional representation can exist.

(4) Explain why sticking together does not occur when the same calculation is carried out at a point on the $\Delta$ line, $\kk=\frac{2\pi}{a}(0,0,\xi)$ ($\xi<1$).

Hint: (2) $S_1S_2=\{\sigma_yC_{2x}|\bm{\tau}\}$, $S_2S_1=\{C_{2x}\sigma_y|C_{2x}\bm{\tau}\}$, and since $C_{2x}\bm{\tau}=\frac{a}{4}(1,-1,-1)$, $\bm{v}=\bm{\tau}-C_{2x}\bm{\tau}=\frac{a}{2}(0,1,1)=\aa_1$. Exercise 11.5: $\Gamma$-point phonons of various crystals

Using Eq. \eqref{eq:11-lat}, find the representation of the $\Gamma$-point lattice vibrations of the following crystals, separate it into acoustic and optical modes, and determine the infrared activity and Raman activity.

(1) Rock-salt-type $\mathrm{NaCl}$ ($Fm\bar{3}m$, two atoms in the primitive cell, both at $O_h$-symmetric positions)

(2) Zinc-blende-type $\mathrm{GaAs}$ ($F\bar{4}3m$, point group $T_d$, two atoms in the primitive cell)

(3) Diamond-type $\mathrm{Si}$ (you may use the result obtained in Example 11.6)

(4) Comparing the above three, state how “the presence or absence of centrosymmetry changes the selection rules.”

Hint: (1) Since $N_R=2$ for every operation, $\Gamma^{\rm lat}=2T_{1u}$. The optical mode is $T_{1u}$: infrared-active, Raman-inactive. (2) $T_d$ has no inversion, and $x,y,z$ and $xy,yz,zx$ both belong to $T_2$. $\Gamma^{\rm lat}=2T_2$, and the optical mode $T_2$ is both infrared-active and Raman-active.
