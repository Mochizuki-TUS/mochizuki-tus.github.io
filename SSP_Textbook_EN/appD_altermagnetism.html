D.0 In three lines
  1. An altermagnet is a collinear magnet whose magnetization is compensated to zero, yet its bands show a \(\mathbf k\)-dependent spin splitting. The sign of the splitting alternates in \(\mathbf k\) space (d-wave, g-wave, i-wave).
  2. Criterion: the symmetry operations that connect sublattices of opposite spin are neither translations nor inversion, but only rotations (including reflections, screws, and glides).
  3. Consequences: (i) Even without SOC, a \(\mathbf k\)-dependent spin splitting arises, and for a d-wave altermagnet an accompanying linear spin-polarized current also arises. (ii) When SOC is included, time reversal \(T\) does not survive as a symmetry operation even in combination with a translation or inversion (\(T\boldsymbol\tau\), \(PT\)); therefore, if the direction of the Néel vector allows it, responses that were thought to belong to ferromagnets, such as the anomalous Hall effect and magneto-optical effects, can also appear.

D.1 Three Kinds of Collinear Magnetism: Ferromagnetism, Antiferromagnetism, and Altermagnetism

Here we deal with collinear magnetic materials, in which all the magnetic moments are parallel or antiparallel to a single axis. Traditionally, these were divided into two classes: ferromagnetism (including ferrimagnetism) if the net magnetization is finite, and antiferromagnetism if it is zero. Altermagnetism is a third case: it is the same as antiferromagnetism in that the magnetization is zero, and the same as ferromagnetism in that the electronic bands are spin-split [Smejkal2022a, Smejkal2022b]. In what follows, the difference of the moments of the two sublattices, \(\mathbf N=\mathbf m_A-\mathbf m_B\), is called the Néel vector.

Intuitive picture. Look at the right column of Figure D.1. The ↑ atoms sit in cages (ligand environments) elongated along the x direction, and the ↓ atoms sit in cages elongated along the y direction. Then ↑ electrons move easily along x, and ↓ electrons move easily along y. In the crystal as a whole the numbers of ↑ and ↓ atoms are equal, so the magnetization is zero; but if we sort the electrons by their direction of travel, electrons moving along x are mostly ↑, and electrons moving along y are mostly ↓. In other words, “the system is spin-polarized when viewed direction by direction.” This is the content of “looks antiferromagnetic but behaves like a ferromagnet.” (This “polarization sorted by direction of travel” is a picture specific to d-wave. For g-wave and i-wave as well, the polarization alternates in \(\mathbf k\) space, but it cancels when averaged over each current direction. See Section D.5(a).)

In the middle column (the conventional antiferromagnet), the environments of the ↑ atoms and the ↓ atoms have the same shape, so ↑ electrons and ↓ electrons can move in exactly the same way. Hence the bands are degenerate at every \(\mathbf k\). The difference between an altermagnet and a conventional antiferromagnet lies not in the arrangement of the magnetic moments itself but in what operation the crystal structure, including the nonmagnetic atoms, uses to connect the ↑ sublattice and the ↓ sublattice.

D.2 Why Do Ordinary Antiferromagnets Show Spin Degeneracy While Altermagnets Show Splitting?

Up to Section D.5(b), we ignore the spin-orbit interaction (SOC) (the nonrelativistic limit). In this case the electronic Hamiltonian has no term that couples the spin direction to the crystal lattice, so rotations in spin space and operations in real space can be carried out independently. We therefore write a symmetry operation as

\[ [\,R_s \,\|\, R_l\,]\qquad (R_s:\ \text{operation in spin space},\ \ R_l = \{A|\boldsymbol\tau\}:\ \text{operation in real space}) \]

In an ordinary magnetic space group, the rotation parts of \(R_s\) and \(R_l\) are fixed to be the same, but without SOC this constraint is absent, and a larger group (the spin group) determines the symmetry [Brinkman1966, Litvin1974]. The notation is summarized in Section D.12.

(a) Rotation about the spin axis: \(s_z\) is a good quantum number

In a collinear magnet, the operation \([C_\phi(\hat{\mathbf n})\,\|\,E]\), which rotates only the spins through an arbitrary angle about the common moment axis \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) (taken to be \(z\) below), is a symmetry operation. Therefore \(s_z\) is a good quantum number, and each band can be labeled ↑ or ↓. Below we write \(\varepsilon(\mathbf k,\sigma)\) (\(\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow\) or \(\pm1\)).

(b) Spin flip × time reversal: \(\varepsilon(\mathbf k,\sigma)=\varepsilon(-\mathbf k,\sigma)\) for any collinear magnet

Time reversal \(T\) reverses the magnetic moments and acts as \((\mathbf k,\sigma)\to(-\mathbf k,-\sigma)\). \(T\) alone is not a symmetry operation, because it reverses the magnetic structure. However, if we then rotate only the spins by 180° about an axis perpendicular to \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) (\(C_{2\perp}\)), the moments return to their original directions. This \([C_{2\perp}T\,\|\,E]\) is always a symmetry operation in the absence of SOC, and it acts as \((\mathbf k,\sigma)\to(-\mathbf k,\sigma)\), so that

\[ \varepsilon(\mathbf k,\sigma)=\varepsilon(-\mathbf k,\sigma)\tag{1} \]

holds even for crystals without an inversion center. Therefore the nonrelativistic spin splitting \(\Delta(\mathbf k)=\varepsilon(\mathbf k,\uparrow)-\varepsilon(\mathbf k,\downarrow)\) is an even function of \(\mathbf k\), and the partial waves are only the even orders s, d, g, i, ….

CautionTo obtain nonrelativistically a spin splitting that is an odd function of \(\mathbf k\) (p-wave, etc.), one needs a noncollinear order that lacks the operation (b) of collinear magnets (systems called “p-wave magnets”; see Section D.8(v)). In a coplanar order, \([C_{2\mathbf n}T\|E]\) about the normal \(\mathbf n\) of the spin plane remains, so only the spin component perpendicular to the plane can be an odd function. The Rashba and Dresselhaus splittings of nonmagnetic noncentrosymmetric crystals with SOC are also odd functions, but they are relativistic effects.

(c) Operations that exchange opposite-spin sublattices: three cases

Suppose that an operation \(g=\{A|\boldsymbol\tau\}\) in real space carries the ↑ sublattice onto the ↓ sublattice. \(g\) alone does not match the directions of the moments, but if we combine it with the spin flip \(C_{2\perp}\), the magnetic structure returns to itself, and \([C_{2\perp}\|g]\) is a symmetry operation. It acts as \(\mathbf k\to A\mathbf k\), \(\sigma\to-\sigma\), so that

\[ \varepsilon(\mathbf k,\sigma)=\varepsilon(A\mathbf k,-\sigma)\tag{2} \]

holds. Depending on the rotation part \(A\), there are three cases.

  1. \(A=E\) (pure translation). Eq. (2) reads \(\varepsilon(\mathbf k,\uparrow)=\varepsilon(\mathbf k,\downarrow)\), so the bands are degenerate at all \(\mathbf k\). A typical example is an order that doubles the unit cell: among the translations \(\mathbf t\) of the parent lattice, those with \(e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf t}=-1\) flip the spins.
  2. \(A=P\) (spatial inversion). Eq. (2) reads \(\varepsilon(\mathbf k,\sigma)=\varepsilon(-\mathbf k,-\sigma)\). Combined with Eq. (1), this gives \(\varepsilon(\mathbf k,\sigma)=\varepsilon(\mathbf k,-\sigma)\), so again the bands are degenerate at all \(\mathbf k\) (the \(PT\) antiferromagnets of the Cr2O3 type).
  3. \(A\) is any other rotation (proper or improper). Splitting is allowed at a general \(\mathbf k\). However, if \(A\mathbf k\equiv\mathbf k\) (up to a reciprocal lattice vector), Eq. (2) gives \(\Delta(\mathbf k)=-\Delta(\mathbf k)=0\), and if \(A\mathbf k\equiv-\mathbf k\), then combined with Eq. (1) we again get \(\Delta=0\). In this way nodes of zero splitting arise in \(\mathbf k\) space. If the exchanging operation is a reflection (including glide) or a twofold rotation (including a screw), the nodes form a nodal plane; if it is a threefold, fourfold, or sixfold rotation, the nodes form a nodal line on the rotation axis. Since \(\Delta(A\mathbf k)=-\Delta(\mathbf k)\), the sign alternates across a nodal plane. This is the origin of the name altermagnetism.

Note that if even one of the operations of the crystal exchanges the sublattices by a translation or an inversion, degeneracy is enforced. An altermagnet is the case in which the operations that exchange the sublattices include only rotations.

(d) Compensation is enforced by symmetry

In case (c)-3, the ↑ band is the ↓ band mapped by \(A\) (\(\varepsilon(\mathbf k,\uparrow)=\varepsilon(A\mathbf k,\downarrow)\)). Since \(A\) maps the Brillouin zone onto itself, for any Fermi level \(E_F\) we have

\[ n_\uparrow=\sum_{\mathbf k} f\big(\varepsilon(\mathbf k,\uparrow)\big)=\sum_{\mathbf k} f\big(\varepsilon(A\mathbf k,\downarrow)\big)=n_\downarrow \]

That is, the zero net magnetization is guaranteed by symmetry, independently of the band filling and the details of the interactions. In a compensated ferrimagnet, by contrast, the two sublattices are not connected by a symmetry operation, and the zero magnetization is an accidental coincidence at a particular composition or temperature.

(e) Practical consequences

  • Altermagnetism basically requires a \(\mathbf q=0\) order (magnetic unit cell = chemical unit cell). This is because in an order that simply doubles the unit cell, the spin-flipping translation of (c)-1 always exists.
  • The primitive cell must contain at least two magnetic atoms, connected only by a rotation. What keeps the two magnetic atoms from being superposed by a translation or an inversion is the arrangement of the surrounding nonmagnetic atoms (ligands).
  • There are also cases in which a more complicated superlattice-type order (one in which no translation flips all the spins simultaneously) becomes an altermagnet.

D.3 Getting a Feel for It with a Minimal Model

Place two sublattices, A \((0,0)\) and B \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\), on a two-dimensional square lattice, and consider the following tight-binding model (lattice constant 1). The hopping within each sublattice is anisotropic, and it is rotated by 90° between A and B. This takes the place of the “cage” of Section D.1.

\[ \varepsilon_A=-2t\big[(1+\delta)\cos k_x+(1-\delta)\cos k_y\big],\qquad \varepsilon_B=-2t\big[(1-\delta)\cos k_x+(1+\delta)\cos k_y\big], \] \[ V=-4t'\cos\tfrac{k_x}{2}\cos\tfrac{k_y}{2},\qquad H_\sigma(\mathbf k)=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_A-\sigma J & V\\ V & \varepsilon_B+\sigma J\end{pmatrix}\quad(\sigma=\pm1).\tag{3} \]

The terms \(-\sigma J\) and \(+\sigma J\) give A and B local exchange fields of opposite directions (an antiferromagnetic arrangement). Letting \(\varepsilon_0=-2t(\cos k_x+\cos k_y)\) and \(\eta=-2t\delta(\cos k_x-\cos k_y)\), we have \(\varepsilon_{A,B}=\varepsilon_0\pm\eta\), so that

\[ E_{\sigma,\pm}(\mathbf k)=\varepsilon_0\pm\sqrt{(\eta-\sigma J)^2+V^2}.\tag{4} \]

The spin splitting of the lower band, for small \(|\eta|\), is

\[ \Delta(\mathbf k)\equiv E_{\uparrow,-}-E_{\downarrow,-}\simeq\frac{2\eta J}{\sqrt{J^2+V^2}}=-\frac{4t\delta J}{\sqrt{J^2+V^2}}\,(\cos k_x-\cos k_y)\tag{5} \]

The distribution of signs is the same as that of \(-\delta J(\cos k_x-\cos k_y)\), but the positive factor \(J/\sqrt{J^2+V^2}\) depends on \(\mathbf k\), so the proportionality is not exact (for the default parameters, \(\Delta/(\cos k_x-\cos k_y)\) varies from \(-1.20\) to \(-0.73\) within the zone). This splitting has d-wave symmetry (\(k_x^2-k_y^2\) type) and has the following properties (the derivation is in Section D.13, and the numerical check is at the end of this section).

  • The nodal lines are \(k_x=\pm k_y\). The magnitude is largest at X \((\pi,0)\) and Y \((0,\pi)\), where the signs are opposite. At X, \(V=0\), so exactly \(\Delta_X=|J+4t\delta|-|J-4t\delta|\) (this is \(8t\delta\) if \(J>4t|\delta|\), and \(2J\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\delta\) if \(J<4t|\delta|\)). That is, the magnitude is \(2\min(J,4t|\delta|)\), so even if \(J\) is increased, it saturates at the scale of the anisotropy (the hopping).
  • It vanishes for \(\delta=0\). In this case the translation \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\) interchanges A and B, so the material is an ordinary antiferromagnet.
  • It also vanishes for \(J=0\). Both magnetic order and an anisotropic environment are required.
  • For \(\delta\neq0\), the operation that interchanges A and B is the four-fold rotation about \((\tfrac12,0)\) (together with the reflection in the diagonal direction), which gives \(\Delta(k_x,k_y)=-\Delta(k_y,k_x)\).

For comparison, we have also prepared a ferromagnetic arrangement in which \(-\sigma J\) is applied to both A and B. In this case \(E_{\sigma,\pm}=\varepsilon_0-\sigma J\pm\sqrt{\eta^2+V^2}\), and the splitting is \(\Delta=-2J\), independent of \(\mathbf k\) (s-wave). A method for systematically constructing minimal models of altermagnetism from the symmetry of the sublattices is summarized in [Roig2024].

D.4 Classification by Symmetry: d-Wave, g-Wave, and i-Wave

Šmejkal, Sinova, and Jungwirth introduced the spin Laue group as the group that determines the spin structure of the nonrelativistic bands, and classified collinear magnets into three kinds [Smejkal2022a]. By Eq. (1), the band of each spin is invariant under \(\mathbf k\to-\mathbf k\), so the point group that acts in \(\mathbf k\) space is the Laue group \(G\), which is the crystallographic point group with inversion added. Let \(H\) be the set of those operations of \(G\) that map each sublattice onto itself. For Type 3 (altermagnet), inversion preserves the sublattices, so \(H\ni P\) and \(H\) is a subgroup of index 2 in \(G\).

DefinitionThe three kinds of spin Laue groups [Smejkal2022a]. In the paper they are called types I, II, and III, but in this appendix we write them as Type 1, Type 2, and Type 3 to distinguish them from types I–IV of the magnetic space groups.
  • Type 1: \([E\|G]\). There is no operation that interchanges the sublattices. Ferromagnets and ferrimagnets.
  • Type 2: \([E\|G]+[C_2\|G]\). Every operation appears both without and with spin reversal (a translation or inversion interchanges the sublattices). Conventional antiferromagnets.
  • Type 3: \([E\|H]+[C_2\|G-H]\), \(H\ne G\). Altermagnets.

For readers familiar with group theory, the clearest way to say this is in the language of representation theory. Let \(s(g)=+1\) for \(g\in H\) and \(s(g)=-1\) for \(g\in G-H\). Since \(H\) is a subgroup of index 2, \(s\) is a one-dimensional real irreducible representation \(\Gamma_s\) of \(G\). Since \(P\in H\), \(\Gamma_s\) is even under inversion (a g representation), and for Type 3 it is a nontrivial representation. Rewriting Eq. (2), we obtain

\[ \Delta(g\mathbf k)=s(g)\,\Delta(\mathbf k)\qquad (g\in G),\tag{6} \]

that is, the spin splitting \(\Delta(\mathbf k)\) is a function of \(\mathbf k\) that transforms according to \(\Gamma_s\). When the degree of the lowest-order polynomial belonging to \(\Gamma_s\) is 2, 4, or 6, we call the splitting d-wave, g-wave, or i-wave, respectively.

The only real one-dimensional representations are \(A_g\) and \(A_u\), and the \(E_g\) of \(\bar3\) and \(m\bar3\) is a pair of complex-conjugate one-dimensional representations. Since \(s(g)=\pm1\) must be real, in these Laue groups a compensated collinear order that interchanges the sublattices necessarily becomes a conventional antiferromagnet (or else the sublattices are not related by a symmetry operation at all).
  2. When the lowest-order space is two-dimensional (the \(B_g\) of \(2/m\), the \(B_g\) of \(4/m\), and the \(B_g\) of \(6/m\)). In this case, only some of the nodal planes are fixed by symmetry, and the orientation of the remaining nodal planes rotates with the ratio of the two coefficients (which is determined by the material). For \(2/m\) and \(6/m\), the only nodal plane enforced by symmetry is the plane \(k_z=0\) (a nodal line, the \(k_z\) axis, is also enforced). For \(4/m\), no nodal plane is enforced at all; what is enforced is only a line, the \(k_z\) axis.
  3. Definition of planar / bulk. The P / B in the table is copied as it is from the classification in Fig. 2 of the paper. We have not verified the details of the definition in the paper in this appendix, so we merely quote the P / B distinction as the paper’s notation.

Everything is determined by “which irreducible representation carries the Néel pattern.” \(\Gamma_s\) is precisely the representation at the Gamma point (\(\mathbf q=0\)) spanned by the \(\pm1\) pattern of a scalar placed on the Wyckoff orbit of the magnetic atoms (\(+1\) on the ↑ sublattice, \(-1\) on ↓). In the language of CrystOD, it is the “irreducible representation of the site-substitution mode (the sign pattern of a scalar).” However, the GM label that crystod-mag prints in its table is something different: it is the irreducible representation \(\Gamma_s\otimes\Gamma(R_{\mathbf N})\) of the magnetic-moment configuration (an axial vector) (see the beginning of Section D.9). When the crystallographic point group has no inversion, we extend it to the Laue group by assigning \(+1\) to inversion. In the worked examples of Section D.9, the rutile type is \(B_{2g}\) of \(4/mmm\), MnTe is \(B_{1g}\) of \(6/mmm\), the sign pattern of the hematite type is \(A_{2g}\) of \(\bar3m\), and the G-, C-, and A-type orders of the Pnma perovskite are \(B_{3g},B_{2g},B_{1g}\) of \(mmm\).

  • Trivial representation (the pattern is invariant under all operations): ferromagnets and ferrimagnets.
  • Odd under inversion (\(P\) interchanges the sublattices): \(PT\) antiferromagnets.
  • Nontrivial and even under inversion: altermagnets. The kind of wave is determined by \(\Gamma_s\).
  • \(\mathbf q\neq0\) to begin with (a translation interchanges the sublattices): conventional antiferromagnets.

D.5 Why Does It Behave “Like a Ferromagnet”?

“Ferromagnet-like behavior” comes in two kinds of different character. It is important not to confuse them.

(a) Effects that arise from the spin-split bands alone (no SOC needed; nonrelativistic)

  • Spin splitting of bands seen in ARPES. It has been observed in MnTe [Krempasky2024, Lee2024, Osumi2024] and CrSb [Reimers2024, Ding2024, Yang2025, Zeng2024] (Section D.7). Spin-resolved measurements are those of CrSb in [Yang2025], for example.
  • Spin-polarized current and the spin-splitter effect [GonzalezHernandez2021]. In linear response this is limited to the d-wave case. \(\sigma^\uparrow_{ij}-\sigma^\downarrow_{ij}\) transforms according to \(\Gamma_s\) as a symmetric second-rank tensor, and a symmetric second-rank tensor contains only components with \(l=0,2\), so it vanishes for g-wave and i-wave (MnTe and CrSb have no linear spin-splitter effect) [Smejkal2022a]. For a splitting of the \(k_x^2-k_y^2\) type, the conductivity tensor of each spin satisfies \(\sigma^\uparrow_{xx}\neq\sigma^\downarrow_{xx}\), but \(\sigma^\uparrow_{xx}=\sigma^\downarrow_{yy}\), and \(\sigma^\sigma_{xy}=0\) for each spin. Therefore an electric field along the x direction drives a spin-polarized current in the longitudinal (x) direction, whereas an electric field along the nodal-line direction (the diagonal) drives a transverse pure spin current with no accompanying charge flow. The observations of the spin-splitter torque [Bai2022, Karube2022, Bose2022] were all made on RuO2, so the reservation about RuO2 in Section D.7 applies.
  • Giant magnetoresistance and tunnel magnetoresistance: since a d-wave altermagnet carries a spin-polarized current, it may be usable in place of a ferromagnetic electrode [Smejkal2022c] (for the same reason, g-wave and i-wave are excluded [Smejkal2022a]).
  • Magnons: the right-handed and left-handed magnon branches split depending on \(\mathbf k\) (with alternating sign) [Smejkal2023]. In MnTe this has been observed by inelastic neutron scattering [Liu2024MnTe], while in MnF2 it could not be resolved [Morano2025]. That the symmetry allows it and that the splitting is large are separate matters.

(b) Effects that require SOC and are determined by the magnetic point group (relativistic)

The anomalous Hall effect (AHE), the magneto-optical Kerr effect, X-ray magnetic circular dichroism (XMCD), and weak ferromagnetism belong here. With SOC, spin and lattice are coupled, and the symmetry is not the spin group but the ordinary magnetic space group (MSG) and magnetic point group (MPG). And the MPG depends on the direction of the Néel vector \(\mathbf N\).

The antisymmetric part of the Hall conductivity is represented by the Hall vector \(\boldsymbol\sigma_H=(\sigma_{yz},\sigma_{zx},\sigma_{xy})\), which, like the magnetization, is axial and odd in \(T\). Therefore the AHE is allowed only when the MPG allows an axial, \(T\)-odd vector, that is, only when it is one of the magnetic point groups compatible with ferromagnetism. Of the 122 magnetic point groups, 31 allow a spontaneous magnetization (an axial, \(T\)-odd vector) (a standard fact). Weak ferromagnetism and the AHE share the same symmetry condition [GonzalezBetancourt2023, Smejkal2022nrm].

  • In conventional antiferromagnets, the AHE is forbidden by either \(T\boldsymbol\tau\) (the MSG contains the product of \(T\) and a translation, and the MPG contains \(T\) itself; all \(T\)-odd responses vanish) or \(PT\) (an axial, \(T\)-odd vector is forbidden; however, a linear magnetoelectric effect may remain, with Cr2O3 as an example).
  • In altermagnets neither is present. Whether an axial vector is allowed is determined by the direction of \(\mathbf N\).

As concrete examples, we tabulate the results obtained by CrystOD (crystod-mag, Section D.9) for the rutile and NiAs types.

Key PointThe nonrelativistic spin splitting exists for any direction of \(\mathbf N\). The anomalous Hall effect does not. The statement “an altermagnet always has an AHE” is wrong.

Piezomagnetism. When a strain belonging to the same irreducible representation as \(\Gamma_s\) is applied (\(\varepsilon_{xy}\) for \(B_{2g}\) of the rutile type; \(\varepsilon_{xx}-\varepsilon_{yy}\) for the minimal model of \(B_{1g}\)), all the operations that interchange the sublattices are lost, the two sublattices are no longer equivalent by symmetry, the guarantee of compensation disappears, and a net magnetization can appear. The rutile-type \(\varepsilon_{xx}-\varepsilon_{yy}\) retains interchanging operations such as \(\{C_{2x}|\tfrac12\tfrac12\tfrac12\}\), so it does not break the compensation. In the MPG \(4'/mmm'\) with SOC (\(\mathbf N\parallel[001]\)), there are two independent piezomagnetic components: \(M_z\propto\varepsilon_{xy}\), and the component by which \(\varepsilon_{yz},\varepsilon_{zx}\) produce the in-plane \(M_x,M_y\) (this corresponds to the “two components” of CrystOD in Section D.9 (a); confirmed by numerical calculation). It was Dzyaloshinskii who predicted piezomagnetism from magnetic symmetry [Dzyaloshinskii1958pm], and it was first measured in MnF2 and CoF2 [BorovikRomanov1960]. In the context of altermagnetism, there is a measurement on MnTe [Aoyama2024]. Piezomagnetism without SOC (nonrelativistic) has also been discussed [Ma2021, NakaPiezo2025]. Under a strain belonging to \(\Gamma_s\), the spin group is reduced to Type 1, so even without SOC a spin magnetization along \(\mathbf N\), linear in the strain, is allowed by symmetry. The result of [NakaPiezo2025] that shear strain of an organic altermagnet produces an s-wave splitting is consistent with this.

Answer to the reader's question. Even though the magnetization is zero, in terms of symmetry the material is on the side where “time reversal is broken,” that is, on the same side as ferromagnets. The reason an antiferromagnet shows no ferromagnetic response is not that its magnetization is zero, but that \(T\boldsymbol\tau\) or \(PT\) remains.

D.6 The Real-Space View: Anisotropic Spin Density and Magnetic Multipoles

Viewed in real space, the spin (magnetization) density around each magnetic atom in an altermagnet is anisotropic, reflecting the shape of the ligands, and in the two sublattices this shape is carried into itself by a rotation (Figure D.5). The magnetic dipole moments of the two sublattices cancel, but once the shape of the magnetization density is taken into account, a component that does not cancel remains. This is a magnetic multipole of higher order than the dipole that is even under inversion and odd under \(T\). In d-wave altermagnets it has been argued to be a ferroically ordered magnetic octupole (rank 3) [Bhowal2024], and for g-wave hematite the same idea has been used to argue for ferroic order of a rank-5 triakontadipole [Verbeek2024]. Prior work that discussed the momentum-dependent spin splitting from the multipole viewpoint is [Hayami2019, Hayami2020]; from the standpoint of Landau's theory, these multipoles appear as secondary order parameters [McClarty2024].

Why the splitting can be large. The splitting requires both the exchange splitting (of the order of eV) and the anisotropy of the crystal potential (due to the ligands), and its magnitude is roughly capped by the smaller of the two. In the minimal model, \(\Delta\to2\eta\) in the limit of large \(J\), and at the X point it saturates at \(8t\delta\) for \(J>4t|\delta|\). It does not depend on SOC. The maximum values from first-principles calculations differ greatly among materials, from 10 meV for La2CuO4 (the value for the stacking with \(\mathbf q=0\); the stacking measured in zero magnetic field is conventional antiferromagnetism; Section D.9 (e)) to more than 1 eV for RuO2, CrSb, and MnTe [Smejkal2022a, Smejkal2022b]. The large ones are said to be an order of magnitude larger than even the Rashba-type splittings of the largest class (BiTeI [Ishizaka2011]) [Smejkal2022a]. The measured values are collected in Section D.7.

D.7.2 RuO2: the first representative example and doubts about its magnetic order

RuO2 is the material that became the starting point of the discussion of altermagnetism. A moment of about 0.05 \(\mu_B\) and antiferromagnetic order up to at least 300 K were reported by neutron diffraction [Berlijn2017], and G-type order with \(T_N>300\) K was also reported by resonant X-ray scattering [Zhu2019]. On this premise, d-wave spin splitting [Ahn2019] (the DFT maximum is about 1 eV [Smejkal2022a]), the AHE [Feng2022], spin-splitter torque [Bose2022, Bai2022, Karube2022], magnetic circular dichroism in ARPES [Fedchenko2024], and a theory of chiral magnons [Smejkal2023] were published one after another.

Subsequent measurements (including \(\mu\)SR, a local probe), however, deny the order. In muon spin rotation (\(\mu\)SR) on a single crystal, no internal magnetic field is seen at 5–400 K, and the upper limit of the moment is \(4.8(2)\times10^{-4}\,\mu_B\) [Hiraishi2024]. In another \(\mu\)SR study, upper limits of \(1.4\times10^{-4}\,\mu_B/\)Ru for the bulk and \(7.5\times10^{-4}\,\mu_B/\)Ru for a film were given, and it was argued that the earlier neutron signal was due to multiple scattering [Kessler2024]. In spin-resolved ARPES, the bands of both films and crystals are nonmagnetic in character, and no altermagnetic splitting is seen [Liu2024RuO2]. There is also a report that X-ray linear dichroism likewise finds no order in epitaxial films (the arXiv version; arXiv lists an APS DOI, but we have not checked the journal of publication) [Wang2026XLD]. Low-energy \(\mu\)SR sees magnetism only in a small volume near the film surface (about 8.5% or less) [Akashdeep2026].

The present view (late 2026) is that bulk RuO2 is for the most part regarded as nonmagnetic. For films, proposals that the strain of (100)/(110) films stabilizes altermagnetism have appeared from both theory and experiment, but both are still at the preprint stage [Lee2026strain, Jia2026strain]. A 2026 review [Li2026RuO2review] also deals with this controversy (the summary of its conclusion is based on an excerpt from search results, and the full text has not been checked in this appendix). In this appendix we treat RuO2 as “the prototype of the symmetry argument for the rutile type,” and cite MnTe and CrSb as the experimentally established representative examples.

Limitations stated by the authors themselves

  • Completeness of MAGNDATA. MAGNDATA contains only about 2% of the magnetic materials of the Materials Project [Gao2025], or only about 2000 of the tens of thousands of magnetic compounds in ICSD [Zhou2026db].
  • Restriction to \(\mathbf q=0\). If only \(\mathbf q=0\) is examined, superlattice-type altermagnets are missed [Jaeschke2024].
  • Restriction to collinear order. Noncollinear orders and temperature-dependent orders cannot be treated [Gao2025].
  • Dependence on \(U\). The DFT+U ground state is sensitive to the value of \(U\) [Bose2026MC2D]. The RuO2 example (D.7.2) also illustrates this problem.
  • “Allowed by symmetry” does not mean “the splitting is large.” The maximum of the splitting often lies off the high-symmetry lines [Sufyan2026]. There is also an example in which the magnon splitting of MnF2 could not be resolved by neutrons [Morano2025].

The output of crystod-mag --qpoint GM in Section D.9 enumerates, for one structure, all collinear \(\mathbf q=0\) configurations on the orbit of a given magnetic atom, irreducible representation by irreducible representation, and attaches a classification to each.

D.8 Common Misconceptions

(i) Is it the same as a canted antiferromagnet (weak ferromagnetism)?
No. The nonrelativistic spin splitting of an altermagnet arises in a perfectly collinear configuration in which the moments are not canted at all. When SOC is included, a weak magnetization can appear at the same time if the MPG allows it (Section D.5(b)), but this is one of the consequences, not the cause. How SOC changes the nodal structure (the crossover from a splitting with nodes to one without nodes) is discussed in [Fernandes2024]. The weak magnetization is of two kinds: one due to canting, as for the rutile-type \(\mathbf N\parallel[100]\) (transverse), and one due to the inequivalence of the sublattices, as for \(\mathbf N\parallel[110]\) (longitudinal) (see the table in Section D.5).
(ii) Is spin-orbit interaction the cause?
No. The spin splitting itself arises from the exchange interaction and the anisotropy of the crystal potential, and it exists even when SOC is zero (Eq. (5)). SOC is needed for the effects of Section D.5(b), such as the AHE.
(iii) Is every \(\mathbf q=0\) antiferromagnet an altermagnet?
No. Even for \(\mathbf q=0\), if inversion exchanges the sublattices, the bands are degenerate at all \(\mathbf k\). Cr2O3 is an example: it is a \(PT\)-symmetric antiferromagnet. Even with the same corundum structure, a hematite-type sign pattern gives an altermagnet (Section D.9(c)). One must examine individually which operations exchange the sublattices.
(iv) How does it differ from a compensated ferrimagnet?
In a compensated ferrimagnet the two sublattices are not related by a symmetry operation (Type 1), and the vanishing magnetization is accidental. The band splitting contains an s-wave component (a component independent of \(\mathbf k\)). In an altermagnet the two sublattices are related by a rotation, the vanishing magnetization is guaranteed by symmetry, and the splitting has no s-wave component.
(v) How does it differ from a p-wave magnet?
By Eq. (1), the nonrelativistic splitting of a collinear magnet is an even function of \(\mathbf k\). A p-wave (odd-function) splitting requires noncollinear (coplanar) order and belongs to a classification separate from altermagnetism [Hellenes2023, Hayami2020b]. In experiments, NiI2 [Song2025NiI2] and a metallic helimagnet [Yamada2025] have been reported as p-wave magnets.
(vi) Is it a “new phase,” or a “relabeling of the classification”?

It depends on one's standpoint, but the facts are as follows.

Prior work. The nonrelativistic, \(\mathbf k\)-dependent spin splitting of collinear antiferromagnets had been reported by several groups before the name “altermagnetism” appeared. These include, in DFT, rutile-type MnO2 [Noda2016] and Pnma perovskites LaMO3 [Okugawa2018] (both from the group of Noda, Ohno, Nakamura, and co-workers); a design principle from the viewpoint of cluster multipoles [Hayami2019, Hayami2020] (Hayami, Yanagi, and Kusunose); the d-wave splitting and spin current in the organic antiferromagnet κ-(BEDT-TTF)2X [Naka2019] and its extension to Pnma perovskites [Naka2021] (Naka, Motome, Seo, and co-workers); a description of RuO2 as a d-wave Pomeranchuk instability [Ahn2019]; the design principle of “spin-split antiferromagnets” by Zunger and co-workers [Yuan2020, YuanZunger2023]; the crystal Hall effect [Smejkal2020]; the spin-splitter effect [GonzalezHernandez2021]; and the “C-pair spin–valley locking” and nonrelativistic piezomagnetism of V2Se2O [Ma2021]. The consequences of magnetic symmetry (weak ferromagnetism, piezomagnetism) go back to Dzyaloshinskii [Dzyaloshinsky1958, Dzyaloshinskii1958pm] and Borovik-Romanov [BorovikRomanov1960], and the formalism of spin groups to Brinkman and Elliott [Brinkman1966] and to Litvin and Opechowski [Litvin1974, Litvin1977]. In addition, [Smejkal2022a] cites, as one of the earlier mechanisms considered for spin splitting, the paper by Pekar and Rashba [PekarRashba1965], which dealt with coupling resonance in an inhomogeneous magnetic field (we have not been able to confirm in this appendix whether that paper discusses the spin splitting of an antiferromagnet without SOC).

Name and place in the literature. Using spin Laue groups, [Smejkal2022a] exhaustively divided collinear magnets into three types and named the third type “altermagnetism,” an independent phase alongside ferromagnetism and antiferromagnetism. In an editorial [Mazin2022], the PRX editors noted that a referee had expressed the opinion that it should be treated as a subset of antiferromagnetism, and argued that the new name is justified. On the other hand, Yuan and Zunger continue to use the term “spin-split antiferromagnets” [Yuan2020, YuanZunger2023], and [Liu2022] uses similar terminology. Hayami and co-workers describe the same physics in the language of multipoles [Hayami2019, Hayami2020]. In our literature survey we found no formal Comment paper asserting that it is “merely a relabeling.”

Summarizing neutrally, the phenomenon itself had been calculated and predicted before the name existed, and what is new is the framework that exhaustively divides collinear magnetism into three classes on the basis of spin groups, together with the claim that places it as a “third phase” alongside ferromagnetism and antiferromagnetism. Whether to accept that placement differs among researchers. For the historical background, the short essay [Mazin2024] is also helpful.

D.9 Identifying Altermagnets with CrystOD

In words, the identification procedure is as follows.

  1. Find the Wyckoff orbits of the magnetic atoms (the magnetic atoms in the primitive cell and the operations that connect them).
  2. Assign a sign pattern (\(+1\) to ↑ and \(-1\) to ↓), and find its \(\mathbf q\) (whether \(\mathbf q=0\) or not) and its irreducible representation.
  3. Check whether a translation or an inversion exchanges the sublattices. If it does, the order is a conventional antiferromagnet (Type 2).
  4. If it does not, the order is an altermagnet. The type of wave is determined by the irreducible representation \(\Gamma_s\) (even under inversion, nontrivial) (see the table in Section D.4).
  5. If SOC is to be considered, find the MSG and MPG for the chosen direction of the Néel vector, and check whether an axial, \(T\)-odd vector (weak magnetization, Hall vector) is allowed.

In CrystOD, crystod-mag carries out this procedure in one step. The input is a POSCAR of the primitive cell, and --qpoint takes a label such as Γ (GM) or coordinates in the primitive reciprocal lattice. With --classify-only only the table is printed; without it, the output also contains, for each row, the allowed responses (net magnetization, electric polarization, linear magnetoelectric effect, piezomagnetism) and, for altermagnet rows, a collinear order: line giving the wave type, the polynomial, and the nodal planes. The class column is a nonrelativistic classification determined by the sign pattern alone, whereas the MSG and MPG are relativistic symmetries that use the direction of the moments. The MSG and MPG symbols are given in the standard BNS setting, and the directions and polynomials are written in the Cartesian coordinates of the input cell.

The output below was produced with CrystOD 0.4.8 on 2026-11-11 (2–7 seconds each). The MSG (UNI) column is omitted, and the collinear order: lines were extracted from the full output and placed below the table. For the structures, rutile-type TiO2, corundum-type Cr2O3, cubic SrRuO3, Pnma CaTiO3, I4/mmm La2CuO4, and NiO are the example files in the repository; MnF2 and MnTe were built from standard lattice constants and coordinates; and the LTO-phase La2CuO4 was built from approximate literature values. Because the results are determined by symmetry alone, the fine values of the atomic coordinates and the chemical species have no effect (we also confirmed that for the rutile type, TiO2 and MnF2 give the same table).

D.10 A Bridge to Phonons: From Spin Splitting to Phonon Angular Momentum

Phonon angular momentum (PAM)

For a phonon of wave vector \(\mathbf q\) (eigenvector \(\epsilon_{\mathbf q}\), mass-weighted and normalized to \(\epsilon^\dagger\epsilon=1\)), the \(z\) component of the angular momentum can be written, using the circular-polarization basis \(|R\rangle=(1,i)/\sqrt2\) and \(|L\rangle=(1,-i)/\sqrt2\) (the \(x,y\) components of each atom), as

\[ l_z(\mathbf q)=\hbar\,\langle\epsilon_{\mathbf q}|\big(|R\rangle\langle R|-|L\rangle\langle L|\big)|\epsilon_{\mathbf q}\rangle =\hbar\sum_\kappa \mathrm{Im}\big(\epsilon_{\kappa}^{*}\times\epsilon_{\kappa}\big)_z\tag{7} \]

(where \(\kappa\) labels the atoms and the time dependence is \(e^{-i\omega t}\)) [Zhang2014]. The quantity \(\mathbf l\) is an axial vector and is odd under \(T\). In this respect it has the same transformation properties as the electron spin.

Constraints from symmetry

For an operation with rotational part \(R\) (proper or improper), the following holds (when the branch is not degenerate).

\[ [R]:\ \ \mathbf l(R\mathbf q)=\det(R)\,R\,\mathbf l(\mathbf q),\qquad [RT]:\ \ \mathbf l(-R\mathbf q)=-\det(R)\,R\,\mathbf l(\mathbf q).\tag{8} \]

Note that \(T\) brings about both \(\mathbf q\to-\mathbf q\) and \(\mathbf l\to-\mathbf l\), so for \(RT\) a factor of \(-1\) is attached to the image of \(\mathbf q\). As special cases, \(T\): \(\mathbf l(-\mathbf q)=-\mathbf l(\mathbf q)\); \(P\): \(\mathbf l(-\mathbf q)=+\mathbf l(\mathbf q)\); hence, if \(PT\) is present, \(\mathbf l\equiv0\).

Check with a mirror reflection and a twofold axis

Mirror reflection \(m_z=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,-1)\) (\(\det=-1\)): \(\mathbf l(q_x,q_y,-q_z)=(-l_x,-l_y,l_z)\). For \(\mathbf q\) in the mirror plane (\(q_z=0\)), \(l_x=l_y=0\) and only \(l_z\) remains. This agrees with the intuition that a mode polarized in the plane rotates in the plane, so its angular momentum is perpendicular to the plane. Twofold axis \(C_{2z}\) (\(\det=+1\)): \(\mathbf l(-q_x,-q_y,q_z)=(-l_x,-l_y,l_z)\). For \(\mathbf q\) on the axis, only \(l_z\) remains. Eq. (8) was checked numerically, as the transformation of \(\mathrm{Im}(\epsilon^*\times\epsilon)\) under \(\epsilon\to R\epsilon\) (a polar vector) and \(\epsilon\to\epsilon^*\) (\(T\)), for random complex vectors and all 48 operations of \(m\bar3m\) (maximum difference \(5.8\times10^{-15}\)).

Supplement: In a centrosymmetric altermagnet, when every operation of the Laue group takes \(\mathbf N\) to \(\pm\mathbf N\), \(l_{\mathbf N}(\mathbf q)\) obeys the same representation \(\Gamma_s\) as \(\Delta(\mathbf k)\) (derivation in this appendix)

Conditions. (1) The crystal is centrosymmetric, and inversion preserves the sublattices (for an altermagnet this holds automatically, and inversion remains in the MPG without a prime). (2) The moments are collinear and parallel to the \(z\) axis. (3) Every operation of the Laue group \(G\) takes \(\mathbf N\) to \(\pm\mathbf N\) (for a uniaxial group with \(\mathbf N\) parallel to the principal axis, for orthorhombic \(mmm\) with \(\mathbf N\) parallel to any one of the three axes, and for \(2/m\) with \(\mathbf N\) parallel or perpendicular to the twofold axis). Below, we take the direction of \(\mathbf N\) as \(z\). Then the following can be said. Let \(\mathbf N\parallel\hat z\). The elements of the MPG are the operations that take \(\mathbf N\) to \(\pm\mathbf N\), and a spatial operation \(g\) (with rotational part \(R\)) is primed (combined with \(T\)) when the product of the sign \(s(g)\) from the exchange of the sublattices and the transformation \(a(g)=[\det(R)R]_{zz}=\pm1\) of the \(z\) component of the axial vector is \(-1\). That is, the sign of the prime is \(p(g)=s(g)\,a(g)\). For the \(z\) component of Eq. (8), when \(P\) is present \(\mathbf l(-\mathbf q)=\mathbf l(\mathbf q)\), so the \(-R\mathbf q\) of \(RT\) can be replaced by \(R\mathbf q\), and

\[ l_z(R\mathbf q)=p(g)\,a(g)\,l_z(\mathbf q)=s(g)\,l_z(\mathbf q). \]

This has the same form as Eq. (6). For example, in the rutile type with \(\mathbf N\parallel[001]\), both \(l_z(\mathbf q)\) and \(\Delta(\mathbf k)\) obey \(B_{2g}\) (the \(q_xq_y\) type). Note, however, that this only states the allowed form; for the magnitude to be nonzero, the \(T\)-breaking term described next is needed in the dynamical matrix. The \(l_x,l_y\) components obey other representations, and in general they appear as well. When condition (3) does not hold (for example, for cubic groups, or for tetragonal and hexagonal groups with \(\mathbf N\) in the plane), the same equation holds only for the subgroup that preserves the \(z\) axis; moreover, the MPG itself changes with the direction of \(\mathbf N\), so the PAM pattern in general differs from the electronic \(\Delta(\mathbf k)\). In a noncentrosymmetric crystal condition (1) breaks down and odd-parity components are added. In an independent verification, this relation held for the rutile type and MnTe with \(\mathbf N\parallel z\) (16/16 and 24/24 operations), for the hematite type with \(\mathbf N\parallel z\) (12/12), and for the G type of Pnma with \(\mathbf N\) parallel to any of \(x,y,z\) (8/8); for the rutile type with \(\mathbf N\parallel x\), it held only for the eight operations that preserve \(x\).

Key PointThe essential difference. The spin splitting of electrons in an altermagnet is nonrelativistic. Spin space is decoupled from the lattice (spin group), and the magnitude is determined by the exchange interaction. In contrast, the phonon angular momentum is the motion of the atoms in real space itself and is bound to the lattice. The relevant symmetry is the magnetic (point) group, the result depends on the direction of the moments, and moreover the breaking of \(T\) must be transmitted from the spins to the dynamical matrix through some spin–lattice coupling.

Concretely, the equation of motion needs a term that is odd under \(T\) and antisymmetric. Commonly used are the gyroscopic term proportional to the velocity (written as a molecular Berry curvature [Saparov2022]; a first-principles formulation of the adiabatic coupling between spins and the lattice [Ren2024]; the splitting of chiral phonons in CrI3 [Bonini2023]; the formulation of the thermal Hall conductivity including the Berry curvature of phonons is [Qin2012]), the Raman-type spin–phonon term \(\mathbf h\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf p)\) (used in the first theory of the phonon Hall effect [Sheng2006], and the type discussed by [Zhang2010] from the viewpoint of the Berry curvature and Chern numbers of the phonon bands), or magnon–phonon hybridization. In a dynamical matrix built from the real force constants obtained by ordinary DFT, \(D(-\mathbf q)=D(\mathbf q)^*\) gives \(\epsilon_{-\mathbf q}=\epsilon_{\mathbf q}^*\), and hence \(\mathbf l(-\mathbf q)=-\mathbf l(\mathbf q)\) is built in from the start. For a centrosymmetric crystal, this together with \(P\) gives \(\mathbf l\equiv0\). That is, even if the DFT force constants of a magnetic material are used as they are, no even-parity PAM appears.

Reading the transformation rule with representation theory: relation to SALCs

Let \(j_m(\mathbf q)\) denote the PAM along the magnetization axis \(\mathbf e_m\). Then the \(\mathbf e_m\) component of Eq. (8) obeys the transformation rule \(j_m(G\mathbf q)=\eta(T)\chi(R)\,j_m(\mathbf q)\) for an element \(G\) of the magnetic point group (\(\eta=-1\) if \(T\) is included, and \(\chi=-1\) if the point operation reverses the sense of rotation about \(\mathbf e_m\)). This rule answers the following questions: is this transformation rule a kind of SALC construction, and can phonons with an angular-momentum texture be built by multiplying the characters of p-, d-, f-, g-, and i-waves into the permutation representation of the Wyckoff orbitals? The statements below were checked by exact group-theoretical calculations with sympy and by calculations on a spring model of the honeycomb lattice with numpy.

(a) How to read the rule. When every operation maps the axis \(m\) to \(\pm m\), \(\eta(T)\chi(R)\) is the character of the one-dimensional (co)representation \(\Gamma(M_m)\) obeyed by a \(T\)-odd axial-vector component (the magnetization component \(M_m\)). Therefore \(j_m(\mathbf q)\), as a function of \(\mathbf q\) (with \(\mathbf q\to R\mathbf q\) for a unitary operation and \(\mathbf q\to-R\mathbf q\) for an operation containing \(T\)), is a basis function of \(\Gamma(M_m)\), and the type of wave is given by the lowest degree \(l\) of a homogeneous polynomial satisfying \(f(g\mathbf q)=\eta\chi(g)f(\mathbf q)\) (p = 1, d = 2, f = 3, g = 4, h = 5, i = 6, j = 7). For the in-plane components, which mix with one another, the vector rule of Eq. (8) can be used as it stands.

In two dimensions \(C_{2z}\) acts in the same way as \(\mathbf q\to-\mathbf q\), so even for the same group the type of wave differs from the three-dimensional one. (2) For the magnetic point group \(\bar6'm'2\) (\(\bar6\) and the mirror primed, the twofold axis unprimed), the lowest degree remains third (f-wave), but the enforced nodal planes disappear. Thus the lowest degree and the number of nodal planes whose positions are fixed by a single symmetry operation do not necessarily coincide.

In other words, the characters of the p-, d-, f-, g-, and i-waves are multiplied into the space of polynomials in \(\mathbf q\), not into the permutation representation.

(c) The number of states does not increase. The number of branches remains \(3N\). An atomic displacement is always a vector, and a single phonon is an elliptical motion in which two orthogonal components oscillate with a phase difference of 90° (for circular polarization, \(\epsilon=(1,\pm i,0)/\sqrt2\)). The label of the wave only expresses how the sense of that rotation changes with the propagation direction. For example, for a d-wave of the \(q_x^2-q_y^2\) type, the sense of rotation about the \(z\) axis is opposite for \(\mathbf q\parallel x\) and \(\mathbf q\parallel y\), and \(l_z=0\) along the diagonal directions (for the \(q_xq_y\) type, the roles of the axes and the diagonals are interchanged). This is the same as for the electron spin splitting, where “d-wave” does not refer to d orbitals.

(d) Writing it with invariants. In the group of the paramagnetic parent, the allowed \(j_m(\mathbf q)\) is given by the third-order invariant \(j_m\times(\text{magnetic order parameter})\times f(\mathbf q)\). For the rutile type (\(x\parallel a\), \(4/mmm1'\)) with \(\mathbf N\parallel z\), \(j_z\sim A_{2g}\) and \(N_z\sim\Gamma_s\otimes\Gamma(R_z)=B_{2g}\otimes A_{2g}=B_{1g}\) (both are \(T\)-odd), so \(f\sim A_{2g}\otimes B_{1g}=B_{2g}=q_xq_y\) (we confirmed that \(j_zN_zq_xq_y\) is invariant under all 16 operations; the characters of \(N_z\) are \(C_4:-1,\ C_{2x}:+1,\ C_{2[110]}:-1\), which is \(B_{1g}\)). For a nonmagnetic crystal, the invariant is \(j_mf(\mathbf q)\), and since \(j_m\) is \(T\)-odd, \(f\) is an odd function of \(\mathbf q\).

(e) Where the Wyckoff positions matter. The site-resolved angular momentum obeys \(j_{g\kappa,m}(g\mathbf q)=\eta\chi(g)\,j_{\kappa,m}(\mathbf q)\). Therefore, an atom \(\kappa\) that is not moved by an operation with \(\eta\chi=-1\) in the magnetic little co-group of \(\mathbf q\) cannot rotate about the \(m\) axis at that \(\mathbf q\). Atoms that are interchanged by such an operation rotate in opposite directions (the sum is zero, but each site has a finite value).

Check with the spring model of the honeycomb lattice

We calculated the site-resolved \(l_z\) for a two-atom honeycomb lattice with in-plane displacements only (nearest-neighbor longitudinal spring 1, transverse spring 0.25, second-neighbor longitudinal spring 0.15; the force constants are real, so the model is \(T\)-symmetric).

  • Equal masses (inversion interchanges A and B): for 2000 random \(\mathbf q\) and the four branches, \(|l_A+l_B|\le1.1\times10^{-12}\). For the two nondegenerate branches at the K point, \(l_A=\mp0.5,\ l_B=\pm0.5\); for a general \(\mathbf q=(0.83,1.71)\), \((l_A,l_B)=(\pm0.162,\mp0.162)\), and so on, and the sign is reversed at \(-\mathbf q\) (the two degenerate branches at K are excluded because the individual \(l\) is not determined).
  • At the M point (\(\mathbf q\) is preserved by \(T\) and a lattice translation, the atoms do not move, \(\eta\chi=-1\)): \(l_A=l_B=0\) for all branches, independent of the masses.
  • Mass ratio 1.6 (inversion is lost): the sum has odd parity, and at K the sums of \(l\) over the four branches are \(+0.474,\ -1,\ +1,\ -0.474\); at K′ the signs are exactly reversed.

(f) Terminology. The phonon angular momentum is a \(T\)-odd axial vector (it transforms like \(M\)), whereas static ferroaxial order is a \(T\)-even axial vector. A component of the PAM independent of \(\mathbf q\) (s-wave) is allowed when the magnetic point group allows \(M_m\), and this is a condition separate from the crystal being ferroaxial. For example, the nonmagnetic ferroaxial point group \(4/m\), as the gray group \(4/m1'\), contains \(PT\), so \(j\equiv0\) (we confirmed that there is no allowed polynomial up to the eighth degree).

(g) Do such states actually exist? Odd-parity textures in nonmagnetic noncentrosymmetric crystals (valley chiral phonons) arise from ordinary force constants alone and have been observed experimentally [Zhu2018]. Even-parity textures require a \(T\)-odd term in the dynamical matrix, and within the range of the literature checked in this appendix they are theoretical proposals [Bendin2026, Rieger2026] ([Okamoto2025] concerns noncollinear order). We are not asserting that there is no experiment.

CautionPoints to look for when reading studies of phonon angular momentum.
  1. Which group is used (a spin group or a magnetic group), and why.
  2. Which term of the dynamical matrix breaks \(T\), and how large it realistically is.
  3. Whether the PAM discussed is of even or odd parity, and what each requires (for even parity, a breaking of \(T\); for odd parity, a breaking of inversion).
  4. Which conclusions are enforced by symmetry and which depend on the model.
  5. How the texture is to be measured (note that it is a quantity that integrates to zero over the whole zone).

D.12 Summary of Spin-Group Notation

  • \([R_s\|R_l]\): the left entry is the operation in spin space and the right entry is the operation in real space \(\{A|\boldsymbol\tau\}\). Without SOC the two can be chosen independently. Time reversal \(T\) is written on the spin-space side (\(C_{2\perp}T\) is sometimes written \(\bar C_2\)).
  • Spin-only group of a collinear magnet:\(\mathbf r_s=\{[C_\phi(\hat{\mathbf n})\|E]\}\rtimes\{[E\|E],[C_{2\perp}T\|E]\}\). The former gives the conservation of \(s_z\) (Section D.2 (a)), and the latter gives Eq. (1) (Section D.2 (b)).
  • Nontrivial spin group: after dividing out the spin-only group, the only remaining elements are those of the form \([E\|g]\) (which preserve the sublattices) and \([C_2\|g]\) (which exchange the sublattices). Extending its point-group part to a Laue group gives the spin Laue group. The classification and representation theory of spin groups have recently been worked out systematically [Liu2022, Xiao2024, Chen2024, Jiang2024]. The symbols of the spin Laue groups follow Litvin's notation [Litvin1977].
  • Three kinds: Type 1 \([E\|G]\), Type 2 \([E\|G]+[C_2\|G]\), Type 3 \([E\|H]+[C_2\|G-H]\) (Section D.4; types I, II, and III in the papers).
  • Relation to magnetic groups: When SOC is included, the rotation parts of \(R_s\) and \(R_l\) are locked to be the same, and only the \(g\) that map \(\mathbf N\) to \(\pm\mathbf N\) remain. An element \(g\) enters the magnetic group with a prime (\(gT\)) when \(p(g)=s(g)\,a(g)\) equals \(-1\), and without a prime when it equals \(+1\) (\(s\): \(-1\) if the sublattices are exchanged; \(a\): \(-1\) if the action as an axial vector, \(\det(R)R\), reverses \(\mathbf N\)). An element of the form \([E\|g]\), which preserves the sublattices, can also acquire a prime (\(2'_{[110]}\) and \(m'_{[110]}\) in the rutile type with \(\mathbf N\parallel[001]\)).

D.13 Derivation of the Minimal Model

Eigenvalues. Since we can write \(\varepsilon_{A,B}=\varepsilon_0\pm\eta\), we have \(H_\sigma=\varepsilon_0\,\mathbb 1+(\eta-\sigma J)\tau_z+V\tau_x\) (\(\tau\) is the Pauli matrix in sublattice space). Eq. (4) follows. In the ferromagnetic configuration, \(H_\sigma=(\varepsilon_0-\sigma J)\mathbb 1+\eta\tau_z+V\tau_x\).

Expansion for small \(\eta\). \(R_\mp=\sqrt{(\eta\mp J)^2+V^2}\) \(=\sqrt{J^2+V^2\mp2\eta J+\eta^2}\) \(\simeq R_0\mp\eta J/R_0\) (\(R_0=\sqrt{J^2+V^2}\)). \(\Delta=E_{\uparrow,-}-E_{\downarrow,-}=R_+-R_-\simeq2\eta J/R_0\). This is Eq. (5); from \(\eta=-2t\delta(\cos k_x-\cos k_y)\) we obtain \(\Delta\simeq-4t\delta J(\cos k_x-\cos k_y)/R_0\). For small \(\mathbf k\), \(\cos k_x-\cos k_y\simeq-(k_x^2-k_y^2)/2\), so for \(\delta J>0\) we have \(\Delta\propto+(k_x^2-k_y^2)\).

The X point. From \(\cos(\pi/2)=0\), \(V=0\), \(\varepsilon_0=0\), and \(\eta=4t\delta\). Since \(E_{\sigma,-}=-|4t\delta-\sigma J|\), \(\Delta_X=|J+4t\delta|-|J-4t\delta|\). If \(J>4t|\delta|\) this is \(8t\delta\), and if \(J<4t|\delta|\) it is \(2J\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\delta\).

Operations that exchange the sublattices. Consider the 4-fold rotation \(C_4\) through \((\tfrac12,0)\): A at \((0,0)\) is mapped to \((\tfrac12,-\tfrac12)\), which is equivalent to B by a lattice translation. The strong hopping of A along the x direction is mapped to strong hopping along the y direction, which coincides with that of B. In \(\mathbf k\) space this is \((k_x,k_y)\to(-k_y,k_x)\), under which \(\cos k_x-\cos k_y\) changes sign. From \([C_2\|C_4]\), which combines this with a spin flip, we obtain \(E_{\uparrow}(k_x,k_y)=E_{\downarrow}(-k_y,k_x)=E_\downarrow(k_y,k_x)\) (the last equality follows from Eq. (1) and the symmetry under \(k_x\to-k_x\)). The reflection along the diagonal direction that maps A to B (the line \(x-y=\tfrac12\)) works in the same way. On the other hand, the translation \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\) maps A to B, but for \(\delta\neq0\) it maps the strong hopping along the x direction onto the x direction again, so it is not a symmetry operation.

Compensation. Since \(E_\uparrow(k_x,k_y)=E_\downarrow(k_y,k_x)\) and \(k_x\leftrightarrow k_y\) is a permutation of the Brillouin zone, \(n_\uparrow=n_\downarrow\). The number of electrons in the interactive figure is also exactly 0 to within floating-point precision, because it uses lattice points that are closed under this permutation.
