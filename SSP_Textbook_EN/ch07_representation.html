Chapter 7Representation Theory — Irreducible Representations and the Great Orthogonality Theorem

In Chapter 6 we acquired the framework of groups. We filled in multiplication tables, searched for subgroups, and divided the elements into conjugacy classes. But what we handled there were symbols such as $E$, $C_3$, and $\sigma_v$, together with the rules for multiplying those symbols. As bare symbols, they cannot be brought into the Schrödinger equation.

The task of this chapter is to translate the elements of a group into matrices. This translation is called a representation. There is more than one way to translate; in fact there are infinitely many. The question then becomes, “Which translation is the essential one?” The answer is the irreducible representation—a translation that cannot be decomposed into anything smaller—and all the properties of a group are folded into its irreducible representations.

This chapter has three high points: Schur's lemmas (the first and the second), and the great orthogonality theorem (also called the wonderful orthogonality theorem) that follows from them. The great orthogonality theorem is, at first sight, a plain-looking formula stating that the matrix elements of different irreducible representations are mutually orthogonal. Yet this formula is the foundation that supports the character tables of Chapter 8, the projection operators of Chapter 9, the selection rules of Chapter 10, and Bloch's theorem of Chapter 11—in short, every application of group theory. It is no accident that Dresselhaus's textbook describes it as “wonderful.”

The proofs are long. But each individual step consists of nothing more than matrix multiplication and the rearrangement theorem. If you follow them without skipping, you will always reach the end. In this chapter we do not omit a single line of the intermediate steps.

7.1 What Is a Representation?

7.1.1 Definition

Definition: Representation

To each element $R$ of a group $G=\{R_1, R_2, \dots, R_h\}$ of order $h$, assign one $\ell\times\ell$ nonsingular matrix (a matrix that has an inverse) $D(R)$. Suppose this assignment satisfies, for every pair of elements $(R,S)$ of the group,

$$ \begin{equation} D(R)\,D(S) = D(RS) \label{eq:7-hom} \end{equation} $$

Then the set of matrices $\{D(R)\}$ is called a representation of the group $G$, and $\ell$ is called its dimension.

What Eq. \eqref{eq:7-hom} says is that “multiplying in the group first and then translating into a matrix” and “translating into matrices first and then multiplying the matrices” always give the same answer. This property is called a homomorphism.

Caution: The order $h$ and the dimension $\ell$ are different things

This is where beginners first get confused.

  • $h$ = the number of elements of the group ($h=6$ for $C_{3v}$). It is a property of the group itself and does not depend on how the representation is chosen.
  • $\ell$ = the size of the matrices ($\ell=3$ for $3\times3$). It differs from one representation to another.

For a single group there are representations of dimension 1, of dimension 2, and of dimension 6. The question “What is the dimension of a representation of $C_{3v}$?” has no answer. You must always specify “which representation we are talking about.”

Derivation: $D(E)=I$ and $D(R^{-1})=D(R)^{-1}$

We show that the definition \eqref{eq:7-hom} alone automatically determines the images of the identity element and of the inverse elements.

(1) Identity element. In a group, $EE=E$. Putting this into Eq. \eqref{eq:7-hom} gives

$$ D(E)\,D(E) = D(EE) = D(E) $$

Since we agreed that the matrices of a representation are nonsingular, $D(E)^{-1}$ exists. Multiplying both sides by $D(E)^{-1}$ from the right, the left-hand side becomes $D(E)D(E)D(E)^{-1} = D(E)$ and the right-hand side becomes $D(E)D(E)^{-1} = I$, so that

$$ D(E) = I $$

The identity element is always mapped to the identity matrix.

(2) Inverse element. In a group, $RR^{-1}=E$. Putting this into Eq. \eqref{eq:7-hom} gives

$$ D(R)\,D(R^{-1}) = D(E) = I $$

Multiplying both sides by $D(R)^{-1}$ from the left, we obtain

$$ D(R^{-1}) = D(R)^{-1} $$

∎ The image of an inverse element is the inverse matrix of the image. We will use these two results many times in the proofs below, so keep them in mind.

7.1.2 Constructing a representation from basis functions

A representation is not handed down from above. In practice, if we prepare a set of functions (or vectors) that are carried into one another by the symmetry operations, the matrices come out automatically. This is the standard way of constructing representations when they are used in physics.

Derivation: How a set of basis functions determines the matrices

Let $\hat{P}_R$ denote the transformation operator for functions corresponding to the symmetry operation $R$. This is exactly the same operator that we wrote $\hat{R}$ in Chapter 6 ($\hat{R}f(\rr)\equiv f(R^{-1}\rr)$); from this chapter on, we use the symbol $\hat{P}_R$ so that it is not confused with the matrix $D(R)$ of a representation. Prepare $\ell$ functions $\{\phi_1,\phi_2,\dots,\phi_\ell\}$, and suppose that the space they span is closed under all operations of the group, that is, that $\hat{P}_R\phi_j$ can again be written as a linear combination of the $\{\phi_i\}$. The matrix is then the array of the coefficients:

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{P}_R\,\phi_j = \sum_{i=1}^{\ell} \phi_i\, D(R)_{ij} \label{eq:7-basis} \end{equation} $$

Note here the order of the indices. The second index $j$ of $D(R)_{ij}$ is “the number of the function being transformed,” and the first index $i$ is “the number of the function in the expansion.” In other words, the $j$th column of $D(R)$ is the array of the coefficients of the destination of $\phi_j$.

With this convention, let us verify that Eq. \eqref{eq:7-hom} really holds. If we accept $\hat{P}_R\hat{P}_S = \hat{P}_{RS}$ (which we confirmed in Chapter 6),

$$ \hat{P}_R\hat{P}_S\,\phi_k = \hat{P}_R\left(\sum_j \phi_j D(S)_{jk}\right) = \sum_j \left(\hat{P}_R\phi_j\right) D(S)_{jk} $$

Substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:7-basis} further into the inner factor, we obtain

$$ = \sum_j \left(\sum_i \phi_i D(R)_{ij}\right) D(S)_{jk} = \sum_i \phi_i \underbrace{\left(\sum_j D(R)_{ij}D(S)_{jk}\right)}_{=\ \left[D(R)D(S)\right]_{ik}} $$

On the other hand, if we regard the left-hand side as a whole as $\hat{P}_{RS}$, then

$$ \hat{P}_{RS}\,\phi_k = \sum_i \phi_i\, D(RS)_{ik} $$

Since the $\{\phi_i\}$ are linearly independent, comparing the coefficients of the two expressions gives

$$ \left[D(R)D(S)\right]_{ik} = D(RS)_{ik} $$

∎ That is, Eq. \eqref{eq:7-hom} holds. Merely by preparing a closed set of functions, we obtain a representation.

Why? The reason this connects with quantum mechanics

At the end of Chapter 6 we saw that the Hamiltonian commutes with the symmetry operations, $[\Ham,\hat{P}_R]=0$. In that case, a set of degenerate eigenfunctions $\{\psi_{n1},\dots,\psi_{n\ell}\}$ belonging to an energy eigenvalue $E_n$ cannot leave the space of the same eigenvalue $E_n$ when $\hat{P}_R$ acts on it. Indeed,

$$ \Ham\left(\hat{P}_R\psi_{nj}\right) = \hat{P}_R\left(\Ham\psi_{nj}\right) = E_n\left(\hat{P}_R\psi_{nj}\right) $$

That is, $\hat{P}_R\psi_{nj}$ is also an eigenfunction with eigenvalue $E_n$, and can be written as a linear combination of the $\{\psi_{ni}\}$. This is exactly the situation of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-basis}.

Therefore, a set of degenerate eigenfunctions is always a basis of a representation of the group. Moreover, as we will see later, that representation is irreducible (unless there is an accidental degeneracy). This is why the dictionary “dimension of an irreducible representation” = “degeneracy” holds. That the $d$ orbitals split into a threefold and a twofold level in an octahedral field is possible only because the irreducible representations of $O_h$ include three-dimensional and two-dimensional ones. However, the fact that the dimensions of the irreducible representations match is only a necessary condition for the way the level splits. Which irreducible representations it actually splits into is determined only after we calculate the characters of the $d$ orbitals (Chapter 10).

7.1.3 Writing down the three-dimensional representation of $C_{3v}$

Enough of abstract theory; let us actually do the calculation. For the six elements of $C_{3v}$,

$$ C_{3v} = \{E,\ C_3,\ C_3^2,\ \sigma_1,\ \sigma_2,\ \sigma_3\} $$

we take the $3$ functions $(x, y, z)$ as the basis. Let the principal axis (the threefold axis) be the $z$ axis, let the mirror plane $\sigma_1$ be the $xz$ plane (that is, the plane containing the $x$ axis), and let $\sigma_2$ and $\sigma_3$ be the planes located at $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$ from the $x$ axis. The three planes are offset by $60^\circ$ each because a plane rotated by $180^\circ$ coincides with the original plane (rotating twice by $120^\circ$ gives $240^\circ \equiv 60^\circ$).

Derivation: Writing out all six matrices

Rotations. In the formula of Chapter 4 (the rotation matrix about the $z$ axis), setting $\phi=2\pi/3=120^\circ$, we have $\cos120^\circ=-\tfrac12$ and $\sin120^\circ=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, so that

$$ D(C_3)=\begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12 & -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} & 0\\[2pt] \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} & -\tfrac12 & 0\\[2pt] 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad D(C_3^2)=\begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12 & \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} & 0\\[2pt] -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} & -\tfrac12 & 0\\[2pt] 0&0&1\end{pmatrix} $$

(For $C_3^2$, $\phi=240^\circ$, that is, $\cos=-\tfrac12$ and $\sin=-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$.)

Reflections. A reflection in a plane that contains the $z$ axis and is inclined by an angle $\theta$ from the $x$ axis is, within the $xy$ plane, “a reflection about the line at angle $\theta$.” Since this reflection carries a vector in direction $\alpha$ to direction $2\theta-\alpha$, for $x=r\cos\alpha,\ y=r\sin\alpha$ we have

$$ x' = r\cos(2\theta-\alpha) = x\cos2\theta + y\sin2\theta,\qquad y' = r\sin(2\theta-\alpha) = x\sin2\theta - y\cos2\theta $$

(We simply expanded using the addition theorems and substituted $r\cos\alpha=x$ and $r\sin\alpha=y$.) The coordinate $z$ is unchanged. Therefore

$$ D(\sigma_\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos2\theta & \sin2\theta & 0\\ \sin2\theta & -\cos2\theta & 0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix} $$

Substitute $\theta=0^\circ,\ 60^\circ,\ 120^\circ$. Since $2\theta=0^\circ,120^\circ,240^\circ$,

$$ D(\sigma_1)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad D(\sigma_2)=\begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12&\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}&0\\[2pt] \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}&\tfrac12&0\\[2pt] 0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad D(\sigma_3)=\begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12&-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}&0\\[2pt] -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}&\tfrac12&0\\[2pt] 0&0&1\end{pmatrix} $$

Of course, $D(E)=I_3$. ∎ This completes the three-dimensional representation of $C_{3v}$.

Example 7.1: Checking one homomorphism by hand

In the group, $C_3\sigma_1 = \sigma_2$ holds. Verify that this also holds for the matrices, that is, that $D(C_3)D(\sigma_1)=D(\sigma_2)$.

Solution. The $z$ components are both $1$ and trivial, so we only need to compute the upper-left $2\times2$ block.

$$ \begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12 & -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[2pt] \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} & -\tfrac12\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix} $$

The matrix on the right simply multiplies the second column by $-1$, so the answer is

$$ =\begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12 & +\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[2pt] \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} & +\tfrac12\end{pmatrix} $$

This is exactly the $2\times2$ block of $D(\sigma_2)$. ∎

The result is also plausible geometrically. Reflecting in a plane containing the $x$ axis and then rotating by $120^\circ$ is the same as reflecting in a plane tilted by $60^\circ$ (two reflections give a rotation, and a rotation followed by one reflection gives a reflection).

Supplement: Counting the traces here

The traces will play the leading role later as the characters (Chapter 8), so we compute them now.

$$ \Tr D(E)=3,\quad \Tr D(C_3)=\Tr D(C_3^2)=-\tfrac12-\tfrac12+1=0,\quad \Tr D(\sigma_i)=1 $$

(For $\sigma_1$ we get $1-1+1=1$, and for $\sigma_2$ we get $-\tfrac12+\tfrac12+1=1$. All three mirror planes give the same value.) As we saw in Chapter 6, the conjugacy classes of $C_{3v}$ are the three sets $\{E\}$, $\{C_3,C_3^2\}$, and $\{\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3\}$, and elements in the same class have equal traces. Together, these can be written as $\chi=(3,\,0,\,1)$.

7.2 The Identity, Faithful, and Regular Representations

For a single group, we can construct any number of representations that differ in both dimension and content. Let us look at three representative ones.

7.2.1 The identity representation

Definition: Identity representation

Assign the $1\times1$ matrix $(1)$ to every element:

$$ D(R)=1\qquad(\text{for all }R\in G) $$

Since $1\cdot1=1=D(RS)$, this indeed satisfies Eq. \eqref{eq:7-hom}. It is a one-dimensional representation that exists for every group without exception, and it is also called the totally symmetric representation. In Mulliken symbols it is written $A_1$ or $A_{1g}$, and in Bethe symbols $\Gamma_1$ (Chapter 8).

In terms of basis functions, “a function invariant under all symmetry operations” is a basis of the identity representation. For $C_{3v}$, $z$, $x^2+y^2$, and $z^2$ are examples.

7.2.2 Faithful representations

Definition: Faithful representation

When different elements always correspond to different matrices ($R\neq S\ \Rightarrow\ D(R)\neq D(S)$), the representation is said to be faithful. In a faithful representation, the group and the set of matrices are in one-to-one correspondence, so the group can be recovered directly from the matrices.

The identity representation is not faithful if $h\ge2$ (all elements collapse onto the same $1$). The three-dimensional representation of $C_{3v}$ constructed in Section 7.1.3 is faithful, because its six matrices are all different.

Example 7.2: Constructing another one-dimensional representation that is not faithful

For $C_{3v}$, assign $+1$ to the proper rotations ($E,C_3,C_3^2$) and $-1$ to the reflections ($\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3$). Verify that this is a representation.

Solution. This assignment is the same as “taking the determinant.” Indeed, as we saw in Chapter 4, the determinant of a rotation is $+1$ and that of a reflection is $-1$. Since the determinant has the property $\det(AB)=\det A\cdot\det B$,

$$ D(R)D(S) = \det\!\big[D_{3\text{D}}(R)\big]\cdot\det\!\big[D_{3\text{D}}(S)\big] = \det\!\big[D_{3\text{D}}(R)D_{3\text{D}}(S)\big] = \det\!\big[D_{3\text{D}}(RS)\big] = D(RS) $$

Hence Eq. \eqref{eq:7-hom} holds, and this is a representation. ∎ This representation is called $A_2$. It is not faithful (the six elements collapse onto two values), but it is a perfectly good representation.

This construction works in general. Taking the determinant of any representation always gives a one-dimensional representation.

7.2.3 The regular representation

There is a representation that can be constructed mechanically for any group and that has dimension $h$. This is the regular representation. It plays the leading role when we derive the dimension theorem in Section 7.9.

Definition: Regular representation

Regard the elements of the group themselves, $\{G_1,G_2,\dots,G_h\}$, as a basis of an $h$-dimensional vector space. Acting with an element $R$ rearranges the basis, and we write this rearrangement as a matrix:

$$ R\,G_j = G_i \quad\text{implies}\quad D^{\rm reg}(R)_{ij}=1,\qquad \text{and all other entries are }0 $$

In other words, $D^{\rm reg}(R)$ is a permutation matrix with exactly one $1$ in each row and each column.

Derivation: Why the regular representation is really a representation

Consider $\left[D^{\rm reg}(R)D^{\rm reg}(S)\right]_{ik} = \sum_j D^{\rm reg}(R)_{ij}\,D^{\rm reg}(S)_{jk}$.

For a term of the sum to be nonzero, we need $D^{\rm reg}(S)_{jk}=1$ and $D^{\rm reg}(R)_{ij}=1$, that is, both

$$ S\,G_k = G_j \quad\text{and}\quad R\,G_j = G_i $$

must hold. Since $G_j$ is determined by $S G_k$, there is at most one such $j$. And for that $j$,

$$ G_i = R\,G_j = R(S\,G_k) = (RS)\,G_k $$

This is precisely the condition for $D^{\rm reg}(RS)_{ik}=1$. Conversely, if $(RS)G_k=G_i$, then defining $j$ by $G_j=SG_k$ satisfies the two conditions above. Therefore

$$ \left[D^{\rm reg}(R)D^{\rm reg}(S)\right]_{ik} = D^{\rm reg}(RS)_{ik} $$

∎ The regular representation is a representation.

Example 7.3: Writing out the whole regular representation of the cyclic group $C_3$

The multiplication table of $C_3=\{E,\ A,\ B\}$ ($A=C_3$, $B=C_3^2$) is as follows.

Table 7.1: Multiplication table of $C_3$
$E$$A$$B$
$E$$E$$A$$B$
$A$$A$$B$$E$
$B$$B$$E$$A$

Solution. Let the order of the basis be $(G_1,G_2,G_3)=(E,A,B)$. Multiplying by $A$ from the left gives

$$ A\,G_1 = A\,E = A = G_2,\qquad A\,G_2 = A\,A = B = G_3,\qquad A\,G_3 = A\,B = E = G_1 $$

Following the convention “put a $1$ in row $i$ of column $j$,” the $1$ appears in row 2 for column 1, in row 3 for column 2, and in row 1 for column 3:

$$ D^{\rm reg}(A)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad D^{\rm reg}(B)=\left[D^{\rm reg}(A)\right]^2=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad D^{\rm reg}(E)=I_3 $$

∎ The traces are $\Tr D^{\rm reg}(E)=3$ and $\Tr D^{\rm reg}(A)=\Tr D^{\rm reg}(B)=0$.

Theorem 7.1: Character of the regular representation

The trace of the regular representation is

$$ \begin{equation} \chi^{\rm reg}(R)=\begin{cases} h & (R=E)\\ 0 & (R\neq E)\end{cases} \label{eq:7-chireg} \end{equation} $$

Proof. We have $\Tr D^{\rm reg}(R)=\sum_i D^{\rm reg}(R)_{ii}$. The diagonal element $D^{\rm reg}(R)_{ii}$ equals $1$ only when $R\,G_i=G_i$. Multiplying both sides by $G_i^{-1}$ from the right gives $R=E$. Therefore, if $R\neq E$, all the diagonal elements are $0$ and the trace is also $0$; if $R=E$, then $D^{\rm reg}(E)=I_h$ and the trace is $h$. ∎

This extreme form—huge only at $E$ and zero everywhere else—is the device that yields $\sum_i\ell_i^2=h$ in one stroke in Section 7.9.

7.3 Similarity Transformations and Equivalence of Representations

7.3.1 Changing the basis

In Section 7.1.3 we chose $(x,y,z)$ as the basis. However, there are countless ways to choose a basis. Changing the basis changes the matrix elements, but the structure as a group should not change. The similarity transformation is what organizes this “difference in appearance only.”

Definition: Similarity transformation and equivalent representations

Let $U$ be a nonsingular matrix (a single fixed matrix that does not depend on the group element). Then

$$ \begin{equation} D'(R) = U^{-1}D(R)\,U \qquad(\text{for all }R\in G) \label{eq:7-sim} \end{equation} $$

defines $\{D'(R)\}$, which is again a representation. $D$ and $D'$ are said to be equivalent, and we write $D\sim D'$.

Derivation: $D'$ really is a representation

It suffices to check Eq. \eqref{eq:7-hom}. Substituting the definition \eqref{eq:7-sim}, we obtain

$$ D'(R)\,D'(S) = \left(U^{-1}D(R)U\right)\left(U^{-1}D(S)U\right) $$

The $U U^{-1}$ in the middle cancels, giving

$$ = U^{-1}D(R)\underbrace{UU^{-1}}_{=I}D(S)\,U = U^{-1}\big[D(R)D(S)\big]U = U^{-1}D(RS)\,U = D'(RS) $$

∎ The relation holds. It is decisive that $U$ is a single fixed matrix that does not depend on the group element. If $U$ differed for each $R$, the $UU^{-1}$ would not cancel.

Theorem 7.2: Characters are invariant under similarity transformations

$\Tr D'(R) = \Tr D(R)$.

Proof. We use the cyclic property of the trace, $\Tr(ABC)=\Tr(CAB)$.

$$ \Tr D'(R) = \Tr\left(U^{-1}D(R)U\right) = \Tr\left(U U^{-1}D(R)\right) = \Tr D(R) $$

∎

Therefore the trace is “a property of the representation itself, independent of the choice of basis.” We call it the character and write $\chi(R)\equiv\Tr D(R)$. The whole of Chapter 8 is built on this single line.

7.3.2 Concrete example 1: Passing to a complex basis

Example 7.4: From $(x,y)$ to $(x+iy,\ x-iy)$

What happens if we replace the basis of the upper-left $2\times2$ block of the $C_{3v}$ representation in Section 7.1.3 with $u_\pm \equiv x\pm iy$?

Solution. For the operation of rotating by an angle $\phi$ about the $z$ axis, we had $x'=x\cos\phi-y\sin\phi$, $y'=x\sin\phi+y\cos\phi$. Therefore

$$ u_+' = x'+iy' = (x\cos\phi-y\sin\phi)+i(x\sin\phi+y\cos\phi) $$

Collecting the terms in $x$ and in $y$, we get

$$ = x(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi) + y(-\sin\phi+i\cos\phi) = x\,e^{i\phi} + y\cdot i(\cos\phi + i\sin\phi) = e^{i\phi}(x+iy) = e^{i\phi}u_+ $$

(In the second equality we used $-\sin\phi+i\cos\phi = i(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi)$. This is correct because $i\cdot i\sin\phi=-\sin\phi$.) Similarly, $u_-'=e^{-i\phi}u_-$. That is,

$$ D'(C_3) = \begin{pmatrix} e^{2\pi i/3} & 0\\ 0 & e^{-2\pi i/3}\end{pmatrix} $$

The rotation has been diagonalized. On the other hand, the reflection $\sigma_1$ sends $x\to x,\ y\to-y$, so $u_+\leftrightarrow u_-$, and

$$ D'(\sigma_1)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} $$

Let us compare the characters. $\Tr D'(C_3) = e^{2\pi i/3}+e^{-2\pi i/3} = 2\cos120^\circ = -1$, and for the original real matrix also $-\tfrac12-\tfrac12=-1$. $\Tr D'(\sigma_1)=0$, and for the original real matrix also $1-1=0$. They agree, as Theorem 7.2 states. ∎

The transformation matrix of the basis is $\begin{pmatrix}u_+\\u_-\end{pmatrix}=S\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}$, $S=\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\1&-i\end{pmatrix}$, and $D'(R)=S\,D(R)\,S^{-1}$ is a similarity transformation (setting $U=S^{-1}$ gives the form of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-sim}).

Caution: Diagonalizing the rotation does not mean the representation has been reduced

In Example 7.4, $D'(C_3)$ became a diagonal matrix. One must not jump to the conclusion that “the representation has split into two one-dimensional representations.” If we transform $\sigma_1$ with the same $S$, it becomes the off-diagonal matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.

To say that a representation can be reduced means that it becomes block-diagonal simultaneously for all elements of the group. Diagonalizing a single matrix is easy, but whether all the elements of the group can be block-diagonalized simultaneously is an entirely different problem. This difference is the subject of the next section.

7.3.3 Concrete example 2: Equivalent representations that look completely different

Example 7.5: A representation from the three vertices of an equilateral triangle

Picture the three hydrogen atoms of an $\mathrm{NH_3}$ molecule, or the three vertices of an equilateral triangle. Let $\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3$ be functions placed at vertices $1,2,3$ (for example, the hydrogen $1s$ orbitals). Construct a representation from how the operations of $C_{3v}$ interchange them.

Solution. $C_3$ permutes them cyclically, $\phi_1\to\phi_2\to\phi_3\to\phi_1$. Following the convention of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-basis} (the $j$th column is the destination of $\phi_j$), column 1 has a $1$ in row 2, column 2 has a $1$ in row 3, and column 3 has a $1$ in row 1:

$$ D(C_3)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad D(C_3^2)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix} $$

Let $\sigma_1$ be the mirror plane through vertex 1; then $\phi_1$ stays fixed and $\phi_2\leftrightarrow\phi_3$. Before writing down the remaining two, note that the number of a plane does not coincide with the number of a vertex. In the convention of Section 7.1.3, the number of $\sigma_k$ corresponds to the inclination of the plane, $0^\circ,\,60^\circ,\,120^\circ$. If we place vertex 1 on $\sigma_1$ (in the direction $0^\circ$) and take $C_3$ to be a counterclockwise rotation by $120^\circ$, then vertex 2 comes to the direction $120^\circ$ and vertex 3 to the direction $240^\circ\equiv 60^\circ$. Therefore $\sigma_2$ ($60^\circ$) passes through vertex 3, and $\sigma_3$ ($120^\circ$) passes through vertex 2:

$$ D(\sigma_1)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad D(\sigma_2)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad D(\sigma_3)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&1&0\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix} $$

Taking the characters, since the trace of a permutation matrix equals the number of elements left fixed,

$$ \chi(E)=3,\qquad \chi(C_3)=\chi(C_3^2)=0,\qquad \chi(\sigma_i)=1 $$

∎ This agrees exactly with the characters $(3,0,1)$ of the $(x,y,z)$ representation in Section 7.1.3.

Physical Meaning: If the characters agree, the representations are equivalent

The permutation matrices of Example 7.5 and the rotation and reflection matrices of Section 7.1.3 look completely different. The former contain only $0$ and $1$; the latter have $\sqrt3/2$ in them. Nevertheless, since the characters are the same, these two representations are equivalent. That is, by choosing a suitable $U$, one can be transformed into the other.

In fact, a necessary and sufficient condition for two representations to be equivalent is that their characters agree for all elements. This follows from the reduction formula \eqref{eq:7-reduction}, which we derive in Section 7.9.1 of this chapter. The number of times each irreducible representation appears (its multiplicity) $a_i$ is determined by the characters alone, so, together with complete reducibility, two representations with equal characters decompose into the same combination of the same irreducible representations, and are therefore equivalent. There is no need to compare the matrices themselves. It suffices to compare $h$ numbers (in fact, only as many numbers as there are classes). This is the decisive power of the character as a tool.

It also has physical meaning. The space spanned by the three hydrogen $1s$ orbitals of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ and the space spanned by $(x,y,z)$ have the same structure from the standpoint of the $C_{3v}$ symmetry. Therefore both split in the same pattern. The calculation of SALCs (symmetry-adapted linear combinations) in Chapter 9 makes full use of this fact.

7.4 Reducible and Irreducible Representations

7.4.1 Invariant subspaces

Definition: Invariant subspace, reducible, irreducible

Let $W$ be a subspace ($0<\dim W<\ell$) of the $\ell$-dimensional space $V$ on which a representation $\{D(R)\}$ acts. If, for every element $R$ of the group,

$$ D(R)\,W \subseteq W $$

holds, then $W$ is called an invariant subspace.

  • A representation that has an invariant subspace is called a reducible representation.
  • A representation that has none is called an irreducible representation (irrep for short).

“Irreducible” means that it cannot be divided into any smaller pieces. Irreducible representations play the role of “elementary particles” for a group.

Why? An invariant subspace makes the matrices block-diagonal

Let $W$ be an $m$-dimensional invariant subspace. List a basis $\{w_1,\dots,w_m\}$ of $W$ first, and complete it with the remaining vectors to form a basis $\{w_1,\dots,w_m,\,v_{m+1},\dots,v_\ell\}$ of $V$. When the matrices are written in this basis, $D(R)w_j$ again lies in $W$, so the coefficients of the $v$ components vanish. That is, the lower part (row $m+1$ and below) of columns $1\sim m$ is entirely zero:

$$ D'(R)=\begin{pmatrix} A(R) & B(R)\\ 0 & C(R)\end{pmatrix} $$

This makes the matrices block-triangular. Whether we can further make $B(R)=0$ (that is, make them block-diagonal) is a separate question, but for a unitary representation of a finite group it is always possible. Indeed, in Section 7.5 we show that any representation can be made unitary, and if the representation is unitary, the orthogonal complement $W^\perp$ of $W$ is also an invariant subspace (Exercise 7.2), so $B(R)=0$ is guaranteed.

This situation is expressed by saying that a representation of a finite group is completely reducible. From here on, you may take a reducible representation to mean one that can be made block-diagonal.

Notation: Direct sum

A block-diagonalized representation is written as

$$ D = D^{(1)}\oplus D^{(2)}\oplus\cdots \quad\text{or}\quad \Gamma = a_1\Gamma^{(1)}\oplus a_2\Gamma^{(2)}\oplus\cdots $$

and is called a direct sum. Here $a_i$ is the number of times the irreducible representation $\Gamma^{(i)}$ appears (its multiplicity). Dimensions and traces simply add:

$$ \ell = \sum_i a_i\ell_i,\qquad \chi(R)=\sum_i a_i\chi^{(i)}(R) $$

The latter is evident because the trace of a block-diagonal matrix is the sum of the traces of the blocks.

7.4.2 Decomposing the three-dimensional representation of $C_{3v}$

Derivation: The $(x,y,z)$ representation is $A_1\oplus E$

Let us look once more at the six matrices written down in Section 7.1.3. All of them have the form

$$ D(R)=\begin{pmatrix} \ast & \ast & 0\\ \ast & \ast & 0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix} $$

That is, they are already block-diagonal. The reason is clear.

  • Every symmetry operation of $C_{3v}$ is either a rotation about the $z$ axis or a reflection in a plane containing the $z$ axis. Neither changes the $z$ coordinate. Therefore the one-dimensional space spanned by $z$ alone is an invariant subspace.
  • At the same time, the $z$ component never mixes into the $xy$ components. Therefore the two-dimensional space spanned by $(x,y)$ is also an invariant subspace.

The one-dimensional part spanned by $z$ has $D(R)_{33}=1$ (for all $R$), so it is the identity representation $A_1$.

Let us write the remaining $2\times2$ block as $D^{(E)}(R)$. We show that it is irreducible. If it were reducible, then, since it is two-dimensional, there would have to exist a one-dimensional invariant subspace, that is, a common eigenvector of all the $D^{(E)}(R)$.

First we find the eigenvectors of $D^{(E)}(C_3)$. Multiplying the vector $\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix}$ by the rotation matrix $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\phi&-\sin\phi\\ \sin\phi&\cos\phi\end{pmatrix}$ for the angle $\phi$ gives

$$ \begin{pmatrix}\cos\phi+i\sin\phi\\ \sin\phi-i\cos\phi\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\phi}\\ -i(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi)\end{pmatrix} = e^{i\phi}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix} $$

(In the second row we used $\sin\phi-i\cos\phi = -i(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi)$.) Similarly, $\begin{pmatrix}1\\ i\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalue $e^{-i\phi}$. For $\phi=120^\circ$ we have $e^{i\phi}\neq e^{-i\phi}$, so the eigenvalues are not degenerate, and there are only these two eigenvectors (up to constant multiples).

Next we check whether these two are also eigenvectors of $D^{(E)}(\sigma_1)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$.

$$ \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\ i\end{pmatrix} $$

This is the other eigenvector, not a constant multiple of the original vector. For $\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix}$ the two likewise interchange. Therefore no common eigenvector exists.

∎ Hence $D^{(E)}$ is irreducible, and it is the two-dimensional irreducible representation $E$. In summary,

$$ \begin{equation} \Gamma_{(x,y,z)} = A_1 \oplus E,\qquad 3 = 1 + 2 \label{eq:7-c3v-decomp} \end{equation} $$

This can also be checked with the characters. Adding $\chi^{A_1}=(1,1,1)$ and $\chi^{E}=(2,-1,0)$ gives $(3,0,1)$, which agrees with the calculation in Section 7.1.3.

Caution: The irrep symbol $E$ and the identity element $E$ are different things

This is the biggest trap in group-theoretic notation. As an element of $C_{3v}$, $E$ is the identity operation; as the name of an irreducible representation, $E$ denotes the two-dimensional irreducible representation (it derives not from the German zweidimensional but from Entartung, meaning degeneracy). One has no choice but to distinguish them by context. In this book we try to write superscripts, as in $\Gamma^{(E)}$ or $D^{(E)}$, when we mean the irreducible representation.

Example 7.6: Actually block-diagonalizing a permutation representation

The three-vertex representation of Example 7.5 has character $(3,0,1)$, so it should decompose into $A_1\oplus E$. Yet the permutation matrices are not block-diagonal as they stand. Construct explicitly the similarity transformation $U$ that decomposes it.

Solution. As the new basis we take the combinations that suggest themselves naturally from the symmetry.

$$ s=\frac{\phi_1+\phi_2+\phi_3}{\sqrt3},\qquad p_x=\frac{2\phi_1-\phi_2-\phi_3}{\sqrt6},\qquad p_y=\frac{\phi_2-\phi_3}{\sqrt2} $$

(The normalization constants were chosen so that the sum of the squares of the coefficients is $1$: $1+1+1=3$, $4+1+1=6$, $1+1=2$.) The transformation matrix is obtained by arranging the coefficients of the expansion of the new basis in terms of the old basis as its columns:

$$ U=\begin{pmatrix} 1/\sqrt3 & 2/\sqrt6 & 0\\ 1/\sqrt3 & -1/\sqrt6 & 1/\sqrt2\\ 1/\sqrt3 & -1/\sqrt6 & -1/\sqrt2 \end{pmatrix} $$

The three columns are mutually orthogonal (for example, the inner product of the first and second columns is $(2-1-1)/\sqrt{18}=0$) and each has length 1, so $U$ is an orthogonal matrix. Hence $U^{-1}=U^{T}$.

Calculation for $C_3$. The permutation matrix $D(C_3)$ acts on a vector $u=(u_1,u_2,u_3)^T$ as $D(C_3)u=(u_3,u_1,u_2)^T$ (just read it row by row).

First column $u=(1,1,1)^T/\sqrt3$: it is invariant under the cyclic permutation, remaining $(1,1,1)^T/\sqrt3$. Taking the inner product with each row of $U^T$ gives $(1,0,0)^T$.

Second column $u=(2,-1,-1)^T/\sqrt6$: the cyclic permutation gives $(-1,2,-1)^T/\sqrt6$. The inner products with the rows of $U^T$ are

$$ \frac{-1+2-1}{\sqrt3\sqrt6}=0,\qquad \frac{2(-1)+(-1)(2)+(-1)(-1)}{6}=\frac{-3}{6}=-\frac12,\qquad \frac{0\cdot(-1)+1\cdot2+(-1)(-1)}{\sqrt2\sqrt6}=\frac{3}{2\sqrt3}=\frac{\sqrt3}{2} $$

Third column $u=(0,1,-1)^T/\sqrt2$: the cyclic permutation gives $(-1,0,1)^T/\sqrt2$. Taking the inner products in the same way gives $0,\ -\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\ -\dfrac12$.

Arranging these results, we obtain

$$ U^{-1}D(C_3)U=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\[2pt] 0&-\tfrac12&-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[2pt] 0&\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\tfrac12\end{pmatrix} $$

∎ The matrix is beautifully block-diagonal, and the $2\times2$ block coincides exactly, symbol for symbol, with $D^{(E)}(C_3)$ that we obtained for $(x,y)$ in Section 7.1.3. Carrying out the same calculation for $\sigma_1$ gives $\mathrm{diag}(1,\,1,\,-1)$, which also has the form $A_1\oplus E$ ($p_x$ is invariant under $\sigma_1$, while $p_y$ changes sign).

We have now confirmed, with $U$ written out explicitly, that the representation of Example 7.5 and the representation of Section 7.1.3 are equivalent. The $s,\,p_x,\,p_y$ constructed here are precisely the SALCs (symmetry-adapted linear combinations) that we will construct systematically with projection operators in Chapter 9. We have written them down out of the blue for now, but in Chapter 9 a mechanical recipe for constructing them will be given.

7.5 The Unitarity Theorem for Representations

From here the proofs continue. As groundwork, we first prove the theorem that "a representation may be assumed to be unitary." With it, from here on we can use $D(R)^{-1}=D(R)^\dagger$ freely, and the arguments become dramatically lighter.

Theorem 7.3 (Unitarity theorem)

Any representation of a finite group can be transformed, by a suitable similarity transformation, into a unitary representation (one with $D(R)^\dagger D(R)=I$ for all $R$).

Proof: Construct $H=\sum_R D(R)D(R)^\dagger$ and diagonalize it

Step 1: Construct the matrix $H$. Define the sum over all elements of the group

$$ H \equiv \sum_{R\in G} D(R)\,D(R)^\dagger $$

Since it is a sum of $h$ matrices, it is a single $\ell\times\ell$ matrix that does not depend on $R$.

Step 2: $H$ is Hermitian. Taking $\dagger$, for each term of the sum we have $\left(DD^\dagger\right)^\dagger = \left(D^\dagger\right)^\dagger D^\dagger = DD^\dagger$, so

$$ H^\dagger = \sum_R \left(D(R)D(R)^\dagger\right)^\dagger = \sum_R D(R)D(R)^\dagger = H $$

Step 3: $H$ is positive definite. For any nonzero vector $v$,

$$ v^\dagger H v = \sum_R v^\dagger D(R)D(R)^\dagger v = \sum_R \left(D(R)^\dagger v\right)^\dagger\left(D(R)^\dagger v\right) = \sum_R \left\|D(R)^\dagger v\right\|^2 \ \ge\ 0 $$

For equality to hold, we would need $D(R)^\dagger v=0$ for every $R$. But the term with $R=E$ is $D(E)^\dagger v = I v = v$, so if $v\neq0$ this term alone is positive. Hence $v^\dagger Hv>0$; that is, $H$ is positive definite.

Step 4: Diagonalize $H$. Since it is a Hermitian matrix, it can be diagonalized by some unitary matrix $U$:

$$ U^\dagger H U = d \equiv \mathrm{diag}(d_1,d_2,\dots,d_\ell) $$

Since it is positive definite, all the eigenvalues are positive, $d_k>0$. Therefore

$$ d^{1/2}\equiv \mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{d_1},\dots,\sqrt{d_\ell}),\qquad d^{-1/2}\equiv \mathrm{diag}(1/\sqrt{d_1},\dots,1/\sqrt{d_\ell}) $$

can both be defined as real diagonal matrices, and $d^{1/2}d^{1/2}=d$, $d^{1/2}d^{-1/2}=I$ hold. It is here that $d_k>0$ matters. If any eigenvalue were zero or negative, these square roots could not be constructed and the proof would break down.

Step 5: Define the new representation. First put $\tilde{D}(R)\equiv U^\dagger D(R) U$ (this is a similarity transformation, so it is a representation). From the definition,

$$ \sum_R \tilde{D}(R)\tilde{D}(R)^\dagger = \sum_R U^\dagger D(R)UU^\dagger D(R)^\dagger U = U^\dagger\left(\sum_R D(R)D(R)^\dagger\right)U = U^\dagger H U = d $$

Furthermore, define

$$ D'(R) \equiv d^{-1/2}\,\tilde{D}(R)\,d^{1/2} $$

This is also a similarity transformation (the transformation matrix is $U d^{1/2}$), so it is a representation.

Step 6: Show that $D'$ is unitary. Noting that $d^{1/2}$ is a real diagonal matrix, so that $\left(d^{1/2}\right)^\dagger = d^{1/2}$, we have

$$ D'(R)D'(R)^\dagger = d^{-1/2}\tilde{D}(R)d^{1/2}\cdot d^{1/2}\tilde{D}(R)^\dagger d^{-1/2} = d^{-1/2}\,\tilde{D}(R)\,d\,\tilde{D}(R)^\dagger\,d^{-1/2} $$

We compute the middle factor $\tilde{D}(R)\,d\,\tilde{D}(R)^\dagger$. Rewriting $d$ as the sum of Step 5, we obtain

$$ \tilde{D}(R)\,d\,\tilde{D}(R)^\dagger = \tilde{D}(R)\left[\sum_S \tilde{D}(S)\tilde{D}(S)^\dagger\right]\tilde{D}(R)^\dagger = \sum_S \left[\tilde{D}(R)\tilde{D}(S)\right]\left[\tilde{D}(S)^\dagger \tilde{D}(R)^\dagger\right] $$

The first factor is $\tilde{D}(RS)$, and the second is $\left[\tilde{D}(R)\tilde{D}(S)\right]^\dagger=\tilde{D}(RS)^\dagger$ (recall that $\dagger$ reverses the order of a product). Hence

$$ = \sum_{S\in G} \tilde{D}(RS)\,\tilde{D}(RS)^\dagger $$

Here we use the rearrangement theorem. If we fix $R$ and let $S$ run over the whole group, the product $RS$ also runs over the whole group, once each (Chapter 6). Therefore, relabeling $S'=RS$,

$$ = \sum_{S'\in G} \tilde{D}(S')\tilde{D}(S')^\dagger = d $$

Going back to the original expression, we obtain

$$ D'(R)D'(R)^\dagger = d^{-1/2}\,d\,d^{-1/2} = I $$

∎ Since this holds for all $R$, $D'$ is a unitary representation.

Why? Why this theorem is so useful

If the representation is unitary, then $D(R)^{-1}=D(R)^\dagger$, or in components,

$$ \left[D(R)^{-1}\right]_{\mu\nu} = \left[D(R)^\dagger\right]_{\mu\nu} = D(R)^{*}_{\nu\mu} $$

The unwieldy inverse matrix turns into the simple operation of complex conjugation and exchange of indices. The complex conjugate $D^{*}$ appears in the great orthogonality theorem because this replacement is used.

Moreover, since a unitary matrix preserves the inner product, the orthogonal complement $W^\perp$ of an invariant subspace $W$ is also an invariant subspace. This upgrades "block-triangular" to "block-diagonal" and guarantees complete reducibility (Exercise 7.2).

From here on, in this chapter we assume that all representations are unitary. By Theorem 7.3, this is without loss of generality.

7.6 Schur's First Lemma

We have finally reached the heart of this chapter. Schur's lemma is called a “lemma” in name only; nearly all of the representation theory of groups flows from it. First, let us state in plain words what it asserts.

What We Are About to Do

An irreducible representation is a representation that “can no longer be split.” That it cannot be split means that the space on which the representation acts contains no direction that can be singled out for special treatment.

Suppose a matrix $M$ commutes with all the matrices of the representation. If we take $M$ to be Hermitian, $M$ decomposes the space into eigenspaces. If there are two or more distinct eigenvalues, those eigenspaces are closed under the representation (they become invariant subspaces)—that is, a “direction that can be singled out” has been created. This contradicts irreducibility. Therefore there can be only one eigenvalue, and $M$ must be a constant multiple of the identity matrix.

This is the whole content of Schur's first lemma. The crux is the single point that “if it commutes, the eigenspaces become invariant subspaces.” In the proof below, we follow that point carefully.

Theorem 7.4 (Schur's first lemma)

Let $\{D(R)\}$ be an $\ell$-dimensional irreducible representation of a group $G$. If an $\ell\times\ell$ matrix $M$ satisfies

$$ \begin{equation} M\,D(R) = D(R)\,M \qquad(\text{for all }R\in G) \label{eq:7-schur1} \end{equation} $$

then $M$ is a constant multiple of the identity matrix: $M=cI$ ($c$ is a complex number).

Proof

Step 1: Show that we may take $M$ to be Hermitian. Take the $\dagger$ of both sides of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur1}. The order of the products is reversed, so

$$ D(R)^\dagger M^\dagger = M^\dagger D(R)^\dagger $$

Since the representation may be taken to be unitary (Theorem 7.3), $D(R)^\dagger=D(R)^{-1}$, and

$$ D(R)^{-1}M^\dagger = M^\dagger D(R)^{-1} $$

Multiply both sides of this equation by $D(R)$ from the left and from the right. The left-hand side becomes $D(R)D(R)^{-1}M^\dagger D(R)=M^\dagger D(R)$, and the right-hand side becomes $D(R)M^\dagger D(R)^{-1}D(R)=D(R)M^\dagger$. Therefore

$$ M^\dagger D(R) = D(R)M^\dagger $$

that is, $M^\dagger$ also satisfies Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur1}. We now define

$$ H_1 \equiv M+M^\dagger,\qquad H_2\equiv i\left(M-M^\dagger\right) $$

Since $H_1^\dagger = M^\dagger+M = H_1$ and $H_2^\dagger = -i(M^\dagger-M)=i(M-M^\dagger)=H_2$, both are Hermitian. Moreover, Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur1} is a linear relation, so both $H_1$ and $H_2$ satisfy it. Solving backward, we obtain

$$ H_1 - iH_2 = (M+M^\dagger) - i\cdot i(M-M^\dagger) = (M+M^\dagger)+(M-M^\dagger) = 2M \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M=\tfrac12\left(H_1-iH_2\right) $$

Hence, if we can show $H=cI$ for Hermitian matrices, then $M=\tfrac12(c_1-ic_2)I$, and the conclusion follows for a general $M$ as well. From here on, $M$ is taken to be Hermitian.

Step 2: Diagonalize $M$. Since it is Hermitian, there is a unitary matrix $V$ such that

$$ V^\dagger M V = \Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_\ell) $$

diagonalizes it (the eigenvalues $\lambda_\mu$ are real). The ordering of the eigenvalues is free, so we arrange them so that eigenvalues with the same value are adjacent. At the same time, we transform the representation as $\tilde{D}(R)\equiv V^\dagger D(R)V$. This is a similarity transformation, so $\tilde{D}$ is also an irreducible representation.

Sandwiching Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur1} in the form $V^\dagger\,(\cdots)\,V$, we obtain

$$ V^\dagger M D(R)V = V^\dagger M VV^\dagger D(R)V = \Lambda\tilde{D}(R),\qquad V^\dagger D(R)MV = \tilde{D}(R)\Lambda $$

Therefore

$$ \Lambda\,\tilde{D}(R) = \tilde{D}(R)\,\Lambda $$

Step 3: Write it in components. Since $\Lambda$ is a diagonal matrix, $\left[\Lambda\tilde{D}\right]_{\mu\nu} = \lambda_\mu \tilde{D}_{\mu\nu}$ and $\left[\tilde{D}\Lambda\right]_{\mu\nu}=\tilde{D}_{\mu\nu}\lambda_\nu$. Thus

$$ \begin{equation} \left(\lambda_\mu-\lambda_\nu\right)\tilde{D}(R)_{\mu\nu} = 0 \qquad(\text{for all }\mu,\nu,R) \label{eq:7-schur1-key} \end{equation} $$

This is the key to the proof. Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur1-key} says that if $\lambda_\mu\neq\lambda_\nu$, then $\tilde{D}(R)_{\mu\nu}=0$ holds for all $R$.

Step 4: Derive a contradiction with irreducibility. Now suppose that there are two or more distinct eigenvalues. Since we arranged equal values next to each other in Step 2, the indices $1,\dots,\ell$ are divided into “clusters of equal eigenvalues.” By Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur1-key}, all components that straddle different clusters are zero. That is, $\tilde{D}(R)$ becomes a block-diagonal matrix whose blocks are the clusters—and it does so simultaneously for all $R$ of the group.

This means that $\tilde{D}$ is reducible, which contradicts the assumption that $\tilde{D}$ (and hence $D$) is irreducible.

Step 5: Conclusion. Hence there is only one eigenvalue. If we set $\lambda_1=\cdots=\lambda_\ell\equiv c$, then $\Lambda=cI$, and transforming back,

$$ M = V\Lambda V^\dagger = V(cI)V^\dagger = c\,VV^\dagger = cI $$

∎

Corollary 7.1: Every irreducible representation of an abelian group is one-dimensional

Proof. Let $G$ be a commutative group (an abelian group), and let $\{D(R)\}$ be an irreducible representation of it. Since the group is commutative, for an arbitrarily fixed element $S$ we have $D(S)D(R)=D(SR)=D(RS)=D(R)D(S)$ for all $R$. That is, $M=D(S)$ satisfies Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur1}. By Schur's first lemma,

$$ D(S)=c_S I\qquad(\text{for each }S\text{, a constant }c_S) $$

But if every matrix is a constant multiple of the identity matrix, then every one-dimensional subspace becomes an invariant subspace. For the representation to be irreducible, no proper subspace can exist in the first place, that is, we must have $\ell=1$. ∎

Example 7.7: Finding all the irreducible representations of $C_3$

$C_3=\{E,A,B\}$ ($A^3=E$) is a commutative group. By Corollary 7.1, all of its irreducible representations are one-dimensional. Find them all.

Solution. In a one-dimensional representation, the matrices are numbers, $D(A)=\epsilon$. From $A^3=E$ we get $\epsilon^3=D(E)=1$. The cube roots of 1 are

$$ \epsilon = 1,\quad \omega\equiv e^{2\pi i/3},\quad \omega^2 = e^{4\pi i/3} $$

three in all. Therefore there are three irreducible representations:

Table 7.2: The irreducible representations of $C_3$ (= the character table)
$E$$A$$B=A^2$
$\Gamma^{(1)}$$1$$1$$1$
$\Gamma^{(2)}$$1$$\omega$$\omega^2$
$\Gamma^{(3)}$$1$$\omega^2$$\omega^4=\omega$

∎ The sum of the squares of the dimensions is $1^2+1^2+1^2=3=h$, which satisfies the dimension theorem of Section 7.9. Note also that $1+\omega+\omega^2=0$ (since $\epsilon^3-1=(\epsilon-1)(\epsilon^2+\epsilon+1)=0$, and the roots with $\epsilon\neq1$ are $\omega,\omega^2$). We will use this relation many times in the orthogonality calculations in the following sections.

7.7 Schur's Second Lemma

What We Are About to Do

The first lemma was a statement about the inside of a single irreducible representation. The second lemma is about a matrix connecting two different irreducible representations.

Let $D^{(i)}$ and $D^{(j)}$ be irreducible representations, and suppose that a matrix $M$, which may be rectangular, satisfies $D^{(i)}(R)M=MD^{(j)}(R)$ for every $R$. This equation says that “transferring with $M$ and then rotating in the language of $i$” gives the same result as “rotating in the language of $j$ and then transferring with $M$.” In other words, $M$ is a structure-preserving map between the two representations.

The claim is that “such a map is either $M=0$ (it transmits nothing) or $M$ is invertible (the two representations are in fact the same); there is no other possibility.” A halfway, partial connection is not allowed. The crux of the proof is to form $MM^\dagger$ and bring it into the setting of the first lemma, and then to rule out the case in which $M$ is not square.

Theorem 7.5 (Schur's second lemma)

Let $D^{(i)}$ (dimension $\ell_i$) and $D^{(j)}$ (dimension $\ell_j$) be irreducible representations of a group $G$. Suppose that an $\ell_i\times\ell_j$ matrix $M$ satisfies

$$ \begin{equation} D^{(i)}(R)\,M = M\,D^{(j)}(R)\qquad(\text{for all }R\in G) \label{eq:7-schur2} \end{equation} $$

Then the following hold.

  • If $D^{(i)}$ and $D^{(j)}$ are inequivalent, then $M=0$.
  • If $\ell_i\neq\ell_j$, then $M=0$.
  • If $M\neq0$, then $\ell_i=\ell_j$, $M$ is nonsingular, and $D^{(i)}$ and $D^{(j)}$ are equivalent.

Proof

Step 1: Prepare the relation obtained by taking $\dagger$. Taking $\dagger$ of both sides of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur2} gives

$$ M^\dagger D^{(i)}(R)^\dagger = D^{(j)}(R)^\dagger M^\dagger $$

By unitarity, $D(R)^\dagger = D(R)^{-1}=D(R^{-1})$ (the derivation in Section 7.1.1), so

$$ M^\dagger D^{(i)}(R^{-1}) = D^{(j)}(R^{-1})\,M^\dagger $$

Here, as $R$ runs over the whole group, $R^{-1}$ also runs over the whole group (inverse elements are in one-to-one correspondence). Therefore we may rename $R^{-1}$ as $R$, which gives

$$ \begin{equation} M^\dagger D^{(i)}(R) = D^{(j)}(R)\,M^\dagger \qquad(\text{for all }R) \label{eq:7-schur2-dag} \end{equation} $$

Step 2: $MM^\dagger$ satisfies the condition of the first lemma. $MM^\dagger$ is an $\ell_i\times\ell_i$ square matrix. Using Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur2} and then Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur2-dag}, we obtain

$$ D^{(i)}(R)\,MM^\dagger \ \overset{\eqref{eq:7-schur2}}{=}\ M\,D^{(j)}(R)\,M^\dagger \ \overset{\eqref{eq:7-schur2-dag}}{=}\ M\,M^\dagger D^{(i)}(R) $$

(In the second equality, we read Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur2-dag} from right to left and replaced the left-hand side with the right-hand side in $D^{(j)}(R)M^\dagger = M^\dagger D^{(i)}(R)$.) That is, $MM^\dagger$ commutes with all the matrices of $D^{(i)}$. Since $D^{(i)}$ is irreducible, Schur's first lemma gives

$$ \begin{equation} MM^\dagger = c\,I_{\ell_i} \label{eq:7-MMdag} \end{equation} $$

Step 3: The case $\ell_i=\ell_j$. $M$ is a square matrix.

(a) When $c\neq0$. Taking the determinant of both sides of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-MMdag} gives $\det M\cdot\det M^\dagger = c^{\ell_i}\neq0$, so $\det M\neq0$ and $M$ is nonsingular. Then multiplying both sides of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur2} by $M^{-1}$ from the left gives

$$ M^{-1}D^{(i)}(R)M = D^{(j)}(R) $$

This is precisely the similarity-transformation equation \eqref{eq:7-sim}, so the two representations are equivalent.

(b) When $c=0$. Writing out the diagonal elements of $MM^\dagger=0$, we have

$$ \left(MM^\dagger\right)_{\mu\mu} = \sum_{\nu} M_{\mu\nu}\left(M^\dagger\right)_{\nu\mu} = \sum_\nu M_{\mu\nu}M^{*}_{\mu\nu} = \sum_\nu \left|M_{\mu\nu}\right|^2 = 0 $$

If a sum of squared absolute values is zero, then every term is zero, so $M_{\mu\nu}=0$ for all $\mu,\nu$; that is, $M=0$.

To summarize, when the dimensions are equal there is a dichotomy: “either the representations are equivalent, or $M=0$.” Taking the contrapositive, if they are inequivalent, then $M=0$.

Step 4: The case $\ell_i\neq\ell_j$. First suppose $\ell_i>\ell_j$ ($M$ is tall). Append $\ell_i-\ell_j$ zero columns to the right of $M$ to make an $\ell_i\times\ell_i$ square matrix $N$:

$$ N \equiv \left(\,M \ \big|\ 0\,\right) $$

Then $N^\dagger$ has the form of $M^\dagger$ with rows of zeros stacked beneath it, so multiplying in blocks gives

$$ NN^\dagger = \left(M\ \big|\ 0\right)\begin{pmatrix}M^\dagger\\ 0\end{pmatrix} = MM^\dagger + 0 = MM^\dagger = c\,I_{\ell_i} $$

On the other hand, $N$ has zero columns, so $\det N=0$. Therefore

$$ \det\left(NN^\dagger\right) = \det N\cdot\det N^\dagger = 0 $$

But $\det\left(cI_{\ell_i}\right) = c^{\ell_i}$, so

$$ c^{\ell_i}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad MM^\dagger=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M=0 $$

(The last implication is the same argument with the sum of squared absolute values as in Step 3(b).)

Next, the case $\ell_i<\ell_j$. Looking at Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur2-dag}, we see that, for $M^\dagger$ (an $\ell_j\times\ell_i$ matrix), it reads $D^{(j)}(R)M^\dagger = M^\dagger D^{(i)}(R)$, which has the form of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur2} with the roles of $i$ and $j$ interchanged. Since now $\ell_j>\ell_i$, the argument just above applies as it stands and gives $M^\dagger=0$, hence $M=0$.

∎ This exhausts all the cases.

Supplement: An alternative proof using the rank

Step 4 can also be settled in one line in the language of the rank in linear algebra. Suppose $\ell_i<\ell_j$. From Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur2-dag} and Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur2}, one can show in the same way that $M^\dagger M$ (an $\ell_j\times\ell_j$ matrix) commutes with $D^{(j)}$, so by Schur's first lemma $M^\dagger M = c'I_{\ell_j}$. If $c'\neq0$, the rank of $M^\dagger M$ would be $\ell_j$, which contradicts the general rule for ranks, $\mathrm{rank}(M^\dagger M)\le \mathrm{rank}(M)\le\min(\ell_i,\ell_j)=\ell_i<\ell_j$. Hence $c'=0$; that is, $M=0$.

Either way, the conclusion is the same. The approach using determinants is closer to the convention of Dresselhaus's textbook.

7.8 The Great Orthogonality Theorem

What We Are About to Do

Schur's lemma has the form “a matrix $M$ satisfying a certain condition must be either $0$ or $cI$.” To use it, we first have to construct, by hand, an $M$ that satisfies that condition. There is a standard recipe: sum over all elements of the group—that is, take a group average.

Applying Schur's lemma to the $M$ we have built gives an equality, either “$M=0$” or “$M=cI$.” Since the content of $M$ is a sum of products of matrix elements of $D^{(i)}$ and $D^{(j)}$, this equality is directly a relation among matrix elements. That relation is the great orthogonality theorem.

There are three key points. (1) We use the rearrangement theorem to show that the $M$ built by the group average indeed satisfies Schur's condition. (2) We choose the arbitrary matrix $X$ to be a matrix with “$1$ in just one place,” so as to pick out only the component we want. (3) We determine the coefficient $c$ from the trace. This is where $h/\ell_i$ appears.

Theorem 7.6 (great orthogonality theorem, also called the wonderful orthogonality theorem)

Let $D^{(i)}$ (of dimension $\ell_i$) be mutually inequivalent irreducible unitary representations of a group $G$ (of order $h$). Then

$$ \begin{equation} \sum_{R\in G} D^{(i)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\nu}\,D^{(j)}(R)_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{h}{\ell_i}\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta_{\mu\alpha}\,\delta_{\nu\beta} \label{eq:7-got} \end{equation} $$

Proof

Step 1: Construct $M$ by a group average. Take an arbitrary $\ell_i\times\ell_j$ matrix $X$ and define

$$ \begin{equation} M \equiv \sum_{R\in G} D^{(i)}(R)^{-1}\,X\,D^{(j)}(R) \label{eq:7-Mdef} \end{equation} $$

Checking the dimensions, we have $(\ell_i\times\ell_i)(\ell_i\times\ell_j)(\ell_j\times\ell_j)$, so $M$ is an $\ell_i\times\ell_j$ matrix.

Step 2: Show that $M$ satisfies Schur's condition \eqref{eq:7-schur2}. Take an arbitrary element $A$ of the group and compute $D^{(i)}(A)MD^{(j)}(A)^{-1}$. Substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:7-Mdef}, we obtain

$$ D^{(i)}(A)\,M\,D^{(j)}(A)^{-1} = \sum_R D^{(i)}(A)D^{(i)}(R)^{-1}\,X\,D^{(j)}(R)D^{(j)}(A)^{-1} $$

Using the properties of a representation, $D(A)D(R)^{-1}=D(A)D(R^{-1})=D(AR^{-1})$ and $D(R)D(A)^{-1}=D(RA^{-1})$, we get

$$ = \sum_R D^{(i)}\!\left(AR^{-1}\right)X\,D^{(j)}\!\left(RA^{-1}\right) $$

Now we change variables. Letting $S\equiv RA^{-1}$, we have $R=SA$, and

$$ AR^{-1} = A(SA)^{-1} = A\,A^{-1}S^{-1} = S^{-1} $$

With $A$ fixed, as $R$ runs over the whole group, $S=RA^{-1}$ also runs over the whole group exactly once each (rearrangement theorem). Therefore, rewriting the summation index as $S$,

$$ = \sum_{S\in G} D^{(i)}\!\left(S^{-1}\right)X\,D^{(j)}(S) = \sum_S D^{(i)}(S)^{-1}X D^{(j)}(S) = M $$

Multiplying by $D^{(j)}(A)$ from the right, we obtain

$$ D^{(i)}(A)\,M = M\,D^{(j)}(A)\qquad(\text{for all }A\in G) $$

This is precisely Eq. \eqref{eq:7-schur2}. Note that it holds whatever $X$ is.

Step 3: Choice of $X$. As $X$, take the matrix whose $(\mu,\alpha)$ component alone is $1$ and whose other components are all $0$:

$$ X_{\lambda\sigma} = \delta_{\lambda\mu}\,\delta_{\sigma\alpha} $$

We now compute the $(\nu,\beta)$ component of $M$. Writing Eq. \eqref{eq:7-Mdef} in components,

$$ M_{\nu\beta} = \sum_R \sum_{\lambda,\sigma} \left[D^{(i)}(R)^{-1}\right]_{\nu\lambda} X_{\lambda\sigma}\left[D^{(j)}(R)\right]_{\sigma\beta} $$

Thanks to $X$, only the term with $\lambda=\mu$, $\sigma=\alpha$ survives:

$$ M_{\nu\beta} = \sum_R \left[D^{(i)}(R)^{-1}\right]_{\nu\mu}\,D^{(j)}(R)_{\alpha\beta} $$

Here we use unitarity. Since $\left[D^{-1}\right]_{\nu\mu}=\left[D^\dagger\right]_{\nu\mu}=D^{*}_{\mu\nu}$ (note that the subscripts are interchanged),

$$ \begin{equation} M_{\nu\beta} = \sum_{R\in G} D^{(i)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\nu}\,D^{(j)}(R)_{\alpha\beta} \label{eq:7-Mcomp} \end{equation} $$

The right-hand side is precisely the left-hand side of the great orthogonality theorem. It remains only to determine $M_{\nu\beta}$ by Schur's lemma.

Step 4: The case $i\neq j$ (inequivalent). By Step 2, $M$ satisfies the condition of Schur's second lemma. Since the representations are inequivalent, Theorem 7.5 gives $M=0$, that is, every component is zero:

$$ \sum_R D^{(i)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\nu}D^{(j)}(R)_{\alpha\beta} = 0 $$

This accounts for the $\delta_{ij}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-got}.

Step 5: The case $i=j$. In this case Eq. \eqref{eq:7-Mdef} becomes $M=\sum_R D^{(i)}(R)^{-1}XD^{(i)}(R)$, and the conclusion of Step 2 is $D^{(i)}(A)M=MD^{(i)}(A)$; that is, $M$ commutes with the irreducible representation $D^{(i)}$. By Schur's first lemma (Theorem 7.4),

$$ M = c\,I_{\ell_i}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad M_{\nu\beta} = c\,\delta_{\nu\beta} $$

This yields the $\delta_{\nu\beta}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-got}. Since the constant $c$ depends on the choice of $X$, we write $c_{\mu\alpha}$ for its value for the $X$ we have just chosen (the one with $1$ only in the $(\mu,\alpha)$ component).

Step 6: Determine $c_{\mu\alpha}$ from the trace. This is where $h/\ell_i$ comes from. We compute the trace of $M=c_{\mu\alpha}I_{\ell_i}$ in two ways and compare.

First, from the right-hand side:

$$ \Tr M = \Tr\left(c_{\mu\alpha}I_{\ell_i}\right) = c_{\mu\alpha}\,\ell_i $$

Next, from the definition \eqref{eq:7-Mdef}:

$$ \Tr M = \sum_R \Tr\left[D^{(i)}(R)^{-1}X D^{(i)}(R)\right] $$

By the cyclic property of the trace, $\Tr(ABC)=\Tr(CAB)$, for each term we have

$$ \Tr\left[D^{(i)}(R)^{-1}XD^{(i)}(R)\right] = \Tr\left[D^{(i)}(R)D^{(i)}(R)^{-1}X\right] = \Tr\left[I\,X\right] = \Tr X $$

Since this value does not depend on $R$, all $h$ terms take the same value, and

$$ \Tr M = h\,\Tr X $$

Now $X$ has $1$ only in the $(\mu,\alpha)$ component, so the sum of its diagonal elements is $1$ only when $\mu=\alpha$ and $0$ otherwise:

$$ \Tr X = \sum_\lambda X_{\lambda\lambda} = \delta_{\mu\alpha} $$

Therefore

$$ c_{\mu\alpha}\,\ell_i = h\,\delta_{\mu\alpha} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad c_{\mu\alpha} = \frac{h}{\ell_i}\,\delta_{\mu\alpha} $$

Step 7: Putting it together. Substituting Steps 5 and 6 into Eq. \eqref{eq:7-Mcomp}, we obtain

$$ \sum_R D^{(i)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\nu}D^{(i)}(R)_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{h}{\ell_i}\delta_{\mu\alpha}\delta_{\nu\beta} $$

Combining this with the result of Step 4, we obtain Eq. \eqref{eq:7-got}. ∎

Physical Meaning: This is an “orthogonality relation of vectors”

If we regard the elements $R$ of the group as labels numbered from $1$ to $h$, then $D^{(i)}(R)_{\mu\nu}$ can be viewed as a list of $h$ numbers obtained by letting $R$ vary, that is, as an $h$-dimensional vector. Let us write this as

$$ \bm{V}^{(i)}_{\mu\nu} \equiv \Big(D^{(i)}(R_1)_{\mu\nu},\ D^{(i)}(R_2)_{\mu\nu},\ \dots,\ D^{(i)}(R_h)_{\mu\nu}\Big) $$

Then the left-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-got} is precisely the (complex) inner product of $\bm{V}^{(i)}_{\mu\nu}$ and $\bm{V}^{(j)}_{\alpha\beta}$. The theorem can be read as follows.

  • If the representations differ ($i\neq j$), the inner product is zero.
  • Even for the same representation, if the position of the matrix element differs ($(\mu,\nu)\neq(\alpha,\beta)$), the inner product is zero.
  • The inner product with itself (the square of the norm) is $h/\ell_i$.

In other words, all the matrix elements of all the irreducible representations form a set of mutually orthogonal vectors in an $h$-dimensional space. This is the origin of the name “great orthogonality theorem,” and, as we will see in Section 7.10, the inequality $\sum_i\ell_i^2\le h$ follows immediately from this geometry (to state that equality holds, that is, the dimension theorem, the discussion of the regular representation in Section 7.9 is needed).

7.9 The Dimension Theorem $\sum_i \ell_i^2 = h$

We now harvest the first fruit of the great orthogonality theorem. The dimension of an irreducible representation cannot take an arbitrary value: a strong constraint holds, namely that the sum of the squares of the dimensions is exactly equal to the order of the group.

7.9.1 The orthogonality theorem for characters (a corollary of the great orthogonality theorem)

Derivation: From orthogonality of matrix elements to orthogonality of characters

In the great orthogonality theorem \eqref{eq:7-got}, set $\nu=\mu$ and $\beta=\alpha$ (we look only at the diagonal elements).

$$ \sum_R D^{(i)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\mu}D^{(j)}(R)_{\alpha\alpha} = \frac{h}{\ell_i}\delta_{ij}\delta_{\mu\alpha}\delta_{\mu\alpha} $$

Next we sum over $\mu$ from $1$ to $\ell_i$ and over $\alpha$ from $1$ to $\ell_j$. The left-hand side becomes

$$ \sum_R \left(\sum_\mu D^{(i)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\mu}\right)\left(\sum_\alpha D^{(j)}(R)_{\alpha\alpha}\right) = \sum_R \chi^{(i)}(R)^{*}\chi^{(j)}(R) $$

(this is just the definition of the trace). For the right-hand side, noting that $\delta_{\mu\alpha}\delta_{\mu\alpha}=\delta_{\mu\alpha}$, we obtain

$$ \frac{h}{\ell_i}\delta_{ij}\sum_{\mu,\alpha}\delta_{\mu\alpha} = \frac{h}{\ell_i}\delta_{ij}\cdot\ell_i = h\,\delta_{ij} $$

(The expression is meaningful only for $i=j$, and then $\sum_{\mu\alpha}\delta_{\mu\alpha}=\ell_i$.) Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \sum_{R\in G}\chi^{(i)}(R)^{*}\,\chi^{(j)}(R) = h\,\delta_{ij} \label{eq:7-charorth} \end{equation} $$

∎ This is the orthogonality theorem for characters. It is beautiful that $\ell_i$ cancels completely and only $h$ remains. Chapter 8 starts from this equation.

Derivation: The reduction formula

When a reducible representation $\Gamma$ contains the irreducible representations as $\Gamma=\bigoplus_i a_i\Gamma^{(i)}$, its character is $\chi(R)=\sum_i a_i\chi^{(i)}(R)$ (Section 7.4.1). Multiplying both sides by $\chi^{(j)}(R)^{*}$ and summing over $R$, we obtain

$$ \sum_R \chi^{(j)}(R)^{*}\chi(R) = \sum_i a_i\sum_R\chi^{(j)}(R)^{*}\chi^{(i)}(R) = \sum_i a_i\,h\,\delta_{ij} = h\,a_j $$

Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} a_j = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{R\in G}\chi^{(j)}(R)^{*}\,\chi(R) \label{eq:7-reduction} \end{equation} $$

∎ As long as the characters are known, how many times each irreducible representation is contained is determined by a single division. This is the formula we will use most often from Chapter 8 onward.

7.9.2 Reducing the regular representation

Theorem 7.7 (dimension theorem)

Let $\ell_1,\ell_2,\dots$ be the dimensions of the mutually inequivalent irreducible representations of a group $G$ (of order $h$). Then

$$ \begin{equation} \sum_i \ell_i^2 = h \label{eq:7-dim} \end{equation} $$

Proof: Applying the reduction formula to the regular representation

By Theorem 7.1, the character of the regular representation $D^{\rm reg}$ (of dimension $h$) is $\chi^{\rm reg}(E)=h$, and $\chi^{\rm reg}(R)=0$ for $R\neq E$.

This extreme form is what makes the argument work. In the sum of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-reduction}, only the term $R=E$ survives:

$$ a_i = \frac{1}{h}\sum_R \chi^{(i)}(R)^{*}\chi^{\rm reg}(R) = \frac{1}{h}\,\chi^{(i)}(E)^{*}\cdot h = \chi^{(i)}(E) $$

However, $\chi^{(i)}(E)=\Tr D^{(i)}(E)=\Tr I_{\ell_i}=\ell_i$. That is,

$$ \begin{equation} a_i = \ell_i \label{eq:7-regmult} \end{equation} $$

The regular representation contains each irreducible representation exactly as many times as its dimension. This is itself an important conclusion.

Next we write down the count of dimensions. Since the dimension of a reducible representation is the sum of the dimensions of its irreducible components,

$$ h = \dim D^{\rm reg} = \sum_i a_i\,\ell_i = \sum_i \ell_i\cdot\ell_i = \sum_i \ell_i^2 $$

∎ The same result also follows by evaluating the characters at $R=E$: it suffices to equate $\chi^{\rm reg}(E)=h$ with $\sum_i a_i\chi^{(i)}(E)=\sum_i\ell_i^2$.

7.9.3 Checking with concrete examples

Example 7.9: Checking the dimension theorem for $C_{3v}$, $C_{4v}$, and $O_h$

(a) $C_{3v}$ ($h=6$). There are three irreducible representations: $A_1$ (one-dimensional), $A_2$ (one-dimensional), and $E$ (two-dimensional).

$$ 1^2+1^2+2^2 = 1+1+4 = 6 = h\quad\checkmark $$

(b) $C_{4v}$ ($h=8$). There are five conjugacy classes: $\{E\}$, $\{C_4,C_4^3\}$, $\{C_2\}$, $\{\sigma_v,\sigma_v'\}$, and $\{\sigma_d,\sigma_d'\}$. As we show in Chapter 8, the number of irreducible representations equals the number of conjugacy classes, so there are five irreducible representations. Letting their dimensions be $\ell_1,\dots,\ell_5$, we have

$$ \ell_1^2+\ell_2^2+\ell_3^2+\ell_4^2+\ell_5^2 = 8 $$

Each $\ell_i$ is an integer at least 1, and because the identity representation exists, at least one of them is $\ell=1$. Searching for combinations of five square numbers that sum to 8, we find only $1+1+1+1+4$ ($1+1+1+1+1=5$ is not enough, and using two $4$s gives $4+4+1+1+1=11$, which overshoots). Therefore

$$ (\ell_i) = (1,1,1,1,2)\quad\checkmark $$

That is, $C_{4v}$ has four one-dimensional irreducible representations and one two-dimensional irreducible representation ($A_1, A_2, B_1, B_2, E$). Before we construct the character table, the dimensions alone are already fixed.

(c) $O_h$ ($h=48$). From the direct-product structure $O_h = O\times\{E,i\}$, each irreducible representation of $O$ splits into two, $g$ (even) and $u$ (odd). First, for $O$ ($h=24$, with five conjugacy classes $E, 8C_3, 3C_2, 6C_4, 6C_2'$) we look for combinations satisfying

$$ \sum \ell^2 = 24,\qquad 5\ \text{terms},\qquad \ell_1=1 $$

First, if $\ell\ge5$ then $\ell^2\ge25>24$, so even a single such term overshoots; hence $\ell$ is limited to $1,2,3,4$. Letting $n_1,n_2,n_3,n_4$ be the numbers of irreducible representations of dimension $1,2,3,4$, respectively, we have

$$ n_1+n_2+n_3+n_4 = 5,\qquad n_1+4n_2+9n_3+16n_4 = 24 $$

Subtracting the first equation from the second gives $3n_2+8n_3+15n_4=19$. Since every term on the left-hand side is nonnegative, $15n_4\le19$ gives $n_4\le1$, and $8n_3\le19$ gives $n_3\le2$. If $n_4=1$, then $3n_2+8n_3=4$, which has no nonnegative integer solution. If $n_4=0$, then for $n_3=2$ we are left with $3n_2=3$, that is, $n_2=1$; for $n_3=1$ we get $3n_2=11$, and for $n_3=0$ we get $3n_2=19$, neither of which is an integer. Hence $(n_1,n_2,n_3,n_4)=(2,1,2,0)$, that is, $1+1+4+9+9=24$, is the only solution. Therefore the irreducible representations of $O$ have dimensions $(1,1,2,3,3)$, namely $A_1, A_2, E, T_1, T_2$. In $O_h$ these are doubled by the $g/u$ labels, so

$$ \sum_i\ell_i^2 = 2\times(1+1+4+9+9) = 2\times24 = 48 = h\quad\checkmark $$

∎ The ten irreducible representations are $A_{1g},A_{2g},E_g,T_{1g},T_{2g},A_{1u},A_{2u},E_u,T_{1u},T_{2u}$. The existence of a $3$-dimensional irreducible representation is the group-theoretical basis for the fact that threefold degeneracy can occur in a field of cubic symmetry. When we calculate in Chapter 10 how the $d$ orbitals split into $E_g\oplus T_{2g}$, these $2$ and $3$ will appear directly as the degeneracies.

Physical Meaning: Degeneracy cannot take arbitrary values

The dimension theorem severely restricts “the degeneracies allowed under a given symmetry.” In a crystal field of $O_h$ symmetry, the dimensions of the irreducible representations are only $1,2,3$, so apart from accidental degeneracy, the degeneracy of an electron level can only be 1, 2, or 3. Fourfold or fivefold degeneracy does not occur in $O_h$. However, this holds only when the spin is ignored (for the orbital part alone). When the spin-orbit interaction is included, we must use the double group, which contains a four-dimensional irreducible representation, so fourfold degeneracy does in fact occur even with $O_h$ symmetry (Chapter 11).

The $d$ orbitals of a free atom are fivefold degenerate ($2\ell+1=5$ for the azimuthal quantum number $\ell=2$; here $\ell$ is the azimuthal quantum number, not the dimension of an irreducible representation), but this is an irreducible representation of the spherical symmetry group $O(3)$. When the atom is placed in a crystal and the symmetry is lowered to $O_h$, no $5$-dimensional irreducible representation exists any longer, so the level must split. This is as far as the dimension theorem can take us. How it splits is not determined by counting dimensions: since the dimensions of the irreducible representations of $O_h$ are $1,2,3$, numerically $5=1+1+3$ and $5=1+2+2$ are possible as well as $5=2+3$ (indeed, in a $D_{4h}$ field the $d$ orbitals split as $1+1+1+2$). The result $E_g\oplus T_{2g}$ is fixed only when we compute the characters of the $d$ orbitals and insert them into the reduction formula \eqref{eq:7-reduction} (Chapter 10). That crystal-field splitting occurs at all is itself a consequence of the dimension theorem.

As stated in the Physical Meaning box of Section 7.8, the great orthogonality theorem can be read as saying that “vectors are orthogonal in an $h$-dimensional space.” From this viewpoint, the dimension theorem \eqref{eq:7-dim} begins to look obvious.

Why? $\sum_i\ell_i^2\le h$ follows just by counting

How many vectors $\bm{V}^{(i)}_{\mu\nu}$ are there? For each irreducible representation $i$, the row index $\mu$ takes $\ell_i$ values and the column index $\nu$ takes $\ell_i$ values, so there are $\ell_i\times\ell_i=\ell_i^2$ vectors. Taking all the irreducible representations together,

$$ \text{number of vectors} = \sum_i \ell_i^2 $$

On the other hand, all of these are elements of an $h$-dimensional vector space, and by the great orthogonality theorem they are mutually orthogonal. An $h$-dimensional space can contain at most $h$ mutually orthogonal nonzero vectors (a basic fact of linear algebra: orthogonal nonzero vectors are linearly independent). Therefore

$$ \sum_i \ell_i^2 \ \le\ h $$

This gives the inequality. To show that equality holds—that is, that the orthogonal vectors span the $h$-dimensional space exactly—we need the argument on the regular representation in Section 7.9. The regular representation is $h$-dimensional, and the fact that the irreducible representations fill it with neither excess nor deficiency guarantees that “nothing is left over.”

Supplement: Normalization gives an orthonormal basis

If we divide the great orthogonality theorem \eqref{eq:7-got} by $h/\ell_i$, that is, consider the vectors whose components are

$$ \sqrt{\frac{\ell_i}{h}}\,D^{(i)}(R)_{\mu\nu} $$

their norm is exactly $1$. These therefore form a complete orthonormal set in the $h$-dimensional space. That the set is complete means that any quantity with $h$ components (any function of the group elements) can be expanded in terms of the matrix elements of the irreducible representations. The projection operators of Chapter 9 are nothing other than a tool for extracting these expansion coefficients.

7.11 Summary and Exercises

7.11.1 Summary

  • A representation assigns to each group element $R$ a nonsingular matrix $D(R)$ such that $D(R)D(S)=D(RS)$. From the definition, $D(E)=I$ and $D(R^{-1})=D(R)^{-1}$ follow automatically. Do not confuse the order $h$ with the dimension $\ell$.
  • If we prepare a set of functions closed under the symmetry operations, a representation is constructed automatically through $\hat{P}_R\phi_j=\sum_i\phi_i D(R)_{ij}$. In quantum mechanics, a set of degenerate eigenfunctions plays this role.
  • The identity representation is the one-dimensional representation that exists for every group. The regular representation is the $h$-dimensional representation built from the multiplication table; its character is $\chi^{\rm reg}(E)=h$ and $0$ otherwise.
  • Representations related by a similarity transformation $D'=U^{-1}DU$ are equivalent and are regarded as the same. The trace (the character) is invariant under a similarity transformation. The $(x,y,z)$ representation of $C_{3v}$ and the permutation representation of the three vertices of an equilateral triangle look entirely different but are equivalent.
  • A representation that has an invariant subspace is reducible; one that has none is irreducible. A representation of a finite group is completely reducible, and a single $U$ block-diagonalizes all the group elements simultaneously. The three-dimensional representation of $C_{3v}$ is $A_1\oplus E$.
  • Unitarity theorem: form $H=\sum_R D(R)D(R)^\dagger$, diagonalize it, and sandwich with $d^{-1/2}$; then any representation can be made unitary. From here on, $D^{-1}=D^\dagger$ may be used freely.
  • Schur's first lemma: a matrix $M$ that commutes with all the matrices of an irreducible representation must be of the form $cI$. The point is that “when $M$ is diagonalized, $(\lambda_\mu-\lambda_\nu)\tilde D_{\mu\nu}=0$ holds, and if there are two or more distinct eigenvalues the representation becomes block-diagonal, that is, reducible.” As a corollary, every irreducible representation of an abelian group is one-dimensional.
  • Schur's second lemma: if $D^{(i)}(R)M=MD^{(j)}(R)$, then either $M=0$, or $M$ is invertible and the two representations are equivalent; there is no other possibility. We form $MM^\dagger$ and reduce to the first lemma; when $M$ is not square, we pad it with zero columns and obtain $c=0$ from $\det=0$.
  • Great orthogonality theorem \eqref{eq:7-got}. If we form the group average $M=\sum_R D^{(i)}(R)^{-1}XD^{(j)}(R)$, the rearrangement theorem guarantees that Schur's condition is satisfied. Choosing $X$ to be “$1$ in a single place” extracts the desired component, and the coefficient $h/\ell_i$ comes from the cyclic property of the trace.
  • As corollaries we obtain the orthogonality theorem for characters \eqref{eq:7-charorth} and the reduction formula \eqref{eq:7-reduction}.
  • Dimension theorem $\sum_i\ell_i^2=h$. It follows from the fact that the regular representation contains each irreducible representation exactly $\ell_i$ times. For $C_{3v}$: $1+1+4=6$. For $C_{4v}$: $1+1+1+1+4=8$. For $O_h$: $2(1+1+4+9+9)=48$.
  • Geometrically, the matrix elements of the irreducible representations are orthogonal vectors in an $h$-dimensional space, and their number $\sum_i\ell_i^2$ coincides exactly with the dimension $h$ of that space. Since the dimension of an irreducible representation sets the upper limit of the degeneracy, if we ignore spin and exclude accidental degeneracies, no degeneracy of fourfold or higher occurs in $O_h$.

7.11.2 Exercises

Exercise 7.1: Checking the great orthogonality theorem for $C_3$

For the three one-dimensional irreducible representations $\Gamma^{(1)},\Gamma^{(2)},\Gamma^{(3)}$ of $C_3$ obtained in Example 7.7, verify Eq. \eqref{eq:7-got} directly. That is:

(1) For each representation, verify that $\sum_R |D^{(i)}(R)|^2 = h/\ell_i = 3$.

(2) For two different representations, verify that $\sum_R D^{(i)}(R)^{*}D^{(j)}(R)=0$.

Hint: (1) $|1|^2+|\omega|^2+|\omega^2|^2=1+1+1=3$ (note that $|\omega|=1$). (2) For example, for $i=1, j=2$ we have $1^{*}\cdot1+1^{*}\cdot\omega+1^{*}\cdot\omega^2 = 1+\omega+\omega^2 = 0$. For $i=2, j=3$ the sum is $1^{*}\cdot1+\omega^{*}\omega^2+(\omega^2)^{*}\omega^4$. Using $\omega^{*}=\omega^{-1}=\omega^2$ and $\omega^3=1$, the second term is $\omega^2\cdot\omega^2=\omega^4=\omega$ and the third term is $\omega\cdot\omega=\omega^2$. The total reduces to $1+\omega+\omega^2=0$.

Exercise 7.2: For a unitary representation the orthogonal complement is also invariant

Let $\{D(R)\}$ be a unitary representation and $W$ an invariant subspace of it. Show that the orthogonal complement of $W$,

$$ W^\perp = \{\,v\ \mid\ \langle w, v\rangle = 0\ \ \text{for all}\ w\in W\,\} $$

is also an invariant subspace. Use this to explain how the “block-triangular” form described in Section 7.4.1 is upgraded to “block-diagonal.”

Hint: Let $v\in W^\perp$ and evaluate $\langle w, D(R)v\rangle$ for an arbitrary $w\in W$. By unitarity, $\langle w, D(R)v\rangle = \langle D(R)^\dagger w, v\rangle = \langle D(R^{-1})w, v\rangle$. Since $W$ is invariant, $D(R^{-1})w\in W$, and because $v\in W^\perp$ this inner product vanishes. Hence $D(R)v\in W^\perp$.

Exercise 7.3: Making a non-unitary representation unitary

Consider the two-dimensional representation of the group $C_2=\{E,A\}$ of order 2 ($A^2=E$) given by

$$ D(E)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad D(A)=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix} $$

.

(1) Verify that $D(A)^2=I$ and show that this is indeed a representation.

(2) Show that $D(A)$ is not unitary.

(3) Compute $H=\sum_R D(R)D(R)^\dagger$ and verify that it is Hermitian and positive definite.

(4) To which matrix is the $D'(A)$ obtained by the procedure of Theorem 7.3 equivalent? Predict it from the characters.

Hint: (1) $\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}^2=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1+1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=I$. (3) $H=I+\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=I+\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. The eigenvalues follow from $\lambda^2-5\lambda+5=0$ as $\lambda=(5\pm\sqrt5)/2>0$. (4) Since $\Tr D(A)=0$ and $D(A)^2=I$, the eigenvalues are $+1$ and $-1$. Hence $D'(A)\sim\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$, which is unitary.

Exercise 7.4: Completing Table 7.3

Exercise 7.5: Determining the dimensions of irreducible representations from the dimension theorem

For each of the following groups, determine the set of dimensions of the irreducible representations from the number of conjugacy classes and the order (you may use the fact that the number of irreducible representations equals the number of conjugacy classes).

(1) $C_{2v}$: $h=4$, four conjugacy classes $\{E\},\{C_2\},\{\sigma_v\},\{\sigma_v'\}$

(2) isomorphic to $D_3=C_{3v}$: $h=6$, three conjugacy classes

(3) $T_d$: $h=24$, five conjugacy classes $E, 8C_3, 3C_2, 6S_4, 6\sigma_d$

(4) $D_{6h}$: $h=24$, 12 conjugacy classes

Hint: (1) Four squares summing to 4 → $1+1+1+1$. All are one-dimensional (consistent with Corollary 7.1, since the group is commutative). (2) Three squares summing to 6 → $1+1+4$. (3) Five squares summing to 24 → $1+1+4+9+9$ (the same as for $O$). (4) Twelve squares summing to 24 → eight $1$s and four $4$s, that is, $1,1,1,1,1,1,1,1,2,2,2,2$.

Exercise 7.6: Evaluating a group average using Schur's lemma

For an irreducible representation $D^{(i)}$ (its dimension $\ell_i$ may be $1$), consider the group average

$$ \bar{D}\equiv\frac{1}{h}\sum_{R\in G}D^{(i)}(R) $$

.

(1) Using the rearrangement theorem, show that $D^{(i)}(A)\bar{D}=\bar{D}$ holds for every $A\in G$.

(2) From (1), deduce that $\bar{D}$ commutes with all the matrices of $D^{(i)}$, and apply Schur's first lemma to conclude $\bar{D}=cI$.

(3) Determine $c$, and show that for an irreducible representation other than the identity representation $\bar{D}=0$, that is,

$$ \sum_{R\in G}D^{(i)}(R)_{\mu\nu}=0\qquad(\Gamma^{(i)}\neq\text{identity representation}) $$

. It is helpful to treat separately the case $\ell_i\ge2$ and the case $\ell_i=1$ with a non-identity representation.

Hint: (1) $D^{(i)}(A)\bar D = \frac1h\sum_R D^{(i)}(AR)$, and $AR$ runs through the whole group exactly once. (2) Similarly one can show $\bar{D}D^{(i)}(A)=\bar D$, so $D^{(i)}(A)\bar D = \bar D D^{(i)}(A)$. (3) Substituting $\bar D = cI$ into (1) gives $cD^{(i)}(A)=cI$. For an irreducible representation with $\ell_i\ge2$ there is always an $A$ with $D^{(i)}(A)\neq I$, so $c=0$. Likewise $c=0$ for a one-dimensional non-identity representation. Note that if $\Gamma^{(i)}$ is the identity representation, $\bar D=1$. This result is the special case of the great orthogonality theorem \eqref{eq:7-got} in which $j$ is taken to be the identity representation.
