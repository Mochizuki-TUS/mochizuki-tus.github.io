Chapter 3Reciprocal Lattice, Brillouin Zone, and X-Ray Diffraction

The periodicity of a crystal is expressed in real space by the relation $f(\rr + \TT) = f(\rr)$. If we simply expand this relation in a Fourier series, a “lattice of wavenumbers” on which the expansion coefficients sit appears automatically. This is the reciprocal lattice.

The reciprocal lattice is not merely a mathematical convenience. What we see directly in experiments is not the real-space lattice but the reciprocal lattice. What X-ray diffraction measures is the positions of reciprocal lattice points, the horizontal axis of a band diagram is a path through reciprocal space, and the phonon dispersion relation is also drawn in reciprocal space. This is why it is said in solid-state physics that “thinking in reciprocal space is the iron rule” (Mochizuki Research Group materials).

In this chapter we derive the reciprocal lattice from the Fourier expansion, show that Bragg's law and the Laue condition have the same content, and follow how the structure factor determines the peak heights. We then complete, as a proof, the claim announced in Chapter 2 that “the peak positions coincide if the Bravais lattice is the same.”

What you will learn in this chapter
  • That the reciprocal lattice vectors $\GG$ necessarily appear from the Fourier expansion of a periodic function, and the derivation of $\bb_i = 2\pi\dfrac{\aa_j\times\aa_k}{V_{\text{p}}}$
  • The duality relation $\aa_i\cdot\bb_j = 2\pi\delta_{ij}$ and $e^{i\GG\cdot\TT}=1$
  • A proof that Bragg's law $2d\sin\theta = n\lambda$ and the Laue condition $\Delta\kk = \GG$ are equivalent
  • A geometric understanding of the diffraction condition by means of the Ewald sphere
  • The interplanar spacing $d_{hkl} = 2\pi/\abs{\GG_{hkl}}$, and $d = a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$ for cubic crystals
  • The structure factor $S_{\GG}$ and systematic absences — why peaks with $h+k+l$ odd vanish for bcc, and peaks with mixed even and odd $hkl$ vanish for fcc
  • A proof that the peak positions are determined by the Bravais lattice alone, and the basis affects only the peak heights
  • Construction of the first Brillouin zone, and the high-symmetry points of fcc (truncated octahedron) and bcc (rhombic dodecahedron)
Prerequisites: Chapters 1 and 2. Fourier series (Appendix A, Section A.1), the cross product of vectors and the scalar triple product, complex exponential functions.

3.1 From the Fourier Expansion of a Periodic Function to the Reciprocal Lattice

3.1.1 Starting point

As we saw in Chapter 1, any physical quantity in a crystal, for example the electron density $n(\rr)$, is invariant under lattice translations:

$$ \begin{equation} n(\rr + \TT) = n(\rr), \qquad \TT = n_1\aa_1 + n_2\aa_2 + n_3\aa_3 \label{eq:3-periodic} \end{equation} $$

A periodic function can be expanded in a Fourier series. In three dimensions we can write

$$ \begin{equation} n(\rr) = \sum_{\GG} n_{\GG}\, e^{i\GG\cdot\rr} \label{eq:3-fourier} \end{equation} $$

Here the sum runs over “some set $\{\GG\}$ of wave vectors,” whose identity is not yet known. The purpose of this section is to determine what this set is from the condition of periodicity.

Derivation: The condition $e^{i\GG\cdot\TT} = 1$ for reciprocal lattice vectors

Substitute Eq. \eqref{eq:3-fourier} into Eq. \eqref{eq:3-periodic}. The left-hand side is

$$ n(\rr+\TT) = \sum_{\GG} n_{\GG}\, e^{i\GG\cdot(\rr+\TT)} = \sum_{\GG} n_{\GG}\, e^{i\GG\cdot\rr}\, e^{i\GG\cdot\TT} $$

This must be equal to the right-hand side $\sum_{\GG} n_{\GG} e^{i\GG\cdot\rr}$. Since the functions $e^{i\GG\cdot\rr}$ are linearly independent of one another, comparing coefficients gives

$$ n_{\GG}\, e^{i\GG\cdot\TT} = n_{\GG} $$

For those $\GG$ with $n_{\GG}\neq0$,

$$ \begin{equation} e^{i\GG\cdot\TT} = 1 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \GG\cdot\TT = 2\pi m \quad (m \in \mathbb{Z}) \label{eq:3-G-condition} \end{equation} $$

is required. ∎

This is the definition of the reciprocal lattice. That is, the set of all $\GG$ for which $\GG\cdot\TT$ is an integer multiple of $2\pi$ for all lattice translation vectors $\TT$ is called the reciprocal lattice.

3.1.2 Construction of the primitive reciprocal lattice vectors

Let us construct explicitly the $\GG$ that satisfy condition \eqref{eq:3-G-condition}.

Derivation: $\bb_1 = 2\pi\dfrac{\aa_2\times\aa_3}{\aa_1\cdot(\aa_2\times\aa_3)}$

We look for primitive vectors $\bb_i$ such that we can write $\GG = h\bb_1 + k\bb_2 + l\bb_3$ ($h,k,l$ integers). The inner product with $\TT = n_1\aa_1+n_2\aa_2+n_3\aa_3$ is

$$ \GG\cdot\TT = \sum_{i,j} (\text{integer})_i (\text{integer})_j\, \bb_i\cdot\aa_j $$

The simplest sufficient condition for this to be $2\pi\times$ an integer for any choice of integers is

$$ \begin{equation} \aa_i\cdot\bb_j = 2\pi\,\delta_{ij} \label{eq:3-dual} \end{equation} $$

(where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta). Indeed, in this case $\GG\cdot\TT = 2\pi(hn_1 + kn_2 + ln_3)$, which is certainly $2\pi\times$ an integer.

Constructing $\bb_1$. Equation \eqref{eq:3-dual} requires that $\bb_1$ be orthogonal to both $\aa_2$ and $\aa_3$ ($\aa_2\cdot\bb_1 = \aa_3\cdot\bb_1 = 0$). A vector orthogonal to two vectors can be built from the cross product, so we can put

$$ \bb_1 = C\,(\aa_2\times\aa_3) $$

From the remaining condition $\aa_1\cdot\bb_1 = 2\pi$ we obtain

$$ C\,\aa_1\cdot(\aa_2\times\aa_3) = 2\pi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C = \frac{2\pi}{\aa_1\cdot(\aa_2\times\aa_3)} = \frac{2\pi}{V_{\text{p}}} $$

Hence

$$ \begin{equation} \bb_1 = 2\pi\frac{\aa_2\times\aa_3}{V_{\text{p}}},\qquad \bb_2 = 2\pi\frac{\aa_3\times\aa_1}{V_{\text{p}}},\qquad \bb_3 = 2\pi\frac{\aa_1\times\aa_2}{V_{\text{p}}} \label{eq:3-b-vectors} \end{equation} $$

Here $V_{\text{p}} = \aa_1\cdot(\aa_2\times\aa_3)$ is the volume of the primitive cell. ∎

Math Note: Conventions with and without the factor $2\pi$

In crystallography (X-ray diffraction), the definition without $2\pi$, $\aa_i\cdot\bb_j = \delta_{ij}$, is often used. In physics (solid-state physics), $2\pi$ is included, as in Eq. \eqref{eq:3-dual}.

Physics includes $2\pi$ because we want to write plane waves as $e^{i\kk\cdot\rr}$. Crystallography omits it because it wants the compact forms $\abs{\GG_{hkl}} = 1/d_{hkl}$ for the relation to the interplanar spacing and $1/\lambda$ for the radius of the Ewald sphere. Since this book is a textbook of solid-state physics, we consistently use the convention with $2\pi$. When consulting other books, it is a good habit to check first which convention they use.

3.2 Properties of Reciprocal Lattice Vectors

3.2.1 Reciprocal lattice vectors are perpendicular to planes

Theorem 3.1

The reciprocal lattice vector $\GG_{hkl} = h\bb_1 + k\bb_2 + l\bb_3$ is perpendicular to the plane with Miller indices $(hkl)$. Moreover, the interplanar spacing is

$$ \begin{equation} d_{hkl} = \frac{2\pi}{\abs{\GG_{hkl}}} \label{eq:3-dspacing} \end{equation} $$

Derivation

Step 1: Perpendicularity. The $(hkl)$ plane cuts the axes at $\aa_1/h$, $\aa_2/k$, $\aa_3/l$ (the definition in Section 1.8 of Chapter 1). Consider the two vectors in this plane

$$ \bm{u} = \frac{\aa_1}{h} - \frac{\aa_2}{k}, \qquad \bm{v} = \frac{\aa_2}{k} - \frac{\aa_3}{l} $$

Both lie in the plane (since each is a vector connecting two points of the plane). We compute their inner products with $\GG_{hkl}$ using Eq. \eqref{eq:3-dual}:

$$ \GG_{hkl}\cdot\bm{u} = (h\bb_1+k\bb_2+l\bb_3)\cdot\left(\frac{\aa_1}{h}-\frac{\aa_2}{k}\right) = h\cdot\frac{2\pi}{h} - k\cdot\frac{2\pi}{k} = 2\pi - 2\pi = 0 $$

Similarly, $\GG_{hkl}\cdot\bm{v} = 2\pi - 2\pi = 0$. Since $\bm{u}, \bm{v}$ are linearly independent within the plane, $\GG_{hkl}$ is perpendicular to the plane.

Step 2: Interplanar spacing. We find the distance between the plane through the origin (the type $hx+ky+lz=0$) and the next plane (the one that cuts the axis at $\aa_1/h$). The unit vector in the direction of $\GG_{hkl}$ is $\hat{\bm{n}} = \GG_{hkl}/\abs{\GG_{hkl}}$. Projecting a point $\aa_1/h$ on the plane from the origin onto this direction gives the distance:

$$ d_{hkl} = \left| \frac{\aa_1}{h}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}} \right| = \left| \frac{\aa_1}{h}\cdot\frac{h\bb_1+k\bb_2+l\bb_3}{\abs{\GG_{hkl}}} \right| = \left| \frac{1}{h}\cdot\frac{2\pi h}{\abs{\GG_{hkl}}} \right| = \frac{2\pi}{\abs{\GG_{hkl}}} $$

∎

Example 3.1: Interplanar spacing of a cubic crystal

Find the primitive reciprocal lattice vectors of the simple cubic lattice ($\aa_1 = a\hat{x}$, etc.) and derive $d_{hkl}$.

Solution. Since $V_{\text{p}} = a^3$ and $\aa_2\times\aa_3 = a\hat{y}\times a\hat{z} = a^2\hat{x}$,

$$ \bb_1 = 2\pi\frac{a^2\hat{x}}{a^3} = \frac{2\pi}{a}\hat{x} $$

Similarly, $\bb_2 = \frac{2\pi}{a}\hat{y}$ and $\bb_3 = \frac{2\pi}{a}\hat{z}$. That is, the reciprocal lattice of a simple cubic lattice is a simple cubic lattice with lattice constant $2\pi/a$. Therefore

$$ \GG_{hkl} = \frac{2\pi}{a}(h,k,l), \qquad \abs{\GG_{hkl}} = \frac{2\pi}{a}\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2} $$ $$ d_{hkl} = \frac{2\pi}{\abs{\GG_{hkl}}} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}} $$

This agrees with the formula derived geometrically in Exercise 1.5 of Chapter 1. ∎

3.2.2 The reciprocal of the reciprocal lattice is the original lattice

Example 3.2: Volume of the reciprocal lattice cell

Show that the volume $V_{\text{p}}^*$ of the primitive cell of the reciprocal lattice equals $(2\pi)^3/V_{\text{p}}$.

Solution. We compute $V_{\text{p}}^* = \bb_1\cdot(\bb_2\times\bb_3)$. Substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:3-b-vectors} gives

$$ \bb_2\times\bb_3 = \frac{(2\pi)^2}{V_{\text{p}}^2}(\aa_3\times\aa_1)\times(\aa_1\times\aa_2) $$

The vector triple product formula $(\bm{A}\times\bm{B})\times\bm{C} = (\bm{A}\cdot\bm{C})\bm{B} - (\bm{B}\cdot\bm{C})\bm{A}$ is to be used in the form with $\bm{A}=\aa_3\times\aa_1$, so we use the other form $\bm{P}\times(\bm{Q}\times\bm{R}) = \bm{Q}(\bm{P}\cdot\bm{R}) - \bm{R}(\bm{P}\cdot\bm{Q})$. With $\bm{P} = \aa_3\times\aa_1$, $\bm{Q}=\aa_1$, $\bm{R}=\aa_2$,

$$ (\aa_3\times\aa_1)\times(\aa_1\times\aa_2) = \aa_1\bigl[(\aa_3\times\aa_1)\cdot\aa_2\bigr] - \aa_2\bigl[(\aa_3\times\aa_1)\cdot\aa_1\bigr] $$

The second term vanishes because $(\aa_3\times\aa_1)\perp\aa_1$. The bracket in the first term is a scalar triple product: $(\aa_3\times\aa_1)\cdot\aa_2 = \aa_1\cdot(\aa_2\times\aa_3) = V_{\text{p}}$. Hence

$$ \bb_2\times\bb_3 = \frac{(2\pi)^2}{V_{\text{p}}^2}\cdot V_{\text{p}}\,\aa_1 = \frac{(2\pi)^2}{V_{\text{p}}}\aa_1 $$

Therefore

$$ V_{\text{p}}^* = \bb_1\cdot\frac{(2\pi)^2}{V_{\text{p}}}\aa_1 = \frac{(2\pi)^2}{V_{\text{p}}}\cdot 2\pi = \frac{(2\pi)^3}{V_{\text{p}}} $$

∎ We will use this result repeatedly when counting the density of states in Chapters 14 and 15.

This example also gives the fact stated in the section heading, “the reciprocal of the reciprocal lattice is the original lattice.” If we regard the reciprocal lattice as a real lattice and construct its reciprocal lattice once more, its first primitive vector is $2\pi(\bb_2\times\bb_3)/V_{\text{p}}^*$. Substituting the $\bb_2\times\bb_3 = \frac{(2\pi)^2}{V_{\text{p}}}\aa_1$ obtained above and $V_{\text{p}}^* = (2\pi)^3/V_{\text{p}}$, we obtain

$$ 2\pi\,\frac{\bb_2\times\bb_3}{V_{\text{p}}^*} = 2\pi\cdot\frac{(2\pi)^2\aa_1/V_{\text{p}}}{(2\pi)^3/V_{\text{p}}} = \aa_1 $$

and exactly the same holds for $\aa_2$ and $\aa_3$. That is, applying the reciprocal lattice transformation twice returns the original lattice. The fact that fcc and bcc are reciprocal lattices of each other in Table 3.1 below is a manifestation of this involutive property.

3.3 Bragg's Law

We now turn to diffraction. We begin with Bragg's law, familiar even from high-school physics, and in the next section we see how it is translated into the language of the reciprocal lattice.

Derivation: Bragg's law $2d\sin\theta = n\lambda$

Suppose that X-rays of wavelength $\lambda$ are incident on atomic planes stacked in parallel with spacing $d$, making an angle $\theta$ with the planes (in crystallography the angle of incidence is measured from the plane; note that this differs by $90^\circ$ from the optical convention of measuring the angle from the normal).

We find the path difference between the waves reflected from two adjacent planes. Comparing the light reflected at the upper plane with the light that penetrates to the lower plane and is reflected there, the extra distance traveled, counting both the entry to the lower plane and the exit from it, is

$$ \Delta = d\sin\theta + d\sin\theta = 2d\sin\theta $$

When this path difference is an integer multiple of the wavelength, the two waves are in phase and reinforce each other:

$$ \begin{equation} 2d\sin\theta = n\lambda \qquad (n = 1,2,3,\ldots) \label{eq:3-bragg} \end{equation} $$

∎

How to treat $n$ — the second-order reflection from $(100)$ is the first-order reflection from $(200)$

The $n$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:3-bragg} is not used much in practice. Instead of considering the $n$th-order reflection from the $(hkl)$ planes, the standard approach is to read it as the first-order reflection from the $(nh, nk, nl)$ planes. Indeed,

$$ 2d_{hkl}\sin\theta = n\lambda \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2\frac{d_{hkl}}{n}\sin\theta = \lambda \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2 d_{nh,nk,nl}\sin\theta = \lambda $$

(since for a cubic crystal $d_{nh,nk,nl} = a/(n\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}) = d_{hkl}/n$.)

Doing so gives a one-to-one correspondence with the Laue condition $\Delta\kk = \GG$ of the next section. Since $\GG_{200}$ is a different reciprocal lattice point from $\GG_{100}$, in the language of the reciprocal lattice $n$ never appears in the first place.

3.4 The Laue Condition and the Ewald Sphere

3.4.1 From the scattering amplitude to the Laue condition

Derivation: The Laue condition $\Delta\kk = \GG$

Let the incident X-ray be the plane wave $e^{i\kk\cdot\rr}$ and the scattered wave be $e^{i\kk'\cdot\rr}$ (since the scattering is elastic, $\abs{\kk}=\abs{\kk'}=2\pi/\lambda$). The contribution to the scattering from the electron density $n(\rr)$ at position $\rr$ carries the phase factor $e^{-i(\kk'-\kk)\cdot\rr}$. The scattering amplitude from the whole crystal is

$$ \begin{equation} F = \int_{\text{crystal}} n(\rr)\, e^{-i\Delta\kk\cdot\rr}\, \dd^3r, \qquad \Delta\kk \equiv \kk' - \kk \label{eq:3-amplitude} \end{equation} $$

That is, the scattering amplitude is the Fourier transform of the electron density.

Substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:3-fourier} here, we obtain

$$ F = \sum_{\GG} n_{\GG} \int e^{i(\GG - \Delta\kk)\cdot\rr}\, \dd^3r $$

If the crystal is sufficiently large, this integral oscillates violently and cancels out when $\GG - \Delta\kk \neq 0$, so it is almost zero. A nonzero contribution survives only when

$$ \begin{equation} \Delta\kk = \kk' - \kk = \GG \label{eq:3-laue} \end{equation} $$

holds. This is called the Laue condition. In this case $F = V\, n_{\GG}$ ($V$ is the volume of the crystal). ∎

Theorem 3.2: Bragg and Laue are equivalent

Under elastic scattering $\abs{\kk'}=\abs{\kk}$, the Laue condition $\kk'-\kk=\GG$ is equivalent to Bragg's law $2d\sin\theta=\lambda$.

Derivation

Take the square of both sides of $\kk' = \kk + \GG$. Since the scattering is elastic, $\abs{\kk'}^2 = \abs{\kk}^2$, and therefore

$$ \abs{\kk}^2 = \abs{\kk+\GG}^2 = \abs{\kk}^2 + 2\kk\cdot\GG + \abs{\GG}^2 $$ $$ \begin{equation} \Longrightarrow\quad 2\kk\cdot\GG + \abs{\GG}^2 = 0 \label{eq:3-laue2} \end{equation} $$

Now $\GG$ is perpendicular to the $(hkl)$ planes (Theorem 3.1), and we suppose that the incident wave $\kk$ makes an angle $\theta$ with the planes. Then the angle between $\kk$ and $\GG$ (the plane normal) is $90^\circ + \theta$ (choosing the direction of $\GG$ appropriately). Hence

$$ \kk\cdot\GG = \abs{\kk}\abs{\GG}\cos(90^\circ+\theta) = -\abs{\kk}\abs{\GG}\sin\theta $$

Putting this into Eq. \eqref{eq:3-laue2}, we obtain

$$ -2\abs{\kk}\abs{\GG}\sin\theta + \abs{\GG}^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2\abs{\kk}\sin\theta = \abs{\GG} $$

Substituting $\abs{\kk} = 2\pi/\lambda$ and $\abs{\GG} = 2\pi/d$ (Theorem 3.1), we find

$$ 2\cdot\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\sin\theta = \frac{2\pi}{d} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2d\sin\theta = \lambda $$

∎ Bragg's law has been obtained.

Physical Meaning: The division of roles between the two pictures

  • The Bragg picture is intuitive, and it lets us form an image of planes and reflection. It is convenient when reading powder X-ray diffraction data.
  • The Laue picture is general, and it gives the powerful image that “the diffraction spots are the reciprocal lattice points themselves.” It connects directly to single-crystal diffraction, to electron diffraction and neutron diffraction, and to the band theory of Chapter 11.

In particular, Eq. \eqref{eq:3-laue2} can be rewritten in a form that uses $\GG/2$. One point needs care here. If we simply divide Eq. \eqref{eq:3-laue2} by 4, we get $\kk\cdot(\GG/2) = -(\GG/2)^2$ with a minus sign; but if $\GG$ is a reciprocal lattice vector, then $-\GG$ is also a reciprocal lattice vector, and replacing $\GG$ by $-\GG$ does not change what the condition expresses. We therefore redirect it toward the $\kk$ side, writing $2\kk\cdot\GG = \abs{\GG}^2$, and dividing both sides by 4 gives

$$ \kk\cdot\frac{\GG}{2} = \left(\frac{\GG}{2}\right)^2 $$

This is the condition that “the component of $\kk$ along $\GG$ equals $\abs{\GG}/2$,” that is, $\kk$ lies on the perpendicular bisecting plane of $\GG$. This plane is precisely the boundary of the Brillouin zone (Section 3.7), and it is the key to understanding “why a band gap opens at the zone boundary” in Chapter 16. Diffraction and the band gap are two sides of the same conditional equation.

3.4.2 The Ewald sphere

A geometric visualization of the Laue condition is the Ewald sphere.

Construction of the Ewald sphere

  1. Draw the reciprocal lattice. Take one reciprocal lattice point as the origin $O$.
  2. Draw the incident wave vector $\kk$ so that its end point falls on $O$. Let its starting point be $C$.
  3. Draw a sphere centered at $C$ with radius $\abs{\kk} = 2\pi/\lambda$. This is the Ewald sphere.
  4. If another reciprocal lattice point $P$ lies on this spherical surface, diffraction occurs in the direction of $\kk' = \overrightarrow{CP}$. Indeed, $\kk'-\kk = \overrightarrow{CP}-\overrightarrow{CO} = \overrightarrow{OP} = \GG$.

3.5 The Structure Factor — What Determines the Peak Heights

So far we have looked only at the “lattice.” Where does the basis (the atomic arrangement within the unit cell) come in? The answer is the structure factor.

Derivation: The structure factor and the atomic form factor

We decompose the electron density into contributions from the individual unit cells. Let $\rr_j$ be the position of the $j$th atom in the unit cell, and let $n_j$ be the electron density produced by one such atom. Then

$$ n(\rr) = \sum_{\TT}\sum_{j} n_j(\rr - \TT - \rr_j) $$

We substitute this into Eq. \eqref{eq:3-amplitude}. For $\Delta\kk = \GG$,

$$ F = \sum_{\TT}\sum_j \int n_j(\rr-\TT-\rr_j)\, e^{-i\GG\cdot\rr}\,\dd^3r $$

Changing the integration variable to $\bm{\rho} = \rr - \TT - \rr_j$, we have $e^{-i\GG\cdot\rr} = e^{-i\GG\cdot\bm{\rho}}e^{-i\GG\cdot\TT}e^{-i\GG\cdot\rr_j}$, and by the definition of the reciprocal lattice $e^{-i\GG\cdot\TT}=1$. Hence

$$ F = \underbrace{\sum_{\TT} 1}_{N\ \text{cells}} \times \underbrace{\sum_j e^{-i\GG\cdot\rr_j} \int n_j(\bm{\rho})e^{-i\GG\cdot\bm{\rho}}\dd^3\rho}_{\displaystyle \equiv\, S_{\GG}} $$

Here

$$ \begin{equation} S_{\GG} = \sum_{j} f_j\, e^{-i\GG\cdot\rr_j}, \qquad f_j = \int n_j(\bm{\rho})\, e^{-i\GG\cdot\bm{\rho}}\,\dd^3\rho \label{eq:3-structure-factor} \end{equation} $$

$S_{\GG}$ is called the structure factor, and $f_j$ the atomic form factor. ∎

The diffraction intensity is $I \propto \abs{F}^2 \propto N^2\abs{S_{\GG}}^2$.

Physical Meaning: $f_j$ measures the atom's “number of electrons”

In the limit $\GG\to0$, $e^{-i\GG\cdot\bm{\rho}}\to1$, so

$$ f_j \to \int n_j(\bm{\rho})\,\dd^3\rho = Z_j \quad(\text{electron count of the scatterer; atomic number if neutral}) $$

In other words, in the limit $\GG\to0$ the atomic form factor is exactly equal to the number of electrons the scatterer has, and as $\GG$ becomes finite it decays from that value. This is why heavier elements scatter more strongly. Conversely, hydrogen ($Z=1$) is almost invisible to X-rays. The reason neutron diffraction is used when one wants to determine the positions of hydrogen is that neutrons interact with the atomic nucleus, so the dependence on $Z$ is entirely different.

The statement in the instructor's materials that “the peak height is determined by the structure factor (the number of electrons of the constituent elements and the element sites)” refers precisely to this Eq. \eqref{eq:3-structure-factor}. The $f_j$ corresponds to the “number of electrons,” and $e^{-i\GG\cdot\rr_j}$ to the “element sites.”

3.5.1 Systematic absences

Example 3.3: Structure factor and systematic absences of bcc

When the body-centered cubic lattice is described by the conventional cell (a cube), the positions of the identical atoms in the unit cell are $(0,0,0)$ and $\left(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12\right)$. Calculate $S_{\GG}$ and find for which $(hkl)$ the reflection vanishes.

Solution. Since we work with the conventional cell, $\GG = \frac{2\pi}{a}(h,k,l)$, and the atomic positions are $\rr_1 = (0,0,0)$ and $\rr_2 = \frac{a}{2}(1,1,1)$. Therefore

$$ \GG\cdot\rr_2 = \frac{2\pi}{a}(h,k,l)\cdot\frac{a}{2}(1,1,1) = \pi(h+k+l) $$ $$ S_{\GG} = f\left(e^{0} + e^{-i\pi(h+k+l)}\right) = f\left(1 + (-1)^{h+k+l}\right) $$

(We used $e^{-i\pi m} = (-1)^m$.) Hence

$$ S_{\GG} = \begin{cases} 2f & (h+k+l \text{ is even}) \\ 0 & (h+k+l \text{ is odd}) \end{cases} $$

That is, reflections with $h+k+l$ odd vanish. $(100)$, $(111)$, $(210)$, and so on vanish, while $(110)$, $(200)$, $(211)$, $(220)$, and so on remain. ∎

This is another way of expressing the fact, seen in Chapter 2, that “the reciprocal lattice of bcc is fcc.” Of the reciprocal lattice (simple cubic) of the conventional cell, only the points with $h+k+l$ even survive, and these form an fcc lattice.

Example 3.4: Systematic absences of fcc

In fcc there are four lattice points in the unit cell: $(0,0,0)$, $\left(0,\frac12,\frac12\right)$, $\left(\frac12,0,\frac12\right)$, and $\left(\frac12,\frac12,0\right)$. Carry out the same calculation.

Solution.

$$ S_{\GG} = f\left[1 + e^{-i\pi(k+l)} + e^{-i\pi(h+l)} + e^{-i\pi(h+k)}\right] = f\left[1 + (-1)^{k+l} + (-1)^{h+l} + (-1)^{h+k}\right] $$

Case analysis.

  • When $h,k,l$ are all even or all odd: $k+l$, $h+l$, and $h+k$ are all even, so $S = f(1+1+1+1) = 4f$.
  • When even and odd are mixed: for example, if $h$ is even and $k,l$ are odd, then $k+l$ is even, $h+l$ is odd, and $h+k$ is odd, so $S = f(1+1-1-1) = 0$. The other mixed cases likewise give zero.

That is, a reflection appears only when $h,k,l$ all have the same parity. $(111)$, $(200)$, $(220)$, $(311)$, $(222)$, … remain, while $(100)$, $(110)$, $(210)$, and so on vanish. ∎

Why can systematic absences be used? — The first step in determining the space group

When we measure a powder X-ray diffraction pattern and work out the $(hkl)$ of the observed peaks (indexing), which pattern of Table 3.2 they fit determines the centering symbol. Furthermore, screw axes and glide planes give rise to additional systematic-absence rules (Chapter 5). Taken together, these narrow the candidate space groups down to a few.

The task of an experimentalist determining the space group from a diffraction pattern comes down, in the end, to “reading the pattern of missing reflections.” Systematic absences are one of the few occasions on which symmetry shows itself directly in the form of diffraction.

3.6 Why Do the Peak Positions Coincide When the Bravais Lattice Is the Same?

We can now prove the claim announced in Chapters 1 and 2.

Theorem 3.3

The position of a diffraction peak (the value of $2\theta$) is determined only by the length $\abs{\GG}$ of the reciprocal lattice vector. $\abs{\GG}$ is determined only by the Bravais lattice and the lattice constants, and does not depend on the basis (which element is placed where).

The basis affects only the intensity of the peaks, through the structure factor $S_{\GG}$.

Proof

We rewrite Bragg's law in terms of $\abs{\GG}$. From the intermediate equation $2\abs{\kk}\sin\theta = \abs{\GG}$ in Theorem 3.2, we have

$$ \begin{equation} \sin\theta = \frac{\abs{\GG}}{2\abs{\kk}} = \frac{\lambda\abs{\GG}}{4\pi} \label{eq:3-peak-position} \end{equation} $$

The only quantities appearing on the right-hand side are the wavelength $\lambda$ (a measurement condition) and $\abs{\GG}$.

Now, $\GG = h\bb_1+k\bb_2+l\bb_3$, and by Eq. \eqref{eq:3-b-vectors} the $\bb_i$ are constructed from the $\aa_i$ alone. The $\aa_i$ are determined by the Bravais lattice and the lattice constants. The basis appears nowhere.

On the other hand, the intensity is $I \propto \abs{S_{\GG}}^2 = \abs{\sum_j f_j e^{-i\GG\cdot\rr_j}}^2$, and this contains the kinds of atoms ($f_j$) and their positions ($\rr_j$), that is, the entire basis. ∎

Physical Meaning: What the instructor's numerical experiments show

In the Mochizuki Research Group materials, XRD patterns are calculated with VESTA/RIETAN-FP, keeping the lattice constant fixed, for the following combinations.

Structure calculatedBravais latticePeak positions

SrSO$_4$, rock-salt type ($F\bar{4}3m$)cubic-$F$

All four are cubic-$F$, so by Theorem 3.3 the peak positions must coincide exactly. And in fact they did.

The materials also include calculations for $Pm\bar{3}m$ (CsCl type), $Pn\bar{3}m$ (cuprite-type Ag$_2$O), and $Pm\bar{3}m$ (ReO$_3$-type Na$_3$N); since these are all $P$ lattices, they coincide with one another, but their pattern differs from that of the $F$-lattice group.

Let us quote once more one sentence from the conclusion of the materials: “If the space group is the same, or more precisely, if the Bravais lattice is the same, then no matter what material we put into the ‘box’ called the primitive cell, the peak positions are uniquely determined.”

Caution: Peaks can nevertheless appear to come and go

Theorem 3.3 asserts that “the set of $\abs{\GG}$ = the set of peak positions,” but if the structure factor vanishes, that peak has zero intensity and is not observed. Hence the apparent pattern can change.

For example, in rock-salt-type NaCl, $f_{\mathrm{Na}}$ and $f_{\mathrm{Cl}}$ differ, so the $(111)$ reflection appears, though weakly. But if the two kinds of atoms had the same scattering power (for example, if hypothetically $f_{\mathrm{Na}}=f_{\mathrm{Cl}}$), the $(111)$ reflection would vanish completely, and only the reflections with $hkl$ all even would remain. This is nothing other than the pattern given by a simple cubic lattice of edge $a/2$ (if the two kinds of ions become indistinguishable, the NaCl structure is just a simple cubic arrangement with spacing $a/2$). An fcc metal gives reflections for both all-even and all-odd indices, so this pattern does not coincide with that of an fcc metal. KCl is in fact close to this case: K$^+$ and Cl$^-$ are isoelectronic (both have 18 electrons), so $(111)$ almost disappears. This is a well-known example.

In other words, to be precise, the Bravais lattice determines the positions where peaks can appear, and the basis determines which of them are actually visible and how high they are.

3.7 The Brillouin Zone

Definition: First Brillouin zone

The Wigner–Seitz cell in reciprocal space is called the first Brillouin zone (BZ). That is, it is the smallest region containing the origin that is enclosed by the perpendicular bisecting planes of the line segments drawn from the origin of the reciprocal lattice to all the other reciprocal lattice points.

Why is the Brillouin zone special? — Relation to the equation in Section 3.4.1

The equation obtained by rewriting the Laue condition (using the fact that $-\GG$ is also a reciprocal lattice vector, we have aligned the direction of $\GG$ with the $\kk$ side)

$$ \kk\cdot\frac{\GG}{2} = \left(\frac{\GG}{2}\right)^2 $$

meant that “the endpoint of $\kk$ lies on the perpendicular bisecting plane of the line segment joining the origin and $\GG$.” In other words, the boundary of the Brillouin zone is precisely the set of wavenumbers at which diffraction occurs.

The same thing happens for electron waves. An electron whose wavenumber lies on the zone boundary is Bragg-reflected, can no longer remain a traveling wave, and becomes a standing wave. There are two kinds of standing waves with different phases, and their energy difference becomes the band gap. This is the central argument of Chapter 16.

Diffraction (Chapter 3) and the band gap (Chapter 16) look like entirely different phenomena, yet they follow from the same conditional equation.

3.7.1 The Brillouin zones of fcc and bcc

Example 3.5: The Brillouin zone of fcc is a truncated octahedron

The reciprocal lattice of the fcc lattice (lattice constant $a$) is bcc (lattice constant $4\pi/a$) (Table 3.1). The reciprocal lattice points closest to the origin of the bcc reciprocal lattice are

$$ \GG = \frac{2\pi}{a}(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1) \quad (8\text{ points}), \qquad \abs{\GG} = \frac{2\pi}{a}\sqrt3 \approx \frac{10.88}{a} $$

The next closest are

$$ \GG = \frac{4\pi}{a}(\pm1,0,0)\ \text{etc.} \quad (6\text{ points}), \qquad \abs{\GG} = \frac{4\pi}{a} \approx \frac{12.57}{a} $$

The eight vectors of the $\langle111\rangle$ type give eight hexagonal faces, and the six vectors of the $\langle100\rangle$ type give six square faces, so we obtain a truncated octahedron with 14 faces in total. ∎

The high-symmetry points are as follows.

SymbolPosition (in units of $2\pi/a$)Meaning
$\Gamma$$(0,0,0)$Zone center
$X$$(0,0,1)$Center of a square face
$L$$\left(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12\right)$Center of a hexagonal face
$W$$\left(\frac12,0,1\right)$Vertex
$K$$\left(\frac34,\frac34,0\right)$Midpoint of an edge shared by two hexagonal faces
$U$$\left(\frac14,\frac14,1\right)$Midpoint of an edge where a square face and a hexagonal face meet

The horizontal axis of the band diagram of Si reads $\Gamma\to X\to U|K\to\Gamma\to L\to W\to X$; this is the standard path connecting the high-symmetry points of this truncated octahedron. The figure in the slide “1-6. The Bravais lattice uniquely determines the reciprocal space” in the Mochizuki Research Group materials is exactly this.

Example 3.6: The Brillouin zone of bcc

The reciprocal lattice of the bcc lattice is fcc, so the Brillouin zone is the Wigner–Seitz cell of the fcc lattice, that is, a rhombic dodecahedron (a 12-faced polyhedron). The nearest reciprocal lattice points are the 12 points of the type $\frac{2\pi}{a}(\pm1,\pm1,0)$, each of which gives one rhombic face.

The high-symmetry points are $\Gamma(0,0,0)$, $H(0,0,1)$, $N\left(\frac12,\frac12,0\right)$, and $P\left(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12\right)$ (in units of $2\pi/a$).

Physical Meaning: Why is it enough to consider only the Brillouin zone?

The wavenumbers $\kk$ and $\kk+\GG$ of a Bloch function represent physically the same state (we will prove this in Chapters 11 and 16). Therefore we do not need to consider the whole of $\kk$ space; it suffices to consider only one period of the reciprocal lattice, that is, only the first Brillouin zone, and all the states are then exhausted.

This is why the horizontal axis of a band diagram ends in a finite interval. The Brillouin zone is the result of folding the infinitely wide $\kk$ space by the periodicity of the reciprocal lattice.

3.8 Summary and Exercises

3.8.1 Summary

  • The reciprocal lattice arises inevitably from periodicity. Fourier-expanding $n(\rr+\TT)=n(\rr)$ requires $e^{i\GG\cdot\TT}=1$, and the set of all $\GG$ satisfying this is the reciprocal lattice.
  • From the duality relation $\aa_i\cdot\bb_j = 2\pi\delta_{ij}$ we obtain $\bb_1 = 2\pi(\aa_2\times\aa_3)/V_{\text{p}}$, and the volume of the reciprocal-lattice cell is $(2\pi)^3/V_{\text{p}}$.
  • $\GG_{hkl}$ is perpendicular to the $(hkl)$ planes, and $d_{hkl} = 2\pi/\abs{\GG_{hkl}}$. For a cubic crystal, $d = a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$.
  • Bragg's and Laue's conditions are equivalent. From $\kk'-\kk=\GG$ and $\abs{\kk'}=\abs{\kk}$ we obtain $2d\sin\theta=\lambda$. An $n$th-order reflection is read as the first-order reflection $(nh,nk,nl)$.
  • The scattering amplitude is the Fourier transform of the electron density, and it is nonzero only when $\Delta\kk=\GG$. The structure factor $S_{\GG}=\sum_j f_j e^{-i\GG\cdot\rr_j}$ determines the intensity.
  • Systematic absences: for $I$ centering, reflections appear only when $h+k+l$ is even; for $F$ centering, only when the indices $hkl$ are all even or all odd (unmixed parity). This provides the clue for determining the centering symbol from experiment.
  • The peak positions are determined by the Bravais lattice alone (Theorem 3.3). The basis does not appear in $\sin\theta = \lambda\abs{\GG}/4\pi$. The basis affects only the intensities.
  • The first Brillouin zone is the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice. Its boundary is the set of wave vectors satisfying the diffraction condition, which is the same condition as the origin of the band gap in Chapter 16. The fcc Brillouin zone is a truncated octahedron, and the bcc one is a rhombic dodecahedron.

3.8.2 Exercises

Exercise 3.1: The reciprocal lattice of fcc is bcc

From the primitive translation vectors of fcc, $\aa_1=\frac{a}{2}(0,1,1)$, $\aa_2=\frac{a}{2}(1,0,1)$, $\aa_3=\frac{a}{2}(1,1,0)$, calculate the primitive reciprocal lattice vectors and verify that they have the form of a bcc lattice with lattice constant $4\pi/a$.

Hint: $V_{\text{p}}=a^3/4$ (Example 1.2 of Chapter 1). $\aa_2\times\aa_3 = \frac{a^2}{4}(-1,1,1)$ (same example). Therefore $\bb_1 = \frac{2\pi}{a^3/4}\cdot\frac{a^2}{4}(-1,1,1) = \frac{2\pi}{a}(-1,1,1)$. This is $\frac{4\pi}{a}\cdot\frac12(-1,1,1)$, which is indeed the form of a primitive vector of bcc.

Exercise 3.2: Systematic absences of the diamond structure

The diamond structure is an fcc lattice with a basis of two carbon atoms ($(0,0,0)$ and $\left(\frac14,\frac14,\frac14\right)$).

(1) Show that the structure factor factorizes as $S_{\GG} = S_{\text{fcc}}\times\left[1 + e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}(h+k+l)}\right]$.

(2) Show that the $(200)$ reflection vanishes.

(3) What happens to the $(222)$ reflection?

Hint: (2) Since $h+k+l=2$, $e^{-i\pi}=-1$ and the bracket is $0$. (3) With $h+k+l=6$, $e^{-3i\pi}=-1$, so it is again $0$. In practice the $(222)$ reflection of diamond is a “forbidden reflection,” but it is observed weakly because the bonding electrons are not spherically symmetric. This is a historically famous example.

Exercise 3.3: Indexing a powder X-ray diffraction pattern

In powder XRD of a cubic crystal, using $\lambda = 1.5406\ \mathrm{\AA}$ (Cu K$\alpha$), the following values of $2\theta$ were observed:

$$ 2\theta = 43.3^\circ,\ 50.4^\circ,\ 74.1^\circ,\ 89.9^\circ,\ 95.1^\circ $$

(1) For each peak, calculate $\sin^2\theta$ and express their ratio as the smallest integer ratio.

(2) The integers obtained correspond to $h^2+k^2+l^2$. Index the peaks and determine the Bravais lattice.

(3) Find the lattice constant $a$.

Hint: $\sin^2\theta = \frac{\lambda^2}{4a^2}(h^2+k^2+l^2)$. The ratio should come out as $3:4:8:11:12$, which corresponds to $(111),(200),(220),(311),(222)$, that is, fcc. With $a \approx 3.61\ \mathrm{\AA}$, the crystal is Cu.

Exercise 3.4: The reciprocal lattice of the hexagonal lattice

Take the primitive vectors of hexagonal-$P$ to be

$$ \aa_1 = a(1,0,0),\quad \aa_2 = a\left(-\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},0\right),\quad \aa_3 = c(0,0,1) $$

and find the primitive reciprocal lattice vectors. Also verify that the reciprocal lattice is again hexagonal but rotated by $30^\circ$ relative to the real-space lattice.

Hint: $V_{\text{p}} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}a^2c$. $\bb_1 = \frac{2\pi}{a}\left(1,\frac{1}{\sqrt3},0\right)$, $\bb_2 = \frac{2\pi}{a}\left(0,\frac{2}{\sqrt3},0\right)$, $\bb_3 = \frac{2\pi}{c}(0,0,1)$.

Exercise 3.5: The volume of the Brillouin zone

State that the volume of the first Brillouin zone is $(2\pi)^3/V_{\text{p}}$, and show that for a crystal consisting of $N$ primitive cells (volume $V = NV_{\text{p}}$), the number of allowed $\kk$ points in the Brillouin zone is exactly $N$.

Hint: From the periodic boundary conditions (Appendix A, Section A.2), the density of $\kk$ points is $V/(2\pi)^3$. Multiplying this by the volume of the Brillouin zone gives $\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\cdot\frac{(2\pi)^3}{V_{\text{p}}} = N$. This fact is used decisively in Chapter 13 (the number of phonon modes) and Chapter 15 (the number of electron states).
