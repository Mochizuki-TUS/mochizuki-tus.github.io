Chapter 16Energy Bands

In Chapter 15 we treated the electrons in a metal as completely free particles. We ignored the periodic potential created by the ions altogether and regarded the electrons as a gas merely confined in a box. Even so, result after result came out in agreement with experiment down to the order of magnitude: the $\gamma T$ electronic specific heat, the Hall coefficient, and the Wiedemann–Franz law. Why such a crude approximation works so well is itself a puzzle.

The free electron model, however, has a wall that it cannot get over. It cannot explain the existence of insulators and semiconductors. In the free electron model, as long as there are electrons, the gas always conducts electricity. Real materials, however, have electrical conductivities distributed over more than 24 orders of magnitude, from good conductors ($\mathrm{Cu}$, $\sigma \sim 6\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{S/m}$) to good insulators (fused silica, $\sigma \sim 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{S/m}$). That a physical quantity varies by 24 orders of magnitude means that behind it lies not a quantitative difference but a qualitative difference.

What produces that qualitative difference is the energy gap, the protagonist of this chapter. However weak the periodic potential is, it acts dramatically at specific places in $\kk$ space—the boundaries of the Brillouin zone—and opens an energy range there that electrons cannot enter. If the band below the gap is exactly filled with electrons and the band above is empty, the material is an insulator; if the lower band is not completely filled, it is a metal. This single point accounts for the 24 orders of magnitude.

In this chapter we reach the same conclusion by three completely different routes: (a) the intuitive picture in which Bragg reflection at the zone boundary creates standing waves; (b) the Kronig–Penney model, the only model that can be solved exactly by hand; and (c) the central equation, the general framework on which actual band calculations are built. We will verify, without omitting any intermediate steps, that all three give the same $E_g$. From (c) in particular, the result that the effective mass of the electron becomes negative at the zone boundary, which foretells the existence of holes (Chapter 17), comes out naturally.

16.1 Why Does a Gap Open? — The Standing-Wave Picture

16.1.1 Where the free electron model breaks down

First, let us make clear what we are about to repair. The free electron model of Chapter 15 was obtained by dropping the periodic potential $U(x)$ from the Hamiltonian altogether.

Table 16.1: Successes and failures of the free-electron Fermi gas model

Physical Meaning: The wall of 24 orders of magnitude

Let us line up the electrical conductivities $\sigma$ at room temperature.

$\mathrm{Cu}$: $5.9\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{S/m}$ / $\mathrm{Si}$ (intrinsic): $\sim 4\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{S/m}$ / fused silica $\mathrm{SiO_2}$: $\sim 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{S/m}$

The ratio of $\mathrm{Cu}$ to $\mathrm{SiO_2}$ is $10^{24}$. This does not mean that the number of free electrons differs by a factor of $10^{24}$. Both $\mathrm{Si}$ and $\mathrm{SiO_2}$ contain valence electrons at a density comparable to that in $\mathrm{Cu}$. What differs is whether those electrons can be accelerated by an electric field. And what decides whether they can be accelerated is how the energy bands, which we are about to construct, are filled.

16.1.2 Bragg reflection at the zone boundary

Suppose the periodic potential is weak. For most $k$, the plane wave $\ee^{\ii kx}$ travels through the crystal almost undisturbed. But when $k$ takes certain special values, the situation changes completely. Recall the Laue condition learned in Chapter 3. When elastic scattering takes the wave vector $\kk$ to $\kk'=\kk+\GG$, combining this with energy conservation $\abs{\kk'}=\abs{\kk}$ gives

$$ \begin{equation} 2\,\kk\cdot\GG + G^2 = 0 \label{eq:16-laue} \end{equation} $$

Derivation: Eq. \eqref{eq:16-laue} and its consequence in one dimension

Square both sides of $\kk'=\kk+\GG$.

$$ \abs{\kk'}^2 = \abs{\kk+\GG}^2 = (\kk+\GG)\cdot(\kk+\GG) = k^2 + 2\kk\cdot\GG + G^2 $$

In elastic scattering $\abs{\kk'}=\abs{\kk}$, that is, $\abs{\kk'}^2 = k^2$, so subtracting $k^2$ from both sides gives

$$ 0 = 2\kk\cdot\GG + G^2 $$

This is Eq. \eqref{eq:16-laue}. In one dimension, $\GG \to G = 2\pi n/a$ ($n$ is a nonzero integer) and $\kk\cdot\GG \to kG$, so

$$ 2kG + G^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k = -\frac{G}{2} = -\frac{\pi n}{a} $$

Since $n$ may be any integer, positive or negative, we finally obtain

$$ k = \pm\frac{\pi}{a},\ \pm\frac{2\pi}{a},\ \pm\frac{3\pi}{a},\ \dots $$

∎ These are precisely the boundaries of the first, second, third, … Brillouin zones (Chapter 3). The boundary of a Brillouin zone is nothing other than the set of $\kk$ at which Bragg reflection occurs.

Why? A traveling wave cannot persist once Bragg reflection occurs

An electron with $k=+\pi/a$ is scattered by the lattice to $k'=k-2\pi/a=-\pi/a$. But an electron with $-\pi/a$ is likewise scattered, with exactly the same strength, to $+\pi/a$. The scattering is not one-way.

In other words, a wave trying to travel to the right is necessarily converted into a wave traveling to the left, and the left-moving wave is returned to the right. The result of this back-and-forth settling into a steady state is the standing wave. At $k=\pm\pi/a$, neither the right-moving traveling wave nor the left-moving traveling wave can be a steady-state solution; only the standing waves, which are their equal-weight mixtures, are steady-state solutions.

In optics, this is exactly the same phenomenon as the reflection band (stop band) of a multilayer mirror. Within a certain band of wavelengths only, the wave cannot propagate inside the film and is completely reflected at the entrance. For an electron in a solid, this “band where propagation is impossible” is the energy gap.

16.1.3 Two standing waves

The two plane waves $k=\pi/a$ and $k=-\pi/a$ have exactly the same energy $\lambda = \hbar^2\pi^2/2ma^2$ as free electrons. They are degenerate. Since any linear combination of two degenerate states is a solution with the same energy, looking at free electrons alone does not determine “which combination should be taken.” What settles the matter is the weak potential $U(x)$. The combination in which $U(x)$ is diagonalized, that is, the real standing waves, is selected.

There are only two ways to construct two independent real standing waves: the sum and the difference.

$$ \begin{equation} \psi(+) \;\propto\; \ee^{\ii\pi x/a} + \ee^{-\ii\pi x/a} \;=\; 2\cos\frac{\pi x}{a}, \qquad \psi(-) \;\propto\; \ee^{\ii\pi x/a} - \ee^{-\ii\pi x/a} \;=\; 2\ii\sin\frac{\pi x}{a} \label{eq:16-standing} \end{equation} $$

Derivation: Where does the normalization factor $\sqrt2$ come from?

Kittel writes $\psi(+)=\sqrt2\cos(\pi x/a)$ and $\psi(-)=\sqrt2\,\ii\sin(\pi x/a)$. Let us check where this $\sqrt2$ comes from.

The normalization convention is that “the probability per unit cell is 1,” that is,

$$ \frac{1}{a}\int_0^a \abs{\psi(x)}^2\,\dd x = 1 $$

With this convention, the traveling wave $\ee^{\ii kx}$ satisfies the condition exactly, since $\abs{\psi}^2=1$ (so it can serve as a reference for comparison). Setting $\psi(+)=A\cos(\pi x/a)$, we have

$$ \frac{1}{a}\int_0^a A^2\cos^2\frac{\pi x}{a}\,\dd x = \frac{A^2}{a}\int_0^a \frac{1+\cos(2\pi x/a)}{2}\,\dd x $$

Here we used the half-angle formula $\cos^2\theta = (1+\cos2\theta)/2$. The integral of the second term vanishes, because it integrates $\cos(2\pi x/a)$ over exactly one period:

$$ \int_0^a \cos\frac{2\pi x}{a}\,\dd x = \left[\frac{a}{2\pi}\sin\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right]_0^a = \frac{a}{2\pi}(\sin 2\pi - \sin 0) = 0 $$

Therefore

$$ \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{A^2}{2}\cdot a = \frac{A^2}{2} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A=\sqrt2 $$

The same holds for $\psi(-)$ (with $\sin^2\theta=(1-\cos2\theta)/2$), giving $A=\sqrt2$. ∎

With this normalization, the probability densities are as follows. We only need to use the half-angle formula once more.

$$ \begin{equation} \abs{\psi(+)}^2 = 2\cos^2\frac{\pi x}{a} = 1 + \cos\frac{2\pi x}{a}, \qquad \abs{\psi(-)}^2 = 2\sin^2\frac{\pi x}{a} = 1 - \cos\frac{2\pi x}{a} \label{eq:16-density} \end{equation} $$

Physical Meaning: Where do the electrons gather?

The ions are at $x=0,\ \pm a,\ \pm2a,\dots$.

  • $\abs{\psi(+)}^2 = 1+\cos(2\pi x/a)$ has the maximum value $2$ at $x=0,\pm a,\dots$ and is $0$ at $x=\pm a/2,\dots$. It gathers the electron on top of the ions.
  • $\abs{\psi(-)}^2 = 1-\cos(2\pi x/a)$ is the opposite, with the maximum value $2$ at $x=\pm a/2,\dots$. It gathers the electron midway between ions.
  • The traveling wave $\ee^{\ii kx}$ is uniform, with $\abs{\psi}^2=1$. It is not biased toward any place.

The potential energy is low (attraction) near a positive ion, so an electron in $\psi(+)$ gains relative to an electron in $\psi(-)$. They have the same kinetic energy $\lambda$, and only the potential energy differs. This difference becomes the energy gap as it stands.

16.1.4 Calculating the size of the gap

At last we calculate $E_g$. Since the period of the potential $U(x)$ is $a$, it can be expanded in a Fourier series. The constant term (the mean value) can be removed by the choice of the origin of energy, so we drop it and keep only the lowest-order component.

$$ U(x) = U\cos\frac{2\pi x}{a} \qquad (U\ \text{is real}) $$

Derivation: $E(+)-E(-)=U$

The two standing waves have the same kinetic energy $\lambda=\hbar^2\pi^2/2ma^2$, so the energy difference is only the difference of the expectation values of the potential energy. This is precisely the first-order perturbation theory formula.

$$ E(\pm) = \lambda + \frac{1}{a}\int_0^a U(x)\,\abs{\psi(\pm)}^2\,\dd x $$

Taking the difference, $\lambda$ cancels and

$$ E(+)-E(-) = \frac{1}{a}\int_0^a U(x)\Bigl[\abs{\psi(+)}^2-\abs{\psi(-)}^2\Bigr]\dd x $$

The quantity in the square brackets is found simply by subtracting the two parts of Eq. \eqref{eq:16-density} as they stand.

$$ \abs{\psi(+)}^2-\abs{\psi(-)}^2 = \left(1+\cos\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)-\left(1-\cos\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right) = 2\cos\frac{2\pi x}{a} $$

Substitute this.

$$ E(+)-E(-) = \frac{1}{a}\int_0^a \left(U\cos\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)\left(2\cos\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)\dd x = \frac{2U}{a}\int_0^a \cos^2\frac{2\pi x}{a}\,\dd x $$

Let us dispose of the remaining integral with the half-angle formula once more. From $\cos^2\theta=(1+\cos2\theta)/2$,

$$ \int_0^a \cos^2\frac{2\pi x}{a}\,\dd x = \int_0^a\frac{1}{2}\dd x + \frac12\int_0^a\cos\frac{4\pi x}{a}\,\dd x = \frac{a}{2} + 0 = \frac{a}{2} $$

(The second term vanishes because it integrates $\cos(4\pi x/a)$ over exactly two periods.) Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} E(+)-E(-) = \frac{2U}{a}\cdot\frac{a}{2} = U \label{eq:16-Eg1} \end{equation} $$

∎ The width of the gap is $E_g=\abs{U}$, that is, the amplitude of the Fourier component of the periodic potential itself.

Caution: On the sign (what Kittel does not write)

The left-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:16-Eg1} is “the energy of $\psi(+)$ minus the energy of $\psi(-)$,” which is not necessarily positive. Kittel writes this as $E_g$, but to be precise the situation is as follows.

  • In a real crystal, the ions attract electrons, so $U(x)$ has its minimum at the ion position $x=0$. For $U(x)=U\cos(2\pi x/a)$ to have its minimum at $x=0$, we must have $U<0$.
  • Therefore $E(+)-E(-)=U<0$; that is, $\psi(+)$ is lower. This agrees with the intuition that it is advantageous to gather the electron on the ions.
  • The width of the gap is $E_g=\abs{E(+)-E(-)}=\abs{U}$.

The sign of $U$ does not affect the width of the gap; it only determines “which standing wave becomes the top of the lower band.” When we diagonalize the $2\times2$ matrix in Section 16.5, this situation will be seen more clearly.

16.2 Bloch's Theorem — Proof by the Translation Operator

The discussion in Section 16.1 concerned only one point, the zone boundary. To build a general theory that works for any $k$, we must first determine “what form the wave function takes in a periodic potential.” That is Bloch's theorem.

16.2.1 Review of the group-theoretical derivation in Chapter 11

Supplement: What we did in Chapter 11 (in three lines)

  1. The translation group $\mathcal{T}=\{\{E|\RR\}\}$ is an abelian group. Every irreducible representation of an abelian group is one-dimensional (Chapter 7).
  2. Since $\Ham$ commutes with the translation operations, the eigenfunctions can be chosen as bases of irreducible representations of $\mathcal{T}$. A basis of a one-dimensional representation is merely multiplied by a phase factor under a translation.
  3. When that phase factor is written as $\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\RR}$, the $\kk$ is the wave vector. In other words, $\kk$ is nothing but a label attached to an irreducible representation of the translation group.

This viewpoint is beautiful, and it is a powerful one that connects directly to the discussion of degeneracy from Section 6 of Chapter 11 onward. However, we can also reach the same conclusion using only the language of operators, without going through group theory. This is the route Kittel takes. Knowing both makes clear “where $\abs{C}=1$ comes from.”

16.2.2 The translation operator and its eigenvalues

Definition: Translation operator $\hat{T}_a$

For a one-dimensional lattice (lattice constant $a$), we define the operator that shifts the argument of a function by $a$ as

$$ \hat{T}_a\,f(x) \;\equiv\; f(x+a) $$

Applying it $n$ times gives $\hat{T}_a^{\,n}f(x)=f(x+na)$.

Caution: The sign convention is opposite to that of Chapter 11

In Chapter 11 we adopted the convention of subtracting from the argument, $\hat{P}_{\{E|\RR\}}f(\rr)=f(\rr-\RR)$ (the graph of the function is moved by $+\RR$). Here $\hat{T}_a$ follows the convention of adding, $f(x+a)$. The theory is the same either way; the only difference is whether the sign of the final phase factor is $\ee^{\ii ka}$ or $\ee^{-\ii ka}$. Because this differs from textbook to textbook, always check which convention is used when you compare equations. In this chapter we arrange for the final form to be $\psi(x+a)=\ee^{\ii ka}\psi(x)$.

Derivation: $[\Ham,\hat{T}_a]=0$

The one-electron Hamiltonian is

$$ \Ham = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\dd^2}{\dd x^2} + U(x), \qquad U(x+a)=U(x) $$

Let $\hat{T}_a\Ham$ act on an arbitrary function $\psi$. Since we replace the argument of the finished function $\Ham\psi$ by $x\to x+a$, we have

$$ \hat{T}_a\bigl(\Ham\psi\bigr)(x) = \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''(x') + U(x')\psi(x')\right]_{x'=x+a} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''(x+a) + U(x+a)\,\psi(x+a) $$

Here we use two facts.

(i) $\psi''(x+a) = \dfrac{\dd^2}{\dd x^2}\bigl[\psi(x+a)\bigr]$. By the chain rule, $\dd(x+a)/\dd x=1$, so differentiation and translation commute.

(ii) $U(x+a)=U(x)$. This is the only place where the periodicity of the potential is used.

Therefore

$$ \hat{T}_a\bigl(\Ham\psi\bigr)(x) = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\dd^2}{\dd x^2}\bigl[\psi(x+a)\bigr] + U(x)\,\psi(x+a) = \Ham\bigl[\hat{T}_a\psi\bigr](x) $$

Since $\psi$ was arbitrary, as operators $\hat{T}_a\Ham=\Ham\hat{T}_a$, that is, $[\Ham,\hat{T}_a]=0$. ∎

Two commuting operators can be diagonalized simultaneously. Therefore an eigenfunction $\psi$ of $\Ham$ can be chosen to be an eigenfunction of $\hat{T}_a$ at the same time.

$$ \hat{T}_a\,\psi(x) = \psi(x+a) = C\,\psi(x) $$

The question is what kind of complex number this eigenvalue $C$ can be. This is the crux of this section.

Derivation: $\abs{C}=1$ — three ways to show it

(A) From physics: The probability density of the electron, $\abs{\psi(x)}^2$, is an observable quantity and must have the translational symmetry of the crystal. If $\abs{\psi(x+a)}^2\neq\abs{\psi(x)}^2$, the electron density would differ at the two indistinguishable points $x$ and $x+a$, which is a contradiction. Hence

$$ \abs{\psi(x+a)}^2 = \abs{C}^2\abs{\psi(x)}^2 = \abs{\psi(x)}^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \abs{C}^2=1 $$

(B) From operators: $\hat{T}_a$ is a unitary operator. Indeed, for any two functions,

$$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl(\hat{T}_a f\bigr)^*\bigl(\hat{T}_a g\bigr)\dd x = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f^*(x+a)\,g(x+a)\,\dd x \overset{y=x+a}{=} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f^*(y)g(y)\,\dd y $$

so the inner product is unchanged (we merely shifted the integration variable by a translation). The eigenvalues of a unitary operator have absolute value 1.

(C) From periodic boundary conditions: For a chain of length $L=Na$ consisting of $N$ unit cells, impose the Born–von Kármán periodic boundary condition $\psi(x+L)=\psi(x)$, as in Chapter 15. Applying $\hat{T}_a$ $N$ times gives

$$ \psi(x+Na) = C^N\psi(x) = \psi(x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C^N=1 $$

that is, $C$ is an $N$th root of unity. Every $N$th root of unity has absolute value 1. ∎

(A) and (B) only say that $\abs{C}=1$, but (C) further discretizes the possible values of $C$. All three are worth keeping in mind.

Derivation: $C=\ee^{\ii ka}$ and the quantization of $k$

From (C), $C^N=1$. The $N$th roots of unity are the $N$ values

$$ C = \exp\!\left(\ii\frac{2\pi s}{N}\right), \qquad s=0,1,2,\dots,N-1 $$

To rewrite this as $C=\ee^{\ii ka}$, we set $ka = 2\pi s/N$, which gives

$$ \begin{equation} k = \frac{2\pi s}{Na} = \frac{2\pi s}{L}, \qquad s=0,1,\dots,N-1 \label{eq:16-kquant} \end{equation} $$

This is exactly the quantization of $k$ obtained in Chapters 15 and 11. The spacing of adjacent $k$ values is $2\pi/L$, and the larger the crystal (the larger $L$ is), the closer $k$ comes to being continuous.

Here is one important observation. $s$ and $s+N$ give the same $C$ ($\ee^{\ii2\pi(s+N)/N}=\ee^{\ii2\pi s/N}\cdot\ee^{\ii2\pi}$). Since $s\to s+N$ corresponds to $k\to k+2\pi/a=k+G$,

$$ k \quad\text{and}\quad k+G \quad\text{refer to the same physical state} $$

Therefore there are only $N$ independent values of $k$, and they can be taken in $-\pi/a < k \le \pi/a$ (the first Brillouin zone). ∎

Theorem 16.1 (Bloch's theorem)

The eigenfunctions of the one-electron Schrödinger equation in a periodic potential $U(x+a)=U(x)$ can be labeled by a real number $k$ and satisfy

$$ \psi_k(x+a) = \ee^{\ii ka}\,\psi_k(x) $$

As an equivalent restatement, using a function $u_k$ with the lattice periodicity, they can be written as

$$ \begin{equation} \psi_k(x) = \ee^{\ii kx}\,u_k(x), \qquad u_k(x+a)=u_k(x) \label{eq:16-bloch} \end{equation} $$

$u_k$ is called the periodic part of the Bloch function.

Proof: The two forms are equivalent

($\Rightarrow$) Assume $\psi_k(x+a)=\ee^{\ii ka}\psi_k(x)$ and define $u_k(x)\equiv \ee^{-\ii kx}\psi_k(x)$. Then

$$ u_k(x+a) = \ee^{-\ii k(x+a)}\psi_k(x+a) = \ee^{-\ii kx}\underbrace{\ee^{-\ii ka}\cdot\ee^{\ii ka}}_{=1}\psi_k(x) = \ee^{-\ii kx}\psi_k(x) = u_k(x) $$

$u_k$ has period $a$. By definition, $\psi_k=\ee^{\ii kx}u_k$.

($\Leftarrow$) Conversely, assuming $\psi_k=\ee^{\ii kx}u_k$ and $u_k(x+a)=u_k(x)$, we have

$$ \psi_k(x+a) = \ee^{\ii k(x+a)}u_k(x+a) = \ee^{\ii ka}\,\ee^{\ii kx}u_k(x) = \ee^{\ii ka}\psi_k(x) $$

∎ The two forms are completely equivalent. The former states “what happens under a translation,” and the latter states “what form the function takes.” In calculations we use one or the other depending on the situation.

16.2.3 Extension to three dimensions

In three dimensions, there are translation operators $\hat{T}_1,\hat{T}_2,\hat{T}_3$ corresponding to the three primitive translation vectors $\aa_1,\aa_2,\aa_3$. These commute with one another:

$$ \hat{T}_1\hat{T}_2 f(\rr) = \hat{T}_1 f(\rr+\aa_2) = f(\rr+\aa_1+\aa_2) = \hat{T}_2\hat{T}_1 f(\rr) $$

Since these three operators commute with one another and also with $\Ham$, all four can be diagonalized simultaneously. If we write their eigenvalues as $\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\aa_1}$, $\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\aa_2}$, $\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\aa_3}$, a three-component label $\kk$ is defined. From the periodic boundary conditions $\hat{T}_j^{N_j}=1$, as in Chapter 11,

$$ \kk = \frac{m_1}{N_1}\bb_1 + \frac{m_2}{N_2}\bb_2 + \frac{m_3}{N_3}\bb_3 $$

is quantized ($\bb_j$ are the primitive reciprocal lattice vectors, and $m_j$ are integers). In total there are $N=N_1N_2N_3$ independent $\kk$, and they fill the first Brillouin zone exactly.

16.2.4 What Bloch's theorem does “not say”

Caution: Three common misconceptions

Misconception 1: “$\psi_k$ is a periodic function.”—Wrong. It is $u_k$ that is periodic, not $\psi_k$. Under a translation, $\psi_k$ is multiplied by $\ee^{\ii ka}$. However, $\abs{\psi_k}^2=\abs{u_k}^2$ is periodic.

Misconception 2: “$\hbar k$ is the momentum of the electron.”—Wrong. You can see this by letting the momentum operator $\hat{p}=-\ii\hbar\,\dd/\dd x$ act on a Bloch function.

$$ \hat{p}\,\psi_k = -\ii\hbar\frac{\dd}{\dd x}\Bigl(\ee^{\ii kx}u_k\Bigr) = -\ii\hbar\Bigl(\ii k\,\ee^{\ii kx}u_k + \ee^{\ii kx}u_k'\Bigr) = \hbar k\,\psi_k - \ii\hbar\,\ee^{\ii kx}u_k' $$

Because the second term remains, $\psi_k$ is not an eigenfunction of $\hat p$ unless $u_k$ is a constant (that is, a free electron). $\hbar\kk$ is called the crystal momentum, and it acts as a conserved quantity that includes collisions with the lattice (the same situation as the phonon momentum in Chapter 13). It is not the true momentum, but its response to an external force obeys the momentum-like equation $\hbar\dot{\kk}=\bm{F}$ (Chapter 17).

Misconception 3: “$k$ is uniquely determined.”—Wrong. Since $k$ and $k+G$ refer to the same state, $k$ is determined only up to a reciprocal lattice vector. This is exactly why the operation of “folding back into the first Brillouin zone” has meaning (Section 16.6).

Caution: A proviso when degeneracy is present

The argument “since $\Ham$ and $\hat{T}_a$ commute, they can share eigenfunctions” actually hides one extra step. If the eigenvalue $\epsilon$ of $\Ham$ is $g$-fold degenerate, any vector in that $g$-dimensional eigenspace is an eigenfunction of $\Ham$, but it is not necessarily an eigenfunction of $\hat T_a$. However, since $[\Ham,\hat T_a]=0$, $\hat T_a$ maps this eigenspace into itself ($\Ham(\hat T_a\psi)=\hat T_a(\Ham\psi)=\epsilon(\hat T_a\psi)$), so it suffices to diagonalize $\hat T_a$ within this $g$-dimensional space. Because $\hat T_a$ is unitary, it can always be diagonalized. In this way we can always construct “a complete set consisting of simultaneous eigenfunctions of $\Ham$ and $\hat T_a$.”

This procedure is also the entrance to the discussion in Section 6 of Chapter 11 that “the irreducible representations of the little co-group determine the degeneracy of bands.”

16.3 The Complete Solution of the Kronig–Penney Model

The discussion in Section 16.1 concerned only a single point, the zone boundary, and Section 16.2 merely determined the form of the wave function. Given a potential, then, can we carry the calculation through to the end and find how the bands and gaps are arranged? We can. However, the Kronig–Penney model (1931) is almost the only model for which this can be done completely by hand. It is nothing more than a simple model of periodic rectangular barriers, yet it contains every essential feature of a band structure.

16.3.1 Setting up the model

Definition: Kronig–Penney potential

A one-dimensional potential of period $a+b$. One period is divided into the following two regions.

  • Region I (well): $U=0$ for $0<x<a$
  • Region II (barrier): $U=U_0\ (>0)$ for $-b<x<0$

This pattern repeats under $x\to x+(a+b)$.

Consider an electron of energy $\epsilon$ (with $0<\epsilon<U_0$). In each region the Schrödinger equation is a second-order ordinary differential equation with constant coefficients, so its solution can be written as a superposition of exponentials.

Derivation: General solution in each region

Region I ($U=0$):

$$ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' = \epsilon\psi \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \psi'' = -\frac{2m\epsilon}{\hbar^2}\psi \equiv -K^2\psi, \qquad K \equiv \sqrt{\frac{2m\epsilon}{\hbar^2}} $$

Since $\epsilon>0$, $K$ is real. The solution is oscillatory:

$$ \psi_{\rm I}(x) = A\,\ee^{\ii Kx} + B\,\ee^{-\ii Kx} \qquad (0<x<a) $$

Region II ($U=U_0$):

$$ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + U_0\psi = \epsilon\psi \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \psi'' = \frac{2m(U_0-\epsilon)}{\hbar^2}\psi \equiv Q^2\psi, \qquad Q \equiv \sqrt{\frac{2m(U_0-\epsilon)}{\hbar^2}} $$

Since $\epsilon<U_0$, $Q$ is real. The sign is reversed, so the solution is exponential (the tunneling region):

$$ \psi_{\rm II}(x) = C\,\ee^{Qx} + D\,\ee^{-Qx} \qquad (-b<x<0) $$

∎ There are four unknowns: $A,B,C,D$.

16.3.2 Matching conditions and the Bloch condition — four equations

For the four unknowns we write four conditions. The first two are the matching conditions at $x=0$ and the last two are the matching conditions at $x=a$, but in the latter Bloch's theorem comes into play. This is the most important point of the Kronig–Penney model.

Derivation: The four equations

(1)(2) Matching at $x=0$. Since $U(x)$ has only finite jumps, both $\psi$ and $\psi'$ must be continuous (if $\psi''$ is finite, $\psi'$ is continuous; if $\psi'$ is finite, $\psi$ is continuous).

$$ \psi_{\rm I}(0)=\psi_{\rm II}(0):\qquad A+B = C+D \tag{1} $$ $$ \psi_{\rm I}'(0)=\psi_{\rm II}'(0):\qquad \ii K(A-B) = Q(C-D) \tag{2} $$

(3)(4) Matching at $x=a$. Immediately to the right of $x=a$ lies the barrier region of the next period. By Bloch's theorem, the wave function in this region is the solution for $-b<x<0$ translated by one period and multiplied by $\ee^{\ii k(a+b)}$:

$$ \psi(x) = \ee^{\ii k(a+b)}\,\psi_{\rm II}\bigl(x-(a+b)\bigr) \qquad (a<x<a+b) $$

Evaluating this at $x=a$, the argument is $a-(a+b)=-b$, so

$$ \psi(a^+) = \ee^{\ii k(a+b)}\,\psi_{\rm II}(-b) = \ee^{\ii k(a+b)}\bigl(C\,\ee^{-Qb}+D\,\ee^{Qb}\bigr) $$

This equals $\psi_{\rm I}(a)$. Abbreviating $E\equiv \ee^{\ii k(a+b)}$ from here on, we obtain

$$ A\,\ee^{\ii Ka}+B\,\ee^{-\ii Ka} = E\bigl(C\,\ee^{-Qb}+D\,\ee^{Qb}\bigr) \tag{3} $$

Likewise for the derivative,

$$ \ii K\bigl(A\,\ee^{\ii Ka}-B\,\ee^{-\ii Ka}\bigr) = Q\,E\bigl(C\,\ee^{-Qb}-D\,\ee^{Qb}\bigr) \tag{4} $$

∎ We now have a system of four homogeneous linear equations in four unknowns.

Why? Nothing is determined without the Bloch condition

If we look only at a single well and a single barrier of the periodic potential, the energy is not restricted at all (scattering states exist at any energy). Only when we impose the Bloch condition, “the wave function is multiplied by $\ee^{\ii k(a+b)}$ when we move to the neighboring cell,” does a relation arise between $\epsilon$ and $k$, and the requirement that $k$ be real restricts the energy to bands.

The essential point is that “$k$ is real.” If $k$ were a complex number, then $\abs{\ee^{\ii k(a+b)}}\neq1$, and the wave function would either diverge or decay exponentially toward one end of the crystal. This is not allowed for a stationary state of an infinite crystal. The range of “forbidden $\epsilon$” is the band gap.

16.3.3 Actually expanding the $4\times4$ determinant

Let us rewrite (1)–(4) as a matrix equation for $(A,B,C,D)$. Collecting all terms on the left-hand side, we get

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 & -1 \\[2pt] \ii K & -\ii K & -Q & Q \\[2pt] \ee^{\ii Ka} & \ee^{-\ii Ka} & -E\,\ee^{-Qb} & -E\,\ee^{Qb} \\[2pt] \ii K\ee^{\ii Ka} & -\ii K\ee^{-\ii Ka} & -QE\,\ee^{-Qb} & QE\,\ee^{Qb} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} A\\ B\\ C\\ D\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} $$

The condition for a nontrivial solution $(A,B,C,D)\neq(0,0,0,0)$ to exist is that the determinant of this $4\times4$ matrix vanishes. Expanding a $4\times4$ determinant head-on by cofactors produces 24 terms and gets out of hand, so we first use (1) and (2) to eliminate $C,D$, reduce the problem to $2\times2$, and then expand. This is the same as simplifying the determinant by elementary row operations, and the result does not change.

Derivation (Step 1): Expressing $C,D$ in terms of $A,B$

Equations (1) and (2) can be solved for $C,D$. Dividing (2) by $Q$ gives

$$ C-D = \frac{\ii K}{Q}(A-B) \equiv \alpha\,(A-B), \qquad \alpha \equiv \frac{\ii K}{Q} $$

Equation (1) reads $C+D=A+B$. Adding the two and dividing by 2, and subtracting and dividing by 2, we obtain

$$ C = \frac12\Bigl[(A+B)+\alpha(A-B)\Bigr] = \frac12\Bigl[A(1+\alpha)+B(1-\alpha)\Bigr] $$ $$ D = \frac12\Bigl[(A+B)-\alpha(A-B)\Bigr] = \frac12\Bigl[A(1-\alpha)+B(1+\alpha)\Bigr] $$

∎

Derivation (Step 2): Combining $C\ee^{-Qb}\pm D\ee^{Qb}$ with hyperbolic functions

We compute the combinations that appear on the right-hand sides of (3) and (4). Abbreviate $c\equiv\cosh Qb$ and $s\equiv\sinh Qb$.

First, $C\ee^{-Qb}+D\ee^{Qb}$:

$$ C\ee^{-Qb}+D\ee^{Qb} = \frac{A}{2}\Bigl[(1+\alpha)\ee^{-Qb}+(1-\alpha)\ee^{Qb}\Bigr] + \frac{B}{2}\Bigl[(1-\alpha)\ee^{-Qb}+(1+\alpha)\ee^{Qb}\Bigr] $$

We simplify the coefficient of $A$, separating the terms that contain $\alpha$ from those that do not:

$$ \frac12\Bigl[(\ee^{-Qb}+\ee^{Qb}) + \alpha(\ee^{-Qb}-\ee^{Qb})\Bigr] = \frac{\ee^{Qb}+\ee^{-Qb}}{2} - \alpha\,\frac{\ee^{Qb}-\ee^{-Qb}}{2} = c - \alpha s $$

Similarly, the coefficient of $B$ is $c+\alpha s$. Hence

$$ C\ee^{-Qb}+D\ee^{Qb} = A(c-\alpha s) + B(c+\alpha s) $$

Next, $C\ee^{-Qb}-D\ee^{Qb}$:

$$ \frac{A}{2}\Bigl[(1+\alpha)\ee^{-Qb}-(1-\alpha)\ee^{Qb}\Bigr] = \frac{A}{2}\Bigl[(\ee^{-Qb}-\ee^{Qb})+\alpha(\ee^{-Qb}+\ee^{Qb})\Bigr] = A(-s+\alpha c) $$ $$ \frac{B}{2}\Bigl[(1-\alpha)\ee^{-Qb}-(1+\alpha)\ee^{Qb}\Bigr] = \frac{B}{2}\Bigl[(\ee^{-Qb}-\ee^{Qb})-\alpha(\ee^{-Qb}+\ee^{Qb})\Bigr] = B(-s-\alpha c) $$

Hence

$$ C\ee^{-Qb}-D\ee^{Qb} = A(\alpha c - s) + B(-\alpha c - s) $$

∎

Derivation (Step 3): Reducing to a $2\times2$ determinant

We substitute the result of Step 2 into (3) and (4). Equation (3) becomes, as it stands,

$$ A\,\ee^{\ii Ka}+B\,\ee^{-\ii Ka} = E\Bigl[A(c-\alpha s)+B(c+\alpha s)\Bigr] $$

In (4), we divide both sides by $\ii K$. The coefficient on the right-hand side becomes $Q/(\ii K)=1/\alpha$, so

$$ A\,\ee^{\ii Ka}-B\,\ee^{-\ii Ka} = \frac{E}{\alpha}\Bigl[A(\alpha c-s)+B(-\alpha c-s)\Bigr] = E\Bigl[A\bigl(c-\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr)+B\bigl(-c-\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr)\Bigr] $$

Moving everything to the left-hand side, we obtain a $2\times2$ homogeneous equation for $(A,B)$.

$$ \begin{pmatrix} \ee^{\ii Ka}-E(c-\alpha s) & \ee^{-\ii Ka}-E(c+\alpha s) \\[3pt] \ee^{\ii Ka}-E\bigl(c-\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr) & -\ee^{-\ii Ka}+E\bigl(c+\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr) \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}A\\B\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix} $$

∎ The $4\times4$ problem has become $2\times2$. All that remains is to expand this determinant.

Derivation (Step 4): Expanding the determinant — the crux of the calculation

We simply write down the determinant $=$ (upper left) $\times$ (lower right) $-$ (upper right) $\times$ (lower left).

$$ \underbrace{\Bigl[\ee^{\ii Ka}-E(c-\alpha s)\Bigr]\Bigl[-\ee^{-\ii Ka}+E\bigl(c+\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr)\Bigr]}_{\textstyle \equiv T_1} \;-\; \underbrace{\Bigl[\ee^{-\ii Ka}-E(c+\alpha s)\Bigr]\Bigl[\ee^{\ii Ka}-E\bigl(c-\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr)\Bigr]}_{\textstyle \equiv T_2} =0 $$

Expanding $T_1$ (four terms). Noting that $\ee^{\ii Ka}\ee^{-\ii Ka}=1$, we have

$$ T_1 = -1 + E\,\ee^{\ii Ka}\bigl(c+\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr) + E\,\ee^{-\ii Ka}(c-\alpha s) - E^2(c-\alpha s)\bigl(c+\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr) $$

Expanding $T_2$ (four terms).

$$ T_2 = +1 - E\,\ee^{-\ii Ka}\bigl(c-\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr) - E\,\ee^{\ii Ka}(c+\alpha s) + E^2(c+\alpha s)\bigl(c-\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr) $$

Forming $T_1-T_2$. The constant term is $-1-1=-2$. We group the terms of first order in $E$ according to $\ee^{\ii Ka}$ and $\ee^{-\ii Ka}$.

$$ \ee^{\ii Ka}\ \text{coefficient}:\quad E\Bigl[\bigl(c+\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr)+(c+\alpha s)\Bigr] = E\Bigl[2c + s\bigl(\alpha+\tfrac1\alpha\bigr)\Bigr] $$ $$ \ee^{-\ii Ka}\ \text{coefficient}:\quad E\Bigl[(c-\alpha s)+\bigl(c-\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr)\Bigr] = E\Bigl[2c - s\bigl(\alpha+\tfrac1\alpha\bigr)\Bigr] $$

Here we let $\beta\equiv\alpha+\dfrac1\alpha$. The sum of the terms of first order in $E$ is

$$ E\Bigl[(2c+\beta s)\ee^{\ii Ka}+(2c-\beta s)\ee^{-\ii Ka}\Bigr] = E\Bigl[2c\underbrace{(\ee^{\ii Ka}+\ee^{-\ii Ka})}_{=2\cos Ka}+\beta s\underbrace{(\ee^{\ii Ka}-\ee^{-\ii Ka})}_{=2\ii\sin Ka}\Bigr] $$ $$ = E\Bigl[4c\cos Ka + 2\ii\beta s\sin Ka\Bigr] $$

The $E^2$ terms turn out remarkably clean. Expanding the two products separately gives

$$ (c-\alpha s)\bigl(c+\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr) = c^2 + \tfrac{cs}{\alpha} - \alpha cs - s^2 = (c^2-s^2) + cs\bigl(\tfrac1\alpha-\alpha\bigr) $$ $$ (c+\alpha s)\bigl(c-\tfrac{s}{\alpha}\bigr) = c^2 - \tfrac{cs}{\alpha} + \alpha cs - s^2 = (c^2-s^2) - cs\bigl(\tfrac1\alpha-\alpha\bigr) $$

In $T_1-T_2$ the former is multiplied by $-E^2$ and the latter is also multiplied by $-E^2$, and the two are added, so the $cs$ terms cancel exactly:

$$ -E^2\Bigl[(c^2-s^2)+cs(\cdots)\Bigr]-E^2\Bigl[(c^2-s^2)-cs(\cdots)\Bigr] = -2E^2(c^2-s^2) = -2E^2 $$

In the last step we used the hyperbolic-function identity $\cosh^2\theta-\sinh^2\theta=1$. Putting everything together,

$$ -2 + E\bigl[4c\cos Ka + 2\ii\beta s\sin Ka\bigr] - 2E^2 = 0 $$

We divide both sides by $2E$ (note that $E=\ee^{\ii k(a+b)}\neq0$).

$$ 2c\cos Ka + \ii\beta s\sin Ka = E + \frac{1}{E} = \ee^{\ii k(a+b)}+\ee^{-\ii k(a+b)} = 2\cos k(a+b) $$

All that remains is to compute $\ii\beta$. Since $\alpha=\ii K/Q$,

$$ \beta = \alpha+\frac1\alpha = \frac{\ii K}{Q}+\frac{Q}{\ii K} = \frac{\ii K}{Q}-\frac{\ii Q}{K} = \ii\,\frac{K^2-Q^2}{KQ} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ii\beta = -\frac{K^2-Q^2}{KQ} = \frac{Q^2-K^2}{KQ} $$

(We used $1/\ii=-\ii$.) Substituting and dividing the whole equation by 2, we obtain the exact Kronig–Penney relation.

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{Q^2-K^2}{2KQ}\,\sinh Qb\,\sin Ka \;+\; \cosh Qb\,\cos Ka \;=\; \cos k(a+b) \label{eq:16-kpexact} \end{equation} $$

∎ No approximation has been made so far.

16.3.4 The $\delta$-function limit

Equation \eqref{eq:16-kpexact} is correct, but because both $K$ and $Q$ depend on $\epsilon$, it is hard to see through. If we take the limit in which the barrier becomes “thin and high,” it becomes remarkably simple.

Derivation: The limit $b\to0$, $U_0\to\infty$ ($U_0 b=$ constant)

In this limit the barrier converges to a $\delta$ function of area $U_0b$: $U(x)\to U_0b\sum_n\delta\bigl(x-na\bigr)$. We evaluate three quantities in turn.

(i) $Qb\to0$. We have $Q=\sqrt{2m(U_0-\epsilon)}/\hbar\simeq\sqrt{2mU_0}/\hbar$. Since $U_0b=$ constant is held fixed, $U_0\propto 1/b$, and therefore $Q\propto b^{-1/2}$; that is,

$$ Qb \propto b^{-1/2}\cdot b = b^{1/2} \to 0 $$

Hence we may set $\sinh Qb \simeq Qb$ and $\cosh Qb \simeq 1$ (the lowest-order terms of $\sinh x=x+x^3/6+\cdots$ and $\cosh x=1+x^2/2+\cdots$, respectively).

(ii) $Q\gg K$. Since $Q\to\infty$, $K$ can be neglected, and

$$ \frac{Q^2-K^2}{2KQ} \simeq \frac{Q^2}{2KQ} = \frac{Q}{2K} $$

(iii) The period becomes $a+b\to a$.

Inserting the above into Eq. \eqref{eq:16-kpexact}, the first term becomes

$$ \frac{Q}{2K}\cdot(Qb)\cdot\sin Ka = \frac{Q^2 b}{2K}\sin Ka $$

We substitute $Q^2=2mU_0/\hbar^2$.

$$ = \frac{2mU_0}{\hbar^2}\cdot\frac{b}{2K}\sin Ka = \frac{mU_0b}{\hbar^2K}\sin Ka $$

Here we multiply the numerator and denominator by $a$ to form $\sin(Ka)/(Ka)$.

$$ = \frac{mU_0ba}{\hbar^2}\cdot\frac{\sin Ka}{Ka} $$

If we therefore define the dimensionless parameter

$$ P \equiv \frac{mU_0ba}{\hbar^2} $$

the Kronig–Penney model condenses into the following single equation.

$$ \begin{equation} \boxed{\ \frac{P\sin Ka}{Ka} + \cos Ka \;=\; \cos ka\ }, \qquad K=\sqrt{\frac{2m\epsilon}{\hbar^2}},\qquad P=\frac{mU_0ba}{\hbar^2} \label{eq:16-kp} \end{equation} $$

∎ $P$ is the only parameter that expresses the “strength” of the barrier (it is determined solely by the area $U_0b$).

Physical Meaning: The left-hand side is a function of $\epsilon$, the right-hand side a function of $k$

How to read Eq. \eqref{eq:16-kp} is the crux.

  • The left-hand side is a function only of $Ka$, that is, of the energy $\epsilon=\hbar^2K^2/2m$. Let us write it as $f(Ka)$.
  • The right-hand side is $\cos ka$. As long as $k$ is real, the right-hand side can take values only between $-1$ and $+1$.

Therefore, for an energy with $\abs{f(Ka)}>1$, no real $k$ exists. An electron of that energy cannot propagate through the crystal. This is the band gap. Conversely, the range of $Ka$ in which $\abs{f(Ka)}\le1$ is an allowed band, where the correspondence between $\epsilon$ and $k$ (that is, the dispersion relation) is fixed by $ka=\arccos f(Ka)$.

The phenomenon of “bands and gaps forming” emerges from nothing more than the upper and lower limits of $\pm1$ in a single transcendental equation. This is the beauty of the Kronig–Penney model.

16.3.5 Two limits — the free electron and the isolated atom

Derivation: $P\to0$ (free electron)

$P=mU_0ba/\hbar^2\to0$ is equivalent to having no barrier. Eq. \eqref{eq:16-kp} becomes

$$ \cos Ka = \cos ka $$

This means $Ka=\pm ka+2\pi n$, that is, $K=\pm k+2\pi n/a = \pm k + G$. Since values of $k$ that differ by $G$ describe the same state, effectively $K=k$. Therefore

$$ \epsilon = \frac{\hbar^2K^2}{2m} = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} $$

The free-electron parabola has returned. ∎ The width of the band gap becomes zero.

Derivation: $P\to\infty$ (isolated well)

When $P\to\infty$, the first term $P\sin(Ka)/(Ka)$ on the left-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:16-kp} diverges if $\sin Ka\neq0$. The right-hand side is finite because $\abs{\cos ka}\le1$, so this is not allowed. That is, for an allowed band to exist we must have

$$ \frac{\sin Ka}{Ka}\to 0 \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \sin Ka = 0 $$

Hence

$$ Ka = n\pi \quad (n=1,2,3,\dots) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad K = \frac{n\pi}{a} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \epsilon_n = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{n\pi}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{\hbar^2\pi^2n^2}{2ma^2} $$

These are precisely the energy levels of a particle confined in an infinitely deep well of width $a$. ∎

The bands collapse to zero width and become discrete atomic levels. This agrees with the physics that, when the barrier is high, the electron cannot tunnel into the neighboring well and remains confined in a single well.

Physical Meaning: The bandwidth is the ease of hopping to the neighbor

Placing the two limits side by side makes the physical meaning of a band clear.

  • $P=0$ (no barrier): the electron moves freely. The energy is continuous.
  • $P=\infty$ (infinite barrier): the electron cannot move. The energies are discrete atomic levels. Bandwidth $=0$.
  • In between: the electron can occasionally tunnel into the neighboring well. The atomic levels acquire a width and become bands.

That is, the bandwidth $W$ is proportional to the probability that the electron hops to the neighboring atom (the transfer integral $t$). In one dimension $W=4t$, and in the three-dimensional simple cubic lattice $W=12t$. The tight-binding approximation takes this viewpoint to its logical conclusion; it is treated in Chapter 18. The Kronig–Penney model is valuable in that it connects both the free-electron side and the atomic side with a single equation.

16.4 The Central Equation

The Kronig–Penney model is beautiful, but it can be used only when the potential has a rectangular shape. A real crystal potential is not rectangular, and it is three-dimensional. We need a more general framework. That framework is the central equation. The name sounds grand, but its content is just “the Schrödinger equation written in Fourier space.” And modern band calculations (first-principles calculations with a plane-wave basis) literally solve this equation numerically.

16.4.1 Fourier expansion of the periodic potential

Derivation: Why must $G=2\pi n/a$?

Since $U(x)$ is a function with period $a$, it can be expanded in a Fourier series. Writing the exponential functions available for the expansion as $\ee^{\ii Gx}$, the condition for them to have period $a$ is

$$ \ee^{\ii G(x+a)} = \ee^{\ii Gx} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ee^{\ii Ga}=1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Ga = 2\pi n $$ $$ \therefore\quad G = \frac{2\pi n}{a}, \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} $$

This is precisely the one-dimensional reciprocal lattice vector (Chapter 3). That is,

$$ \begin{equation} U(x) = \sum_G U_G\,\ee^{\ii Gx}, \qquad U_G = \frac1a\int_0^a U(x)\,\ee^{-\ii Gx}\,\dd x \label{eq:16-Ufourier} \end{equation} $$

The formula for the coefficients follows by multiplying both sides by $\ee^{-\ii G'x}$, integrating over one period, and using the orthogonality relation

$$ \frac1a\int_0^a \ee^{\ii (G-G')x}\dd x = \delta_{GG'} $$

(if $G\neq G'$, then $G-G'=2\pi m/a$ with $m\neq0$, so the integral covers exactly an integer number of periods and vanishes). ∎

Supplement: Properties of $U_G$ — three conventions

(1) We take $U_{G=0}=0$. The Fourier coefficient $U_{G=0}$ for $G=0$ is the spatial average of $U(x)$. It merely shifts the total energy uniformly, so we absorb it into the origin of energy. Note that it is something entirely different from $U_0\ (>0)$, which represented the barrier height in the Kronig–Penney model of Section 16.3. Because this is confusing, below we also write the Fourier coefficient for $G=0$ with the subscript made explicit, as $U_{G=0}$.

(2) $U(x)$ real $\Rightarrow$ $U_{-G}=U_G^{*}$. It suffices to take the complex conjugate of Eq. \eqref{eq:16-Ufourier} and replace $G\to-G$.

(3) If the crystal has inversion symmetry, then $U_G$ is real. If we take the origin at an inversion center, then $U(x)=U(-x)$. Then

$$ U_{-G} = \frac1a\int U(x)\ee^{\ii Gx}\dd x \overset{x\to-x}{=} \frac1a\int U(-x)\ee^{-\ii Gx}\dd x = \frac1a\int U(x)\ee^{-\ii Gx}\dd x = U_G $$

Combining this with (2) gives $U_G=U_G^*$; that is, $U_G$ is real. Below we consider this case for simplicity. Inversion symmetry keeps the band calculation real—this is a place where the point-group discussion of Chapter 4 comes into play.

16.4.2 Fourier expansion of the wave function

The wave function, on the other hand, is not periodic (recall Misconception 1 about Bloch's theorem). However, since we imposed the periodic boundary condition $\psi(x+L)=\psi(x)$ on a system of length $L=Na$, it can be expanded in a Fourier series with period $L$.

$$ \psi(x) = \sum_k C(k)\,\ee^{\ii kx}, \qquad k = \frac{2\pi n}{L}\ \ (n\in\mathbb{Z}) $$

The spacing of $k$ is $2\pi/L$, which is one $N$th of the spacing $2\pi/a$ of $G$. As $L\to\infty$, $k$ approaches a continuum, but $G$ remains discrete forever. This difference between the two scales governs the whole of the following discussion.

16.4.3 Substituting into the Schrödinger equation

Derivation: The central equation

The equation to be solved is

$$ \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\dd^2}{\dd x^2}+U(x)\right]\psi(x) = \epsilon\,\psi(x) $$

We convert the three terms in turn into the Fourier-expansion form.

The first term (kinetic energy). Differentiating $\ee^{\ii kx}$ twice gives $(\ii k)^2=-k^2$, so

$$ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\dd^2}{\dd x^2}\sum_k C(k)\ee^{\ii kx} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\sum_k C(k)\,(-k^2)\,\ee^{\ii kx} = \sum_k \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\,C(k)\,\ee^{\ii kx} $$

Below we write $\lambda_k\equiv\dfrac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}$ (the free-electron energy).

The second term (potential). It becomes a product of two series.

$$ U(x)\psi(x) = \left(\sum_G U_G\ee^{\ii Gx}\right)\left(\sum_{k} C(k)\ee^{\ii kx}\right) = \sum_G\sum_{k} U_G\,C(k)\,\ee^{\ii(k+G)x} $$

This is the step that Kittel skips in a single line. As it stands, the exponent is $k+G$, so it cannot be compared with the other terms (whose exponent is $k$). We therefore relabel the summation variable. In the sum over $k$, we rewrite $k'\equiv k+G$. Since $k$ runs over all of $2\pi n/L$, and $G=2\pi m/a=2\pi mN/L$ also lies on the same lattice, $k'$ also runs over all of $2\pi n'/L$. That is, the range of the sum does not change. Substituting $k=k'-G$, we obtain

$$ U\psi = \sum_G\sum_{k'} U_G\,C(k'-G)\,\ee^{\ii k'x} $$

If we rename the dummy variable back from $k'\to k$, then

$$ U(x)\psi(x) = \sum_k\left[\sum_G U_G\,C(k-G)\right]\ee^{\ii kx} $$

The third term (right-hand side). $\epsilon\psi = \sum_k \epsilon\,C(k)\ee^{\ii kx}$.

Collecting everything on the left-hand side, we get

$$ \sum_k\left[(\lambda_k-\epsilon)C(k)+\sum_G U_G C(k-G)\right]\ee^{\ii kx} = 0 $$

We make the operation of comparing coefficients explicit. Multiply both sides by $\ee^{-\ii k'x}$, integrate from $0$ to $L$, and divide by $L$. Since both $k$ and $k'$ are $2\pi\times$ an integer $/L$,

$$ \frac1L\int_0^L \ee^{\ii(k-k')x}\,\dd x = \begin{cases}1 & (k=k')\\[4pt] \dfrac{1}{L}\left[\dfrac{\ee^{\ii(k-k')x}}{\ii(k-k')}\right]_0^L = \dfrac{\ee^{\ii 2\pi(n-n')}-1}{\ii(k-k')L}=0 & (k\neq k')\end{cases} $$

that is, it equals $\delta_{kk'}$. Therefore only the term with $k=k'$ survives in the sum, and

$$ \begin{equation} (\lambda_k-\epsilon)\,C(k) + \sum_G U_G\,C(k-G) = 0, \qquad \lambda_k=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} \label{eq:16-central} \end{equation} $$

∎ This is the central equation. There is one equation for each $k$, so altogether we have as many simultaneous equations as there are values of $k$.

Physical Meaning: What the central equation says

Let us deliberately rewrite Eq. \eqref{eq:16-central} in words.

“The amplitude $C(k)$ of the plane-wave component with wavenumber $k$ is coupled to the components with wavenumbers $k-G$, which differ from it by a reciprocal lattice vector, through a quantity proportional to the mismatch between its own energy $\lambda_k$ and the total energy $\epsilon$.”

The most important point is that the partners to which it is coupled are limited to $k-G$. $C(k)$ mixes with $C(k-2\pi/a)$ and $C(k+2\pi/a)$, but it does not mix at all with $C(k+0.3\times2\pi/a)$. A periodic potential can change the wavenumber only “by units of the lattice scale.” This is precisely the Laue condition $\Delta\kk=\GG$ of Chapter 3, and it has the same structure as the Umklapp process of phonons in Chapter 13.

Derivation: Bloch's theorem follows from the central equation

The fact that the central equation couples only $k$ and $k-G$ means that the whole set of $k$ values

$$ \{\dots,\ k-2G_1,\ k-G_1,\ k,\ k+G_1,\ k+2G_1,\ \dots\} $$

is divided into mutually disjoint families (with $G_1=2\pi/a$). Only the $C$ of a single family appear in a given eigenfunction. If we choose the $k$ in the first Brillouin zone as the representative of the family, the eigenfunction can be written as

$$ \psi_k(x) = \sum_G C(k-G)\,\ee^{\ii(k-G)x} = \ee^{\ii kx}\underbrace{\sum_G C(k-G)\,\ee^{-\ii Gx}}_{\textstyle \equiv\, u_k(x)} $$

Here $u_k$ is a superposition of $\ee^{-\ii Gx}$, so by the definition of $G$ it has period $a$:

$$ u_k(x+a) = \sum_G C(k-G)\ee^{-\ii G(x+a)} = \sum_G C(k-G)\ee^{-\ii Gx}\underbrace{\ee^{-\ii Ga}}_{=1} = u_k(x) $$

∎ Bloch's theorem \eqref{eq:16-bloch} has been reproduced. In Section 16.2 we reached the same conclusion from symmetry; here we have reached it from the structure of the equation.

16.4.4 The central equation as a matrix

Since the families are independent of one another, we can fix a single $k$ (within the first Brillouin zone) and write the problem as an eigenvalue problem for the vector formed by arranging the $C(k-G)$ of that family. With $G_1=2\pi/a$ and the ordering $G=0,\pm G_1,\pm2G_1,\dots$, we have

$$ \begin{pmatrix} \ddots & & & & \\ & \lambda_{k+G_1} & U_{G_1} & U_{2G_1} & \\ & U_{-G_1} & \lambda_{k} & U_{G_1} & \\ & U_{-2G_1} & U_{-G_1} & \lambda_{k-G_1} & \\ & & & & \ddots \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\vdots\\ C(k+G_1)\\ C(k)\\ C(k-G_1)\\ \vdots\end{pmatrix} =\epsilon \begin{pmatrix}\vdots\\ C(k+G_1)\\ C(k)\\ C(k-G_1)\\ \vdots\end{pmatrix} $$

Formally it is infinite-dimensional, but the diagonal elements $\lambda_{k-G}=\hbar^2(k-G)^2/2m$ grow rapidly with $\abs{G}$. If we only want the low-energy eigenvalues, the components with large $\abs{G}$ may be truncated. When a first-principles calculation with a plane-wave basis asks us to specify a “cutoff energy $E_{\rm cut}$,” it is precisely specifying where to truncate this matrix.

Example 16.2: Truncate to $3\times3$ and solve at $k=0$

Let $U_{\pm G_1}=U$ (real) and all other $U_G$ be zero. Keeping only the three components $G=0,\pm G_1$, find the eigenvalues at $k=0$.

Solution: We have $\lambda_0=0$ and $\lambda_{\pm G_1}=\hbar^2G_1^2/2m\equiv\Lambda$. The matrix is

$$ H=\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda & U & 0\\ U & 0 & U\\ 0 & U & \Lambda\end{pmatrix} \qquad \text{(in the order }C(G_1),\ C(0),\ C(-G_1)\text{)} $$

The element directly connecting $C(G_1)$ and $C(-G_1)$ is $U_{2G_1}=0$, so it is $0$.

This matrix is invariant under the exchange $C(G_1)\leftrightarrow C(-G_1)$, so the eigenvectors can be separated into symmetric ones and antisymmetric ones (the idea of the projection operators of Chapter 9).

The antisymmetric combination $(1,0,-1)/\sqrt2$:

$$ H\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda\\ U-U\\ -\Lambda\end{pmatrix} =\Lambda\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \epsilon=\Lambda $$

The symmetric combination has the form $(1,\gamma,1)$.

$$ \begin{cases}\Lambda + U\gamma = \epsilon\\ 2U + 0 = \epsilon\gamma\end{cases} $$

From the second equation, $\gamma=2U/\epsilon$. Substituting into the first gives $\Lambda+2U^2/\epsilon=\epsilon$, that is,

$$ \epsilon^2-\Lambda\epsilon-2U^2=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \epsilon = \frac{\Lambda\pm\sqrt{\Lambda^2+8U^2}}{2} $$

For $\abs{U}\ll\Lambda$, expanding the square root gives $\sqrt{\Lambda^2+8U^2}\simeq\Lambda(1+4U^2/\Lambda^2)=\Lambda+4U^2/\Lambda$, so

$$ \epsilon_{\text{lower}} \simeq -\frac{2U^2}{\Lambda}, \qquad \epsilon_{\text{upper}} \simeq \Lambda+\frac{2U^2}{\Lambda} $$

Reading the result: The lowest band is lowered from the free-electron value $\epsilon=0$ by $2U^2/\Lambda$. This is the $-\abs{U}^2/(\Lambda-0)$ of second-order perturbation theory, counted twice (once from each of $G=\pm G_1$). It does not act at first order in $U$ but at second order—since $k=0$ is far from the zone boundary, this is the correct behavior. Only the zone boundary is special: there it acts at first order. That is the subject of the next section.

16.5 The Nearly Free Electron Approximation and the Effective Mass

The nearly free electron (NFE) approximation is a way of solving the central equation perturbatively under the assumption $\abs{U_G}\ll\epsilon_F$. It agrees surprisingly well with simple metals such as $\mathrm{Al}$ and $\mathrm{Na}$. Above all, the physics at the zone boundary can be followed completely by hand.

16.5.1 Why can we keep only two components?

Why? Only the places where the energy denominator is small matter

Solving the central equation \eqref{eq:16-central} for $C(k)$, we can write

$$ C(k) = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_G U_G\,C(k-G)}{\epsilon-\lambda_k} $$

The magnitude of the mixing is determined by the ratio $\dfrac{U_G}{\epsilon-\lambda_{k}}$. Even if the numerator $U_G$ is small, the ratio becomes large if the denominator is small. A small denominator means $\lambda_k\simeq\lambda_{k-G}$, that is,

$$ k^2 = (k-G)^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad -2kG+G^2=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k=\frac{G}{2} $$

This is precisely the zone boundary (the same condition as in Eq. \eqref{eq:16-laue}). Thus, near the zone boundary only one particular pair $\{C(k),C(k-G)\}$ is strongly mixed, and the other components (whose $\lambda$ lie far away) can be neglected. This is the reason we may truncate to $2\times2$.

Conversely, for $k$ far from the zone boundary every denominator is large, so the mixing is weak and, as we saw in Example 16.2, it acts only at second order in $U$.

16.5.2 The $2\times2$ secular equation

Fix one $G$ and let $U_G=U_{-G}\equiv U$ (inversion symmetry, real). Keeping only the two equations of the central equation for $C(k)$ and $C(k-G)$, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_{k}-\epsilon & U\\ U & \lambda_{k-G}-\epsilon \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}C(k)\\ C(k-G)\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix} \label{eq:16-2x2} \end{equation} $$

Derivation: How the two equations become this matrix

In the central equation for $k$, $(\lambda_k-\epsilon)C(k)+\sum_G U_G C(k-G)=0$, keeping only the term $G$ in the sum gives

$$ (\lambda_k-\epsilon)C(k) + U\,C(k-G)=0 $$

Next, we write the central equation for the wavenumber $k-G$. Setting $k\to k-G$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:16-central}, we have

$$ (\lambda_{k-G}-\epsilon)C(k-G) + \sum_{G'} U_{G'}C(k-G-G')=0 $$

The term in the sum that returns to $C(k)$ is the one with $G'=-G$, whose coefficient is $U_{-G}=U$. Hence

$$ U\,C(k) + (\lambda_{k-G}-\epsilon)C(k-G)=0 $$

Collecting these two equations in a matrix gives Eq. \eqref{eq:16-2x2}. ∎

Derivation: Solving the secular equation

The condition for a nontrivial solution is that the determinant vanish.

$$ (\lambda_{k}-\epsilon)(\lambda_{k-G}-\epsilon) - U^2 = 0 $$

Expand and arrange as a quadratic equation in $\epsilon$.

$$ \epsilon^2 - (\lambda_k+\lambda_{k-G})\,\epsilon + \lambda_k\lambda_{k-G} - U^2 = 0 $$

We use the quadratic formula. The content of the discriminant is

$$ (\lambda_k+\lambda_{k-G})^2 - 4(\lambda_k\lambda_{k-G}-U^2) = \underbrace{(\lambda_k+\lambda_{k-G})^2-4\lambda_k\lambda_{k-G}}_{=(\lambda_k-\lambda_{k-G})^2} + 4U^2 $$

(We used $(x+y)^2-4xy=(x-y)^2$.) Therefore

$$ \epsilon = \frac{(\lambda_k+\lambda_{k-G}) \pm \sqrt{(\lambda_k-\lambda_{k-G})^2+4U^2}}{2} $$

Taking the $2$ in the denominator into the square root and rearranging gives a more transparent form.

$$ \begin{equation} \epsilon_\pm(k) = \frac{\lambda_k+\lambda_{k-G}}{2} \ \pm\ \sqrt{\left(\frac{\lambda_k-\lambda_{k-G}}{2}\right)^2 + U^2} \label{eq:16-nfe} \end{equation} $$

∎ This is the central result of the NFE approximation.

Physical Meaning: Repulsion in a two-level system

Equation \eqref{eq:16-nfe} is the formula for the repulsion of a two-level system, which appears everywhere in quantum mechanics. For $U=0$, $\epsilon_\pm$ are just $\lambda_k$ and $\lambda_{k-G}$ themselves (the two parabolas cross). For $U\neq0$, a term $+U^2$ is always added inside the square root, so the upper branch is pushed higher and the lower branch is pushed lower. The two lines that used to cross never meet (avoided crossing). This “gap where they no longer meet” is the energy gap.

The same mathematics appears in the splitting of the bonding and antibonding orbitals of a diatomic molecule, in two coupled pendulums, and in the avoided crossing of nuclear magnetic resonance—in every case.

16.5.3 Exactly at the zone boundary

Derivation: $E_g=2\abs{U}$ and the correspondence with standing waves

At $k=G/2$ ($k=\pi/a$ in one dimension), $k-G=-G/2$ and $\abs{k}=\abs{k-G}=G/2$. Therefore

$$ \lambda_k=\lambda_{k-G}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{G}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{\hbar^2G^2}{8m}\equiv\lambda $$

Since the first term inside the square root of Eq. \eqref{eq:16-nfe} vanishes,

$$ \begin{equation} \epsilon_\pm = \lambda \pm \abs{U}, \qquad E_g = \epsilon_+-\epsilon_- = 2\abs{U} \label{eq:16-gap} \end{equation} $$

Let us also find the eigenvectors. Substituting $\lambda_k=\lambda$, $\epsilon=\lambda+U$ into the first row of Eq. \eqref{eq:16-2x2}, we obtain

$$ (\lambda-\lambda-U)C(k) + U\,C(k-G) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C(k-G)=C(k) $$

Then (with $k=G/2=\pi/a$)

$$ \psi = C(k)\ee^{\ii\pi x/a}+C(k-G)\ee^{-\ii\pi x/a} = C(k)\left(\ee^{\ii\pi x/a}+\ee^{-\ii\pi x/a}\right) = 2C(k)\cos\frac{\pi x}{a} $$

Similarly, for $\epsilon=\lambda-U$ we get $C(k-G)=-C(k)$, and

$$ \psi \propto \ee^{\ii\pi x/a}-\ee^{-\ii\pi x/a} = 2\ii\sin\frac{\pi x}{a} $$

∎ The $\psi(+)$ and $\psi(-)$ of Section 16.1 came out automatically as eigenvectors of the matrix. We did not decide by hand to “form the sum and difference”; this is the result of diagonalizing the $2\times2$ matrix.

Supplement: The relation between the $U$ of Section 16.1 and the $U$ of Section 16.5 (to be checked)

In Section 16.1 we wrote $U(x)=U\cos(2\pi x/a)$ and obtained $E(+)-E(-)=U$. In Section 16.5 we obtained $E_g=2\abs{U_G}$. The numbers seem not to agree, but only the meaning of $U$ differs. Let us check.

$$ U\cos\frac{2\pi x}{a} = \frac{U}{2}\ee^{\ii(2\pi/a)x} + \frac{U}{2}\ee^{-\ii(2\pi/a)x} $$

Comparing this with Eq. \eqref{eq:16-Ufourier}, the Fourier coefficient for $G_1=2\pi/a$ is

$$ U_{G_1}=U_{-G_1}=\frac{U}{2} $$

Therefore

$$ E_g = 2\abs{U_{G_1}} = 2\cdot\frac{\abs{U}}{2} = \abs{U} $$

This agrees perfectly with the result of Section 16.1. The factor of 2 between the amplitude of the $\cos$ and the Fourier coefficient—students always stumble here, but Kittel writes nothing about it. When comparing formulas, make it a habit always to check whether “that $U$ is the coefficient of the $\cos$ or the coefficient of the complex exponential.”

16.5.4 A little away from the zone boundary—expanding the square root

The zone boundary alone does not tell us the shape of the band. Let us shift $k$ slightly away from the boundary and find the dispersion relation in its neighborhood. The effective mass comes out of this.

Derivation: Expansion of $\epsilon_\pm(\tilde k)$ (Kittel's Eq. (7.42))

Let $k=\dfrac{G}{2}+\tilde k$ ($\abs{\tilde k}\ll G$). Then $k-G=-\dfrac{G}{2}+\tilde k$.

Step 1: Write $\lambda$ in terms of $\tilde k$. Using $\lambda_q=\hbar^2q^2/2m$, expand the square.

$$ \lambda_k = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{G}{2}+\tilde k\right)^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{G^2}{4}+G\tilde k+\tilde k^2\right) $$ $$ \lambda_{k-G} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(-\frac{G}{2}+\tilde k\right)^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{G^2}{4}-G\tilde k+\tilde k^2\right) $$

Step 2: Form the sum and difference. Letting $\lambda\equiv\hbar^2G^2/8m$ (the free-electron energy at the zone boundary), we have

$$ \frac{\lambda_k+\lambda_{k-G}}{2} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{G^2}{4}+\tilde k^2\right) = \lambda + \frac{\hbar^2\tilde k^2}{2m} $$ $$ \frac{\lambda_k-\lambda_{k-G}}{2} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,G\tilde k = \frac{\hbar^2G\tilde k}{2m} $$

(Note that the $\tilde k^2$ terms cancel in the difference, and the $G\tilde k$ terms cancel in the sum.)

Step 3: Substitute into Eq. \eqref{eq:16-nfe}.

$$ \epsilon_\pm = \lambda + \frac{\hbar^2\tilde k^2}{2m} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{\hbar^2G\tilde k}{2m}\right)^2 + U^2} $$

Step 4: Expand the square root. This is the crux. Let $X\equiv\dfrac{\hbar^2G\tilde k}{2m}$ and, assuming $\abs{X}\ll\abs{U}$ (that is, sufficiently close to the boundary), factor out $U^2$.

$$ \sqrt{X^2+U^2} = \abs{U}\sqrt{1+\frac{X^2}{U^2}} $$

Using $\sqrt{1+y}=1+\dfrac{y}{2}-\dfrac{y^2}{8}+\cdots$ and keeping terms up to first order in $y=X^2/U^2$, we obtain

$$ \sqrt{X^2+U^2} \simeq \abs{U}\left(1+\frac{X^2}{2U^2}\right) = \abs{U} + \frac{X^2}{2\abs{U}} $$

Step 5: Rewrite $X^2/(2\abs{U})$ in terms of $\lambda$. This is the part that Kittel skips.

$$ X^2 = \left(\frac{\hbar^2G\tilde k}{2m}\right)^2 = \frac{\hbar^4G^2\tilde k^2}{4m^2} $$

Since $\lambda=\hbar^2G^2/8m$, we have $G^2=8m\lambda/\hbar^2$; substituting this,

$$ X^2 = \frac{\hbar^4\tilde k^2}{4m^2}\cdot\frac{8m\lambda}{\hbar^2} = \frac{2\hbar^2\lambda\tilde k^2}{m} $$ $$ \therefore\quad \frac{X^2}{2\abs{U}} = \frac{\hbar^2\lambda\tilde k^2}{m\abs{U}} = \frac{\hbar^2\tilde k^2}{2m}\cdot\frac{2\lambda}{\abs{U}} $$

In the last rearrangement we deliberately factored out the form $\hbar^2\tilde k^2/2m$. With this done, the effective mass can be read off in the next step.

Step 6: Put it together.

$$ \begin{equation} \epsilon_\pm(\tilde k) \simeq \lambda \pm \abs{U} + \frac{\hbar^2\tilde k^2}{2m}\left(1 \pm \frac{2\lambda}{\abs{U}}\right) \label{eq:16-nfeexp} \end{equation} $$

∎ Even near the zone boundary, the dispersion is proportional to $\tilde k^2$ (a parabola). However, the coefficient is shifted from the free-electron value $\hbar^2/2m$ by a factor of $\left(1\pm2\lambda/\abs{U}\right)$.

16.5.5 The effective mass—and the negative mass

Definition: Effective mass

When the dispersion relation can be written in the form $\epsilon(\tilde k)=\text{const}+\dfrac{\hbar^2\tilde k^2}{2m^*}$, $m^*$ is called the effective mass. In general,

$$ \frac{1}{m^*} = \frac{1}{\hbar^2}\frac{\dd^2\epsilon}{\dd k^2} $$

that is, $m^*$ is the inverse of the curvature of the dispersion curve. The larger the curvature (the more sharply peaked the band), the lighter the mass; the flatter the band, the heavier.

Derivation: $m^*$ at the zone boundary

Compare Eq. \eqref{eq:16-nfeexp} with the defining form of the effective mass.

$$ \frac{\hbar^2\tilde k^2}{2m}\left(1\pm\frac{2\lambda}{\abs{U}}\right) = \frac{\hbar^2\tilde k^2}{2m^*} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{m^*} = \frac{1}{m}\left(1\pm\frac{2\lambda}{\abs{U}}\right) $$
$$ \begin{equation} \frac{m^*}{m} = \frac{1}{\,1 \pm \dfrac{2\lambda}{\abs{U}}\,} \qquad \left(\lambda=\frac{\hbar^2G^2}{8m}\right) \label{eq:16-effmass} \end{equation} $$

∎

Physical Meaning: At the top of the lower band the mass becomes negative

The premise of the NFE approximation is $\abs{U}\ll\lambda$, that is, $2\lambda/\abs{U}\gg1$. Then

  • Upper band ($+$, corresponding to the bottom of the conduction band): $m^*/m = 1/(1+2\lambda/\abs{U})$ is a small positive number. The electron is lighter than a free electron.
  • Lower band ($-$, corresponding to the top of the valence band): since $1-2\lambda/\abs{U}<0$, $m^*/m$ is negative. Moreover, its absolute value is small.

A negative effective mass is merely another way of saying that $\epsilon_-(\tilde k)$ has a maximum at $\tilde k=0$ (the zone boundary). If a curve is concave downward, its second derivative is negative.

The physical consequence, however, is dramatic. An electron with $m^*<0$, when an external force $F$ is applied, is accelerated in the direction opposite to $F$. In an electric field $\mathcal{E}$, the combination of a negative charge $(-e)$ and a negative mass $(-\abs{m^*})$ moves in exactly the same way as the combination of a positive charge $(+e)$ and a positive mass $(+\abs{m^*})$. This is the hole. The fact that some metals have a positive Hall coefficient, which the free electron model could never explain, is explained here. Holes are treated in detail in Chapter 17.

Example 16.3: Effective masses in numbers

Consider one dimension with $a=4.0$ Å and $\abs{U}=1.0$ eV. Find the effective masses of the upper and lower bands at the zone boundary ($G=2\pi/a$).

Solution: First we find $\lambda$. Since $G/2=\pi/a$,

$$ \lambda = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{\pi}{a}\right)^2 = 3.810\ \mathrm{eV\cdot\mathring{A}^2}\times\left(\frac{3.1416}{4.0\ \mathrm{\mathring{A}}}\right)^2 = 3.810\times0.6169\ \mathrm{eV} = 2.350\ \mathrm{eV} $$

Therefore

$$ \frac{2\lambda}{\abs{U}} = \frac{2\times2.350}{1.00} = 4.70 $$

From Eq. \eqref{eq:16-effmass},

$$ \frac{m^*_+}{m} = \frac{1}{1+4.70} = \frac{1}{5.70} = 0.175, \qquad \frac{m^*_-}{m} = \frac{1}{1-4.70} = \frac{1}{-3.70} = -0.270 $$

The gap is $E_g=2\abs{U}=2.0$ eV.

Interpretation: Values of $\abs{m^*}$ of $0.1\sim0.3\,m$ are of the same order as the effective masses of real semiconductors (the transverse effective mass of electrons in $\mathrm{Si}$, $m_t=0.19\,m$; electrons in $\mathrm{GaAs}$, $0.067\,m$; light holes in $\mathrm{Si}$, $0.16\,m$). This is no accident. The trend of Eq. \eqref{eq:16-effmass}—the smaller the gap (the smaller $\abs{U}$), the larger $2\lambda/\abs{U}$ becomes and the lighter the effective mass—holds well in real semiconductors ($\mathrm{InSb}$, with its small $E_g$, has an extremely light $m^*=0.014\,m$).

16.5.6 Far from the zone boundary—second-order perturbation

Derivation: The second-order perturbation formula for general $k$

For $k$ far from the zone boundary, every $C(k-G)$ is small. We solve the central equation approximately by treating $C(k)$ as the main coefficient and the others as subordinate.

In the equation for $k-G$ (with $G\neq0$), keeping only $C(k)$ as the term that returns on the right-hand side, we obtain

$$ (\lambda_{k-G}-\epsilon)C(k-G) + U_{-G}\,C(k) \simeq 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C(k-G) \simeq \frac{U_{-G}\,C(k)}{\epsilon-\lambda_{k-G}} $$

We substitute this into the equation for $k$.

$$ (\lambda_k-\epsilon)C(k) + \sum_{G\neq0} U_G\cdot\frac{U_{-G}\,C(k)}{\epsilon-\lambda_{k-G}} = 0 $$

Dividing by $C(k)$ and using $U_GU_{-G}=U_GU_G^*=\abs{U_G}^2$, we get

$$ \epsilon = \lambda_k + \sum_{G\neq0}\frac{\abs{U_G}^2}{\epsilon-\lambda_{k-G}} $$

If we replace $\epsilon$ on the right-hand side by its lowest-order value $\lambda_k$ (an approximation valid up to second order in $U$), then

$$ \begin{equation} \epsilon_k \simeq \lambda_k + \sum_{G\neq0}\frac{\abs{U_G}^2}{\lambda_k-\lambda_{k-G}} \label{eq:16-2ndorder} \end{equation} $$

∎ This is precisely the standard second-order perturbation formula of quantum mechanics. It diverges when the denominator becomes small (that is, as we approach the zone boundary), so it cannot be used there. The place where it fails is where the $2\times2$ treatment of Section 16.5.2 comes in; this is how the roles are divided.

16.6 Three Ways to Draw a Band Diagram

Because $k$ and $k+G$ refer to the same state (Section 16.2.2), there is freedom in how a picture of the bands is drawn. Three kinds of drawing are mixed together in textbooks and papers, so you need to be able to tell which is which.

Definition: The three zone schemes

  • Extended-zone scheme: the $n$th band is drawn within the $n$th Brillouin zone. It looks closest to the free-electron parabola.
  • Reduced-zone scheme: all the bands are folded back and drawn within the first Brillouin zone. The band index $n$ is then needed. Actual band diagrams are almost always of this type.
  • Repeated-zone scheme: using $\epsilon_n(k+G)=\epsilon_n(k)$ directly, the picture of the reduced zone is repeated periodically over the whole of $k$ space. It is convenient for seeing how the Fermi surface connects (Chapter 18).

Example 16.4: Calculation of folding

In a one-dimensional crystal with $a=4.00$ Å, a state at $k'=+2.50\,\pi/a$ in the extended-zone scheme corresponds, in the reduced-zone scheme, to which band and which $k$?

Solution: The first Brillouin zone is $-\pi/a<k\le\pi/a$. We subtract $G=2\pi n/a$ to bring $k'$ into this range.

$$ k = k' - G = \frac{2.50\pi}{a} - \frac{2\pi}{a} = \frac{0.50\pi}{a} $$

Subtracting $G$ for $n=1$ once was enough to bring it in. Numerically, $k=0.50\times3.1416/4.00 = 0.393\ \mathrm{\mathring{A}^{-1}}$.

The band index is determined by which Brillouin zone $\abs{k'}$ falls in. The $n$th zone is $ (n-1)\pi/a < \abs{k'} \le n\pi/a$, so $\abs{k'}=2.50\pi/a$ satisfies $2\pi/a < \abs{k'} \le 3\pi/a$; that is, it lies in the third zone. Therefore, in the reduced-zone scheme it is the point $k=+0.393\ \mathrm{\mathring{A}^{-1}}$ on the $n=3$ band.

Hint (general rule): “How many $G$ we subtracted” and “the band index” are different things. The band index is determined by which zone $\abs{k'}$ lies in, whereas the $G$ to subtract is chosen so as to bring $k$ into the first zone. The two are easily confused, so be careful.

Supplement: Why is the reduced-zone scheme the standard?

For real three-dimensional crystals, the extended-zone scheme is hardly usable. The second and third Brillouin zones of an fcc crystal become complicated, angular polyhedra around the $W$ point and cannot be drawn as they are (Chapter 3).

With the reduced-zone scheme, on the other hand, it suffices to plot $\epsilon_n(\kk)$ along the high-symmetry lines of the first Brillouin zone ($\Gamma\to X$, $\Gamma\to L$, and so on; Chapter 11). The familiar figure whose horizontal axis is a broken line such as $\Gamma\!-\!X\!-\!W\!-\!L\!-\!\Gamma\!-\!K$ is exactly this. How to read it is treated in Section 16.8.

16.7 The Number of States per Band and the Distinction between Metals and Insulators

So far we have seen that “bands and gaps form.” However, what decides whether a material is a metal or an insulator is not the shape of the bands but how they are filled. To determine this, we must count exactly “how many electrons fit into one band.”

16.7.1 $2N$ states per band

Derivation: The one-dimensional case

Let the number of unit cells be $N$ and the length be $L=Na$. From the periodic boundary conditions, the allowed values of $k$ are

$$ k = \frac{2\pi n}{L} = \frac{2\pi n}{Na}, \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z} $$

and their spacing is $\Delta k = 2\pi/L$. The first Brillouin zone $-\pi/a<k\le\pi/a$ has length $2\pi/a$, so the number of values of $k$ that fit into it is

$$ \frac{\text{length of the zone}}{k\ \text{spacing}} = \frac{2\pi/a}{2\pi/(Na)} = \frac{2\pi}{a}\cdot\frac{Na}{2\pi} = N $$

Since there are two spin degrees of freedom, the number of electrons that fit into one band is

$$ \begin{equation} \text{number of states per band} = 2N \qquad (N=\text{number of primitive cells}) \label{eq:16-2N} \end{equation} $$

∎

Derivation: The three-dimensional case (the same conclusion)

Let the volume of the crystal be $V$, the volume of the primitive cell be $V_{\rm cell}$, and the number of primitive cells be $N=V/V_{\rm cell}$.

Step 1: Density of states in $k$-space. As we saw in Chapter 15, under periodic boundary conditions the allowed $\kk$ are distributed uniformly in $k$-space with density $V/(2\pi)^3$ (the volume per $\kk$ is $(2\pi)^3/V$).

Step 2: Volume of the first Brillouin zone. As shown in Chapter 3, the volume of the primitive cell of the reciprocal lattice (= the first Brillouin zone) is

$$ \Omega_{\rm BZ} = \bb_1\cdot(\bb_2\times\bb_3) = \frac{(2\pi)^3}{V_{\rm cell}} $$

Step 3: Divide.

$$ \text{number of}\ \kk\ \text{in the zone} = \Omega_{\rm BZ}\times\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3} = \frac{(2\pi)^3}{V_{\rm cell}}\cdot\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3} = \frac{V}{V_{\rm cell}} = N $$

Multiplying by the spin degeneracy gives $2N$. ∎ This is the same as in one dimension.

Caution: $N$ is the number of primitive cells, not the number of atoms

This is the point in this chapter that is most easily gotten wrong. $N$ is the number of Bravais lattice points, that is, the number of primitive cells (unit cells), not the number of atoms.

  • $\mathrm{Si}$: diamond structure. The Bravais lattice is fcc, and the primitive cell contains two Si atoms. The cubic conventional cell contains eight, but that corresponds to four primitive cells (Chapter 1).
  • $\mathrm{NaCl}$: the Bravais lattice is fcc. The primitive cell contains one $\mathrm{Na}$ and one $\mathrm{Cl}$, two atoms in total. It is not the eight atoms of the conventional cell ($\mathrm{Na_4Cl_4}$).
  • $\mathrm{Mg}$: the structure is hcp. “hcp” is not the name of a Bravais lattice; the Bravais lattice is simple hexagonal (hexagonal-$P$), with a basis of two atoms placed at each lattice point. Therefore the primitive cell contains two Mg atoms.
  • $\mathrm{Cu}$, $\mathrm{Al}$, $\mathrm{Na}$: elemental metals with one atom per primitive cell. It is not the four atoms of the fcc conventional cell or the two of the bcc one.

If you miscount, the parity flips and the verdict of metal versus insulator is reversed. Your knowledge of Chapters 1 and 2 pays off here.

16.7.2 The rule that separates metals from insulators

Theorem 16.2 (band-filling rule)

Let $n_v$ be the number of valence electrons per primitive cell. The following is a rule under the premise that we count the bands of time-reversal-symmetric, spin-degenerate, noninteracting electrons with the periodicity of the original primitive cell (for cases in which this premise breaks down because of splitting due to magnetic order, doubling of the unit cell, or strong electron correlation, see Section 16.7.4).

  1. If $n_v$ is odd, the crystal is necessarily a metal. Since one band holds $2N$ electrons, the $n_vN$ electrons fill $n_v/2$ bands. If $n_v$ is odd, $n_v/2$ is a half-integer, and the last band is necessarily filled only halfway. In a half-filled band there are vacant states just above, to which electrons can move under even the slightest electric field, so a current flows.
  2. If $n_v$ is even, the crystal can be an insulator (or a semiconductor). If exactly $n_v/2$ bands are completely filled and there is a gap above them, an energy of at least $E_g$ is required to move an electron.
  3. Even if $n_v$ is even, the crystal becomes a metal if the bands overlap. In three dimensions, the maximum of one band along some direction of $\kk$ can be higher than the minimum of the next band along another direction. Then both bands are partially filled, and the crystal becomes a metal.

Why? A filled band cannot carry a current

Let us verify that “a filled band carries zero current.” The current of a band is

$$ j = -\frac{e}{L}\sum_{k\ \text{occupied}} v(k), \qquad v(k)=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\dd\epsilon}{\dd k} $$

(where $v$ is the group velocity, the same idea as in Chapter 13). By time-reversal symmetry $\epsilon(-k)=\epsilon(k)$, so $\dd\epsilon/\dd k$ is an odd function of $k$ and $v(-k)=-v(k)$. If the band is filled, every $k$ is occupied, so $+k$ and $-k$ always appear in pairs and their velocities cancel:

$$ j \propto \sum_{k\in\rm BZ} v(k) = 0 $$

Applying an electric field does not change the situation. The field tries to shift the $k$ of all electrons simultaneously, but in a filled band the states they would be shifted into are already occupied (the Pauli exclusion principle). Even if a shifted electron leaves through the zone boundary, the same number enter from the opposite side (because $k$ and $k+G$ are the same state). In the end, the electron distribution of a filled band cannot change at all in response to an electric field.

In contrast, in a band that is only half filled, the electrons on the $-k$ side can move into vacant states, so the distribution becomes asymmetric and $j\neq0$.

Example 16.5: Why is $\mathrm{Si}$ an insulator while $\mathrm{Ca}$ is a metal?

Both have the fcc Bravais lattice, and both have an even number of valence electrons. Yet one is a semiconductor and the other is a metal. Explain why.

Solution:

$\mathrm{Si}$: The primitive cell contains two Si atoms, and each atom has four valence electrons ($3s^23p^2$), so $n_v=8$. Hence $4N$ states are filled, that is, exactly four bands are completely filled. Physically, the four $sp^3$ bonding orbitals formed from the two Si atoms make up the valence band and the four antibonding orbitals make up the conduction band, with a gap of $1.12$ eV between them. At the $\Gamma$ point, at the $X$ point, and at the $L$ point alike, the top of the valence band lies below the bottom of the conduction band. Since the gap does not close at any $\kk$, the bands never overlap. Therefore the crystal is an insulator (semiconductor).

$\mathrm{Ca}$: The primitive cell contains one Ca atom, with two valence electrons in $4s^2$. Since $n_v=2$, rule (2) gives “one band is filled → the crystal can be an insulator.” In reality, however, the band derived from $4s$ and the band derived from $3d$ are close in energy. Part of the $3d$ band (its lowest value along some direction of $\kk$) sinks below part of the $4s$ band (its highest value along another direction of $\kk$). Electrons then flow into the $3d$ band before they have completely filled the $4s$ band, and both bands are partially filled. Hence the crystal is a metal.

To summarize: “An even number, hence an insulator” merely satisfies a necessary condition; it is not a sufficient condition. “An odd number, hence a metal,” on the other hand, is a theorem without exceptions as long as the premise of Theorem 16.2 holds (the nonmagnetic, spin-degenerate band picture used with the periodicity of the primitive cell; for cases in which the premise itself breaks down, see Section 16.7.4).

Why? Bands do not overlap in one dimension, but they do in three dimensions

This is one of the few situations in which being three-dimensional matters in an essential way.

One dimension: The maximum of band $n$ is always at the zone boundary (or the zone center), and the minimum of band $n+1$ is at the same point. Since a gap opens there, the two are always separated by $E_g$. There is no way for them to overlap.

Three dimensions: The zone boundary is a surface, and the free-electron energy $\lambda=\hbar^2k^2/2m$ differs from place to place on the surface. For fcc, the values of $\lambda$ at the $L$ point ($\abs{\kk}=\sqrt3\pi/a$) and the $X$ point ($\abs{\kk}=2\pi/a$) satisfy

$$ \frac{\lambda_X}{\lambda_L} = \frac{(2\pi/a)^2}{(\sqrt3\pi/a)^2} = \frac{4}{3} = 1.33 $$

that is, they differ by more than 30%. It is quite possible for the value of the first band at the $X$ point to be higher than the value of the second band at the $L$ point. A gap opens on each face, yet the bands overlap in energy overall. This is the reason the alkaline-earth metals ($\mathrm{Be}$, $\mathrm{Mg}$, $\mathrm{Ca}$, $\mathrm{Sr}$, $\mathrm{Ba}$) are metals.

16.7.4 When band theory breaks down

Caution: Mott insulators — when the band-filling argument itself breaks down

Theorem 16.2 is correct only as long as the one-electron approximation (each electron moves independently in an average potential) holds. When the Coulomb repulsion between electrons is strong, this premise itself fails, and conclusions obtained by counting bands become unreliable.

A typical example is $\mathrm{NiO}$ (rock-salt type). The primitive cell contains one $\mathrm{Ni}$ and one $\mathrm{O}$, and the number of valence electrons is $10+6=16$, an even number, so it does not conflict with (1) of Theorem 16.2. Even so, the one-electron theory fails. The $3d^8$ configuration of $\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}$ only partially fills the five $3d$ bands, so band theory (the nonmagnetic local density approximation, LDA) predicts a metal. In experiment, however, NiO is a perfectly good insulator with $E_g\simeq4.3$ eV. In LSDA, which incorporates antiferromagnetic order, a small gap of about $0.2\sim0.4$ eV opens, but this falls far short of the measured value of about $4$ eV.

There are also counterexamples to (1) of Theorem 16.2 itself, namely insulators with an odd number of electrons. $\mathrm{MnO}$ ($7+6=13$), $\mathrm{CoO}$ ($9+6=15$), and $\mathrm{La_2CuO_4}$ ($3\times2+11+6\times4=41$) are all insulators even though the number of valence electrons per primitive cell is odd.

The cause is that the Coulomb repulsion energy $U_{\rm eff}$ (several eV) when two $d$ electrons occupy the same atom is larger than the width $W$ of the $d$ band (about 1 eV). Because an electron “pays an extra $U_{\rm eff}$ when it hops to a neighboring atom,” each electron sticks to its atom and cannot move. This is called a Mott–Hubbard insulator.

However, it is not accurate to call $\mathrm{NiO}$ a “pure Mott–Hubbard insulator.” What forms the lower edge of the gap of $\mathrm{NiO}$ is not the $d$ level but a state strongly mixed with the $\mathrm{O}$ $2p$ states. In the Zaanen–Sawatzky–Allen classification, this type is called a charge-transfer insulator and is distinguished from the Mott–Hubbard type described by the one-band Hubbard model.

In practice, one corrects for this with the DFT+$U$ (LDA+$U$) method or dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT). When a value of $U$ is entered in a first-principles calculation of a transition-metal oxide, it is precisely to incorporate this effect.

Supplement: Temperature and imperfection — what “insulator” means in reality

Even for a band that is completely filled at $T=0$, at finite temperature electrons are thermally excited into the conduction band. The carrier density behaves as $n\propto\exp(-E_g/2k_BT)$ (derived in Chapter 17). At room temperature, $k_BT=0.0259$ eV, so

  • $\mathrm{Si}$ ($E_g=1.12$ eV): $\exp(-1.12/0.0518)=4\times10^{-10}$ → intrinsic carrier density $n_i=1.0\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$
  • Diamond ($E_g=5.47$ eV): $\exp(-5.47/0.0518)=10^{-46}$ → effectively zero

The boundary between “semiconductor” and “insulator” is not one of principle; it is a matter of degree, namely how many times $E_g$ exceeds the thermal energy at room temperature. The $n_i=10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ of $\mathrm{Si}$ is 12 orders of magnitude smaller than the $10^{22}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ of a metal, but it is not zero. This is precisely why it can be controlled by doping (Chapter 17).

16.8.2 Si — the standard indirect-gap semiconductor

Physical Meaning: How to read the bands of $\mathrm{Si}$

  • There are four valence bands. As we counted in Section 16.7, the eight valence electrons per primitive cell fill $8N$ states (= four bands).
  • The top of the valence band (VBM) is at the point $\Gamma$, where the state is threefold degenerate and is called $\Gamma_{25'}$ in the notation of Chapter 11 (corresponding to $T_{2g}$ of $O_h$). It is threefold because the state belongs to a three-dimensional irreducible representation of the little co-group $O_h$, and this follows only from group theory (Chapter 11, Section 6).
  • When the spin-orbit interaction is included, this threefold degeneracy splits into $\Gamma_8$ (fourfold) and $\Gamma_7$ (twofold). The splitting is $44$ meV for $\mathrm{Si}$, $0.29$ eV for $\mathrm{Ge}$, and $0.34$ eV for $\mathrm{GaAs}$. The larger the atomic number, the larger the splitting.
  • The bottom of the conduction band (CBM) is not at $\Gamma$ but at $\kk\simeq0.85\times(2\pi/a)(1,0,0)$ on the line $\Delta$. Since $\langle100\rangle$ has six equivalent directions, the conduction band of $\mathrm{Si}$ has six equivalent valleys. This number six directly affects the calculation of the carrier density (Chapter 17).
  • In $\mathrm{Ge}$ the CBM is at the point $L$, and since half of the eight $\langle111\rangle$ directions lie inside the zone, there are four valleys. In $\mathrm{GaAs}$ the CBM is at $\Gamma$, so there is one.

16.8.3 Direct and indirect gaps

Derivation: A photon can hardly change $\kk$

When an electron absorbs light and moves from the valence band to the conduction band, both energy and crystal momentum must be conserved. Let us check the order of magnitude of the photon momentum.

The wavenumber of a photon of energy $\hbar\omega=1.12$ eV is

$$ k_{\rm photon} = \frac{\omega}{c} = \frac{\hbar\omega}{\hbar c} = \frac{1.12\ \mathrm{eV}}{1973\ \mathrm{eV\cdot\mathring{A}}} = 5.7\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{\mathring{A}^{-1}} $$

($\hbar c = 1973\ \mathrm{eV\cdot\mathring{A}}$ is a useful constant to remember.) On the other hand, the size of the zone of $\mathrm{Si}$, with $a=5.43$ Å, is

$$ \frac{2\pi}{a} = \frac{6.283}{5.43} = 1.16\ \mathrm{\mathring{A}^{-1}} $$

Taking the ratio, we obtain

$$ \frac{k_{\rm photon}}{2\pi/a} = \frac{5.7\times10^{-4}}{1.16} = 4.9\times10^{-4} $$

∎ The photon momentum is only five ten-thousandths of the size of the Brillouin zone. Therefore, on a band diagram, optical absorption may be drawn as a perfectly vertical arrow ($\Delta\kk=0$). This is called a direct transition.

Physical Meaning: Why $\mathrm{Si}$ does not emit light

In $\mathrm{Si}$, the VBM ($\Gamma$) and the CBM ($0.85X$) have different $\kk$. With vertical arrows alone, a photon of $1.12$ eV cannot cause a transition anywhere. Rising vertically at the point $\Gamma$ requires $3.4$ eV.

Then why does $\mathrm{Si}$ absorb light from around $1.12$ eV? Because a phonon supplies the momentum. If a single phonon with momentum $\hbar\qq\simeq0.85\times(2\pi/a)\hbar$ and energy $\hbar\Omega\simeq 60$ meV (Chapters 13 and 14) is absorbed or emitted, the books balance for $\kk$.

$$ \hbar\omega = E_g \pm \hbar\Omega, \qquad \kk_{\rm CBM}-\kk_{\rm VBM} = \pm\qq $$

This is called an indirect transition. Because it is a three-body process (electron, photon, phonon), its probability is low. In fact, the absorption coefficients are

  • $\mathrm{Si}$ (indirect, near the absorption edge): $\alpha\sim 10\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ → light is absorbed only after traveling $1$ mm
  • $\mathrm{GaAs}$ (direct, near the absorption edge): $\alpha\sim 10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ → light is absorbed within $1\ \mu$m

The reverse process (light emission) likewise occurs only with difficulty in $\mathrm{Si}$. This is the real content of the statement “LEDs and lasers cannot be made from $\mathrm{Si}$,” a fact that has shaped the semiconductor industry. Direct-gap $\mathrm{GaAs}$, $\mathrm{InP}$, and $\mathrm{GaN}$ are used for LEDs and semiconductor lasers, and in solar cells as well, an $\mathrm{Si}$ cell needs a thickness of $100\ \mu$m or more to absorb the light, whereas a $\mathrm{GaAs}$ cell needs only a few $\mu$m.

16.8.4 Cu and Al — bands of metals

Physical Meaning: The color of $\mathrm{Cu}$ is determined by the $d$ bands

$\mathrm{Cu}$ ($3d^{10}4s^1$) has 11 valence electrons, an odd number. By Theorem 16.2 it must be a metal. The features of the band diagram are as follows.

  • The bands that come from $4sp$ are free-electron-like and wide ($\sim10$ eV), just as in $\mathrm{Al}$. This is why the value $\epsilon_F=7.03$ eV used in Chapter 15 agreed so well.
  • The five bands that come from $3d$ are narrow ($\sim3$ eV). The $d$ orbitals are strongly localized on the atoms, so their overlap with those of neighboring atoms (the transfer integral $t$) is small. The statement in Section 16.3.5 that "the bandwidth measures the ease of hopping" applies here.
  • The $d$ bands lie $2\sim5$ eV below $\epsilon_F$ and are completely filled by the ten $d$ electrons. Only the $sp$ band crosses $\epsilon_F$.

Color: When visible light ($1.8\sim3.1$ eV) is shone on the metal, electrons in the $d$ bands are excited into the empty states just above $\epsilon_F$ (interband transition). For $\mathrm{Cu}$ the threshold of this absorption is about $2.1$ eV ($590$ nm, yellow to orange). Blue and green are absorbed and red and orange are reflected, so $\mathrm{Cu}$ looks reddish. The threshold of $\mathrm{Au}$ is $2.4$ eV, somewhat higher, and that of $\mathrm{Ag}$ is $3.9$ eV, outside the visible range, so $\mathrm{Ag}$ reflects almost uniformly over the whole visible range and is silvery white. The color of a metal is a direct visualization of its band structure.

Supplement: How well the NFE approximation works for $\mathrm{Al}$

The pseudopotential form factors of $\mathrm{Al}$ (fcc, $a=4.05$ Å) (the quantities corresponding to $U_G$) are empirically about

$$ U_{111}\simeq -0.24\ \mathrm{eV}, \qquad U_{200}\simeq +0.76\ \mathrm{eV} $$

On the other hand, $\epsilon_F=11.7$ eV (Chapter 15). Taking the ratio gives $\abs{U}/\epsilon_F \sim 0.02\sim0.07$, so indeed $\abs{U}\ll\epsilon_F$. The premise of the NFE approximation is satisfied.

The gap at a zone boundary is $E_g=2\abs{U_G}$, so it is about $0.48$ eV at the $L$ point and $1.5$ eV at the $X$ point. These are small compared with $\epsilon_F$, so the Fermi surface of $\mathrm{Al}$ has the shape of a free-electron sphere slightly trimmed at the zone boundaries. This is why the Harrison construction of Chapter 18 works surprisingly well for $\mathrm{Al}$.

Example 16.6: Estimating the transfer integral from the $d$ bandwidth of $\mathrm{Cu}$

Let the width of the $3d$ band of $\mathrm{Cu}$ be $W=3.0$ eV. In the tight-binding approximation for the $s$ band of an fcc lattice, the relation between the bandwidth and the transfer integral is $W=16\,t$ (Chapter 18, Table 18.2). The number of nearest neighbors is $z=12$, but the "12" in $W=12t$ for the simple cubic lattice in Section 16.3.5 is $2z=2\times6$ and not the coordination number itself; moreover, the fcc lattice has triangular loops, so $W=2zt$ does not hold in the first place. Use $W=16\,t$ to find the transfer integral $t$. Also compare it with that of the $4sp$ band ($W\simeq10$ eV).

Solution: $t = W/16 = 3.0/16 = 0.19$ eV. For the $4sp$ band, $t=10/16=0.63$ eV.

Interpretation: The ease of hopping of a $d$ electron is only about $1/3$ that of an $sp$ electron. The $3d$ orbitals are strongly contracted inward in the radial direction compared with $4s,4p$, so their overlap with neighboring atoms is small. This is what lies behind the statement that "the $d$ band is narrow."
And a narrow band directly means a large density of states (because the same number of states is packed into a narrow energy range). The facts that the electronic specific heat coefficient $\gamma$ of transition metals is an order of magnitude larger than that of simple metals, and that transition metals readily show magnetism (the Stoner criterion, Chapter 20), both originate from this narrow $d$ band. It is also continuous with the story that when $U_{\rm eff}\gtrsim W$ the system becomes the Mott insulator of Section 16.7.4.

16.9 Summary and Exercises

16.9.1 Summary

  • Gaps open at zone boundaries. At $k=\pm n\pi/a$, Bragg reflection (the Laue condition $2\kk\cdot\GG+G^2=0$) turns traveling waves into standing waves. $\abs{\psi(+)}^2=2\cos^2(\pi x/a)$ gathers the electron on the ions and $\abs{\psi(-)}^2=2\sin^2(\pi x/a)$ gathers it between the ions, and the difference in their potential energies becomes the gap. For $U(x)=U\cos(2\pi x/a)$, $E(+)-E(-)=U$ and the gap width is $\abs{U}$.
  • Bloch's theorem $\psi_k(x)=\ee^{\ii kx}u_k(x)$, $u_k(x+a)=u_k(x)$. It is derived in the following order: the translation operator $\hat T_a$ commutes with $\Ham$; its eigenvalue satisfies $\abs{C}=1$ (this follows from any of the periodicity of the probability density, unitarity, or periodic boundary conditions); and $C^N=1$ quantizes $k=2\pi s/L$. This is completely equivalent to the group-theoretical derivation in Chapter 11.
  • $\hbar k$ is not the momentum. In $\hat p\psi_k = \hbar k\psi_k -\ii\hbar\ee^{\ii kx}u_k'$ the second term remains. $\hbar\kk$ is the crystal momentum, and it is determined only up to differences by $\GG$.
  • The Kronig–Penney model can be solved exactly from the $4\times4$ matching conditions (continuity of $\psi,\psi'$ plus the Bloch condition), and in the $\delta$-function limit it becomes $\dfrac{P\sin Ka}{Ka}+\cos Ka=\cos ka$ ($P=mU_0ba/\hbar^2$). The values of $\epsilon$ for which the left-hand side exceeds $\pm1$ form the band gap. For $P\to0$ the electron is free, and for $P\to\infty$ the discrete levels of a well of width $a$ (zero bandwidth) are obtained.
  • The central equation $(\lambda_k-\epsilon)C(k)+\sum_G U_GC(k-G)=0$. A periodic potential can change the wavenumber only by $\GG$, so each family of $k$ gives an independent matrix eigenvalue problem. Bloch's theorem also follows from this automatically. First-principles calculations with a plane-wave basis solve this equation numerically.
  • NFE approximation: Truncating to two components near a zone boundary gives $\epsilon_\pm=\dfrac{\lambda_k+\lambda_{k-G}}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\lambda_k-\lambda_{k-G}}{2}\right)^2+U^2}$. Exactly at the boundary, $\epsilon_\pm=\lambda\pm\abs{U}$ and $E_g=2\abs{U_G}$. The eigenvectors are standing waves of $\cos$ and $\sin$, in agreement with Section 16.1.
  • Effective mass: From $\epsilon_\pm\simeq\lambda\pm\abs{U}+\dfrac{\hbar^2\tilde k^2}{2m}\left(1\pm\dfrac{2\lambda}{\abs{U}}\right)$ we get $\dfrac{m^*}{m}=\dfrac{1}{1\pm2\lambda/\abs{U}}$. For $\abs{U}\ll\lambda$, $m^*<0$ in the lower band. This is the origin of the hole (Chapter 17) and the reason why some metals have a positive Hall coefficient.
  • Each band holds $2N$ states ($N$ is the number of primitive cells, not the number of atoms). If the number of valence electrons per primitive cell is odd, the material is necessarily a metal (as long as the nonmagnetic, spin-degenerate band picture is used with the period of the primitive cell as it is); if it is even, the material can be an insulator, but in three dimensions the bands may overlap and make it a metal ($\mathrm{Ca}$, $\mathrm{Mg}$). $\mathrm{Si}$ has two atoms and eight valence electrons per primitive cell, so four bands are filled and it is a semiconductor.
  • A filled band cannot carry current. This follows from the cancellation $v(-k)=-v(k)$ and from the fact that, even if we try to shift the states with an electric field, all the destination states are occupied (the Pauli exclusion principle plus $k\equiv k+G$).
  • The actual band diagram is drawn in the reduced-zone scheme along a broken line connecting the high-symmetry points ($\Gamma,X,L,K,W$ for fcc). In $\mathrm{Si}$ the VBM is at $\Gamma_{25'}$ (threefold degenerate) and the CBM is at $0.85X$, so it has an indirect gap. The photon momentum is only $5\times10^{-4}$ of the zone size, so optical absorption is a vertical transition, and an indirect transition requires a phonon. This is why radiative recombination in $\mathrm{Si}$ is remarkably inefficient compared with direct-gap semiconductors.
