Chapter 2The Seven Crystal Systems and the 14 Bravais Lattices

In Chapter 1 we saw that there are two kinds of boxes for describing a crystal structure: the conventional cell and the primitive cell. In this chapter we answer the question of what determines the shape of each of these two boxes. The answer is summed up in the following two lines from the materials of the Mochizuki Research Group study session.

The shape of the conventional cell is determined by the seven crystal systems,
and the shape of the primitive cell is determined by the 14 Bravais lattices.

Seven and 14. Where do these numbers come from? Why seven, and why 14? Why not “eight crystal systems” or “15 Bravais lattices”? The goal of this chapter is not to memorize these numbers but to be able to derive them ourselves by counting. For the 14 in particular, the key point is that “$7 \times$ (four ways of centering) $= 28$” does not hold. If we carefully remove the lattices that coincide with others and those that do not exist in the first place, we arrive at exactly 14.

At the end of this chapter we sort out the three-way tangle that confuses beginners most: trigonal, hexagonal, and rhombohedral. This is the place where the instructor's materials say, “Trigonal is a little tricky.”

What you will learn in this chapter
  • The seven crystal systems (cubic, hexagonal, trigonal, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic) and the conditions on the lattice parameters of each
  • The order of the crystal systems is “from highest to lowest symmetry,” and the height of the symmetry means the order of the point group (although the order alone does not place the systems in a single line)
  • The meaning of the ways of packing lattice points, $P, I, F, C(A,B), R$
  • Why we get 14 and not $7 \times 4 = 28$ — the lattices that coincide with others (tetragonal-$C$ = tetragonal-$P$, etc.) and those that do not exist
  • The explicit forms of the primitive basis vectors of each Bravais lattice, the volume ratios, and the numbers of lattice points
  • The relation among trigonal, rhombohedral, and hexagonal, and how to choose the $R$ centering and the hexagonal axes (obverse setting)
  • Once the Bravais lattice is fixed, the Brillouin zone is fixed (a bridge to Chapter 3)
Prerequisites: Chapter 1 (lattices and bases, the primitive cell, the conventional cell, transformation matrices and volume ratios). Computing determinants. The details of symmetry operations are treated in Chapter 4, so here an intuitive understanding of “there is a rotation axis / there is none” is enough.

2.1 The Seven Crystal Systems

The conventional cell is a parallelepiped. Its shape is determined by six parameters: the lengths $a, b, c$ of the three edges and the three angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$. Here $\alpha$ is the angle between $\bb$ and $\cc$, $\beta$ is the angle between $\cc$ and $\aa$, and $\gamma$ is the angle between $\aa$ and $\bb$.

Depending on what constraints are imposed on these six parameters, parallelepipeds are classified into seven types. These are the crystal systems.

Caution: “$\neq$” means “may be equal, but equality is not required”

The $a \neq b \neq c$ in Table 2.1 does not mean that “$a$ and $b$ must not happen to coincide numerically.” It means that equality is not guaranteed by symmetry.

For example, suppose that a measurement of an orthorhombic material happens to give $a = b$. If there is no fourfold rotation axis, it is orthorhombic, not tetragonal. What determines the crystal system is the symmetry operations, not the numerical values of the lattice parameters. This point actually becomes a problem when determining the space group from experimental data.

Why this order? — From highest to lowest symmetry

The instructor's materials say, “In fact, this order puts the systems with higher symmetry on top. Note: Higher symmetry = more group elements, that is, more symmetry operations.” The rightmost column of Table 2.1 gives the number of group elements (the order of the point group).

The $m\bar{3}m$ of the cubic system has as many as 48 symmetry operations. By contrast, the $\bar{1}$ of the triclinic system has only two: the identity operation and the inversion. We will actually construct these point groups in Chapter 4; what we should take away now is that “the ordering of the crystal systems is meaningful.”

However, the seven systems are not lined up by the order alone. In Table 2.1, trigonal ($\bar{3}m$, order 12) comes above tetragonal ($4/mmm$, order 16), so the order does not decrease monotonically. Since these two are not related as subgroups of each other, the order alone cannot place them in a sequence in the first place. The actual ordering should be understood as the conventional order of the International Tables, which follows the group–subgroup relations ($m\bar{3}m \supset 4/mmm$, $m\bar{3}m \supset \bar{3}m$, $6/mmm \supset \bar{3}m$, and so on).

This ranking matters when we consider phase transitions. Many materials are in a highly symmetric phase at high temperatures (for example, the cubic perovskite $Pm\bar{3}m$), and on cooling the symmetry is lowered and they become tetragonal or orthorhombic. A famous example is $\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$, which undergoes as many as three transitions, cubic → tetragonal → orthorhombic → rhombohedral; we treat it in Chapter 19.

2.2 Why Seven? — Classification by Symmetry

If we merely “impose constraints on the six parameters,” it seems that there should be many more possible combinations. Why does the list stop at seven?

To state the conclusion first, the crystal systems are defined not by the lattice parameters but by symmetry (the point group). The conditions on the lattice parameters appear only as a consequence, as necessary conditions for that symmetry to hold.

Definition: Crystal system (the correct definition)

The crystal systems are the classification of lattices by their point-group symmetry (that is, the set of symmetry operations that leave the lattice points fixed, called the holohedry). There are exactly seven possible holohedries in three dimensions, namely

$$ \bar{1},\quad 2/m,\quad mmm,\quad 4/mmm,\quad \bar{3}m,\quad 6/mmm,\quad m\bar{3}m $$

.

Derivation: Why the condition for a cubic lattice is $a=b=c$, $\alpha=\beta=\gamma=90^\circ$

To be cubic means to have four threefold rotation axes along the $\langle111\rangle$ directions (as we will see in Chapter 4, this is the minimal condition that characterizes the cubic system; it means the point group is $23$ or higher). Let us derive the relations among the lattice parameters from this condition.

Step 1: Consider the $120^\circ$ rotation $C_3$ about $[111]$. In Cartesian coordinates, this operation permutes the three axes cyclically:

$$ C_3: \quad x \to y \to z \to x $$

In matrix form,

$$ C_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0&0&1 \\ 1&0&0 \\ 0&1&0 \end{pmatrix} $$

(Indeed, $(1,0,0)^{\mathsf T} \mapsto (0,1,0)^{\mathsf T}$.)

Step 2: For this operation to be a symmetry operation of the lattice, it must map lattice translation vectors to lattice translation vectors. Let the basis vectors be $\aa, \bb, \cc$. Since $C_3$ permutes the axes cyclically,

$$ \aa \to \bb \to \cc \to \aa $$

is required (this is what we get if the axes are chosen suitably).

Step 3: Since a rotation does not change lengths, $\abs{\aa} = \abs{\bb} = \abs{\cc}$, that is, $a = b = c$. Since a rotation does not change angles either, the angle between $\aa$ and $\bb$ equals the angle between $\bb$ and $\cc$, and also equals the angle between $\cc$ and $\aa$: $\alpha = \beta = \gamma$.

Step 4: So far we have obtained only the conditions $a=b=c$, $\alpha=\beta=\gamma$ for a rhombohedral lattice. To make the lattice cubic, we additionally need fourfold rotation axes (about $\langle100\rangle$). The $90^\circ$ rotation about $[001]$ requires $\aa \to \bb \to -\aa$, so $\aa \perp \bb$, that is, $\gamma = 90^\circ$. Similarly, $\alpha = \beta = 90^\circ$. ∎

From this derivation we see that the rhombohedral (trigonal-$R$) and cubic systems are separated only by whether a fourfold axis is present or not. In fact, the primitive cell of a cubic crystal is a rhombohedron (the bcc example we saw in Section 1.4.1 of Chapter 1), a rhombohedron with the special angles $\alpha = 60^\circ$ (fcc) or $109.47^\circ$ (bcc).

Example 2.1: The primitive cell of fcc is a rhombohedron with $\alpha = 60^\circ$

For the fcc basis vectors given in Chapter 1, $\aa_1 = \frac{a}{2}(0,1,1)$, $\aa_2 = \frac{a}{2}(1,0,1)$, $\aa_3 = \frac{a}{2}(1,1,0)$, find the lengths and the mutual angles.

Solution. The length is

$$ \abs{\aa_1} = \frac{a}{2}\sqrt{0^2+1^2+1^2} = \frac{a}{\sqrt2} $$

and it is the same for all three vectors. The inner product is

$$ \aa_1\cdot\aa_2 = \frac{a^2}{4}(0\cdot1 + 1\cdot0 + 1\cdot1) = \frac{a^2}{4} $$

Hence

$$ \cos\alpha = \frac{\aa_1\cdot\aa_2}{\abs{\aa_1}\abs{\aa_2}} = \frac{a^2/4}{a^2/2} = \frac12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha = 60^\circ $$

The same holds for all three pairs. Therefore the primitive cell of fcc is a rhombohedron with edge $a/\sqrt2$ and angle $60^\circ$. ∎

For bcc, we already obtained $109.47^\circ$ in the example of Section 1.4.1 of Chapter 1. Indeed, $\cos^{-1}(-1/3) = 109.47^\circ$.

2.3 Ways of Centering the Lattice — $P, I, F, C, R$

The crystal system determined the shape of the conventional cell. Next we consider how to place lattice points inside a box of that shape. This is what gives rise to the Bravais lattices.

$I$body-centered$(0,0,0)$, $\left(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12\right)$2
$F$face-centered$(0,0,0)$, $\left(0,\frac12,\frac12\right)$, $\left(\frac12,0,\frac12\right)$, $\left(\frac12,\frac12,0\right)$4

$A$, $B$, $C$ differ only in the names of the axes

$C$ centering adds a lattice point at the “center of the $\aa\bb$ face,” $A$ centering at the “$\bb\cc$ face,” and $B$ centering at the “$\cc\aa$ face.” However, the way the axes $a, b, c$ are named is merely a human choice, so $A$, $B$, and $C$ are essentially the same lattice, and they can be transformed into one another by relabeling the axes.

Therefore, when counting the Bravais lattices, we count them as one. By convention we use $C$ as the representative (although for the monoclinic system $B$ is sometimes used, depending on the setting).

2.4 Why 14 and Not 28?

Seven crystal systems $\times$ four ways of centering ($P, I, F, C$) = 28. Yet the answer is 14. The number is cut in half. The reason comes down to the following two points.

Two reasons why the number drops to 14

  1. Duplication: the centered lattice turns out to be identical to another, smaller lattice. It produces no new lattice, so we do not count it.
  2. Loss of symmetry: adding the centering destroys the symmetry that the crystal system requires, and the lattice becomes one of a different (lower-symmetry) crystal system. We do not count this either.

However, these two sieves alone do not bring us to 14. The $R$ centering is a fifth centering that is not among the four, $P, I, F, C$; it is not a survivor from among the 28 but something added from outside. Indeed, when we actually sieve the 28, we get cubic 3 + hexagonal 1 + trigonal 0 (trigonal-$P$ is the same lattice as hexagonal-$P$, so it drops out as a duplicate) + tetragonal 2 + orthorhombic 4 + monoclinic 2 + triclinic 1 = 13, and adding trigonal-$R$ makes 14.

2.4.1 An example of duplication: tetragonal-$C$ = tetragonal-$P$

Derivation: Adding $C$ centering to a tetragonal lattice does not produce a new lattice

Take the conventional cell of a tetragonal lattice to be a rectangular box whose base is a square, with the $a$ axis and the $b$ axis both of length $a$ and the $c$ axis of length $c$. Suppose that we add a lattice point at the $C$ centering position, that is, at the center of the base, $\left(\frac12,\frac12,0\right)$.

Viewed from above (along the $c$ axis), the lattice points sit at the vertices and the center of the square. Here we choose new axes along the diagonals of the square:

$$ \aa' = \frac{1}{2}(\aa + \bb), \qquad \bb' = \frac{1}{2}(-\aa + \bb), \qquad \cc' = \cc $$

Their lengths are

$$ \abs{\aa'} = \frac12\sqrt{a^2+a^2} = \frac{a}{\sqrt2}, \qquad \abs{\bb'} = \frac{a}{\sqrt2} $$

and are equal, and the inner product is

$$ \aa'\cdot\bb' = \frac14(\aa+\bb)\cdot(-\aa+\bb) = \frac14(-a^2 + 0 - 0 + a^2) = 0 $$

(We used $\aa\cdot\bb = 0$.) That is, $\aa' \perp \bb'$. Moreover, $\cc'$ is orthogonal to both.

Therefore the new cell is a rectangular box with a square base of side $a/\sqrt2$ and height $c$; that is, it is a smaller simple tetragonal lattice (tetragonal-$P$). Checking the number of lattice points: the original $C$-centered cell contains two, and the volume of the new cell is $\frac{a}{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{a}{\sqrt2}\cdot c = \frac{a^2c}{2}$, half the original, so it contains one lattice point. The counts are consistent. ∎

Hence tetragonal-$C$ is not a new Bravais lattice; it is simply tetragonal-$P$ with the axes rotated by $45^\circ$ and taken finer by a factor of $\sqrt2$. We do not count it.

2.4.2 An example of symmetry breaking: cubic-$C$ is not cubic

What happens if we add $C$ centering (on the $\aa\bb$ face only) to a cubic lattice? Because the $\aa\bb$ face alone is singled out, the $\cc$ axis is no longer equivalent to the other two axes. Then the threefold rotation axis along $\langle111\rangle$ ($x\to y\to z\to x$) is lost.

Once the threefold axis is lost, the lattice is no longer cubic. In fact, the same transformation as in Section 2.4.1 turns it into tetragonal-$P$. Hence cubic-$C$ is not counted.

The total is $3+1+1+2+4+2+1 = 14$. These are the 14 Bravais lattices.

Why do all four centerings survive only for the orthorhombic system?

In an orthorhombic crystal $a\neq b\neq c$, so the three axes are inequivalent from the start. Hence even if we add $C$ centering and single out the $\aa\bb$ face, no symmetry is lost (there was never a symmetry operation that interchanges the three axes). Also, since $a\neq b$, the $45^\circ$-rotation trick of Section 2.4.1 cannot be used. Indeed, if we take $\aa' = \frac12(\aa+\bb)$, $\bb' = \frac12(-\aa+\bb)$, the lengths are equal, $\abs{\aa'} = \abs{\bb'} = \frac12\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$, even for $a\neq b$, but the inner product is $\aa'\cdot\bb' = \frac14(b^2-a^2) \neq 0$, so orthogonality is broken. The new base is not a rectangle but a rhombus, and it cannot be used as the cell of an orthorhombic lattice, which requires $\alpha=\beta=\gamma=90^\circ$ (only for $a=b$ are the axes orthogonal, and we return to the tetragonal argument of Section 2.4.1).

As a result, all four of $P, I, F, C$ survive as distinct lattices. This is the somewhat paradoxical situation that “the lower the symmetry, the more kinds of lattices there are to distinguish.”

Example 2.2: Show that $I$ for the monoclinic system is the same as $C$

For the monoclinic system ($\alpha=\gamma=90^\circ\neq\beta$, with the $b$ axis the unique twofold axis), show that the body-centered lattice $I$ is the same lattice as the base-centered lattice $C$.

Solution. The lattice points of the monoclinic $I$ lattice are $(0,0,0)$ and $\left(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12\right)$ (in fractional coordinates). Here we choose new axes

$$ \aa' = \aa, \qquad \bb' = \bb, \qquad \cc' = \aa + \cc $$

(the $b$ axis is not moved, so the twofold axis is preserved). The determinant of this transformation is

$$ \det\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\1&0&1\end{pmatrix} = 1 $$

so the volume does not change, and the number of lattice points remains two.

Expressing the original body-center point $\frac12(\aa+\bb+\cc)$ in the new basis, since $\cc = \cc' - \aa'$ we have

$$ \frac12(\aa+\bb+\cc) = \frac12(\aa' + \bb' + \cc' - \aa') = \frac12(\bb' + \cc') $$

This is the center of the $\bb'\cc'$ face of the new cell, that is, an $A$ centering. Since $A$ and $C$ are interchanged by renaming the axes (Section 2.3), the monoclinic $I$ is the same lattice as $C$ (or $A$). ∎

The instructor's materials state: “(You may notice this if you look closely, but) basically, when the Bravais lattice is a simple lattice ($P$), the conventional and primitive cells have exactly the same structure.” In Table 2.3, these are the rows with $n_{\text{lp}} = 1$.

Counting them, the $P$ lattices with $n_{\text{lp}} = 1$ are the six cubic, hexagonal, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, and triclinic ones (trigonal-$P$ is the same lattice as hexagonal-$P$, so it is not counted separately here). 2.6 Sorting Out Trigonal, Rhombohedral, and Hexagonal

This is the easiest place to get confused. There are three terms, and what they refer to differs with the context. Let us sort them out.

Using the three terms correctly

  • hexagonal: one of the crystal systems. It has a sixfold rotation axis (or a sixfold screw axis, or $\bar{6}$). Its only Bravais lattice is hexagonal-$P$.
  • trigonal: one of the crystal systems. It has a threefold rotation axis (or $\bar{3}$) but no sixfold axis. Its Bravais lattice is either hexagonal-$P$ or trigonal-$R$.
  • rhombohedral: the name of a lattice (trigonal-$R$), and also the name of a cell shape (the parallelepiped with $a=b=c$, $\alpha=\beta=\gamma\neq90^\circ$).

Trigonal is not the same as rhombohedral

This is the biggest pitfall. There are 25 trigonal space groups, but only 7 of them have an $R$ lattice; the remaining 18 use the hexagonal-$P$ lattice.

This is the situation expressed by the parenthesized $P$ in “Trigonal: $R$ ($P$)” in the table of the instructor's materials. Of the examples given, $\mathrm{La_2O_3}$ ($P\bar{3}m1$) is trigonal but has a $P$ lattice, whereas the high-temperature phase of $\mathrm{LaMnO_3}$ ($R\bar{3}c$) is trigonal with an $R$ lattice ($\mathrm{LaMnO_3}$ becomes orthorhombic $Pnma$ at room temperature because of a cooperative Jahn–Teller distortion; see Chapter 10).

2.6.1 The meaning of $R$ centering — hexagonal axes and rhombohedral axes

A rhombohedral lattice can be described in two ways.

(a) Rhombohedral axes. We use the parallelepiped with $a=b=c$, $\alpha=\beta=\gamma\neq90^\circ$. This is a primitive cell, and it contains one lattice point.

(b) Hexagonal axes. We use the hexagonal cell with $a=b\neq c$, $\alpha=\beta=90^\circ$, $\gamma=120^\circ$. This is a conventional cell, and it contains three lattice points ($R$ centering).

Derivation: Why the $R$ centering positions are $\left(\frac23,\frac13,\frac13\right)$

Let the hexagonal axes be $\aa_h, \bb_h, \cc_h$ and the rhombohedral axes be $\aa_r, \bb_r, \cc_r$. The standard relation connecting the two (the obverse setting) is

$$ \aa_r = \frac{2}{3}\aa_h + \frac{1}{3}\bb_h + \frac{1}{3}\cc_h,\quad \bb_r = -\frac{1}{3}\aa_h + \frac{1}{3}\bb_h + \frac{1}{3}\cc_h,\quad \cc_r = -\frac{1}{3}\aa_h - \frac{2}{3}\bb_h + \frac{1}{3}\cc_h $$

The coefficients of $\aa_r$ themselves give the fractional coordinates $\left(\frac23,\frac13,\frac13\right)$ of a lattice point as seen from the hexagonal cell. Similarly, computing $\aa_r + \bb_r$ gives

$$ \aa_r + \bb_r = \left(\frac23-\frac13\right)\aa_h + \left(\frac13+\frac13\right)\bb_h + \left(\frac13+\frac13\right)\cc_h = \frac13\aa_h + \frac23\bb_h + \frac23\cc_h $$

which is the second centering point $\left(\frac13,\frac23,\frac23\right)$.

Let us check the volume ratio. For the transformation matrix

$$ M = \begin{pmatrix} \frac23 & \frac13 & \frac13 \\[2pt] -\frac13 & \frac13 & \frac13 \\[2pt] -\frac13 & -\frac23 & \frac13 \end{pmatrix} $$

the determinant, after factoring the common factor $\frac13$ out of each of the three rows, is

$$ \det M = \frac{1}{27}\det\begin{pmatrix}2&1&1\\-1&1&1\\-1&-2&1\end{pmatrix} $$

Expanding this $3\times3$ determinant along the first row, we obtain

$$ 2(1\cdot1 - 1\cdot(-2)) - 1((-1)\cdot1 - 1\cdot(-1)) + 1((-1)(-2) - 1\cdot(-1)) = 2(3) - 1(0) + 1(3) = 9 $$

Hence $\det M = 9/27 = 1/3$. The volume of the rhombohedral cell is $1/3$ that of the hexagonal cell; that is, the hexagonal cell contains three lattice points. This agrees with the definition of $R$ centering. ∎

Example 2.3: Describing a cubic crystal with a rhombohedral cell

In Section “5-3. Changing the choice of cell (advanced)” of the instructor's materials, there is a matrix that converts a $Pm\bar{3}m$ perovskite into an $R\bar{3}c$-like cell in VESTA.

$$ \text{cubic} \to \text{rhombohedral conventional}: \quad \begin{pmatrix} 0&1&2 \\ -1&0&2 \\ 1&-1&2 \end{pmatrix} $$

Calculate the determinant of this matrix and find by what factor the cell becomes larger.

Solution. Expand along the first row.

$$ \det = 0\bigl(0\cdot2 - 2\cdot(-1)\bigr) - 1\bigl((-1)\cdot 2 - 2\cdot 1\bigr) + 2\bigl((-1)(-1) - 0\cdot 1\bigr) $$ $$ = 0 - 1(-2-2) + 2(1-0) = 4 + 2 = 6 $$

That is, the volume becomes six times larger. From the cubic perovskite ($Z=1$, five atoms), we obtain a hexagonal cell with 30 atoms. This agrees with the fact that the conventional cell of an $R\bar{3}c$ perovskite (such as $\mathrm{LaAlO_3}$) has $Z=6$.

Another matrix in the materials,

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1&1&0 \\ 0&1&1 \\ 1&0&1 \end{pmatrix} $$

has $\det = 1(1-0) - 1(0-1) + 0 = 1 + 1 = 2$, so the volume doubles. This gives the rhombohedral primitive cell ($Z=2$, ten atoms). ∎

2.7 What Can We Learn from the Bravais Lattice?

We have put a great deal of effort into classification so far, but what is the benefit of knowing the Bravais lattice in the first place? To borrow the words of the instructor's materials: “Everyone should know this much by now. Understood… but so what? I think many people will wonder.” There are three answers.

2.7.1 The positions of the X-ray diffraction peaks are determined

As we will prove in Chapter 3, the positions of the diffraction peaks (the values of $2\theta$) are determined only by the lengths of the reciprocal lattice vectors $\GG$, and $\GG$ is determined by the Bravais lattice and the lattice constants. The basis (which element is placed where) has no effect at all on the positions where peaks can appear. What the basis affects is the structure factor, that is, the peak heights.

The experiment in the instructor's materials (a calculation with VESTA) shows this vividly. Fixing the lattice constant and taking

  • NaCl rock-salt type ($Fm\bar{3}m$)
  • NaCl zinc-blende type ($F\bar{4}3m$)
  • NaCl$_2$ fluorite type ($Fm\bar{3}m$)
  • SrSO$_4$ rock-salt type ($F\bar{4}3m$)

we find that the peak positions all coincide perfectly. This is because all four are cubic-$F$ and, moreover, the lattice constants have been made equal. When the basis changes, what changes is the peak height (the structure factor), and a reflection whose structure factor becomes exactly zero may even disappear.

2.7.2 The shape of the Brillouin zone is determined

The first Brillouin zone is the Wigner–Seitz cell that we saw in Section 1.5 of Chapter 1, constructed not in real space but in reciprocal space. Since the reciprocal lattice is uniquely determined by the Bravais lattice, the shape of the Brillouin zone is also determined by the Bravais lattice alone.

As the materials put it: “The primitive cell of the reciprocal space of a crystal is the Brillouin zone. The Bravais lattice determines the Brillouin zone.”

Table 2.4: Bravais lattices and the shapes of their Brillouin zones (cubic system)
Real-space Bravais latticeReciprocal latticeShape of the first Brillouin zoneHigh-symmetry points
cubic-$P$cubic-$P$cube$\Gamma, X, M, R$
cubic-$I$ (bcc)cubic-$F$rhombic dodecahedron$\Gamma, H, N, P$
cubic-$F$ (fcc)cubic-$I$truncated octahedron$\Gamma, X, L, W, K, U$

The horizontal axis of the band diagram of Si (diamond structure, cubic-$F$) is always labeled $\Gamma \to X \to U|K \to \Gamma \to L \to W \to X$ because the Brillouin zone of fcc is a truncated octahedron. This very figure appears on the slide “1-6. The Bravais lattice uniquely determines the reciprocal space” in the materials.

2.7.3 The upper limit of the symmetry is determined

The Bravais lattice determines the upper limit of the symmetry that can be placed on it. A fourfold rotation axis cannot be placed on a hexagonal-$P$ lattice. This constraint is the source of the finite answers, the “32 point groups” and the “230 space groups,” in the next chapters, Chapter 4 and Chapter 5.

2.8 Summary and Exercises

2.8.1 Summary

  • The shape of the conventional cell is determined by the seven crystal systems. A crystal system is defined not by the lattice constants but by the point-group symmetry of the lattice (the holohedry), and in three dimensions there are only seven kinds: $\bar{1}, 2/m, mmm, 4/mmm, \bar{3}m, 6/mmm, m\bar{3}m$.
  • The crystal systems are listed in order of decreasing symmetry, where the height of the symmetry is the order of the point group (cubic 48 to triclinic 2). However, the order of the point group alone does not give a single linear ordering (trigonal and tetragonal are not subgroups of each other), and what can be said about phase transitions is that “for a continuous transition, the point group of the low-symmetry phase is a subgroup of the point group of the high-symmetry phase,” not that one descends this sequence step by step (as with the orthorhombic phase $mm2$ and the rhombohedral phase $3m$ of $\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$, two consecutive phases need not be related as subgroups. Even so, every phase is a subgroup of the cubic parent phase $m\bar{3}m$).
  • The shape of the primitive cell is determined by the 14 Bravais lattices. From $7 \times 4 = 28$, removing the duplicates (tetragonal-$C$ = tetragonal-$P$, etc.) and the symmetry-breaking case (cubic-$C$) leaves 13, and adding to this trigonal-$R$, which has the fifth centering $R$, gives 14.
  • Only the orthorhombic system has all four of $P, I, F, C$. Because its three axes are inequivalent to begin with, adding a centering loses no symmetry, and the trick of a $45^\circ$ rotation cannot be used either.
  • For a $P$ lattice, the conventional cell = the primitive cell. In Table 2.3 there are six $P$ lattices with $n_{\text{lp}} = 1$ (trigonal-$P$ is identical to hexagonal-$P$ and is therefore not counted), and the remaining eight are the lattices with $I, F, C, R$.
  • Trigonal ≠ rhombohedral. Of the 25 trigonal space groups, only seven have an $R$ lattice, and the rest use a hexagonal-$P$ lattice. The $R$-centering lattice points are $\left(\frac23,\frac13,\frac13\right)$, $\left(\frac13,\frac23,\frac23\right)$ in hexagonal axes, and the volume ratio is $1/3$.
  • Once the Bravais lattice and the lattice constants are given, the positions where X-ray diffraction peaks can appear, the shape of the Brillouin zone, and the upper limit of the symmetry are determined. This is the practical benefit of classifying Bravais lattices.

2.8.2 Exercises

Exercise 2.1: Showing that hexagonal-$I$ does not exist

Suppose that a lattice point is added at the center $\left(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12\right)$ of the conventional cell of the hexagonal system ($a=b\neq c$, $\gamma=120^\circ$).

(1) Show that this arrangement does not have sixfold rotational symmetry.

(2) Which Bravais lattice of which crystal system is the resulting lattice?

Hint: (1) Examine where the added point goes under a $60^\circ$ rotation about the $c$ axis. (2) If we choose the axes anew as $\bm{A}_1 = \aa+\bb$, $\bm{A}_2 = -\aa+\bb$, $\bm{A}_3 = \cc$ (mutually orthogonal, with $\abs{\bm{A}_1} = a$ and $\abs{\bm{A}_2} = \sqrt3\,a$), the lattice points become the four points $(0,0,0)$, $\left(\frac12,\frac12,0\right)$, $\left(\frac12,0,\frac12\right)$, $\left(0,\frac12,\frac12\right)$. That is, it is the face-centered orthorhombic lattice (orthorhombic-$F$).

Exercise 2.2: Tetragonal-$F$ = tetragonal-$I$

Show that adding face centering $F$ to the tetragonal system gives a smaller body-centered tetragonal lattice $I$.

Hint: As in Section 2.4.1, choose the axes anew along the diagonals of the base. With $\aa' = \frac12(\aa+\bb)$, $\bb' = \frac12(-\aa+\bb)$, $\cc'=\cc$, among the original face-center points $\left(\frac12,\frac12,0\right)$ becomes a vertex of the new lattice, and $\left(\frac12,0,\frac12\right)$ and $\left(0,\frac12,\frac12\right)$ become body centers of the new cell.

Exercise 2.3: Predicting that the reciprocal lattice of bcc is fcc

As preparation for Chapter 3, consider the following. The primitive translation vectors of bcc were of the type $\frac{a}{2}(-1,1,1)$, and those of fcc of the type $\frac{a}{2}(0,1,1)$.

(1) Using the definition of the reciprocal lattice vectors $\bb_1 = 2\pi\dfrac{\aa_2\times\aa_3}{\aa_1\cdot(\aa_2\times\aa_3)}$, find the primitive reciprocal lattice vectors of bcc.

(2) Verify that the vectors obtained have the form of the primitive vectors of an fcc lattice with lattice constant $4\pi/a$.

Hint: (1) Use $V_{\text{p}} = a^3/2$. The answer is of the type $\frac{2\pi}{a}(0,1,1)$.

Exercise 2.4: Classifying real materials

For the following materials, read off the crystal system and the Bravais lattice from the space-group symbol. Also find the number of atoms in the conventional cell and in the primitive cell.

(1) $\mathrm{TiO_2}$ rutile type $P4_2/mnm$ ($Z=2$)
(2) $\mathrm{ZnO}$ wurtzite type $P6_3mc$ ($Z=2$)
(3) $\mathrm{LaAlO_3}$ $R\bar{3}c$ ($Z=6$ in hexagonal axes)
(4) $\mathrm{BaCO_3}$ witherite (aragonite type) $Pnma$ ($Z=4$)

Hint: The first letter is the centering symbol, and the crystal system is read from the symbols that follow (see Chapter 5 for details). For $P$, conventional = primitive. For the $R$ in (3), there are three lattice points in hexagonal axes, so the number of atoms in the primitive cell is $1/3$ of that.

Exercise 2.5: Why is there no lattice with fivefold symmetry? (A preview of Chapter 4)

A regular pentagon cannot fill the plane without gaps. As preparation for showing this from the translational symmetry of a lattice, consider the following.

Suppose that lattice points are lined up with spacing $a$ on a straight line containing lattice points. Take one of them as the origin, and suppose there is an $n$-fold rotation axis there. Let $\aa$ ($\abs{\aa} = a$) be the position vector of the neighboring lattice point. Then $R(\theta)\aa$ and $R(-\theta)\aa$, obtained by rotating it by $\pm\theta$ ($\theta = 2\pi/n$), must also be lattice vectors. Since the sum of lattice vectors is again a lattice vector, $R(\theta)\aa + R(-\theta)\aa = 2\cos\theta\,\aa$ is also a lattice vector. But this is parallel to $\aa$, and the length of a lattice vector parallel to $\aa$ is restricted to integer multiples of $a$. From this fact, derive

$$ 2a\cos\frac{2\pi}{n} = ma \qquad (m \in \mathbb{Z}) $$

and show that only $n = 1,2,3,4,6$ are allowed.

Hint: From $\abs{\cos(2\pi/n)} \le 1$, $m \in \{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$. Find the $n$ corresponding to each $m$. This is the crystallographic restriction theorem, which is treated in detail in Section 4.2 of Chapter 4.
