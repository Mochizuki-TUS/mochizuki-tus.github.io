Chapter 4The 32 Crystallographic Point Groups

In Chapters 1 through 3 we acquired the means to describe a crystal in the language of “what is placed where.” From this chapter on, we enumerate what symmetry such a crystal can have.

The symmetry of a crystal divides into operations that do not involve translation (rotations, reflections, inversion, and so on) and operations that do (screw axes and glide planes). The set consisting of the former alone is a point group, and there are exactly 32 kinds allowed for three-dimensional crystals. The goal of this chapter is not to memorize the number 32 but to be able to follow for yourself why the list ends at 32.

The key is the crystallographic restriction theorem. From the single condition that translational symmetry exists, the allowed rotations are limited to the five kinds: 1-, 2-, 3-, 4-, and 6-fold. Neither fivefold nor sevenfold symmetry can exist. This theorem narrows the seemingly infinite possibilities for symmetry down to a finite number at a stroke.

At the end of the chapter we touch on the relation between point groups and physical properties. The presence or absence of centrosymmetry decides whether piezoelectricity is possible, and the presence or absence of polarity decides whether pyroelectricity is possible; you will be able to understand, in the language of point groups, why the search for ferroelectrics is said to be “harder than the search for new superconductors.” This is also an area that the Mochizuki Research Group actually studies.

What you will learn in this chapter
  • Matrix representations of symmetry operations—rotation $C_n$, reflection $\sigma$, inversion $i$, rotoinversion $\bar{n}$, rotoreflection $S_n$
  • The crystallographic restriction theorem: from $2\cos(2\pi/n) \in \mathbb{Z}$, only $n=1,2,3,4,6$ are allowed
  • The correspondence between Schoenflies symbols and international (Hermann–Mauguin) notation, and the fact that the two define $\bar{n}$ and $S_n$ differently
  • The list of the 32 crystallographic point groups and their classification by crystal system
  • The 11 point groups with centrosymmetry (paraelectricity only) and the 21 without (of which the 20 other than $432$ can show piezoelectricity), and the 10 polar point groups (pyroelectricity)
  • The hierarchy: polar = the crystal structure has a polarization; a pyroelectric whose polarization can be reversed by an electric field = a ferroelectric
  • The idea of Laue groups, and the fact that the presence or absence of centrosymmetry is hard to see in X-ray diffraction
Prerequisites: Chapters 1–3. Products of 3×3 matrices, the trace, and the determinant. The axioms of a group are treated rigorously in Chapter 6, but in this chapter you may read on with the intuition of “a collection of symmetry operations.”

4.1 Symmetry Operations and Their Matrix Representations

4.1.1 What is a symmetry operation?

Definition: Symmetry operation

An operation that, when applied to an object (here, a crystal), carries it into a state completely indistinguishable from the state before the operation is called a symmetry operation of that object.

The symmetry operations that make up a point group are restricted to those that leave at least one point fixed (that is, that involve no translation). The axes and planes passing through that fixed point are the symmetry elements.

Physical Meaning: What the sign of the determinant means

An operation with determinant $+1$ is called a proper rotation, and one with determinant $-1$ an improper rotation.

$\det = +1$ is an operation that keeps a right-handed system right-handed, and $\det = -1$ is an operation that flips a right-handed system into a left-handed one. Therefore a chiral molecule (a molecule whose right-handed and left-handed forms can be distinguished) cannot have an operation with $\det=-1$ as a symmetry operation. Conversely, the point groups that have no operation with $\det=-1$ at all (the 11 groups $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 222, 32, 422, 622, 23, 432$) are the point groups that allow chiral crystals.

This is why quartz ($\alpha$-SiO$_2$, point group $32$) occurs as right-handed and left-handed crystals and exhibits optical rotation.

4.1.2 Matrix representation

A symmetry operation is a linear transformation that does not move the origin, so it can be represented by a $3\times3$ orthogonal matrix.

Derivation: The rotation matrix for $n$-fold rotation about the $z$ axis

The operation of rotating by an angle $\phi$ about the $z$ axis is a rotation within the $xy$ plane. Suppose a point $(x,y)$ moves to $(x',y')$. Writing $x = r\cos\alpha$ and $y=r\sin\alpha$ in polar coordinates, after the rotation $\alpha \to \alpha+\phi$, so

$$ x' = r\cos(\alpha+\phi) = r(\cos\alpha\cos\phi - \sin\alpha\sin\phi) = x\cos\phi - y\sin\phi $$ $$ y' = r\sin(\alpha+\phi) = r(\sin\alpha\cos\phi + \cos\alpha\sin\phi) = x\sin\phi + y\cos\phi $$

The coordinate $z$ does not change. Written as a matrix,

$$ \begin{equation} C_n(z) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi & -\sin\phi & 0 \\ \sin\phi & \cos\phi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \phi = \frac{2\pi}{n} \label{eq:4-rotation} \end{equation} $$

∎ The trace is

$$ \begin{equation} \Tr\, C_n = 2\cos\phi + 1 = 2\cos\frac{2\pi}{n} + 1 \label{eq:4-trace-rotation} \end{equation} $$

This trace plays the leading role both in the crystallographic restriction theorem of the next section and in the discussion of characters in Chapter 8.

Example 4.1: Matrices and traces of the main operations

Write down the matrix and the trace of each of the following operations. (a) Inversion $i$ (b) Reflection $\sigma_h$ in the $xy$ plane (c) $C_2(z)$ (d) $C_3(z)$ (e) $C_4(z)$

Solution.

$$ i = \begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix},\ \Tr = -3; \qquad \sigma_h = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix},\ \Tr = 1 $$ $$ C_2(z) = \begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\ \Tr = -1; \qquad C_4(z) = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\ \Tr = 1 $$ $$ C_3(z) = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&0\\[3pt] \frac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\frac12&0\\[3pt] 0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\ \Tr = 0 $$

Checking with Eq. \eqref{eq:4-trace-rotation}, for $n=2$: $2(-1)+1=-1$, for $n=3$: $2(-\tfrac12)+1=0$, and for $n=4$: $2(0)+1=1$. All agree with the traces in (c)–(e). ∎

Math Note: The trace does not depend on the choice of basis

Even for the same rotation operation, the components of the matrix change if the choice of coordinate axes is changed. However, the trace does not change. A change of basis is a similarity transformation $A' = P^{-1}AP$, and from the cyclic property of the trace, $\Tr(ABC) = \Tr(BCA)$, we have

$$ \Tr A' = \Tr(P^{-1}AP) = \Tr(APP^{-1}) = \Tr A $$

Thanks to this property, the trace becomes a quantity that expresses “a property of the operation itself.” In Chapter 8 we call it the character and place it at the center of group theory. The reason the trace is given special treatment in group theory already appears here.

4.2 The Crystallographic Restriction Theorem — Why There Is No Fivefold Symmetry

Snowflakes have sixfold symmetry, and table salt has fourfold symmetry. Why, then, do we never see a crystal with fivefold symmetry? The answer is that fivefold symmetry is incompatible with translational symmetry.

Theorem 4.1 (crystallographic restriction theorem)

The rotational symmetries allowed in a lattice with translational symmetry are limited to the five values

$$ n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 $$

The cases $n=5$ and $n \ge 7$ do not occur.

Proof 1: A geometric proof using lattice translation vectors

Suppose that lattice points are lined up on a straight line with spacing $a$ (where $a$ is the length of the shortest lattice translation). Let $A$ and $B$ be two adjacent lattice points on this line ($\abs{\overrightarrow{AB}} = a$), and consider an $n$-fold rotation axis perpendicular to the plane of the page. Since the lattice has translational symmetry, if there is an $n$-fold axis at one lattice point, then the same $n$-fold axis exists at every lattice point. That is, $n$-fold axes stand at both $A$ and $B$. This fact that “two centers can be used at the same time” is the key to the argument below.

Now let $A'$ be the point obtained by rotating, about $A$ through $-\phi = -2\pi/n$, the lattice point that lies next to $A$ on the side opposite to $B$. Similarly, let $B'$ be the point obtained by rotating, about $B$ through $+\phi$, the lattice point that lies next to $B$ on the side opposite to $A$. Because there are $n$-fold axes at both $A$ and $B$, both $A'$ and $B'$ are lattice points.

Let us now consider $\overrightarrow{A'B'}$. Taking $A$ as the origin and $B = (a,\,0)$, the point $A'$ is obtained by rotating $(-a,\,0)$ through $-\phi$ about the origin, and the point $B'$ is obtained by rotating $(2a,\,0)$ through $+\phi$ about $B$, so that

$$ A' = (-a\cos\phi,\ a\sin\phi), \qquad B' = (a + a\cos\phi,\ a\sin\phi) $$

Since the two points have the same $y$ coordinate, $A'$ and $B'$ lie on a single straight line parallel to the original lattice line. The difference of their $x$ coordinates is

$$ x_{B'} - x_{A'} = (a + a\cos\phi) - (-a\cos\phi) = a + 2a\cos\phi $$

Both $A'$ and $B'$ are lattice points, and the line joining them is parallel to the original lattice line, so the spacing of the lattice points on this line is again $a$. Therefore the difference of the $x$ coordinates just obtained must be an integer multiple of $a$:

$$ a + 2a\cos\phi = m a \qquad (m \in \mathbb{Z}) $$

Dividing both sides by $a$, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} 2\cos\phi = m - 1 \equiv M \in \mathbb{Z} \label{eq:4-restriction} \end{equation} $$

Since $\abs{\cos\phi}\le1$, we have $M \in \{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$. We examine each case.

$M$$\cos\phi$$\phi$$n = 2\pi/\phi$
$-2$$-1$$180^\circ$2
$-1$$-1/2$$120^\circ$3
$0$$0$$90^\circ$4
$1$$1/2$$60^\circ$6
$2$$1$$0^\circ$ ($360^\circ$)1

Therefore only $n=1,2,3,4,6$ are possible. For $n=5$ we have $\phi=72^\circ$ and $2\cos72^\circ = 0.618$, which is not an integer. For $n=7$, $2\cos(360^\circ/7) = 1.247$, which again is not an integer. ∎

Supplement: Relation to Exercise 2.5 of Chapter 2

In Exercise 2.5 of Chapter 2, we took only one rotation center and derived $2\cos\phi = m$ ($m \in \mathbb{Z}$) from the fact that the sum of $R(\theta)\aa$ and $R(-\theta)\aa$ is again a lattice vector. Proof 1 just given is a different construction that uses two rotation centers, so the condition appears in the form $1 + 2\cos\phi = m'$ ($m' \in \mathbb{Z}$). Since $m' = m + 1$, the two conditions are equivalent, and it is of course natural that the sets of allowed $n$ coincide. All that happens is that a different choice of construction shifts the integer appearing along the way by $1$, so there is no need to worry that the form of the equation differs from that in Chapter 2.

Proof 2: An algebraic proof using the trace (this one is shorter)

Represent the rotation operation $C_n$ as a matrix with the lattice translation vectors as the basis. Since $C_n$ maps the lattice onto itself, it maps each lattice vector to a linear combination of lattice vectors with integer coefficients. Therefore the matrix $\tilde{C}_n$ in this basis has only integer entries. The trace of an integer matrix is an integer:

$$ \Tr \tilde{C}_n \in \mathbb{Z} $$

On the other hand, if we take Cartesian coordinates as the basis, Eq. \eqref{eq:4-trace-rotation} gives $\Tr C_n = 1 + 2\cos(2\pi/n)$. The trace is invariant under a change of basis (see the Math Note in Section 4.1.2), so

$$ 1 + 2\cos\frac{2\pi}{n} \in \mathbb{Z} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2\cos\frac{2\pi}{n} \in \mathbb{Z} $$

This is the same condition as Eq. \eqref{eq:4-restriction}. ∎

This proof applies unchanged to $\bar{n}$ (rotoinversion) as well (with $\Tr\bar{C}_n = -1-2\cos(2\pi/n)$, the integer condition again leads to the same conclusion).

Quasicrystals — A loophole in the theorem

In 1984, Shechtman observed a diffraction pattern with clear fivefold symmetry in electron diffraction from an Al–Mn alloy. This discovery, which seemed to contradict Theorem 4.1, was at first met with fierce criticism, but it was later confirmed to be correct and led to the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

There is no contradiction. What Theorem 4.1 assumes is that “translational symmetry is present.” A quasicrystal does not have translational symmetry. Even so, it has long-range order and gives sharp diffraction peaks. As the Penrose tiling shows, structures that are aperiodic and yet orderly really do exist.

This is an example that teaches us the importance of checking the assumptions of a theorem.

4.3 Rotoinversion and Rotoreflection — The Difference Between Two Notations

This is a point that always confuses beginners. The instructor's materials also emphasize it in red: “The definitions differ between the international notation and Schoenflies!”

Definition: Rotoinversion and rotoreflection

  • Rotoinversion $\bar{n}$ (international notation): rotate by $360^\circ/n$ about the axis, and then invert through a point on the axis. $\bar{n} = i \cdot C_n$.
  • Rotoreflection $S_n$ (Schoenflies): rotate by $360^\circ/n$ about the axis, and then reflect in the plane perpendicular to the axis. $S_n = \sigma_h \cdot C_n$.

Derivation: Correspondence between $\bar{n}$ and $S_m$

Take the $z$ axis as the symmetry axis. Let us examine the relation between the inversion $i$ and the reflection $\sigma_h$ in the plane perpendicular to the $z$ axis.

$$ i = \begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_h = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix} $$

Their product is

$$ i\,\sigma_h = \begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix} = C_2(z) $$

That is, $i = \sigma_h C_2$, or equivalently $\sigma_h = i\, C_2^{-1} = i\,C_2$ (since $C_2^2 = E$, we have $C_2^{-1}=C_2$).

Using this, let us translate $\bar{4}$, for example, into the language of $S$:

$$ \bar{4} = i\,C_4 = (\sigma_h C_2)C_4 = \sigma_h (C_2 C_4) = \sigma_h C_4^{3} = S_4^{3} $$

(Here $C_2 C_4 = C_4^2 C_4 = C_4^3$.) We have $S_4^3 = S_4^{-1}$, and $S_4$ and $S_4^{-1}$ generate the same group, so in practice we may identify $\bar{4} \leftrightarrow S_4$.

Similarly,

$$ \bar{3} = i\,C_3 = \sigma_h C_2 C_3 = \sigma_h C_6^{5} = S_6^{5} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bar{3} \leftrightarrow S_6 $$ $$ \bar{6} = i\,C_6 = \sigma_h C_2 C_6 = \sigma_h C_6^{4} = \sigma_h C_3^{2} = S_3^{5} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bar{6} \leftrightarrow S_3 \ (= C_{3h}) $$

(Note that $C_2 C_6 = C_6^3 C_6 = C_6^4 = C_3^2$, not $C_3$. We have $S_3^5 = S_3^{-1}$, and, as in the case of $\bar{4}$, $S_3$ and $S_3^{-1}$ generate the same group $C_{3h}$, so we may take the correspondence to be $\bar{6}\leftrightarrow S_3$.)

∎

The equality $\bar{2} = m$

As the second row of Table 4.2 shows, a twofold rotoinversion is exactly the same operation as a reflection. That is why the international notation does not write $\bar{2}$ but writes $m$. The $m$ in $Pm\bar{3}m$ is precisely this.

Note also that $\bar{4}$ does not contain a fourfold axis. Applying $\bar{4}$ twice gives $(\bar{4})^2 = (iC_4)^2 = i^2 C_4^2 = C_2$, so it does contain a twofold axis. However, $\bar{4}$ by itself has neither a fourfold axis nor an inversion center. This is why the zinc-blende type $F\bar{4}3m$ has no centrosymmetry and is piezoelectric (this connects with the discussion in Section 1.7.1 of Chapter 1).

A caution in the instructor's materials: horizontal and vertical

On slide 2-3 of the materials, a red frame states “horizontal = perpendicular, vertical = horizontal.” At first glance this seems backwards, but it is because these names are defined relative to the principal axis.

  • $\sigma_h$ (horizontal): a mirror plane perpendicular to the principal axis. The plane itself “lies flat,” so it is horizontal.
  • $\sigma_v$ (vertical): a mirror plane that contains the principal axis. The plane “stands upright,” so it is vertical.
  • $\sigma_d$ (dihedral): a kind of $\sigma_v$, a plane that bisects the angle between two twofold axes.

In other words, the subscript describes how the plane is oriented, not its relation to the axis. $C_{4v}$ (a fourfold axis + mirror planes containing it) and $C_{4h}$ (a fourfold axis + a mirror plane perpendicular to it) are entirely different point groups.

4.5 How to Read the International Notation

The international (Hermann–Mauguin) notation is not a mere string of symbols. Each position is fixed to represent symmetry elements along a particular direction. This rule carries over unchanged to the space-group symbols of Chapter 5, so we make sure to grasp it firmly here.

The three positions of the international symbol and the directions for each crystal system

A point-group symbol consists of at most three positions, and each position represents symmetry elements along a prescribed direction (viewing direction (Blickrichtung)).

Crystal systemFirst positionSecond positionThird position
Triclinic— (no direction specified)——
Monoclinic$[010]$ (or $[001]$)——
Orthorhombic$[100]$$[010]$$[001]$
Tetragonal$[001]$$[100]$$[110]$
Trigonal / Hexagonal$[001]$$[100]$$[210]$
Cubic$[100]$$[111]$$[110]$

If the symbol is a “number,” it means a rotation axis (or rotoinversion axis) along that direction; if it is “$m$,” a mirror plane perpendicular to that direction; and if it is “$n/m$,” both are present.

How to read $Fm\bar{3}m$ (point group $m\bar{3}m$) — the point the instructor emphasized most

The Mochizuki Research Group materials state the following (slide 2-8).

In fact, $Fm\bar{3}m$ is $F\,\dfrac{4}{m}\bar{3}\,\dfrac{2}{m}$. (Check in the International Tables.)
• There is a mirror plane perpendicular to the 100 axis, and a fourfold rotation axis along the 100 axis
• There is a threefold rotoinversion axis along the 111 axis (rotate by 120 degrees, then move to $-x,-y,-z$)
• There is a mirror plane perpendicular to the 110 axis, and a twofold rotation axis along the 110 axis

Let us reread this in the language of the table. Since the crystal is cubic, the first position is $[100]$, the second position is $[111]$, and the third position is $[110]$.

PositionDirectionSymbol in $m\bar{3}m$Full symbolMeaning
First$[100]$$m$$\dfrac{4}{m}$Fourfold rotation axis along $[100]$, mirror plane perpendicular to $[100]$
Second$[111]$$\bar{3}$$\bar{3}$Threefold rotoinversion axis along $[111]$
Third$[110]$$m$$\dfrac{2}{m}$Twofold rotation axis along $[110]$, mirror plane perpendicular to $[110]$

In other words, the first $m$ and the last $m$ of $m\bar{3}m$ refer to mirror planes in completely different directions. The letter is the same, but a different position gives a different meaning. This is the heart of the international notation, and it is the place where one most easily stumbles until one gets used to it.

In the short symbol $m\bar{3}m$, the $4$ of $4/m$ and the $2$ of $2/m$ are omitted, but this is because “even when they are omitted, the group is uniquely determined among the 32 point groups.” The omitted $4$ and $2$ are generated from the remaining symmetry elements. Indeed, if we apply successively a mirror plane with normal $[100]$ and a mirror plane with normal $[110]$ (the angle between them is $45^\circ$), the result is a rotation by twice $45^\circ$, that is, $90^\circ$, and its axis is $[001]$. In this way a fourfold axis along $\langle100\rangle$ appears automatically.

However, we must not jump to the conclusion that “the first position is $m$, so the axis is fourfold.” The full symbol of $m\bar{3}$ ($T_h$) is $\frac{2}{m}\bar{3}$, and what lies behind the same first-position $m$ is a twofold axis. The order of the axis hidden by the $m$ of the short symbol differs from group to group, so when in doubt it is best to check the full symbol. The International Tables list the full symbol $\frac{4}{m}\bar{3}\frac{2}{m}$ alongside.

Example 4.3: Reading various symbols

(a) $4mm$ (tetragonal). The $4$ in the first position $[001]$ → a fourfold rotation axis. The $m$ in the second position $[100]$ → mirror planes perpendicular to $[100]$ and $[010]$ (two planes). The $m$ in the third position $[110]$ → mirror planes perpendicular to $[110]$ and $[1\bar{1}0]$ (two planes). In total, one fourfold axis and four mirror planes. The order is $4\times2 = 8$. This agrees with Table 4.3.

(b) $\bar{4}2m$ (tetragonal). The $\bar{4}$ in the first position $[001]$ → a fourfold rotoinversion axis. The $2$ in the second position $[100]$ → a twofold rotation axis. The $m$ in the third position $[110]$ → a mirror plane. There is no fourfold rotation axis, so this is a group different from $4mm$.

(c) $\bar{4}3m$ (cubic, zinc-blende type). $\bar{4}$ in the first position $[100]$, $3$ in the second position $[111]$, and $m$ in the third position $[110]$. It contains no inversion center (no combination of $\bar{4}$, $3$, and $m$ can produce $i$). This is why ZnS and GaAs are piezoelectric.

(d) $6/mmm$ (hexagonal). $6/m$ in the first position $[001]$ → a sixfold axis plus a mirror plane perpendicular to it. $m$ in the second position $[100]$, and $m$ in the third position $[210]$. The order is 24, and this is the highest-symmetry point group of the hexagonal system.

Example 4.4: Following slide 2-8 of the materials — checking the symmetry elements of $Fm\bar{3}m$ in the rock-salt structure

Picture rock-salt-type NaCl ($Fm\bar{3}m$) in your mind, and verify the following.

(1) Fourfold rotation about the $[100]$ axis. If we rotate by $90^\circ$ about the axis passing through the center of a cube face, both the Na sublattice and the Cl sublattice are brought into coincidence with themselves. Taking Na at the origin, Cl lies at the six positions $\left(\pm\frac12,0,0\right),\left(0,\pm\frac12,0\right),\left(0,0,\pm\frac12\right)$ (regular octahedron coordination), and under the $90^\circ$ rotation about the $z$ axis these are merely interchanged with one another. ✓

(2) Mirror plane perpendicular to $[100]$. Reflecting in the plane $x = 0$ (the plane containing the Na atoms) leaves the structure invariant. ✓ This confirms the $4/m$ of the first position.

(3) Threefold rotoinversion about the $[111]$ axis. A $120^\circ$ rotation about the body diagonal gives $x\to y\to z\to x$, that is, the six Cl positions are permuted cyclically. If we then invert, $\left(\frac12,0,0\right)\to\left(-\frac12,0,0\right)$, which is also a Cl position. ✓

(4) Twofold rotation about the $[110]$ axis and the perpendicular mirror plane. The $180^\circ$ rotation about a face diagonal is $x \leftrightarrow y$, $z\to-z$. The set of Cl positions is invariant. ✓ Next, what the $\frac{2}{m}$ of the third position requires is a mirror plane perpendicular to $[110]$, that is, the plane $x+y=0$ (the plane $x=y$ has normal $[1\bar{1}0]$ and is a plane that contains $[110]$, so it is something different). This reflection is $(x,y,z)\to(-y,-x,z)$, and the Cl at $\left(\frac12,0,0\right)$ is carried to $\left(0,-\frac12,0\right)$. This is also a Cl position, so it is invariant. ✓

With this, all the positions of $\frac{4}{m}\bar{3}\frac{2}{m} = m\bar{3}m$ have been verified. ∎

4.6 Point Groups and Physical Properties — Dielectric Behavior, Piezoelectricity, and Pyroelectricity

Here we come to the greatest practical payoff of studying point groups: which physical properties a crystal can have is determined by its point group alone.

Neumann's principle

The tensor that represents a physical property of a crystal must be invariant under every symmetry operation of the point group of that crystal.

In other words, the symmetry of a physical property (tensor) cannot be lower than the symmetry of the point group of the crystal. The property may have higher symmetry than the crystal, but it is not allowed to have lower symmetry. The higher the symmetry of the crystal, the fewer independent tensor components there are, and many physical properties are “forbidden.”

4.6.1 Centrosymmetry and piezoelectricity

Derivation: A centrosymmetric crystal cannot be piezoelectric

The piezoelectric effect is the phenomenon in which applying a stress $\sigma_{jk}$ produces a polarization $P_i$; it is described by a third-rank tensor $d_{ijk}$:

$$ P_i = \sum_{j,k} d_{ijk}\,\sigma_{jk} $$

Suppose now that the crystal has inversion symmetry $i$. Under the inversion operation $\rr\to-\rr$,

  • the polarization $\bm{P}$ is a polar vector, so it changes sign: $P_i \to -P_i$
  • the stress $\sigma_{jk}$ is a second-rank tensor, so it does not change sign: $\sigma_{jk}\to(-1)^2\sigma_{jk} = \sigma_{jk}$

By Neumann's principle, $d_{ijk}$ must be invariant under the inversion operation. From the transformation law of tensors, a third-rank tensor is multiplied by $(-1)^3 = -1$ under inversion:

$$ d_{ijk} \to (-1)^3 d_{ijk} = -d_{ijk} $$

For it to be invariant we need $d_{ijk} = -d_{ijk}$, that is,

$$ d_{ijk} = 0 \quad(\text{all components}) $$

∎ In a centrosymmetric crystal, the piezoelectric tensor vanishes identically.

In general, any odd-rank physical property represented by an ordinary polar tensor necessarily vanishes in a centrosymmetric crystal. Piezoelectricity (third rank), second-order nonlinear optical effects (third rank), and the electro-optic effect (third rank) are examples. Conversely, the permittivity (second rank) and the elastic constants (fourth rank) are of even rank, so they survive even in centrosymmetric crystals.

However, we must not generalize “odd rank implies zero” unconditionally. Axial tensors (pseudotensors) and magnetic properties that are odd under time reversal obey a different transformation law from polar tensors under the inversion operation, so this criterion cannot be applied to them as it stands. When we deal with quantities such as magneto-optical coefficients, we have to think in terms not of the point group but of the magnetic point group (Chapter 20), which incorporates the time-reversal operation.

One more caution. The phrase “paraelectricity only” in the table means that the crystal is neither piezoelectric nor pyroelectric; it does not mean that “only centrosymmetric crystals are dielectrics.” A dielectric response (the induction of polarization when an electric field is applied) is exhibited by insulators of all 32 point groups, so every insulator is a dielectric; the piezoelectrics (the 20 point groups excluding $432$) form a subset of these, the pyroelectrics with a spontaneous polarization (the 10 polar point groups) a subset of those, and the ferroelectrics, whose polarization can be reversed, a further subset of those—a nested hierarchy.

4.6.2 Polarity, pyroelectricity, and ferroelectricity

Definition: polar point group

A point group that has an axis whose direction is not changed by any symmetry operation (a polar axis) is called a polar point group.

If a polar axis exists, placing the spontaneous polarization $\bm{P}_s$ along that direction does not contradict the symmetry. Therefore a spontaneous polarization is possible.

Example 4.5: Why $mm2$ is polar and $222$ is not

$222$ ($D_2$). There are twofold axes in the three directions $x, y, z$. Let us place a polarization $\bm{P} = (0,0,P)$ along the $z$ direction. The $C_2$ operation about the $x$ axis is $(x,y,z)\to(x,-y,-z)$, so $\bm{P}\to(0,0,-P)$. This differs from the original $\bm{P}$. The same holds if we place it along the $x$ or $y$ direction. Therefore a polarization cannot be placed in any direction. Nonpolar.

$mm2$ ($C_{2v}$). There is a twofold axis along the $z$ axis and mirror planes at the $xz$ plane and the $yz$ plane. Placing $\bm{P}=(0,0,P)$, we have

  • $C_2(z)$: $(x,y,z)\to(-x,-y,z)$, so $\bm{P}\to(0,0,P)$ ✓
  • $\sigma(xz)$: $(x,y,z)\to(x,-y,z)$, so $\bm{P}\to(0,0,P)$ ✓
  • $\sigma(yz)$: $(x,y,z)\to(-x,y,z)$, so $\bm{P}\to(0,0,P)$ ✓

It is invariant under all the operations. The $z$ axis is a polar axis. ∎

In general, a polar axis exists when the group has at most one rotation axis and has no twofold axis perpendicular to that axis, no mirror plane $\sigma_h$ perpendicular to that axis, no inversion center $i$, and no rotoinversion axis about that axis ($\bar{4}=S_4$, $\bar{3}=S_6$). Even if there are mirror planes, polarity is preserved as long as they are only $\sigma_v$ planes containing the polar axis (the direction is unchanged because $\bm{P}$ lies in the plane). The inversion center and the rotoinversion axes must be excluded because $\bar{1}$, $\bar{4}$, and $\bar{3}$ do satisfy the condition “at most one rotation axis, and neither a perpendicular twofold axis nor $\sigma_h$” (for example, in $\bar{4}$ the $S_4$ takes $\bm{P}=(0,0,P)$ to $(0,0,-P)$, so the axis cannot be a polar axis). $C_s$ ($m$), which has no rotation axis at all, is also a polar point group, because any direction within the mirror plane serves as a polar axis. Looking at the “Polar” column of Table 4.3, we see that only the $C_n$ type and the $C_{nv}$ type, together with the groups $C_1, C_s$, are polar.

Physical Meaning: Why are ferroelectrics hard to find?

The materials state: “Note: The search for new ferroelectrics is said to be more difficult than the search for new superconductors.” The reason can be seen from the counting of point groups.

To be a ferroelectric, a material must satisfy at least the following three conditions at the same time.

  1. It must belong to a polar point group (10/32 = about 31%). This alone narrows the candidates to about one third.
  2. The spontaneous polarization must actually be nonzero. Even in a polar point group, depending on the structure the polarization may cancel out and be almost zero.
  3. The polarization must be reversible with a realistic electric field. The electric field needed for reversal (the coercive field) must be smaller than the dielectric breakdown field. This is the most severe condition, and many polar crystals remain at “pyroelectric but not ferroelectric.” ZnO ($6mm$) is a typical example: it is a polar crystal, but its polarization cannot be reversed.

Moreover, for many materials “having inversion symmetry is energetically favorable.” Indeed, the fact that $\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$ shows ferroelectricity at room temperature is due to rather special circumstances: the $d^0$ electron configuration of Ti$^{4+}$ and the second-order Jahn–Teller effect with the O-2p orbitals. The search for such mechanisms is the theme of “the search for new ferroelectrics,” which is also pursued in the Mochizuki Research Group. We treat this in detail in Chapter 19.

Centrosymmetry is hard to see in X-ray diffraction — Friedel's law

When the space group is determined experimentally, the difficulty that remains to the end is “whether or not there is centrosymmetry.”

In ordinary X-ray diffraction, $\abs{S_{\GG}}^2 = \abs{S_{-\GG}}^2$ always holds (if absorption is neglected). This is called Friedel's law. That is, the pattern of diffraction intensities looks as if the crystal were centrosymmetric, regardless of whether it actually is centrosymmetric.

As a result, what can be learned directly from diffraction is not the point group itself but only the “group obtained by adding inversion to the point group” (the Laue group; there are 11 of them). To decide whether centrosymmetry is present, other experiments are needed, such as using anomalous dispersion, measuring second-harmonic generation (SHG), or measuring piezoelectricity.

It is for this reason that the question “is it really polar?” is often disputed in studies of ferroelectrics.

4.7 Summary and Exercises

4.7.1 Summary

  • Symmetry operations are represented by $3\times3$ orthogonal matrices. $\det=+1$ is a proper rotation and $\det=-1$ is an improper rotation. The trace of a rotation matrix is $1+2\cos(2\pi/n)$.
  • Crystallographic restriction theorem: the condition that a lattice be mapped onto itself requires $2\cos(2\pi/n)\in\mathbb{Z}$, so that only $n=1,2,3,4,6$ are possible. The shortest proof uses the fact that the trace does not depend on the basis. Quasicrystals have no translational symmetry, so they fall outside this theorem.
  • Rotoinversion $\bar{n}$ (international notation) and rotoreflection $S_n$ (Schoenflies) are defined differently. $\bar{4}\leftrightarrow S_4$, $\bar{3}\leftrightarrow S_6$, $\bar{6}\leftrightarrow S_3$, $\bar{2}=m$. $\sigma_h$ is perpendicular to the principal axis, and $\sigma_v$ is a plane containing the principal axis.
  • There are 32 crystallographic point groups. Eleven are centrosymmetric, 11 are noncentrosymmetric and nonpolar, and 10 are polar.
  • In the international notation, each position fixes a direction. In cubic crystals, the first position is $[100]$, the second position is $[111]$, and the third position is $[110]$. $m\bar{3}m$ has the full form $\frac{4}{m}\bar{3}\frac{2}{m}$, in which the first $m$ and the last $m$ refer to mirror planes in different directions.
  • By Neumann's principle, a material tensor must be invariant under the point-group operations. Odd-rank properties described by polar tensors (such as piezoelectricity) vanish identically in centrosymmetric crystals.
  • Only the polar point groups (10 of them) can have a spontaneous polarization. Among these, those whose polarization can be reversed by an electric field are ferroelectrics. Because of this three-stage narrowing, ferroelectrics are rare.
  • Because of Friedel's law, ordinary X-ray diffraction reveals the symmetry only down to the Laue group (11 of them), and determining whether a crystal is centrosymmetric requires a separate experiment.

Exercise 4.1: Verifying that $\bar{6} = C_{3h}$

Exercise 4.2: $T_d$ and $O$ — same order, but different as subgroups of $O(3)$

$\bar{4}3m$ ($T_d$) and $432$ ($O$) both have order 24.

(1) Count the symmetry operations of each group class by class, and compare them.

(2) Show that $T_d$ contains 12 operations with $\det=-1$ and that $O$ contains none.

(3) Which of the two is the point group of methane $\mathrm{CH_4}$? Give the reason.

Hint: (1) $T_d = \{E, 8C_3, 3C_2, 6S_4, 6\sigma_d\}$ and $O=\{E,8C_3,3C_2,6C_4,6C_2'\}$. (3) $\mathrm{CH_4}$ has mirror planes, so it is $T_d$.

Caution. Here, calling them “different groups” means that they are different as subgroups of $O(3)$ (that is, as collections of transformations acting on space). $T_d$ contains operations of determinant $-1$ and $O$ does not, so their physical actions are completely different. However, as abstract groups the two are isomorphic, and each has the same structure as the symmetric group on four letters, that is, the group of all permutations of four objects (order 24); this corresponds to the permutations of the four $\langle111\rangle$ axes for $T_d$ and to the permutations of the four body diagonals of the cube for $O$. Some authors write this “symmetric group” as $S_4$, but in this chapter $S_4$ is the symbol of a rotoreflection operation, so please take care not to confuse the two. For this reason, the character tables we construct in Chapter 8 for $T_d$ and for $O$ differ only in the names of the classes, and the arrays of numbers are the same. The distinction between “the actions differ” and “the abstract structures differ,” which are separate matters, will become important from Chapter 6 onward.
