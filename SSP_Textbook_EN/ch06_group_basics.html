Chapter 6Group Basics — Multiplication Tables, Subgroups, and Classes

In Chapter 4 we enumerated the 32 crystallographic point groups, and in Chapter 5 the 230 space groups. There we got by with the intuitive phrase “a collection of symmetry operations,” and set aside the question of what that collection is mathematically. The answer is a group. In Part II, from this chapter through Chapter 11, we sharpen the concept of a group into a tool, with the ultimate aim of drawing out “what can be known from symmetry without calculation.”

Open a textbook on group theory and you will usually find abstract definitions and theorems filling the first ten pages, where many readers drop out. This book does not do that. Our strategy is to keep one example at hand—the symmetry of the ammonia molecule $\mathrm{NH_3}$, that is, the point group $C_{3v}$—and to work it out by hand to the very end. We fill in the $6\times6$ multiplication table ourselves, enumerate all the subgroups, and compute every conjugation to sort the elements into classes. Once you have done this, all the abstract theory that follows can be read as “so that is what we did back then.”

The moment at which the abstraction of group theory pays off comes in Section 6.8. What $[\Ham,\hat{R}]=0$ means is that a symmetry operation is a transformation among degenerate eigenstates; in other words, the structure of the degeneracy is determined by symmetry. This one line governs crystal-field splitting, selection rules, band degeneracies, and the classification of phonons. Everything from Chapter 7 onward starts here. There is only one attitude we would like you to acquire in this chapter: whenever you read a theorem of group theory, apply it to $C_{3v}$ on the spot and check it.

6.1 What Is a Group? — The Four Axioms

6.1.1 Definition

Definition: Group

Suppose we are given a set $G = \{A, B, C, \dots\}$ and an operation (called the product) that assigns a third element to any two of its elements. When the following four conditions are satisfied, $G$ is called a group.

  1. Closure: if $A, B \in G$, then $AB \in G$.
  2. Associativity: $(AB)C = A(BC)$.
  3. Identity element: there exists an element $E \in G$ such that $EA = AE = A$ for all $A \in G$.
  4. Inverse element: for every $A \in G$ there exists an $A^{-1} \in G$ such that $AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = E$.

The number $h$ of elements contained in $G$ is called the order of the group. When $h$ is finite, the group is called a finite group.

Supplement: The “product” need not be multiplication

The “product” that appears in the group axioms does not have to be the multiplication of numbers at all. All that is required is that it satisfy the four conditions above. In the groups actually treated in this book,

  • group of symmetry operations: product = applying the operations in succession (composition)
  • group of matrices: product = matrix multiplication
  • the set of integers $\mathbb{Z}$: product = addition (the identity is $0$, and the inverse is $-n$)

completely different things play the role of the “product.” Conversely, the theorems of group theory hold regardless of what the product is. This is why group theory has such broad applications.

Note also that the axioms do not include commutativity $AB=BA$. This is the crux of group theory. Symmetry operations are in general not commutative.

Why? Are these four enough?

The axioms are narrowed down to these four because they extract “the properties that a collection of symmetry operations satisfies naturally.” Indeed, consider the set of all symmetry operations of some object.

  • Applying the symmetry operation $A$ leaves the object unchanged, and so does applying $B$. Then applying $A$ after $B$ also leaves it unchanged. $\Rightarrow$ closure
  • Composition of operations is by definition “applying them one after another,” so how the parentheses are placed does not affect the result. $\Rightarrow$ associativity
  • The operation “do nothing” is always a symmetry operation. $\Rightarrow$ identity element
  • A symmetry operation is an invertible mapping that carries the object onto itself, so an operation that undoes it exists, and that too is a symmetry operation. $\Rightarrow$ inverse element

In other words, the group axioms are nothing but a translation of “what symmetry is” into the language of mathematics.

6.1.2 The six elements of $C_{3v}$

We now introduce the protagonist of this chapter. The ammonia molecule $\mathrm{NH_3}$ has the shape of a trigonal pyramid, with the nitrogen atom at the apex and the three hydrogen atoms forming the base. Its symmetry is the point group $C_{3v}$ (international notation $3m$).

We fix the coordinates. Place N at the origin and take the $C_3$ axis as the $z$ axis. The three H atoms lie in a plane with $z<0$, and their azimuthal angles (angles from the $x$ axis) are, respectively,

$$ \text{H1}: \varphi = 0^\circ, \qquad \text{H2}: \varphi = 120^\circ, \qquad \text{H3}: \varphi = 240^\circ $$

The three mirror planes are the planes containing the $z$ axis and each of the H atoms.

Derivation: The matrix of a mirror plane containing the $z$ axis and making an angle $\theta$ with the $x$ axis

In Chapter 4 we wrote down only the reflection $\sigma_h$ in the $xy$ plane. Here we construct the matrix of a vertical mirror plane (a plane containing the $z$ axis).

The $z$ coordinate is unchanged by the reflection, so it suffices to argue within the $xy$ plane. Write a point in the $xy$ plane in polar coordinates as $x = \rho\cos\varphi$, $y = \rho\sin\varphi$. The line of intersection of the mirror plane with the $xy$ plane is the line through the origin making an angle $\theta$ with the $x$ axis. Reflection in this line is the operation that changes the polar angle as $\varphi \to 2\theta - \varphi$ (because the angles are symmetric on the two sides of the line; for $\varphi=\theta$ we get $2\theta-\theta=\theta$, so the point is fixed, which corresponds to the fact that points on the line do not move). The value of $\rho$ does not change. Therefore

$$ x' = \rho\cos(2\theta-\varphi), \qquad y' = \rho\sin(2\theta-\varphi) $$

We expand with the addition theorem. Since $\cos(2\theta-\varphi) = \cos2\theta\cos\varphi + \sin2\theta\sin\varphi$, using $\rho\cos\varphi = x$ and $\rho\sin\varphi = y$ we obtain

$$ x' = x\cos2\theta + y\sin2\theta $$

Similarly, since $\sin(2\theta-\varphi) = \sin2\theta\cos\varphi - \cos2\theta\sin\varphi$,

$$ y' = x\sin2\theta - y\cos2\theta $$

Collecting these into a matrix gives

$$ \begin{equation} \sigma(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos2\theta & \sin2\theta & 0 \\ \sin2\theta & -\cos2\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \label{eq:6-mirror} \end{equation} $$

Let us check the calculation. For $\theta=0$ (reflection in the $xz$ plane), we have $\cos0=1,\sin0=0$ and so $\sigma(0)=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,1)$. This is $y\to-y$, which is indeed the reflection in the $xz$ plane. Also, $\det\sigma(\theta) = (-\cos^2 2\theta - \sin^2 2\theta)\cdot 1 = -1$ and $\Tr\,\sigma(\theta) = \cos2\theta - \cos2\theta + 1 = 1$. This agrees with Table 4.1 of Chapter 4 (a reflection has $\det=-1$). ∎

We substitute $\theta = 0^\circ, 120^\circ, 240^\circ$ into Eq. \eqref{eq:6-mirror}. Since $2\theta = 0^\circ, 240^\circ, 480^\circ(\equiv 120^\circ)$, we have

  • $\theta=0^\circ$: $\cos 0 = 1$, $\sin 0 = 0$
  • $\theta=120^\circ$: $\cos240^\circ = -\tfrac12$, $\sin240^\circ = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$
  • $\theta=240^\circ$: $\cos120^\circ = -\tfrac12$, $\sin120^\circ = +\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$

We can now write down the matrices of all six elements. For the rotations, we substitute $\phi=120^\circ, 240^\circ$ into the rotation matrix about the $z$ axis from Chapter 4.

Matrix representation of the six elements of $C_{3v}$ (used throughout this chapter)

$$ E = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad C_3 = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&0\\[3pt] \frac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\frac12&0\\[3pt] 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad C_3^2 = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&0\\[3pt] -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\frac12&0\\[3pt] 0&0&1\end{pmatrix} $$ $$ \sigma_v = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_v' = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&0\\[3pt] -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&\frac12&0\\[3pt] 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_v'' = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&0\\[3pt] \frac{\sqrt3}{2}&\frac12&0\\[3pt] 0&0&1\end{pmatrix} $$

$\sigma_v = \sigma(0^\circ)$ is the plane containing H1, $\sigma_v' = \sigma(120^\circ)$ is the plane containing H2, and $\sigma_v'' = \sigma(240^\circ)$ is the plane containing H3. The order is $h=6$.

The traces are $\Tr E = 3$, $\Tr C_3 = \Tr C_3^2 = 0$, and $\Tr\sigma_v = \Tr\sigma_v' = \Tr\sigma_v'' = 1$. It is worth noting that operations of the same “kind” have the same trace. In Section 6.6 this leads to the discussion of classes.

6.1.3 Actually verifying the four axioms

Example 6.1: Verifying with matrices that $C_{3v}$ is a group

Verify that the set of the six matrices above satisfies the four group axioms under matrix multiplication.

(1) Closure. Checking all combinations is done with the multiplication table in Section 6.2; here we actually multiply two representative pairs. Since the $z$ component is always $1$ and does not change, we write only the upper-left $2\times2$ block.

First, $C_3\,\sigma_v$ (operations are applied from the right, so $\sigma_v$ first, then $C_3$):

$$ C_3\sigma_v = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] \frac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\frac12\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix} $$

We compute component by component. The (1,1) entry is $(-\tfrac12)(1) + (-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})(0) = -\tfrac12$. The (1,2) entry is $(-\tfrac12)(0) + (-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})(-1) = +\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$. The (2,1) entry is $(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})(1) + (-\tfrac12)(0) = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$. The (2,2) entry is $(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})(0) + (-\tfrac12)(-1) = +\tfrac12$. Hence

$$ C_3\sigma_v = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] \frac{\sqrt3}{2}&\frac12\end{pmatrix} = \sigma_v'' $$

The product lies in the set. ✓

Next we compute $\sigma_v'\,\sigma_v''$ (applying two reflections in succession), one entry at a time in the same way. The (1,1) entry is $(-\tfrac12)(-\tfrac12) + (-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}) = \tfrac14-\tfrac34 = -\tfrac12$, the (1,2) entry is $-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, the (2,1) entry is $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, and the (2,2) entry is $-\tfrac34+\tfrac14=-\tfrac12$. Therefore

$$ \sigma_v'\sigma_v'' = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&\frac12\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] \frac{\sqrt3}{2}&\frac12\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] \frac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\frac12\end{pmatrix} = C_3 $$

This too lies in the set. ✓ Here we have encountered the geometrically important fact that applying two reflections in succession gives a rotation.

(2) Associativity. Matrix multiplication is associative by nature ($(AB)C = A(BC)$). This follows from the definition of the matrix product, $(AB)_{ik} = \sum_j A_{ij}B_{jk}$: both sides become $\sum_{j,l}A_{ij}B_{jl}C_{lk}$. Even if we regard symmetry operations as mappings, composition of mappings is always associative. Hence associativity holds automatically. ✓

(3) Identity. $E$ is the identity matrix, and $EA=AE=A$. ✓

(4) Inverse. We find the inverse of each element.

  • $E^{-1}=E$.
  • $C_3^{-1}$: $C_3$ is a $+120^\circ$ rotation, so its inverse is a $-120^\circ$ rotation, that is, a $+240^\circ$ rotation, which is $C_3^2$. Indeed $C_3 C_3^2 = C_3^3 = E$ (a $360^\circ$ rotation). Hence $C_3^{-1}=C_3^2$ and $(C_3^2)^{-1}=C_3$.
  • Applying a reflection twice returns the original: $\sigma^2 = E$. Hence $\sigma_v^{-1}=\sigma_v$, $\sigma_v'^{-1}=\sigma_v'$, and $\sigma_v''^{-1}=\sigma_v''$.

To check with matrices, a reflection matrix is both symmetric and orthogonal ($\sigma^{\mathsf T}=\sigma$, $\sigma^{\mathsf T}\sigma = I$), so $\sigma^2 = I$ follows immediately. ✓

Since all four conditions hold, $C_{3v}$ is a group of order 6. ∎

Caution: Order of products — $AB$ means “$B$ first”

In this book we follow the convention of treating symmetry operations like functions, and we read $AB$ as “first apply $B$, then apply $A$”. This agrees with the order in which $AB\rr$ appears when written with matrices (acting on $\rr$ from the right), so it is the natural choice. Dresselhaus's textbook also follows this convention.

Some books on crystallography, however, use the opposite convention, “$AB$ = first $A$, then $B$.” When this difference in convention is combined with two others — whether $C_3$ is taken clockwise or counterclockwise, and how the mirror planes are numbered — the off-diagonal parts of the multiplication table can appear interchanged from one textbook to another. When comparing a multiplication table with another book, always check these three conventions first.

Even if the conventions differ, the structure as a group (the isomorphism class, the number of subgroups, and how the elements split into classes) is exactly the same. Only the way the elements are named changes.

6.2 How to Construct a Multiplication Table

The most basic piece of information about a finite group is its multiplication table (also called the group table). It is a table listing the product $AB$ for every pair of elements $(A,B)$, and it describes the structure of the group as completely as one could wish.

6.2.1 Tracking the vertices of the triangle

Multiplying matrices 6×6 = 36 times is laborious. There is an easier method that is also less error-prone: number the vertices and follow where they go. The symmetry operations of $C_{3v}$ correspond one to one to the permutations of the three vertices $\{1,2,3\}$ of the equilateral triangle.

Derivation: Finding $\sigma_v C_3 = \sigma_v'$ by tracking the vertices

According to the convention (the caution in Section 6.1), $\sigma_v C_3$ means first $C_3$, then $\sigma_v$. We follow it while looking at the upper row of Figure 6.2.

Step 1: Apply $C_3$ (counterclockwise $120^\circ$). Fix the locations of the vertices at the three azimuthal angles $0^\circ, 120^\circ, 240^\circ$, and regard the numbers sitting there as the things that move. $C_3$ sends the numbers $1\to2\to3\to1$ to the next locations; that is,

$$ \text{number 1: } 0^\circ \to 120^\circ, \qquad \text{number 2: } 120^\circ \to 240^\circ, \qquad \text{number 3: } 240^\circ \to 0^\circ $$

At this point, number 3 sits at location $0^\circ$, number 1 at location $120^\circ$, and number 2 at location $240^\circ$.

Step 2: Apply $\sigma_v$. Since $\sigma_v$ is a reflection in the mirror plane containing location $0^\circ$, location $0^\circ$ stays where it is, while locations $120^\circ$ and $240^\circ$ interchange (because the azimuthal angle goes $\varphi \to -\varphi$, so $120^\circ \to -120^\circ = 240^\circ$). Therefore

$$ \text{location } 0^\circ: \text{number 3 (unchanged)}, \qquad \text{location } 120^\circ: \text{number 2}, \qquad \text{location } 240^\circ: \text{number 1} $$

Step 3: Read the result as a single operation. The initial arrangement (locations $0^\circ$, $120^\circ$, $240^\circ$) was (1, 2, 3). The final arrangement is (3, 2, 1). That is,

$$ 1 \leftrightarrow 3, \qquad 2 \text{ is fixed} $$

Which operation leaves number 2 fixed and interchanges 1 and 3? It is the reflection in the mirror plane $\sigma_v'$ containing H2. Hence

$$ \begin{equation} \sigma_v\,C_3 = \sigma_v' \label{eq:6-svc3} \end{equation} $$

Checking with matrices as well.

$$ \sigma_v C_3 = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] \frac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\frac12\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&\frac12\end{pmatrix} = \sigma_v' $$

(The second row is the inner product of $(0,-1)$ with each column, so it simply reverses the sign.) The two results agree. ∎

Notation Note: Writing the elements as permutations makes the calculation mechanical

If we rewrite the six elements as permutations of the vertex numbers, the computation of products becomes completely mechanical. In cycle notation,

$$ E = (\,), \quad C_3 = (1\,2\,3), \quad C_3^2 = (1\,3\,2), \quad \sigma_v = (2\,3), \quad \sigma_v' = (1\,3), \quad \sigma_v'' = (1\,2) $$

Here $(1\,2\,3)$ means “$1\to2$, $2\to3$, $3\to1$,” and $(2\,3)$ means “$2\leftrightarrow3$, with $1$ fixed.” The reason $\sigma_v$ is $(2\,3)$ is that the reflection in the plane containing H1 does not move H1 and interchanges H2 and H3.

The product $AB$ means “apply $B$ first and then $A$,” so we simply compose the maps as $x \mapsto A(B(x))$. For example, for $\sigma_v C_3$ we have $1 \xrightarrow{C_3} 2 \xrightarrow{\sigma_v} 3$, $2 \xrightarrow{C_3} 3 \xrightarrow{\sigma_v} 2$, and $3 \xrightarrow{C_3} 1 \xrightarrow{\sigma_v} 1$. In summary, $1\to3$, $2\to2$, $3\to1$, that is, $(1\,3) = \sigma_v'$. This agrees with Eq. \eqref{eq:6-svc3}.

This correspondence shows that $C_{3v}$ has the same structure as the symmetric group $S_3$ on three letters (all the rearrangements of three objects, $3!=6$ in number). We will formulate this as an “isomorphism” in Section 6.7.

$$ \sigma(\theta_1)\sigma(\theta_2) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos2\theta_1 & \sin2\theta_1\\ \sin2\theta_1 & -\cos2\theta_1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\cos2\theta_2 & \sin2\theta_2\\ \sin2\theta_2 & -\cos2\theta_2\end{pmatrix} $$

The (1,1) element is $\cos2\theta_1\cos2\theta_2 + \sin2\theta_1\sin2\theta_2 = \cos2(\theta_1-\theta_2)$. The (1,2) element is $\cos2\theta_1\sin2\theta_2 - \sin2\theta_1\cos2\theta_2 = -\sin2(\theta_1-\theta_2)$. Computing the other elements similarly, we obtain

$$ \sigma(\theta_1)\sigma(\theta_2) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos2(\theta_1-\theta_2) & -\sin2(\theta_1-\theta_2)\\ \sin2(\theta_1-\theta_2) & \cos2(\theta_1-\theta_2)\end{pmatrix} $$

This is precisely the rotation matrix for the angle $2(\theta_1-\theta_2)$. For $\theta_1=120^\circ, \theta_2=0^\circ$, the rotation angle is $240^\circ$ = $C_3^2$. ✓

(c) $\sigma_v''C_3^2$ (first $C_3^2=(1\,3\,2)$, then $\sigma_v''=(1\,2)$). Since $1\to3\to3$, $2\to1\to2$, $3\to2\to1$, we get $(1\,3)=\sigma_v'$. In Table 6.1, the entry in the $\sigma_v''$ row and $C_3^2$ column is $\sigma_v'$ ✓. ∎

What we can read directly from the multiplication table

  • The position where $E$ appears on the diagonal tells us the inverse element of that element. In the rows of $\sigma$, the diagonal element ($\sigma\sigma$) is $E$, so $\sigma^{-1}=\sigma$. In the $C_3$ row, the $C_3^2$ column holds $E$, so $C_3^{-1}=C_3^2$.
  • The table is not symmetric about the diagonal (for example, the $\sigma_v$ row and $C_3$ column gives $\sigma_v'$, while the $C_3$ row and $\sigma_v$ column gives $\sigma_v''$). This is the noncommutativity (Section 6.4).
  • The upper-left $3\times3$ block is closed (products among $\{E,C_3,C_3^2\}$ always fall inside it). This implies the existence of a subgroup (Section 6.5).
  • Each row and each column contains each of the six elements exactly once. This is the rearrangement theorem of the next section.

This single table contains the seeds of all the concepts we are about to develop.

6.3 The Rearrangement Theorem

Theorem 6.1 (rearrangement theorem)

For any element $X \in G$ of a finite group $G = \{E, A_2, A_3, \dots, A_h\}$,

$$ XG \equiv \{XE, XA_2, XA_3, \dots, XA_h\} $$

coincides with $G$ itself as a set, although the order changes. That is, each element of the group appears exactly once. The same holds for $GX$, where we multiply from the right.

Proof

Step 1: $XG \subseteq G$. By closure, $XA_i \in G$. Hence $XG$ is a subset of $G$.

Step 2: $XG$ contains no repetitions. Suppose that $XA_i = XA_j$. Multiplying both sides by $X^{-1}$ from the left (it exists by the inverse-element axiom), we obtain

$$ X^{-1}(XA_i) = X^{-1}(XA_j) $$

Regrouping the parentheses by associativity gives $(X^{-1}X)A_i = (X^{-1}X)A_j$, that is, $EA_i = EA_j$, and therefore $A_i = A_j$.

In other words, if $i\ne j$, then $XA_i \ne XA_j$. $XG$ consists of $h$ mutually distinct elements.

Step 3: Conclusion. $XG$ is a subset of $G$ (which has $h$ elements), and it also has $h$ elements. Since the sets are finite, $XG = G$. ∎

Another way to see it. For any $B \in G$, the element $A$ satisfying $XA = B$ is uniquely $A = X^{-1}B$ (both existence and uniqueness follow from the inverse-element axiom). Therefore $B$ appears exactly once in $XG$. This formulation directly answers the question “where in the row is $B$?”

Practical meaning: If, when you construct a multiplication table by hand, the same element appears twice in a row or column, then you have made an error in the calculation. The rearrangement theorem is the best way to check your work.

Why does it matter? We can rearrange sums

The real power of the rearrangement theorem is exhibited in the proofs of the orthogonality theorems in Chapters 7 and 8. When we consider a sum over all elements of the group,

$$ S = \sum_{A \in G} f(A) $$

the sum does not change if we replace $A$ by $XA$:

$$ \begin{equation} \sum_{A \in G} f(XA) = \sum_{A \in G} f(A) \qquad (\text{for any } X \in G) \label{eq:6-rearr-sum} \end{equation} $$

This is because, as $A$ runs over all of $G$, $XA$ also runs over all of $G$ (in a different order). For a finite sum, the order of summation does not affect the result.

In the proof of the great orthogonality theorem (Chapter 7), the essence is finished by using this rearrangement just once. That the operation of “summing over all elements of the group” has a symmetry is the most fundamental basis of the computational technique of group theory.

6.4 Abelian and Non-Abelian Groups

Definition: Commutative group (abelian group)

A group in which $AB = BA$ holds for every pair of elements is called a commutative group or an abelian group. A group that is not commutative is called a non-abelian group.

The multiplication table of a commutative group is symmetric about the main diagonal.

Example 6.4: Showing that $C_{3v}$ is noncommutative

We pick out two products from Table 6.1.

$$ \sigma_v C_3 = \sigma_v', \qquad C_3 \sigma_v = \sigma_v'' $$

Since $\sigma_v' \ne \sigma_v''$, we have $\sigma_v C_3 \ne C_3 \sigma_v$. Hence $C_{3v}$ is a non-abelian group.

Checking with matrices as well.

$$ \sigma_v C_3 = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&\frac12\end{pmatrix}, \qquad C_3 \sigma_v = \begin{pmatrix}-\frac12&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] \frac{\sqrt3}{2}&\frac12\end{pmatrix} $$

The signs of the off-diagonal elements are opposite, so these are indeed different matrices. ∎

Geometric understanding. $\sigma_v C_3$ means “rotate the triangle by $120^\circ$, and then reflect it in the fixed mirror plane at $0^\circ$.” $C_3\sigma_v$ means “reflect in the plane at $0^\circ$, and then rotate by $120^\circ$.” Once the triangle has been rotated, its position relative to the mirror plane used afterward has changed. That is why the order matters. In everyday terms, it is the same as the fact that “facing north and then turning right” differs from “turning right and then facing north.”

Physical Meaning: Noncommutativity and degeneracy

To anticipate, whether a group is commutative or noncommutative is directly tied to physics. Every irreducible representation of an abelian group is one-dimensional. Therefore, in a system whose point group is commutative, no symmetry-required degeneracy occurs. Conversely, only non-abelian groups have irreducible representations of dimension two or higher, and only then does “degeneracy required by symmetry” appear.

$C_{3v}$ has a two-dimensional irreducible representation $E$ (Chapter 8). This is the origin of the degeneracy of the $p_x, p_y$ orbitals of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ and of the twofold degeneracy of vibrational modes in a $C_{3v}$-symmetric field. Conversely, in a molecule with $C_{2v}$ symmetry (commutative), all levels are nondegenerate.

That the origin of degeneracy in quantum mechanics is the noncommutativity of the group is a deep fact, and we will stand at its threshold in Section 6.8.

6.5 Subgroups, Cosets, and Lagrange's Theorem

6.5.1 Subgroups

Definition: Subgroup

A subset $H \subseteq G$ of a group $G$ is called a subgroup of $G$ if it is itself a group under the same product as $G$.

$H = \{E\}$ and $H = G$ are always subgroups and are called trivial subgroups. Any other subgroup is called a proper subgroup.

For a finite group, to decide whether a nonempty subset $H$ is a subgroup, it suffices to check closure alone (the empty set satisfies closure trivially, but it does not contain the identity element, so it is not a group). Associativity is inherited from $G$; by closure, $A, A^2, A^3, \dots$ all lie in $H$, but since $H$ is finite we eventually have $A^n = A^m$ ($n>m$), hence $A^{n-m}=E$, and therefore $E \in H$ and $A^{-1}=A^{n-m-1} \in H$ follow.

Definition: Order of an element

For $A \in G$, the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $A^n = E$ is called the order of the element $A$.

Then $\{E, A, A^2, \dots, A^{n-1}\}$ is a subgroup of order $n$. This is called the cyclic subgroup generated by $A$.

6.5.2 Cosets

Definition: Coset

Let $H = \{H_1=E, H_2, \dots, H_{h_H}\}$ be a subgroup of $G$ (of order $h_H$), and let $X \in G$ be an arbitrary element.

  • Left coset: $XH = \{XH_1, XH_2, \dots, XH_{h_H}\}$
  • Right coset: $HX = \{H_1X, H_2X, \dots, H_{h_H}X\}$

When $X \in H$, we have $XH = H$ (just apply the rearrangement theorem within $H$). When $X \notin H$, the coset is not a subgroup (because it does not contain $E$).

Derivation: Writing out all cosets of $H = \{E, \sigma_v\}$

We compute using Table 6.1. Here $H=\{E,\sigma_v\}$ has order 2.

We write out the left cosets $XH$ for all six $X$. Since $XH = \{X\cdot E,\ X\cdot\sigma_v\} = \{X,\ X\sigma_v\}$, it suffices to read the $\sigma_v$ column of Table 6.1.

  • $X=E$: $\{E, \sigma_v\} = H$ / $X=\sigma_v$: $\{\sigma_v, \sigma_v\sigma_v\}=\{\sigma_v, E\} = H$
  • $X=C_3$: $\{C_3, \; C_3\sigma_v\} = \{C_3, \; \sigma_v''\}$ / $X=\sigma_v''$: $\{\sigma_v'', \; \sigma_v''\sigma_v\}=\{\sigma_v'', C_3\}$ (the same set)
  • $X=C_3^2$: $\{C_3^2, \; C_3^2\sigma_v\} = \{C_3^2, \; \sigma_v'\}$ / $X=\sigma_v'$: $\{\sigma_v', \; C_3^2\}$ (the same set)

There are three distinct left cosets:

$$ \{E, \sigma_v\}, \qquad \{C_3, \sigma_v''\}, \qquad \{C_3^2, \sigma_v'\} $$

These do not overlap one another, and together they cover all of $G$ (six elements). $3 \times 2 = 6$ ✓

For the right cosets $HX = \{X,\ \sigma_v X\}$ we read the $\sigma_v$ row of Table 6.1. Since $\sigma_v C_3 = \sigma_v'$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:6-svc3}) and $\sigma_v C_3^2 = \sigma_v''$, there are again three distinct right cosets:

$$ \{E, \sigma_v\}, \qquad \{C_3, \sigma_v'\}, \qquad \{C_3^2, \sigma_v''\} $$

The left cosets and the right cosets do not coincide! $\{C_3, \sigma_v''\}$ and $\{C_3, \sigma_v'\}$ are different sets. ∎

Whether the two coincide or not is exactly what becomes the definition of a normal subgroup in Section 6.7. $\{E,\sigma_v\}$ is not a normal subgroup.

6.5.3 Lagrange's theorem

Theorem 6.2 (Lagrange's theorem)

For any subgroup $H$ (of order $h_H$) of a finite group $G$ (of order $h$), $h_H$ is a divisor of $h$. That is,

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{h}{h_H} = \ell \quad (\ell \text{ is a positive integer}) \label{eq:6-lagrange} \end{equation} $$

This $\ell$ is called the index of $H$ and is written $[G:H]$.

Proof — Cosets divide $G$ into “boxes of equal size”

We show three lemmas in turn.

Lemma 1: Every element belongs to at least one left coset.
For any $X \in G$, since $E \in H$, we have $X = XE \in XH$. Hence $X$ lies in the coset $XH$ that it itself generates. Therefore the union of all the cosets coincides with $G$.

Lemma 2: Two left cosets either coincide completely or do not intersect at all.
Suppose that $XH$ and $YH$ have a common element $Z$. Then there exist $H_i, H_j \in H$ such that

$$ Z = X H_i = Y H_j $$

Multiply both sides of the right-hand equality of this equation by $H_i^{-1}$ from the right ($H$ is a group, so $H_i^{-1}\in H$):

$$ X H_i H_i^{-1} = Y H_j H_i^{-1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X = Y (H_j H_i^{-1}) $$

Here $H_j H_i^{-1} \in H$ (closure), so writing this as $H_k$ we have $X = YH_k$. Hence

$$ XH = (YH_k)H = Y(H_k H) = YH $$

In the last equality we used the rearrangement theorem (Theorem 6.1) within $H$: $H_k H = H$. Therefore $XH$ and $YH$ coincide completely. Since a single common element is enough to make them coincide entirely, taking the contrapositive gives “if they do not coincide, they do not intersect.”

Lemma 3: Every left coset has exactly $h_H$ elements.
It suffices to show that $XH = \{XH_1, XH_2, \dots, XH_{h_H}\}$ contains no repetitions. If $XH_i = XH_j$, multiplying by $X^{-1}$ from the left gives $H_i = H_j$. Hence if $i \ne j$, the elements are different. There are exactly $h_H$ of them.

Conclusion. By Lemmas 1 and 2, $G$ is decomposed into a union of mutually disjoint left cosets. Let $\ell$ be their number; by Lemma 3, each coset has $h_H$ elements. Therefore

$$ h = \ell \times h_H $$

Since $\ell$ is a positive integer, $h_H$ is a divisor of $h$. ∎

Example 6.6: Checking Lagrange's theorem for $C_{3v}$

The orders of the subgroups found in Example 6.5 were $1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 6$. The divisors of $h=6$ are $1,2,3,6$, and all the orders are among them. ✓

The indices are $6/1=6$, $6/2=3$, $6/3=2$, and $6/6=1$, respectively.

Indeed, $H=\{E,\sigma_v\}$ (order 2, index 3) had three left cosets (Section 6.5.2). For $H=\{E,C_3,C_3^2\}$ (order 3, index 2) we have

$$ EH = \{E, C_3, C_3^2\}, \qquad \sigma_v H = \{\sigma_v,\; \sigma_v C_3,\; \sigma_v C_3^2\} = \{\sigma_v, \sigma_v', \sigma_v''\} $$

and the group indeed splits into two. $2\times3=6$ ✓ ∎

Note that the converse does not hold. If we ask, “for a divisor $d$ of $h$, is there always a subgroup of order $d$?”, the answer in general is no (the alternating group $A_4$ of order 12 has no subgroup of order 6). However, if $d$ is a prime number, one always exists (Cauchy's theorem).

Corollary: A group whose order is prime is cyclic and has no proper subgroups

Lagrange's theorem has several powerful corollaries.

Corollary 1: The order of an element is a divisor of the order of the group. If the order of an element $A$ is $n$, then $\{E,A,\dots,A^{n-1}\}$ is a subgroup of order $n$, so by Theorem 6.2, $n \mid h$. In $C_{3v}$ the orders of the elements are $1,2,3$, all of which are divisors of 6 ✓

Corollary 2: $A^h = E$ for every element. By Corollary 1 we can write $h = mn$ ($n$ is the order of $A$), so $A^h = (A^n)^m = E^m = E$. For $C_{3v}$, $C_3^6 = (C_3^3)^2 = E$ and $\sigma_v^6 = (\sigma_v^2)^3 = E$ ✓

Corollary 3: A group whose order is a prime $p$ has no subgroups other than $\{E\}$ and itself, and moreover it is the cyclic group $C_p$ generated by any $A \ne E$. The order of a subgroup is a divisor of $p$, so it can only be $1$ or $p$. The cyclic subgroup generated by $A\ne E$ has order $\ge 2$, hence order $p$, that is, the whole group.

This Corollary 3 means that “there is essentially only one group of order $p$.” If a molecule or crystal has only symmetry of order 2 or order 3, its group structure is already determined.

6.6 Conjugation and Classes

6.6.1 Definition of conjugation

Definition: Conjugate and conjugacy class

For elements $A, B, X$ of a group, if there exists $X \in G$ such that

$$ B = X A X^{-1} $$

then $B$ is said to be conjugate to $A$.

The set of all elements that are conjugate to one another is called a conjugacy class. The group is partitioned into a union of mutually disjoint classes.

Derivation: Conjugation is an equivalence relation (which is why the group is partitioned cleanly)

We check that “$A \sim B$ (conjugate)” is an equivalence relation. The three conditions for an equivalence relation are reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity.

Reflexivity $A\sim A$: Take $X=E$; then $EAE^{-1} = A$. ✓

Symmetry (if $A\sim B$ then $B \sim A$): Multiply both sides of $B = XAX^{-1}$ by $X^{-1}$ from the left and by $X$ from the right.

$$ X^{-1} B X = X^{-1}(XAX^{-1})X = (X^{-1}X)A(X^{-1}X) = A $$

Letting $Y = X^{-1}$, we obtain $A = YBY^{-1}$. ✓

Transitivity (if $A\sim B$ and $B \sim C$ then $A \sim C$): Let $B = XAX^{-1}$ and $C = YBY^{-1}$. Then

$$ C = Y(XAX^{-1})Y^{-1} = (YX)A(X^{-1}Y^{-1}) = (YX)A(YX)^{-1} $$

Here $(YX)^{-1} = X^{-1}Y^{-1}$ (note that the order is reversed; this can be checked from $(YX)(X^{-1}Y^{-1}) = Y(XX^{-1})Y^{-1} = YY^{-1}=E$). Letting $Z=YX \in G$, we have $C = ZAZ^{-1}$. ✓

Since this is an equivalence relation, the group is partitioned into classes exhaustively and without overlap. ∎

6.6.2 Finding the classes of $C_{3v}$ by computing all the conjugations

This is the climax of this chapter. Without skipping anything, we compute all $18$ conjugations. Recall that the inverses are $E^{-1}=E$, $C_3^{-1}=C_3^2$, $(C_3^2)^{-1}=C_3$, and $\sigma^{-1}=\sigma$ (for all three mirror planes). All the products below are read from Table 6.1.

Calculation 1: The class of the identity element $E$

For any $X$,

$$ X E X^{-1} = X X^{-1} = E $$

Whatever $X$ we take, we return to $E$. Hence

$$ \boxed{\ \text{class } \mathcal{C}_1 = \{E\}\ } $$

The identity element always forms a class by itself. ∎

Calculation 2: Computing all the conjugates of $C_3$

We find $XC_3X^{-1}$ for the six cases $X = E, C_3, C_3^2, \sigma_v, \sigma_v', \sigma_v''$.

(i) $X=E$: $EC_3E = C_3$.

(ii) $X=C_3$ ($X^{-1}=C_3^2$): First, $C_3 C_3 = C_3^2$ (Table 6.1, row $C_3$, column $C_3$). Next,

$$ C_3^2 \cdot C_3^2 = C_3 \quad (\text{Table 6.1, }C_3^2\text{ row, }C_3^2\text{ column}) $$

Hence $C_3 C_3 C_3^2 = C_3$.

(iii) $X=C_3^2$ ($X^{-1}=C_3$): $C_3^2 C_3 = E$, followed by $E \cdot C_3 = C_3$. Hence $C_3$.

(This is of course so, because the $C_3$ elements commute with one another. Rotations about the same axis commute.)

(iv) $X=\sigma_v$ ($X^{-1}=\sigma_v$): First, $\sigma_v C_3 = \sigma_v'$ (row $\sigma_v$, column $C_3$). Next,

$$ \sigma_v' \cdot \sigma_v = C_3^2 \quad (\sigma_v'\text{ row, }\sigma_v\text{ column}) $$

Hence $\sigma_v C_3 \sigma_v = C_3^2$.

(v) $X=\sigma_v'$: $\sigma_v' C_3 = \sigma_v''$ (row $\sigma_v'$, column $C_3$). Then $\sigma_v'' \sigma_v' = C_3^2$ (row $\sigma_v''$, column $\sigma_v'$). Hence $C_3^2$.

(vi) $X=\sigma_v''$: $\sigma_v'' C_3 = \sigma_v$ (row $\sigma_v''$, column $C_3$). Then $\sigma_v \sigma_v'' = C_3^2$ (row $\sigma_v$, column $\sigma_v''$). Hence $C_3^2$.

The six results are $C_3, C_3, C_3, C_3^2, C_3^2, C_3^2$. Collecting the distinct ones,

$$ \boxed{\ \text{class } \mathcal{C}_2 = \{C_3,\ C_3^2\}\ } $$

∎ Starting from $C_3^2$ gives the same class (of course, by symmetry). As a check, evaluating $\sigma_v C_3^2 \sigma_v$, we have $\sigma_v C_3^2 = \sigma_v''$ and $\sigma_v''\sigma_v = C_3$, so indeed we obtain $C_3$.

Calculation 3: Computing all the conjugates of $\sigma_v$

We find $X\sigma_v X^{-1}$ for the six cases.

(i) $X=E$: $\sigma_v$.

(ii) $X=C_3$ ($X^{-1}=C_3^2$): $C_3\sigma_v = \sigma_v''$ (row $C_3$, column $\sigma_v$). Then

$$ \sigma_v'' \cdot C_3^2 = \sigma_v' \quad (\sigma_v''\text{ row, }C_3^2\text{ column}) $$

Hence $C_3\sigma_vC_3^2 = \sigma_v'$.

(iii) $X=C_3^2$ ($X^{-1}=C_3$): $C_3^2\sigma_v = \sigma_v'$ (row $C_3^2$, column $\sigma_v$). Then $\sigma_v' C_3 = \sigma_v''$. Hence $\sigma_v''$.

(iv) $X=\sigma_v$: $\sigma_v\sigma_v\sigma_v = E\sigma_v = \sigma_v$.

(v) $X=\sigma_v'$: $\sigma_v'\sigma_v = C_3^2$ (row $\sigma_v'$, column $\sigma_v$). Then $C_3^2\sigma_v' = \sigma_v''$ (row $C_3^2$, column $\sigma_v'$). Hence $\sigma_v''$.

(vi) $X=\sigma_v''$: $\sigma_v''\sigma_v = C_3$ (row $\sigma_v''$, column $\sigma_v$). Then $C_3\sigma_v'' = \sigma_v'$ (row $C_3$, column $\sigma_v''$). Hence $\sigma_v'$.

The results are $\sigma_v, \sigma_v', \sigma_v'', \sigma_v, \sigma_v'', \sigma_v'$. Collecting the distinct ones,

$$ \boxed{\ \text{class } \mathcal{C}_3 = \{\sigma_v,\ \sigma_v',\ \sigma_v''\}\ } $$

∎

$$ \begin{equation} C_{3v} = \underbrace{\{E\}}_{\mathcal{C}_1} \cup \underbrace{\{C_3, C_3^2\}}_{\mathcal{C}_2} \cup \underbrace{\{\sigma_v,\sigma_v',\sigma_v''\}}_{\mathcal{C}_3}, \qquad 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 \label{eq:6-classes} \end{equation} $$

This is the true identity of what is written in the heading of the character table as “$E$, $2C_3$, $3\sigma_v$.” $C_{3v}$ has three classes. In Chapter 8 we will show that the number of irreducible representations equals the number of classes, so from the present calculation alone we can already predict that $C_{3v}$ has exactly three irreducible representations ($A_1, A_2, E$).

Why? Class = operations of the same geometric kind

The way Eq. \eqref{eq:6-classes} splits has a clear geometric meaning.

  • $\{E\}$: the operation of doing nothing. It differs from all the others.
  • $\{C_3, C_3^2\}$: rotations by $\pm120^\circ$ about the same axis. They differ only in the sense of rotation, and the “kind” of operation is the same.
  • $\{\sigma_v, \sigma_v', \sigma_v''\}$: the three mirror planes. Each is a “reflection in a plane containing the $z$ axis and one H atom,” and they are distinguished only by name. Rotating the triangle around carries them into one another.

What the conjugate $XAX^{-1}$ means is “how the operation $A$ looks when the coordinate system is replaced by $X$.” The factor $X^{-1}$ on the right carries us from the new coordinate system to the old one, we apply $A$, and $X$ brings us back to the new coordinate system. Hence two conjugate operations are nothing other than “the same operation seen from different directions.”

Indeed, (ii) of Calculation 3 can be read as $C_3\sigma_v C_3^{-1} = \sigma_v'$. This says: “rotate the triangle by $-120^\circ$ (= $C_3^{-1}$), reflect in the plane of $\sigma_v$, and return by $C_3$” = “reflect in the adjacent plane $\sigma_v'$ from the start.” Please follow this sentence while looking at Figure 6.1(b).

Conversely, $\sigma_v$ and $C_3$ are not conjugate because a reflection ($\det=-1$) and a rotation ($\det=+1$) are geometrically different things. In fact, $\det(XAX^{-1}) = \det X \det A (\det X)^{-1} = \det A$, so conjugate elements have equal determinants. For the same reason, conjugate elements also have equal traces (the Math Note in Section 4.1.2 of Chapter 4). This is the basis of the statement that “elements of the same class have the same character” (Chapter 8).

Theorem 6.3: The number of elements in a class is a divisor of the order of the group

Let the centralizer of an element $A$ be

$$ Z(A) = \{X \in G \mid XA = AX\} $$

(the set of all elements that commute with $A$). Then $Z(A)$ is a subgroup of $G$, and the number of elements in the class of $A$ equals the index $[G:Z(A)] = h/\abs{Z(A)}$. By Lagrange's theorem, this is a divisor of $h$.

Key points of the proof and a check with $C_{3v}$

Key points of the proof. We examine the condition for $XAX^{-1} = YAY^{-1}$. Multiplying both sides by $Y^{-1}$ from the left and by $Y$ from the right gives $(Y^{-1}X)A(Y^{-1}X)^{-1} = A$, that is, $Y^{-1}X \in Z(A)$, or equivalently $X \in YZ(A)$. The converse also holds. Therefore “the set of all $X$ giving the same conjugate element” = “one left coset of $Z(A)$.” The number of distinct conjugate elements = the number of cosets = the index $[G:Z(A)]$. ∎

6.7 Normal Subgroups, Quotient Groups, and Homomorphisms

6.7.1 Normal subgroups

Definition: Normal subgroup (invariant subgroup)

When a subgroup $N \subseteq G$ satisfies, for every $X \in G$,

$$ XNX^{-1} = N $$

we call $N$ a normal subgroup (also called an invariant subgroup) and write $N \triangleleft G$.

There are several equivalent restatements.

  • $XN = NX$ for every $X$ (the left cosets and the right cosets coincide)
  • $N$ is a union of classes

The subgroup $N=\{E,C_3,C_3^2\}$ of $C_{3v}$ has order 3 and index 2, so this argument alone shows that it is normal. In general, the subgroup consisting only of the proper rotations is always a normal subgroup of index 2 in a point group that contains reflections (Section 6.7.3 below).

6.7.2 Quotient groups

Definition: Quotient group (factor group)

When $N \triangleleft G$, the set of all cosets with respect to $N$,

$$ G/N = \{N,\; X_2N,\; X_3N,\; \dots,\; X_\ell N\} \qquad (\ell = [G:N]) $$

becomes a group of order $\ell$ under the product of cosets $(XN)(YN) = (XY)N$. This is called the quotient group. Its identity element is $N$ itself.

For this product to be defined consistently (to be well-defined), $N$ must be normal. Indeed, if $N$ is normal, then $XNYN = XYNN = XYN$; that is, $N$ can be slipped past $Y$ to its right.

6.7.3 Homomorphisms and isomorphisms

Definition: Homomorphism and isomorphism

When a map $\varphi: G \to G'$ between two groups $G$ and $G'$ satisfies, for all $A,B\in G$,

$$ \varphi(AB) = \varphi(A)\varphi(B) $$

we call $\varphi$ a homomorphism. This means that it “preserves the product structure.”

If, in addition, $\varphi$ is one-to-one and onto (a bijection), it is called an isomorphism, and we write $G \cong G'$. Two isomorphic groups differ only in the names of their elements; as groups they are the same.

The kernel of a homomorphism $\varphi$ is defined by $\mathrm{Ker}\,\varphi = \{A \in G \mid \varphi(A) = E'\}$. The kernel is always a normal subgroup of $G$.

Example 6.9: $\varphi(A) = \det A$ is a homomorphism

Consider the map that assigns to each element of $C_{3v}$ its determinant.

$$ \varphi(E)=\varphi(C_3)=\varphi(C_3^2) = +1, \qquad \varphi(\sigma_v)=\varphi(\sigma_v')=\varphi(\sigma_v'') = -1 $$

By the multiplicativity of the determinant, $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$, we have $\varphi(AB) = \varphi(A)\varphi(B)$. This is precisely the definition of a homomorphism. ✓

The target group is $\{+1,-1\}$ (with the ordinary multiplication as the product), which is isomorphic to the group $C_2$ of order 2.

The kernel is the set of elements with $\varphi(A)=+1$, that is, $\mathrm{Ker}\,\varphi = \{E, C_3, C_3^2\} = N$. As we verified in Example 6.7, this is a normal subgroup. ✓

And, as Eq. \eqref{eq:6-quotient} shows, $C_{3v}/N \cong C_2$. This is a concrete instance of the homomorphism theorem (the first isomorphism theorem), which reads

$$ G/\mathrm{Ker}\,\varphi \;\cong\; \mathrm{Im}\,\varphi $$

∎

In the language of Chapter 7, this $\varphi$ is precisely the one-dimensional representation $A_2$ of $C_{3v}$. An abstract homomorphism of group theory is, just as it stands, a representation of the kind used in physics. A homomorphism whose kernel is not just $\{E\}$ (that is, one that is not one-to-one) is called an unfaithful representation, and one whose kernel is limited to $\{E\}$ (that is, one that is one-to-one) is called a faithful representation. In the present example, $\varphi=\det$ has the nontrivial kernel $\{E,C_3,C_3^2\}$, so it is unfaithful.

Example 6.10: $C_{3v} \cong S_3$ (the permutation group on three letters)

In the notation note of Section 6.2.1, we wrote the six elements of $C_{3v}$ as permutations of the vertices. This correspondence

$$ E \mapsto (\,),\quad C_3 \mapsto (1\,2\,3),\quad C_3^2\mapsto(1\,3\,2),\quad \sigma_v \mapsto (2\,3),\quad \sigma_v'\mapsto(1\,3),\quad \sigma_v''\mapsto(1\,2) $$

is one-to-one and onto, and it also preserves products (we used it to compute products in Example 6.2). There are $3!=6$ permutations of three letters in all, and the right-hand side above exhausts all of them. Therefore

$$ C_{3v} \cong S_3 $$

Furthermore, the symmetry group $D_3$ of the equilateral triangle (a group of order 6 consisting of a threefold axis and three twofold axes perpendicular to it) has the same structure, so

$$ C_{3v} \cong D_3 \cong S_3 $$

holds (Exercise 6.2). ∎

This is the idea of an “abstract group.” $C_{3v}$ (the symmetry of ammonia), $D_3$ (the point group $32$ of $\alpha$-quartz), and $S_3$ (the rearrangements of three objects) are physically entirely different things. As groups, however, they are identical, and therefore they have the same character table, the same dimensions of the irreducible representations, and the same form of the selection rules. This means that once we carry out a group-theoretical calculation, the result can be used for all of them.

There are only two groups of order 6

Up to isomorphism, there are only two groups of order 6: $C_6$ (the cyclic group, commutative) and $S_3 \cong C_{3v} \cong D_3$ (noncommutative). (By Cauchy's theorem there is an element $a$ of order 2 and an element $b$ of order 3; if $aba^{-1}$ is $b$ we get $C_6$, and if it is $b^2$ we get $S_3$.)

Searching Table 4.3 for point groups of order 6, we find five: $C_6$, $C_{3i}=S_6$, $D_3=32$, $C_{3v}=3m$, and $C_{3h}=\bar6$. Of these, $C_6, S_6, C_{3h}$ are isomorphic to $C_6$ (commutative), and $D_3$ and $C_{3v}$ are isomorphic to $S_3$ (noncommutative). Here the important fact appears that different point groups can be the same as abstract groups.

6.8 Group Theory and Quantum Mechanics — $[\Ham,\hat{R}]=0$

So far the discussion has been purely mathematical. In this section it connects with quantum mechanics. The content of this section is the starting point for everything from Chapter 7 onward.

6.8.1 Turning a symmetry operation into an operator acting on functions

A symmetry operation $R$ moves a point $\rr$ to the point $R\rr$. In quantum mechanics, however, what we want to handle is the wave function $\psi(\rr)$. We therefore have to define, corresponding to $R$, an operator acting on functions $\hat{R}$.

Definition: The operator corresponding to a symmetry operation

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{R}\,f(\rr) \equiv f(R^{-1}\rr) \label{eq:6-Rop} \end{equation} $$

That is, the value of the new function $\hat R f$ at the point $\rr$ equals the value of the original function $f$ at the point $R^{-1}\rr$.

Why $R^{-1}$ and not $R$?

The point on which every beginner stumbles is that $R^{-1}$ appears on the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-Rop}. There are two reasons.

Reason 1 (intuition): Consider moving the graph of a function by the operation $R$. If the original function had a peak at the point $\rr_0$, we want the peak to be at the point $R\rr_0$ after the move. Denoting the new function by $g$, we have $g(R\rr_0) = f(\rr_0)$; that is, setting $\rr = R\rr_0$, $g(\rr) = f(R^{-1}\rr)$. This is Eq. \eqref{eq:6-Rop}.

Reason 2 (structure): Unless $R^{-1}$ is included, the product of operators does not follow the same order as the product of operations. Let us check this explicitly. Setting $g(\rr) \equiv \hat R_2 f(\rr) = f(R_2^{-1}\rr)$, we have

$$ \hat R_1 \hat R_2 f(\rr) = \hat R_1 g(\rr) = g(R_1^{-1}\rr) = f\!\left(R_2^{-1}R_1^{-1}\rr\right) $$

Using the inverse-matrix formula $(R_1R_2)^{-1} = R_2^{-1}R_1^{-1}$, we obtain

$$ \hat R_1 \hat R_2 f(\rr) = f\!\left((R_1R_2)^{-1}\rr\right) = \widehat{R_1R_2}\,f(\rr) $$

that is,

$$ \begin{equation} \hat R_1 \hat R_2 = \widehat{R_1 R_2} \label{eq:6-homom} \end{equation} $$

The product of operators corresponds exactly to the product in the group. If we had defined $\hat Rf(\rr)=f(R\rr)$ without including $R^{-1}$, the order would be reversed here (an anti-homomorphism would result), and all the subsequent arguments would be turned inside out.

Equation \eqref{eq:6-homom} says that $R \mapsto \hat R$ is a homomorphism. This is precisely what Chapter 7 calls a representation.

6.8.2 Derivation of $[\Ham,\hat R]=0$

Derivation: If the potential is symmetric, the Hamiltonian commutes with the symmetry operation

Let the one-particle Hamiltonian be

$$ \Ham = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V(\rr) $$

Suppose that $R$ is a symmetry operation of the system, that is, that

$$ V(R^{-1}\rr) = V(\rr) \qquad (\text{for all } \rr) $$

holds (for $\mathrm{NH_3}$, $V$ is the potential created by the atomic nuclei, and this holds for all six operations of $C_{3v}$). We then show that $[\Ham,\hat R]=0$.

Step 1: Write out $\hat R \Ham f$. By definition \eqref{eq:6-Rop}, the value of $\hat R(\Ham f)$ at the point $\rr$ is the value of $(\Ham f)$ at the point $R^{-1}\rr$. Setting $\rr' \equiv R^{-1}\rr$, we have

$$ \hat R (\Ham f)(\rr) = \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla_{\rr'}^2 f(\rr') + V(\rr')f(\rr')\right]_{\rr'=R^{-1}\rr} $$

Step 2: The Laplacian is invariant under orthogonal transformations. We use the fact that $R$ is an orthogonal matrix ($R^{\mathsf T}R = I$). Writing out the components of $\rr' = R^{-1}\rr$ gives $x'_i = \sum_j (R^{-1})_{ij}x_j$, so by the chain rule

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} = \sum_i \frac{\partial x'_i}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x'_i} = \sum_i (R^{-1})_{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x'_i} $$

Therefore

$$ \nabla_{\rr}^2 = \sum_j \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_j^2} = \sum_j \sum_{i,k}(R^{-1})_{ij}(R^{-1})_{kj}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x'_i \partial x'_k} $$

Since $R^{-1} = R^{\mathsf T}$ when $R$ is an orthogonal matrix, $(R^{-1})_{ij} = R_{ji}$. Hence

$$ \sum_j (R^{-1})_{ij}(R^{-1})_{kj} = \sum_j R_{ji}R_{jk} = (R^{\mathsf T}R)_{ik} = \delta_{ik} $$

Substituting this, we obtain

$$ \nabla_\rr^2 = \sum_{i,k}\delta_{ik}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x'_i\partial x'_k} = \sum_i \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i'^2} = \nabla_{\rr'}^2 $$

That is, the form of the Laplacian does not change under rotations and reflections. Using this result, the first term of Step 1 can be rewritten as

$$ \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla_{\rr'}^2 f(\rr')\right]_{\rr'=R^{-1}\rr} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla_\rr^2\left[f(R^{-1}\rr)\right] = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla_\rr^2 \left(\hat R f\right)(\rr) $$

.

Step 3: The potential term. The second term of Step 1 is $V(R^{-1}\rr)\,f(R^{-1}\rr) = V(R^{-1}\rr)\,(\hat Rf)(\rr)$.

Step 4: Put everything together and compare. From the above,

$$ \hat R \Ham f = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2(\hat Rf) + V(R^{-1}\rr)\,(\hat Rf) $$

On the other hand, taking the opposite order gives

$$ \Ham \hat R f = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2(\hat Rf) + V(\rr)\,(\hat Rf) $$

Subtracting the two equations, the kinetic-energy terms cancel completely, and

$$ \left(\hat R \Ham - \Ham \hat R\right) f = \left[V(R^{-1}\rr) - V(\rr)\right](\hat R f) $$

By the assumption $V(R^{-1}\rr)=V(\rr)$, the right-hand side is zero. Since $f$ was arbitrary,

$$ \begin{equation} [\Ham,\ \hat R] = \Ham\hat R - \hat R\Ham = 0 \label{eq:6-commute} \end{equation} $$

∎ That the potential is invariant under the symmetry operation $R$ and that $\Ham$ commutes with $\hat R$ are one and the same statement.

6.8.3 What can we conclude? — Symmetry operations mix degenerate eigenstates

Theorem 6.4: A symmetry operation maps a state to a state of the same energy

Suppose that $[\Ham,\hat R]=0$ and that $\psi$ is an eigenfunction with energy $E$, that is, $\Ham\psi = E\psi$. Then $\hat R\psi$ is also an eigenfunction with the same energy $E$.

Proof (finished in three lines)

$$ \Ham\left(\hat R\psi\right) \overset{(1)}{=} \hat R\left(\Ham\psi\right) \overset{(2)}{=} \hat R\left(E\psi\right) \overset{(3)}{=} E\left(\hat R\psi\right) $$

(1) is the commutativity \eqref{eq:6-commute}, (2) is the eigenvalue equation, and (3) uses the fact that $E$ is a constant and $\hat R$ is a linear operator. ∎

These three lines are the foundation of every application of group theory to physics.

Physical Meaning: The origin of degeneracy

Let us read the consequences of Theorem 6.4 carefully. Denote by $\mathcal{V}_E$ the eigenspace of energy $E$ (the linear space spanned by the eigenfunctions having the same $E$).

(a) $\mathcal{V}_E$ is mapped onto itself by every operation of the group. If $\psi \in \mathcal{V}_E$, then $\hat R\psi \in \mathcal{V}_E$ (Theorem 6.4). In mathematical language, $\mathcal{V}_E$ is an invariant subspace under the action of the group $G$.

(b) The nondegenerate case. If $\mathcal{V}_E$ is one-dimensional, $\hat R\psi$ can only be a constant multiple of $\psi$: $\hat R\psi = c_R\psi$. Since $\hat R$ preserves the norm, $\abs{c_R}=1$. That is, a nondegenerate eigenfunction is a simultaneous eigenfunction of all the symmetry operations. For example, the ground state of $C_{3v}$ can be labeled by its symmetry: it is invariant under $C_3$ ($c=1$), and for $\sigma_v$ we have $c=\pm1$ (symmetric if $+1$, antisymmetric, with a sign change, if $-1$).

(c) The degenerate case. If $\mathcal{V}_E$ is $d$-dimensional ($d\ge2$), then choosing a basis $\{\psi_1,\dots,\psi_d\}$ we can expand

$$ \hat R\,\psi_n = \sum_{m=1}^{d} D_{mn}(R)\,\psi_m $$

The collection of these $d\times d$ matrices $D(R)$ is a $d$-dimensional representation of the group $G$. From Eq. \eqref{eq:6-homom} it follows that $D(R_1)D(R_2) = D(R_1R_2)$ (we will confirm this in Chapter 7).

(d) Hence the degeneracy is the dimension of a representation. The answer to “why is a certain level of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ twofold degenerate?” is “because $C_{3v}$ has a two-dimensional irreducible representation.” Even without knowing the explicit form of $\Ham$ at all, the possible values of the degeneracy ($1,1,2$ for $C_{3v}$) are determined by symmetry alone. This is the gift of group theory to physics.

Example 6.11: The $p$ orbitals in a $C_{3v}$-symmetric field

Consider the three $p$ orbitals $p_x, p_y, p_z$ on the N atom of $\mathrm{NH_3}$ ($z$ is the $C_3$ axis). Investigate how they are mixed by the symmetry operations.

Solution. The $p$ orbitals have the form $p_i \propto x_i\,g(r)$, and the radial part $g(r)$ is invariant under rotations and reflections. It therefore suffices to see how $x, y, z$ transform.

$p_z$: Neither $C_3$ nor $\sigma_v$ changes $z$ (the entry in the third row and third column of every operation matrix is $1$, and the other entries of that row and column are $0$). Hence

$$ \hat R\,p_z = p_z \quad (\text{for all } R) $$

$p_z$ forms a one-dimensional invariant subspace. It belongs to the totally symmetric representation $A_1$.

$p_x, p_y$: The upper-left $2\times2$ block of the $C_3$ matrix has off-diagonal elements, so $x$ and $y$ are mixed. Computing according to $\hat R f(\rr) = f(R^{-1}\rr)$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:6-Rop}, we obtain

$$ \hat{C}_3\,(\,p_x,\; p_y\,) = (\,p_x,\; p_y\,)\begin{pmatrix}-\frac12 & -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] \frac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\frac12\end{pmatrix} $$

The reason the matrix multiplies the row vector of basis functions from the right is that we have simply written out the convention $\hat R\,\psi_n = \sum_m D_{mn}(R)\,\psi_m$ stated above; this matrix is precisely the representation matrix $D(C_3)$. Reading off the first and second columns, we have $\hat{C}_3 p_x = -\tfrac12 p_x + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}p_y$ and $\hat{C}_3 p_y = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}p_x - \tfrac12 p_y$.

$p_x$ alone is not closed; the two-dimensional space $\{p_x,p_y\}$ is needed. This two-dimensional space is closed under all the operations of $C_{3v}$, and moreover it cannot be split into smaller invariant subspaces. Within the real numbers it is immediately clear that the $2\times2$ block of $C_3$ cannot be diagonalized; in fact, if we allow complex numbers, $C_3$ alone splits the space into two one-dimensional spaces, $x \pm iy$. It is still irreducible because the reflection $\sigma_v$ ($y \to -y$) interchanges these two, so that for the whole of $C_{3v}$ there is no invariant subspace smaller than two-dimensional.

Conclusion: $p_x$ and $p_y$ are necessarily degenerate under $C_{3v}$ symmetry. This is the two-dimensional irreducible representation $E$. Only $p_z$ separates off and has a different energy. Without calculating any details of $\Ham$, we have shown that a $3$-fold degeneracy splits into $1+2$. ∎

Applying the same argument to $d$ orbitals in an octahedral field $O_h$ gives $\Gamma_d = E_g \oplus T_{2g}$ ($5 = 2+3$). This is the crystal-field splitting, the subject of Chapter 10.

Caution: Accidental degeneracy

What group theory predicts is the degeneracy that symmetry requires. It can also happen that the energies of two levels coincide by chance, even though symmetry does not require it. This is called accidental degeneracy.

However, even when a degeneracy looks “accidental,” there is often in fact an overlooked symmetry (a hidden symmetry). A famous example is the hydrogen atom: the degeneracy of $2s$ and $2p$ cannot be explained by spherical symmetry ($SO(3)$) alone. Behind it lies a larger symmetry, $SO(4)$, arising from the conservation of the Runge–Lenz vector.

As a way of using group theory, the correct attitude is this: first predict from symmetry, and if an extra degeneracy is found in an experiment or a numerical calculation, take it as a signal to “look for a hidden symmetry.”

What we have done in this section can be summarized in a nutshell as follows.

Given the symmetry group $G$ of the Hamiltonian, the eigenspace belonging to each energy eigenvalue becomes a representation space of $G$. Therefore the levels are labeled by the irreducible representations of $G$, and the degeneracy equals the dimension of the irreducible representation.

6.9 Summary and Exercises

6.9.1 Summary

  • The four group axioms are closure, associativity, an identity element, and inverse elements; commutativity is not required. $C_{3v}=\{E,C_3,C_3^2,\sigma_v,\sigma_v',\sigma_v''\}$ is a group of order 6, and the matrix of a vertical mirror plane is given by Eq. \eqref{eq:6-mirror}.
  • The multiplication table completely describes the structure of a group. Tracking the vertex numbers of the triangle is the fastest method, and writing the operations as permutations makes the calculation mechanical. $\sigma_v C_3 = \sigma_v'$ and $C_3\sigma_v = \sigma_v''$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:6-svc3} and Table 6.1).
  • Rearrangement theorem: every element appears exactly once in each row and each column of the multiplication table. The proof is the single line “$XA_i=XA_j \Rightarrow A_i=A_j$.” Its corollary $\sum_{A}f(XA)=\sum_A f(A)$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:6-rearr-sum}) is the key to the orthogonality theorems of Chapters 7 and 8.
  • $C_{3v}$ is noncommutative ($\sigma_vC_3 \ne C_3\sigma_v$), and order 6 is the smallest order of a non-abelian group. Only because of this noncommutativity do irreducible representations of dimension two or higher exist, and degeneracy arising from symmetry occurs.
  • The subgroups are the six groups $\{E\}$, $\{E,\sigma_v\}\times3$, $\{E,C_3,C_3^2\}$, and $C_{3v}$. The orders $1,2,2,2,3,6$ are all divisors of 6 (Lagrange's theorem; the proof rests on the fact that the cosets divide the group into boxes of equal size without overlap).
  • Conjugation $B=XAX^{-1}$ is an equivalence relation and partitions the group into classes. The classes of $C_{3v}$ are the three sets $\{E\}$, $\{C_3,C_3^2\}$, $\{\sigma_v,\sigma_v',\sigma_v''\}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:6-classes}). Since conjugation means “changing the coordinate system and looking at the same operation,” a class = geometrically the same kind of operation. Conjugate elements have equal determinants and equal traces.
  • A normal subgroup is “a subgroup that is a union of classes.” $\{E,C_3,C_3^2\}$ is normal, whereas $\{E,\sigma_v\}$ is not normal (Figure 6.3). The quotient group $C_{3v}/\{E,C_3,C_3^2\}\cong C_2$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:6-quotient}) is the quotient by the kernel of the homomorphism that takes $\det$. As for isomorphism, $C_{3v}\cong D_3 \cong S_3$; even if two systems are physically different, if they are the same as groups, their character tables and selection rules are the same.
  • Connection with quantum mechanics: if we define $\hat Rf(\rr)=f(R^{-1}\rr)$, then $\hat R_1\hat R_2 = \widehat{R_1R_2}$. If the potential is symmetric, then $[\Ham,\hat R]=0$, and from $\Ham\psi=E\psi$ we get $\Ham(\hat R\psi)=E(\hat R\psi)$. In other words, each energy eigenspace is an invariant subspace closed under the action of the group, and the degeneracy equals the dimension of the irreducible representation. In $\mathrm{NH_3}$, $p_z$ forms a one-dimensional invariant subspace and $\{p_x,p_y\}$ forms a two-dimensional one.

Hint: Setting $\theta = 0^\circ, 90^\circ, 45^\circ, 135^\circ$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:6-mirror} gives the matrices of the four mirror planes. If we place vertex 1 on the $+x$ axis and number the vertices $1,2,3,4$ counterclockwise from there, then $C_4 = (1\,2\,3\,4)$, $C_2=(1\,3)(2\,4)$, $\sigma_v = (2\,4)$, $\sigma_v'=(1\,3)$, $\sigma_d=(1\,2)(3\,4)$, and $\sigma_d'=(1\,4)(2\,3)$. Part of the answer: $\sigma_vC_4 = \sigma_d'$ and $C_4\sigma_v = \sigma_d$, which are indeed different.

Exercise 6.2: Showing that $D_3 \cong C_{3v}$

$D_3$ (international notation $32$) is a point group of order 6 consisting of a threefold rotation axis along the $z$ axis and three twofold rotation axes $C_2^{(1)},C_2^{(2)},C_2^{(3)}$ perpendicular to it (the point group of $\alpha$-quartz).

(1) Write down the $3\times3$ matrices of the six elements of $D_3$. Assuming that a twofold axis lies in the $xy$ plane in a direction making an angle $\theta$ with the $x$ axis, find its rotation matrix.
(2) Construct the multiplication table of $D_3$ and compare it with Table 6.1.
(3) Show that the correspondence $E\mapsto E$, $C_3\mapsto C_3$, $C_3^2\mapsto C_3^2$, $C_2^{(k)}\mapsto \sigma_v^{(k)}$ is an isomorphism.
(4) Find the classes of $D_3$ and verify that they split as $1+2+3$, the same as in $C_{3v}$.

Hint: (1) The matrix of a $180^\circ$ rotation about an axis at angle $\theta$ in the $xy$ plane is

$$ C_2(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos2\theta & \sin2\theta & 0\\ \sin2\theta & -\cos2\theta & 0\\ 0&0&-1\end{pmatrix} $$

. Comparing with Eq. \eqref{eq:6-mirror}, only the sign of the $(3,3)$ component differs. Therefore the structure of the products of the upper-left $2\times2$ blocks is exactly the same as in $C_{3v}$, and the isomorphism follows. Since $\det C_2(\theta) = +1$ (a proper rotation) and $\det\sigma(\theta)=-1$, the two groups are different as point groups but the same as abstract groups.

Exercise 6.3: A group in which every element satisfies $X^2=E$ is abelian

Suppose that $X^2 = E$ holds for every element $X$ of a group $G$. Show that $G$ is commutative.

Then, using this result, explain why $C_{2v}=\{E,C_2,\sigma_v,\sigma_v'\}$ is commutative, and point out that this assumption does not hold in $C_{3v}$.

Hint: By assumption, $X^2=E$, that is, $X^{-1}=X$, holds for every element. Applying this also to $AB \in G$ gives $(AB)^{-1} = AB$. On the other hand, by the general formula for the inverse, $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} = BA$. Equating the two gives $AB = BA$. ∎
In $C_{2v}$, $C_2^2=E$, $\sigma_v^2=E$, and $\sigma_v'^2=E$, so the assumption is satisfied throughout. In $C_{3v}$, $C_3^2 \ne E$, so the assumption fails, and the group is in fact noncommutative.

Exercise 6.4: Subgroups and normal subgroups of $C_{4v}$

Using the multiplication table of Exercise 6.1, find the following.

(1) List all subgroups of $C_{4v}$ and verify that all of their orders are divisors of 8.
(2) Find the classes of $C_{4v}$ by computing all the conjugates $X A X^{-1}$.
(3) Find all normal subgroups.
(4) Choose one normal subgroup $N$ of order 4 and construct the multiplication table of the quotient group $C_{4v}/N$.

Hint: The answer to (2) is the five classes $\{E\}$, $\{C_2\}$, $\{C_4,C_4^3\}$, $\{\sigma_v,\sigma_v'\}$, $\{\sigma_d,\sigma_d'\}$. The numbers of elements satisfy $1+1+2+2+2=8$ ✓, and all are divisors of 8. $C_2$ forms a class by itself because $C_2$ commutes with every element of the group ($Z(C_2)=C_{4v}$).
(3) The normal subgroups of order 4 are the three groups $\{E,C_4,C_2,C_4^3\}$, $\{E,C_2,\sigma_v,\sigma_v'\}$, and $\{E,C_2,\sigma_d,\sigma_d'\}$ (all of index 2). In addition, $\{E,C_2\}$ is also normal (it is the union of classes $\{E\}\cup\{C_2\}$).
(4) Whichever is chosen, the quotient group has order 2 and is isomorphic to $C_2$.
Since the number of classes in (2) is 5, $C_{4v}$ has five irreducible representations, and from $\sum\ell_i^2 = 8$ we can predict that their dimensions are $1,1,1,1,2$ (to be confirmed in Chapter 8).

Exercise 6.5: Properties of conjugation and classes

(1) Show that two conjugate elements $A$ and $B=XAX^{-1}$ have the same order (the smallest $n$ such that $A^n=E$).
(2) Show that in an abelian group every element forms a class by itself. Hence in an abelian group, “the number of classes = the order of the group.”
(3) Explain, from the viewpoint of closure, why in $C_{3v}$ the set $\{E\}\cup\{C_3,C_3^2\}$ is a subgroup while $\{E\}\cup\{\sigma_v,\sigma_v',\sigma_v''\}$ is not.

Hint: (1) Show that $B^n = (XAX^{-1})^n = XA^nX^{-1}$. Indeed, $B^2 = XAX^{-1}XAX^{-1} = XA(X^{-1}X)AX^{-1} = XA^2X^{-1}$, and the rest follows by induction. If $A^n=E$, then $B^n = XEX^{-1}=E$, and the converse holds likewise, so the smallest $n$ is the same.
(2) $XAX^{-1} = AXX^{-1} = A$.
(3) Since $\sigma_v\sigma_v' = C_3 \notin \{E,\sigma_v,\sigma_v',\sigma_v''\}$, closure fails. Conversely, $C_3C_3^2=E$ and $C_3C_3=C_3^2$, so the rotations are closed among themselves. In general, “the set of all proper rotations” is always a subgroup (by the multiplicativity of $\det$).

Exercise 6.6: Symmetry of nondegenerate states

Suppose that $[\Ham,\hat R]=0$ (for all $R\in G$), and let $\psi$ be a nondegenerate eigenfunction with energy $E$.

(1) Explain why we can write $\hat R\psi = c_R\psi$ ($c_R$ is a constant).
(2) Show that $\abs{c_R}=1$ from the fact that $\hat R$ preserves the norm ($\int\abs{\hat R\psi}^2\dd^3r = \int\abs{\psi}^2\dd^3r$).
(3) Show that the probability density $\abs{\psi(\rr)}^2$ is invariant under all operations of the group $G$.
(4) For the $p_z$ orbital of $\mathrm{NH_3}$, find $c_R$ for all $R\in C_{3v}$. Also state what happens when we try the same for $p_x$.

Hint: (1) By Theorem 6.4, $\hat R\psi$ is also an eigenfunction with the same $E$. Since the state is nondegenerate, its eigenspace is one-dimensional, and therefore $\hat R\psi$ is a constant multiple of $\psi$.
(2) Compare with $\int\abs{c_R\psi}^2 = \abs{c_R}^2\int\abs{\psi}^2$. The invariance of the integral follows from the fact that the Jacobian of the change of variables $\rr\to R\rr$ is $\abs{\det R}=1$.
(3) $\abs{\hat R\psi}^2 = \abs{c_R}^2\abs{\psi}^2 = \abs{\psi}^2$, that is, $\abs{\psi(R^{-1}\rr)}^2 = \abs{\psi(\rr)}^2$. This is the conclusion that the electron density of a nondegenerate state necessarily has the same symmetry as the crystal (molecule), which is important in density functional calculations as well.
(4) For $p_z$, $c_R = 1$ (for all $R$), and this is the totally symmetric representation $A_1$. For $p_x$, $\hat{C}_3 p_x = -\tfrac12 p_x + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}p_y$ (and similarly $\hat{C}_3 p_y = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}p_x - \tfrac12 p_y$), which is not a constant multiple. That is, $p_x$ alone cannot be an eigenfunction, and we must consider the degenerate two-dimensional space $\{p_x,p_y\}$. This is a case in which the premise of (1), “nondegenerate,” breaks down.
