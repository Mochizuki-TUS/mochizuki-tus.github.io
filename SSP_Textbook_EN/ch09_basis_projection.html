Chapter 9Basis Functions and Projection Operators

In Chapter 8 we learned to construct a character table from scratch. However, when we open an actual character table in a textbook, we notice that there is one more column at the right end of the table of character values. In the table for $C_{3v}$, the row $A_1$ carries $z$ and $x^2+y^2,\ z^2$, the row $A_2$ carries $R_z$, and the row $E$ carries $(x,y)$ and $(xz,yz)$. What is this column? Why are $x$ and $y$ written together as a set, while $z$ is written separately? What does the $R$ in $R_z$ stand for?

This chapter answers these questions head-on. What is written in the rightmost column is a basis function. A basis function of an irreducible representation is “a set of functions that, when all the symmetry operations of the group are applied, mix with one another according to the matrices of that irreducible representation.” The fact that $x$ and $y$ necessarily mix under $C_{3v}$ while $z$ never mixes is condensed into that column.

This chapter has another protagonist: the projection operator $\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}$. When this operator acts on an arbitrarily chosen function, it picks out only the “$\Gamma_n$ component” contained in that function. This tool, which can be derived in two lines from the great orthogonality theorem of Chapter 7, turns group theory from a “science of classification” into a “tool for calculation.” The symmetry-adapted linear combinations (SALCs) of molecular orbitals, the normal modes of phonons, and the crystal-field splitting of $d$ orbitals can all be obtained mechanically with this single operator.

In the second half of the chapter, we actually construct SALCs from three hydrogen $1s$ orbitals placed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle, and use them to decompose a $3\times3$ secular equation into a $1\times1$ and a $2\times2$ equation. Once you work it out by hand, you should feel in your bones that group theory is a “clever method of calculation.”

9.1 Symmetry Operations Acting on Functions and Basis Functions

9.1.1 From operations that move points to operations that move functions

In Chapter 4 we treated a symmetry operation $R$ as “a $3\times3$ matrix that sends a point $\rr$ to the point $R\rr$.” In quantum mechanics, however, the objects we want to handle are not points but wave functions. What does it mean to apply a symmetry operation to a function $\psi(\rr)$?

First we prepare an operator that carries symmetry operations into the world of functions. To distinguish it from the matrix $R$, we write the one that acts on functions as $\hat{P}_R$.

Definition: The symmetry operation $\hat{P}_R$ acting on functions

For a symmetry operation $R$ (a $3\times3$ orthogonal matrix), we define the operator $\hat{P}_R$ acting on a function $f$ by

$$ \begin{equation} \left(\hat{P}_R f\right)(\rr) \;=\; f\!\left(R^{-1}\rr\right) \label{eq:9-PRdef} \end{equation} $$

Here $\hat{P}_R f$ is “the new function obtained by rotating (or reflecting) $f$ as a whole by $R$.”

Here almost every student stumbles. Why $R^{-1}$ rather than $R$? We settle this one point carefully from both the geometric and the algebraic side.

Derivation 1 (geometry): Why $R^{-1}$ appears

Suppose that a function $f$ takes large values near a point $\rr_0$ and is almost zero elsewhere (Figure 9.1, left). We want to construct the new function obtained by physically rotating this “peak” by $R$. Write the new function as $g = \hat{P}_R f$.

Since “the peak of $f$ has been rotated by $R$,” the peak of $g$ must be located at the point $R\rr_0$. And the height of the peak does not change. That is,

$$ g(R\rr_0) \;=\; f(\rr_0) $$

must hold for every point $\rr_0$. This is the entire requirement.

All that remains is to rewrite the variable. Setting $\rr \equiv R\rr_0$, we have $\rr_0 = R^{-1}\rr$. Substituting this into both sides of the equation above gives

$$ g(\rr) \;=\; f\!\left(R^{-1}\rr\right) $$

∎ This is Eq. \eqref{eq:9-PRdef}. The reason $R^{-1}$ appears is that “moving the function” is the same as “moving the argument in the opposite direction.”

Put intuitively: if we shift the place from which we read the function’s value (the argument) to the right by $90^\circ$, the peak that is read off appears shifted to the left by $90^\circ$. If we want to move the peak itself to the right, we must move the argument to the left.

Derivation 2 (algebra): Without $R^{-1}$ the group structure breaks

For $\hat{P}_R$ to be meaningful, it must preserve the structure of the group product. That is, we need

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{P}_R\,\hat{P}_S \;=\; \hat{P}_{RS} \label{eq:9-homo} \end{equation} $$

(this is called a homomorphism). Let us check it with the definition \eqref{eq:9-PRdef}. First set $g \equiv \hat{P}_S f$. By the definition, $g(\rr) = f(S^{-1}\rr)$. Next let $\hat{P}_R$ act on this $g$. Again by the definition,

$$ \left(\hat{P}_R\,\hat{P}_S f\right)(\rr) \;=\; \left(\hat{P}_R\, g\right)(\rr) \;=\; g\!\left(R^{-1}\rr\right) $$

Here we put $R^{-1}\rr$ into the argument of $g$. Since $g(\bm{u}) = f(S^{-1}\bm{u})$, taking $\bm{u} = R^{-1}\rr$ gives

$$ g\!\left(R^{-1}\rr\right) \;=\; f\!\left(S^{-1}R^{-1}\rr\right) $$

Now, by the formula for the inverse of a product of matrices, $(RS)^{-1} = S^{-1}R^{-1}$, we have $S^{-1}R^{-1} = (RS)^{-1}$. Therefore

$$ \left(\hat{P}_R\,\hat{P}_S f\right)(\rr) \;=\; f\!\left((RS)^{-1}\rr\right) \;=\; \left(\hat{P}_{RS}f\right)(\rr) $$

∎ Eq. \eqref{eq:9-homo} holds.

What if we had defined it with $R$ instead of $R^{-1}$? Let us try defining $\tilde{P}_R f(\rr) \equiv f(R\rr)$. Following the same calculation, we obtain

$$ \left(\tilde{P}_R\tilde{P}_S f\right)(\rr) = \left(\tilde{P}_S f\right)(R\rr) = f(S R \rr) = \left(\tilde{P}_{SR}f\right)(\rr) $$

That is, $\tilde{P}_R\tilde{P}_S = \tilde{P}_{SR}$, and the order of the product is reversed. In a noncommutative group ($C_{3v}$ is precisely such a group) we have $RS \ne SR$, so this is fatal. The multiplication of representation matrices and the multiplication of operators would disagree, and all the subsequent arguments would collapse. $R^{-1}$ is not something we have “simply decided on”; it is the only possible choice.

Example 9.1: Computing $\hat{P}_{C_4}x$ and checking the sign

Consider the $+90^\circ$ rotation $C_4$ about the $z$ axis. Let $\hat{P}_{C_4}$ act on the function $f(\rr) = x$.

Solution. First, the matrix of $C_4$ is (from the formula of Chapter 4 with $\phi = 90^\circ$)

$$ C_4 = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad C_4^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\-1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix} $$

($C_4^{-1}$ is the $-90^\circ$ rotation). Therefore

$$ C_4^{-1}\rr = \begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\-1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}y\\-x\\z\end{pmatrix} $$

Since $f$ is the function that “picks out the first component,” putting $C_4^{-1}\rr$ into its argument gives $y$ as the first component:

$$ \hat{P}_{C_4}\,x \;=\; y $$

Check of the sign. The function $x$ is the function that is “positive in the $+x$ direction and negative in the $-x$ direction.” Rotating it by $+90^\circ$ should make it “positive in the $+y$ direction and negative in the $-y$ direction.” That is exactly the function $y$. ✓

If we had mistakenly used $f(C_4\rr)$, then since $C_4\rr = (-y,\ x,\ z)$ we would get $\hat{P}_{C_4}x = -y$, and the sign would be reversed. ∎

9.1.2 Definition of basis functions

We are now ready. Let us define the basis functions, the main subject of this chapter.

Definition: Basis functions of an irreducible representation $\Gamma_n$

Suppose that a set of $\ell_n$ functions $\left\{f^{(\Gamma_n)}_1, f^{(\Gamma_n)}_2, \dots, f^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\ell_n}\right\}$ satisfies, for every element $R$ of the group,

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{P}_R\, f^{(\Gamma_n)}_\mu \;=\; \sum_{\nu=1}^{\ell_n} D^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)_{\nu\mu}\; f^{(\Gamma_n)}_\nu \label{eq:9-basis} \end{equation} $$

Then this set is called the set of basis functions of the irreducible representation $\Gamma_n$. The index $\mu$ is called the partner index, and we say that “$f_\mu$ belongs to the $\mu$th row of $\Gamma_n$.”

Caution: Do not get the order of the indices wrong

On the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:9-basis}, the sum is taken over the row index $\nu$, and the column index $\mu$ is the one that is held fixed. That is, “the expansion coefficients of $\hat{P}_R f_\mu$ are the entries of the $\mu$th column of the matrix $D(R)$, read downward.”

This convention is chosen so that $D$ preserves the group product as a product ($D(R)D(S)=D(RS)$, that is, so that it is a homomorphism). If you want to see it in the form of a multiplication, the following two forms are the natural ones. If we arrange the set of functions as a row vector, $\hat{P}_R(f_1,\dots,f_{\ell_n}) = (f_1,\dots,f_{\ell_n})\,D(R)$ is multiplied from the right, and if we arrange the expansion coefficients of $\psi=\sum_\mu c_\mu f_\mu$ as a column vector $\bm{c}$, then $\bm{c}\to D(R)\bm{c}$ is multiplied from the left. However, if we arrange the functions themselves as a column vector $\bm{f}=(f_1,\dots,f_{\ell_n})^{\!\top}$, we get $\hat{P}_R\bm{f} = D(R)^{\!\top}\bm{f}$, and $D(R)$ is not simply multiplied from the left. This is why Example 6.11 of Chapter 6 writes the $p$ orbitals as a row vector $(p_x,p_y)$ and multiplies $D(C_3)$ from the right. If we reversed it (writing $\sum_\nu D_{\mu\nu}f_\nu$), the order of the product of the coefficient matrices would be interchanged, giving $D(R)D(S) = D(SR)$, so that the order of the group product and that of the matrix product would be reversed (this is called an antihomomorphism). Eq. \eqref{eq:9-homo} itself follows from the definition \eqref{eq:9-PRdef} alone and so does not break, but $D$ would cease to be a representation. Textbooks differ in their conventions, so be sure to check this point when comparing with other books. This book follows the convention of Dresselhaus.

Supplement: The case of a one-dimensional irreducible representation is especially simple

When $\ell_n = 1$, the sum has only one term, and moreover $D^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)$ is a $1\times1$ matrix, that is, a plain number, equal to the character $\chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)$. Therefore

$$ \hat{P}_R\, f^{(\Gamma_n)} = \chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)\, f^{(\Gamma_n)} $$

That is, a basis function of a one-dimensional irreducible representation is a simultaneous eigenfunction of all the $\hat{P}_R$, and its eigenvalue is the character itself. The basis function of $A_1$ (all of whose characters are $1$) is nothing but the “totally symmetric function,” which is invariant under every symmetry operation.

Physical Meaning: Why consider basis functions?

As we saw in Chapter 6, if the Hamiltonian $\Ham$ commutes with all the operations of the group ($[\Ham,\hat{P}_R] = 0$), the following can be said. If $\Ham\psi = E\psi$, multiplying both sides by $\hat{P}_R$ and using the commutativity gives

$$ \hat{P}_R\Ham\psi = \Ham\left(\hat{P}_R\psi\right) = E\left(\hat{P}_R\psi\right) $$

that is, $\hat{P}_R\psi$ is also an eigenfunction with the same eigenvalue $E$. Hence the space spanned by the eigenfunctions belonging to the eigenvalue $E$ is mapped into itself by every $\hat{P}_R$. In other words, this space carries a representation of the group. And (apart from accidental degeneracies) it is an irreducible representation.

The conclusion is this: an energy eigenfunction is always a basis function of some irreducible representation, and the degeneracy equals the dimension $\ell_n$ of the irreducible representation. Therefore, knowing “which functions serve as a basis of which irreducible representation” is the same as knowing “what degeneracies appear where.” The crystal-field splitting of Chapter 10 and the band degeneracies of Chapter 11 are all applications of this principle.

9.2 Checking with $C_{3v}$ — $z$ Is $A_1$ and $(x,y)$ Is $E$

Enough of the abstract discussion; let us now work things out by hand for $C_{3v}$ (the point group of $\mathrm{NH_3}$). The multiplication table and the character table of this group were constructed in Chapters 6 and 8, so we only restate the results.

$A_2$$1$$1$$-1$$R_z$—

We fix the six elements of the group concretely. Take the $z$ axis as the threefold axis, and take the mirror plane $\sigma_1$ to be the $xz$ plane (that is, $y \to -y$). The remaining two planes $\sigma_2,\sigma_3$ are the planes standing in the directions $120^\circ$ and $240^\circ$ measured from the $x$ axis.

$$ C_{3v} = \left\{ E,\; C_3,\; C_3^2,\; \sigma_1,\; \sigma_2,\; \sigma_3 \right\},\qquad h = 6 $$

9.2.1 Acting on $z$ with the symmetry operations

Derivation: $z$ belongs to $A_1$

We apply the six operators $\hat{P}_R$ to $f(\rr) = z$ one by one. By Eq. \eqref{eq:9-PRdef}, what we need is the third component of $R^{-1}\rr$.

(i) Rotation $C_3$. $C_3^{-1}$ is the rotation by $-120^\circ$ about the $z$ axis, and it does nothing to the $z$ component:

$$ C_3^{-1}\rr = \left(-\tfrac12 x + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}y,\;\; -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}x - \tfrac12 y,\;\; z\right) $$

The third component is $z$. Hence $\hat{P}_{C_3} z = z$. Similarly $\hat{P}_{C_3^2} z = z$ and $\hat{P}_E z = z$.

(ii) Reflection $\sigma_1$ (the $xz$ plane). This operation is $(x,y,z) \to (x,-y,z)$, and $\sigma_1^{-1} = \sigma_1$ (applying a reflection twice returns the original, so it is its own inverse element). Hence

$$ \sigma_1^{-1}\rr = (x,\,-y,\,z)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{P}_{\sigma_1}z = z $$

(iii) $\sigma_2,\sigma_3$. These planes also contain the $z$ axis ($\sigma_v$ is a plane containing the principal axis, Chapter 4). Points on the $z$ axis do not move, so again $\hat{P}_{\sigma_2}z = \hat{P}_{\sigma_3}z = z$.

To summarize, for all six operations

$$ \hat{P}_R\, z = 1 \cdot z \qquad(\forall R \in C_{3v}) $$

holds. Comparing with Eq. \eqref{eq:9-basis}, this means $\ell_n = 1$ and $D(R) = 1$ (for all $R$); that is, it is the one-dimensional irreducible representation with characters $(1,1,1)$. Looking at Table 9.1, this is $A_1$. ∎

Therefore $z \in A_1$. This explains why $z$ is written in the $A_1$ row of the rightmost column of Table 9.1.

9.2.2 Acting on $x$ and $y$ — why they come as a set

Derivation: Compute $\hat{P}_{C_3}x$ and $\hat{P}_{C_3}y$ and read off $D^{(E)}(C_3)$

We only need to read off the first and second components of $C_3^{-1}\rr$ written above.

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{P}_{C_3}\,x = -\tfrac12\,x + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,y, \qquad \hat{P}_{C_3}\,y = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,x - \tfrac12\,y \label{eq:9-c3xy} \end{equation} $$

This is the decisive point. $\hat{P}_{C_3}x$ cannot be written in terms of $x$ alone; $y$ is always mixed in. Therefore $x$ by itself cannot form a set of basis functions. Only when $x$ and $y$ are taken together as a set of two does the relation close into the form of Eq. \eqref{eq:9-basis}. This is the meaning of writing “$(x,y)$” enclosed in parentheses.

Let us read off the matrix. Setting $f_1 = x$ and $f_2 = y$ and writing out Eq. \eqref{eq:9-basis}, we get

$$ \hat{P}_{C_3}f_1 = D_{11}f_1 + D_{21}f_2, \qquad \hat{P}_{C_3}f_2 = D_{12}f_1 + D_{22}f_2 $$

(the sum is over the first index; see the Caution in Section 9.1.2). Comparing coefficients with Eq. \eqref{eq:9-c3xy}, we obtain

$$ D_{11} = -\tfrac12,\quad D_{21} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\qquad D_{12} = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\quad D_{22} = -\tfrac12 $$

that is,

$$ D^{(E)}(C_3) = \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac12 & -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \\[4pt] \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} & -\tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Tr D^{(E)}(C_3) = -\tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = -1 $$

∎ This agrees with $\chi^{(E)}(C_3) = -1$ in the $E$ row and the $2C_3$ column of Table 9.1. ✓

Why? $D^{(E)}(C_3)$ turned out to be the rotation matrix of $C_3$ itself

Compare the $D^{(E)}(C_3)$ obtained above with the $xy$ block of the rotation matrix $C_3$ of Chapter 4. They are exactly the same. Neither a transpose nor an inverse matrix has entered. This is not a coincidence.

In general, if we take $f_\mu = x_\mu$ (the coordinates themselves) as the basis, the definition \eqref{eq:9-PRdef} gives

$$ \hat{P}_R\,x_\mu = \left(R^{-1}\rr\right)_\mu = \sum_\nu \left(R^{-1}\right)_{\mu\nu}x_\nu $$

Here the matrix of a symmetry operation is an orthogonal matrix, so $R^{-1} = R^{\!\top}$, that is, $\left(R^{-1}\right)_{\mu\nu} = R_{\nu\mu}$. Therefore

$$ \hat{P}_R\,x_\mu = \sum_\nu R_{\nu\mu}\,x_\nu $$

Comparing this with Eq. \eqref{eq:9-basis}, we immediately obtain

$$ \begin{equation} D(R) = R \qquad (\text{with } (x,y,z) \text{ as the basis}) \label{eq:9-DequalsR} \end{equation} $$

In other words, the definition $\hat{P}_R f(\rr) = f(R^{-1}\rr)$ and the index order of Eq. \eqref{eq:9-basis} are two conventions that, taken together, are chosen so as to realize “representation matrix = geometric rotation matrix”. If only one of them is changed to match the convention of another book, a transpose enters and the correspondence fails. The two come as a set.

Example 9.2: Writing all six matrices of $D^{(E)}$ and checking the characters

Taking $(x,y)$ as the basis, find the $2\times2$ representation matrices of the six elements of $C_{3v}$.

Solution. By Eq. \eqref{eq:9-DequalsR}, we only need to write down the $xy$ block of the geometric matrices. The two-dimensional reflection matrix about the line at angle $\theta$ is $\begin{pmatrix}\cos2\theta & \sin2\theta\\ \sin2\theta & -\cos2\theta\end{pmatrix}$.

$$ D(E) = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad D(C_3) = \begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12&-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\tfrac12\end{pmatrix},\quad D(C_3^2) = \begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12&\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\tfrac12\end{pmatrix} $$ $$ D(\sigma_1) = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad D(\sigma_2) = \begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12&-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}&\tfrac12\end{pmatrix},\quad D(\sigma_3) = \begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12&\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[3pt] \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}&\tfrac12\end{pmatrix} $$

($\sigma_1$ has $\theta = 0$; $\sigma_2$ has $\theta = 120^\circ$, with $\cos240^\circ=-\tfrac12,\ \sin240^\circ=-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$; $\sigma_3$ has $\theta=240^\circ$, with $\cos480^\circ=-\tfrac12,\ \sin480^\circ=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$.)

Taking the trace gives $\chi = 2,\ -1,\ -1,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0$. Grouping by class, $\chi(E)=2$, $\chi(2C_3)=-1$, $\chi(3\sigma_v)=0$. This agrees completely with the $E$ row of Table 9.1. ∎

We can also check, incidentally, that this representation is indeed irreducible. Using the criterion $\frac{1}{h}\sum_R \abs{\chi(R)}^2 = 1$ of Chapter 8, we obtain

$$ \frac{1}{6}\left[2^2 + (-1)^2 + (-1)^2 + 0 + 0 + 0\right] = \frac{6}{6} = 1 \quad ✓ $$

Supplement: $z$ and $(x,y)$ separated “because there is a principal axis”

The three coordinates $x,y,z$ are originally a single unit, as the components of a three-dimensional vector. In $C_{3v}$ they split into $z$ ($A_1$) and $(x,y)$ ($E$). The reason is simple: every operation of this group takes the $z$ axis as its axis and leaves it unmoved. Only the $z$ direction is singled out, while $x,y$ in the plane perpendicular to it are mixed with each other by the rotations.

If the group were $O_h$ (cubic), the three coordinates $x,y,z$ would all be carried into one another by $C_4$ or $C_3$, so they could not be separated and would together span the three-dimensional irreducible representation $T_{1u}$. Which irreducible representation they fall into is determined by what the group is. The question “what representation is $x$?” has no meaning unless the group is specified.

9.3 Projection Operators — Derivation from the Great Orthogonality Theorem

So far we have asked which irreducible representation a given function belongs to. What we want in actual calculations is the reverse. That is, from a set of functions constructed without any thought of symmetry, we want to extract mechanically the “part belonging to $\Gamma_n$.” The projection operator accomplishes this.

9.3.1 Definition and derivation

Definition: Projection operator

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\nu} \;=\; \frac{\ell_n}{h}\sum_{R} D^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\nu}\;\hat{P}_R \label{eq:9-projfull} \end{equation} $$

The sum runs over all elements $R$ of the group ($h$ of them). In particular, the quantity obtained by setting $\mu=\nu$ and summing over $\mu$,

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)} \;\equiv\; \sum_{\mu=1}^{\ell_n}\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\mu} \;=\; \frac{\ell_n}{h}\sum_{R} \chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)^{*}\;\hat{P}_R \label{eq:9-projchi} \end{equation} $$

is called the projection operator onto $\Gamma_n$. All we have used is $\sum_\mu D_{\mu\mu} = \chi$.

What makes Eq. \eqref{eq:9-projchi} so useful in practice is that it can be constructed from the characters alone. We do not need to know all the representation matrices $D(R)$. The character tables are at the back of the book, so anyone can use them right away.

Derivation: Why the projection operator really is a “projection”

What we want to show is the following relation. When $\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\nu}$ acts on a basis function $f^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\nu'}$ of an irreducible representation $\Gamma_{n'}$, we have

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\nu}\, f^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\nu'} \;=\; \delta_{nn'}\,\delta_{\nu\nu'}\, f^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu} \label{eq:9-projact} \end{equation} $$

We calculate step by step. First substitute the definition \eqref{eq:9-projfull} and use Eq. \eqref{eq:9-basis} for $\hat{P}_R f^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\nu'}$:

$$ \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\nu}\, f^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\nu'} = \frac{\ell_n}{h}\sum_{R} D^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\nu}\;\underbrace{\sum_{\mu'} D^{(\Gamma_{n'})}(R)_{\mu'\nu'}\, f^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\mu'}}_{\hat{P}_R f^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\nu'}} $$

Here the sum over $R$ and the sum over $\mu'$ are independent, so we interchange their order and take $f^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\mu'}$ outside the sum:

$$ = \frac{\ell_n}{h}\sum_{\mu'} f^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\mu'} \left[\sum_{R} D^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\nu}\, D^{(\Gamma_{n'})}(R)_{\mu'\nu'}\right] $$

The expression in the square brackets is precisely the left-hand side of the great orthogonality theorem (also called the wonderful orthogonality theorem) of Chapter 7:

$$ \sum_{R} D^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\nu}\, D^{(\Gamma_{n'})}(R)_{\mu'\nu'} = \frac{h}{\ell_n}\,\delta_{nn'}\,\delta_{\mu\mu'}\,\delta_{\nu\nu'} $$

Substituting this gives

$$ = \frac{\ell_n}{h}\cdot\frac{h}{\ell_n}\sum_{\mu'} f^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\mu'}\;\delta_{nn'}\,\delta_{\mu\mu'}\,\delta_{\nu\nu'} $$

The coefficients $\ell_n/h$ and $h/\ell_n$ cancel (this is why we put $\ell_n/h$ in the definition), and $\delta_{\mu\mu'}$ collapses the sum over $\mu'$ to the single term $\mu' = \mu$. The result is

$$ \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\nu}\, f^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\nu'} = \delta_{nn'}\,\delta_{\nu\nu'}\, f^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu} $$

∎ Eq. \eqref{eq:9-projact} has been shown.

What this equation says. If the function acted upon (i) belongs to a different irreducible representation ($n \ne n'$), the result is zero; (ii) if it belongs to the same irreducible representation but the partner index does not match ($\nu \ne \nu'$), the result is zero; (iii) only when both match, it returns the function with the index changed from $\nu$ to $\mu$. For this reason $\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\nu}$ is also called the “transfer operator.” Once we have obtained a single $\nu$th partner, this operator can generate all the remaining $\ell_n - 1$ of them.

Derivation: Action of the form $\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}$ that uses only the characters

Setting $\nu = \mu$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:9-projact} gives

$$ \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\mu}\, f^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\nu'} = \delta_{nn'}\,\delta_{\mu\nu'}\, f^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu} $$

Sum both sides over $\mu = 1,\dots,\ell_n$. On the right-hand side there is a factor $\delta_{\mu\nu'}$, so only the single term $\mu = \nu'$ survives:

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}\, f^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\nu'} = \delta_{nn'}\, f^{(\Gamma_{n})}_{\nu'} \label{eq:9-projchiact} \end{equation} $$

∎ That is, $\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}$ lets through only the component belonging to $\Gamma_n$ while preserving the partner index, and eliminates everything else. It is exactly a “filter.”

9.3.2 Idempotency and completeness

Theorem 9.1: Idempotency

$$ \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}\,\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_{n'})} = \delta_{nn'}\,\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}, \qquad\text{in particular}\qquad \left(\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}\right)^2 = \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)} $$

Proof: Idempotency

Multiply two copies of the definition \eqref{eq:9-projfull}. The operators inside the product satisfy $\hat{P}_R\hat{P}_S = \hat{P}_{RS}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:9-homo}):

$$ \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\nu}\,\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\mu'\nu'} = \frac{\ell_n \ell_{n'}}{h^2}\sum_{R}\sum_{S} D^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\nu}\,D^{(\Gamma_{n'})}(S)^{*}_{\mu'\nu'}\,\hat{P}_{RS} $$

Now we make a change of variables. Fix $R$ and let $T = RS$; when $S$ runs once through the group, $T$ also runs once through the group (the rearrangement theorem of Chapter 6). We have $S = R^{-1}T$. Therefore

$$ = \frac{\ell_n \ell_{n'}}{h^2}\sum_{R}\sum_{T} D^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\nu}\,D^{(\Gamma_{n'})}(R^{-1}T)^{*}_{\mu'\nu'}\,\hat{P}_{T} $$

Writing the property of representation matrices $D(R^{-1}T) = D(R^{-1})D(T)$ in terms of components gives

$$ D^{(\Gamma_{n'})}(R^{-1}T)^{*}_{\mu'\nu'} = \sum_{\lambda} D^{(\Gamma_{n'})}(R^{-1})^{*}_{\mu'\lambda}\, D^{(\Gamma_{n'})}(T)^{*}_{\lambda\nu'} $$

Furthermore, since the representation can be chosen unitary (Chapter 7), $D(R^{-1}) = D(R)^{-1} = D(R)^{\dagger}$, and in components $D(R^{-1})_{\mu'\lambda} = D(R)^{*}_{\lambda\mu'}$; taking its complex conjugate,

$$ D^{(\Gamma_{n'})}(R^{-1})^{*}_{\mu'\lambda} = D^{(\Gamma_{n'})}(R)_{\lambda\mu'} $$

With this, the sum over $R$ has taken the form of the great orthogonality theorem:

$$ \sum_{R} D^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\nu}\,D^{(\Gamma_{n'})}(R)_{\lambda\mu'} = \frac{h}{\ell_n}\,\delta_{nn'}\,\delta_{\mu\lambda}\,\delta_{\nu\mu'} $$

Substituting this and collapsing the sum over $\lambda$ with $\delta_{\mu\lambda}$, we obtain

$$ \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\nu}\,\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_{n'})}_{\mu'\nu'} = \frac{\ell_n \ell_{n'}}{h^2}\cdot\frac{h}{\ell_n}\,\delta_{nn'}\,\delta_{\nu\mu'}\sum_{T} D^{(\Gamma_{n})}(T)^{*}_{\mu\nu'}\,\hat{P}_{T} = \delta_{nn'}\,\delta_{\nu\mu'}\,\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\nu'} $$

(Since only the case $n=n'$ survives, we set $\ell_{n'} = \ell_n$, and $\frac{\ell_n\ell_n}{h^2}\cdot\frac{h}{\ell_n} = \frac{\ell_n}{h}$ returns to the coefficient of the definition.)

Now setting $\nu = \mu$, $\mu' = \nu'$, and $n = n'$, we obtain $\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\mu}\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\nu'\nu'} = \delta_{\mu\nu'}\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\mu}$. Summing both sides over $\mu$ and $\nu'$ gives

$$ \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)} = \sum_{\mu}\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\mu} = \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)} $$

∎

Theorem 9.2: Completeness

$$ \sum_{n}\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)} = \hat{1} $$

The sum runs over all irreducible representations of the group. $\hat{1}$ is the identity operator.

Proof: Completeness

Sum the definition \eqref{eq:9-projchi} as it stands, and interchange the sum over $R$ and the sum over $n$:

$$ \sum_{n}\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)} = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{R}\left[\sum_{n}\ell_n\,\chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)^{*}\right]\hat{P}_R $$

Let us evaluate the expression in the square brackets. The dimension of an irreducible representation equals the character of the identity element, that is, $\ell_n = \chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(E)$ ($D(E)$ is the $\ell_n\times\ell_n$ identity matrix, so its trace is $\ell_n$). Moreover, this is a real number, so we may rewrite $\chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(E)^{*} = \chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(E) = \ell_n$. The square bracket then becomes

$$ \sum_{n}\chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(E)\,\chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)^{*} $$

This is none other than the second orthogonality relation of Chapter 8 (orthogonality of the columns)

$$ \sum_{n}\chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(\mathcal{C}_k)\,\chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(\mathcal{C}_{k'})^{*} = \frac{h}{N_k}\,\delta_{kk'} $$

Here $\mathcal{C}_k$ is a class and $N_k$ is the number of elements contained in that class. In the present case $\mathcal{C}_k = \{E\}$ and $N_k = 1$ (the identity element always forms a class by itself). Therefore

$$ \sum_{n}\ell_n\,\chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)^{*} = \frac{h}{1}\,\delta_{R,E} = h\,\delta_{R,E} $$

That is, it equals $h$ only when $R = E$ and is zero otherwise. Only a single term of the sum over $R$ remains:

$$ \sum_{n}\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)} = \frac{1}{h}\cdot h\,\hat{P}_E = \hat{P}_E = \hat{1} $$

∎ (Since $\hat{P}_E f(\rr) = f(E^{-1}\rr) = f(\rr)$, $\hat{P}_E$ is the identity operator.)

Physical Meaning: The function space decomposes into a direct sum over irreducible representations

Combining Theorem 9.1 and Theorem 9.2, we see that any function $F$ can be uniquely decomposed as

$$ F = \hat{1}F = \sum_{n}\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}F \equiv \sum_{n}F^{(\Gamma_n)} $$

Each term $F^{(\Gamma_n)} = \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}F$ belongs to $\Gamma_n$ (or is zero). In other words, the space of functions splits into a direct sum of subspaces, one for each irreducible representation.

This has exactly the same structure as decomposing a three-dimensional vector into its $x,y,z$ components. The only difference is that the “axes” of the decomposition are not the coordinate axes but the irreducible representations. If the inner product extracts the components of a vector, then the projection operator extracts the irreducible-representation components.

Caution: Projection operators do not normalize

$\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}$ is idempotent ($\hat{P}^2 = \hat{P}$), but it is not unitary and does not preserve the norm. The magnitude of $\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}F$ is in general neither $1$ nor $\abs{F}$. Indeed, in the calculation of the next section an awkward coefficient appears in $\hat{P}^{(A_1)}\phi_1 = \frac13(\phi_1+\phi_2+\phi_3)$. Always do the normalization by hand at the end.

One more point. It is perfectly common for $\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}F = 0$ to occur. This is useful information (“$F$ contained no $\Gamma_n$ component”), not a calculation error.

9.4 Example: Constructing SALCs from the Three Atoms of an Equilateral Triangle

Now we put the machinery into practice. Consider the three hydrogen atoms of $\mathrm{NH_3}$, and from their $1s$ orbitals $\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3$ construct linear combinations that are adapted to the symmetry. The functions obtained in this way are called symmetry-adapted linear combinations (SALCs).

9.4.1 Setting — atomic positions and the action of the operations

Take the $z$ axis as the threefold axis, and project the three hydrogen atoms onto the $xy$ plane, placing them at

$$ \rr_1 = a(1,\,0),\qquad \rr_2 = a\left(-\tfrac12,\;\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right),\qquad \rr_3 = a\left(-\tfrac12,\;-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) $$

. That is, atom 1 is placed on the $+x$ axis, and atoms 2 and 3 follow counterclockwise in steps of $120^\circ$. Then $C_3$ (a rotation by $+120^\circ$) cycles the positions as $\rr_1 \to \rr_2 \to \rr_3 \to \rr_1$. The mirror plane $\sigma_1$ (the $xz$ plane) contains atom 1 and exchanges 2 and 3. The plane $\sigma_2$ contains atom 2 and exchanges 1 and 3, and $\sigma_3$ contains atom 3 and exchanges 1 and 2.

Derivation: Carefully checking that $\hat{P}_{C_3}\phi_1 = \phi_2$

“The atoms move, so the functions move” is intuitively correct, but let us follow it through in equations. Since the $1s$ orbital is spherically symmetric, the orbital sitting on atom $i$ can be written as

$$ \phi_i(\rr) = \phi\!\left(\abs{\rr - \rr_i}\right) $$

($\phi$ is a function of the radial coordinate only). By the definition \eqref{eq:9-PRdef},

$$ \left(\hat{P}_{C_3}\phi_1\right)(\rr) = \phi_1\!\left(C_3^{-1}\rr\right) = \phi\!\left(\abs{C_3^{-1}\rr - \rr_1}\right) $$

Here we factor $C_3^{-1}$ out of the parentheses. We have $C_3^{-1}\rr - \rr_1 = C_3^{-1}\left(\rr - C_3\rr_1\right)$ (indeed, expanding the right-hand side gives $C_3^{-1}\rr - C_3^{-1}C_3\rr_1 = C_3^{-1}\rr - \rr_1$). Since $C_3^{-1}$ is an orthogonal matrix, it does not change the length of a vector:

$$ \abs{C_3^{-1}\left(\rr - C_3\rr_1\right)} = \abs{\rr - C_3\rr_1} $$

And by the setting, $C_3\rr_1 = \rr_2$. Therefore

$$ \left(\hat{P}_{C_3}\phi_1\right)(\rr) = \phi\!\left(\abs{\rr - \rr_2}\right) = \phi_2(\rr) $$

∎ In general, for a spherically symmetric orbital, $\hat{P}_R\phi_i = \phi_j$ (where $R\rr_i = \rr_j$). The operation moves the functions only as a “relabeling of the atom numbers.” For orbitals that have a direction, such as $p$ and $d$ orbitals, the rotation of the orbital itself enters in addition, so things become more complicated (Exercise 9.5).

Table 9.2: Action of each operation of $C_{3v}$ on $\phi_i$. Counting the diagonal elements (the number of $\phi_i \to \phi_i$) gives the character of the reducible representation
$R$$\hat{P}_R\phi_1$$\hat{P}_R\phi_2$$\hat{P}_R\phi_3$$\chi_{1s}(R)$
$E$$\phi_1$$\phi_2$$\phi_3$$3$
$C_3$$\phi_2$$\phi_3$$\phi_1$$0$
$C_3^2$$\phi_3$$\phi_1$$\phi_2$$0$

Why? The character equals “the number of atoms that did not move”

Let us confirm how the rightmost column of Table 9.2 is built. The $3\times3$ representation matrix with $\{\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3\}$ as the basis is a permutation matrix (exactly one $1$ in each row and each column). Its diagonal element $D_{ii}$ equals $1$ only when $\hat{P}_R\phi_i = \phi_i$, that is, when atom $i$ did not move. Therefore

$$ \chi(R) = \left(R \text{ leaves this many atoms in place}\right) $$

$C_3$ moves all three atoms, so $\chi = 0$; $\sigma_1$ leaves only atom 1 in place, so $\chi = 1$; and so on. This rule of “counting the atoms that did not move” also plays a central role in Chapter 10, when we reduce molecular vibrations into irreducible representations.

9.4.2 First, reduce the representation

Derivation: $\Gamma_{1s} = A_1 \oplus E$

We use the reduction formula of Chapter 8,

$$ a_n = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{k} N_k\,\chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(\mathcal{C}_k)^{*}\,\chi(\mathcal{C}_k) $$

. Here $h=6$, and the classes are $\{E\}$ ($N=1$), $\{C_3, C_3^2\}$ ($N=2$), and $\{\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3\}$ ($N=3$). From Table 9.2, the character of the reducible representation is $\chi = (3,\,0,\,1)$.

$$ a_{A_1} = \frac{1}{6}\left[1\cdot 1\cdot 3 + 2\cdot 1\cdot 0 + 3\cdot 1\cdot 1\right] = \frac{1}{6}\left[3 + 0 + 3\right] = 1 $$ $$ a_{A_2} = \frac{1}{6}\left[1\cdot 1\cdot 3 + 2\cdot 1\cdot 0 + 3\cdot (-1)\cdot 1\right] = \frac{1}{6}\left[3 + 0 - 3\right] = 0 $$ $$ a_{E} = \frac{1}{6}\left[1\cdot 2\cdot 3 + 2\cdot(-1)\cdot 0 + 3\cdot 0\cdot 1\right] = \frac{1}{6}\left[6 + 0 + 0\right] = 1 $$

Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \Gamma_{1s} = A_1 \oplus E \label{eq:9-gamma1s} \end{equation} $$

∎ The dimension count $1 + 2 = 3$ agrees with the number of original functions. ✓

At this point, the form of the answer has already been fixed. From the three $1s$ orbitals we obtain one totally symmetric combination and one pair of twofold degenerate combinations. All that remains is to extract their contents with projection operators.

9.4.3 Constructing the SALC of $A_1$

Derivation: Writing out every term of $\hat{P}^{(A_1)}\phi_1$

The characters of $A_1$ are all $1$, the dimension is $\ell = 1$, and $h=6$, so Eq. \eqref{eq:9-projchi} becomes

$$ \hat{P}^{(A_1)} = \frac{1}{6}\left[\hat{P}_E + \hat{P}_{C_3} + \hat{P}_{C_3^2} + \hat{P}_{\sigma_1} + \hat{P}_{\sigma_2} + \hat{P}_{\sigma_3}\right] $$

We apply this to $\phi_1$. Write out the whole $\hat{P}_R\phi_1$ column of Table 9.2, from top to bottom:

$$ \hat{P}^{(A_1)}\phi_1 = \frac{1}{6}\Big[\underbrace{\phi_1}_{E} + \underbrace{\phi_2}_{C_3} + \underbrace{\phi_3}_{C_3^2} + \underbrace{\phi_1}_{\sigma_1} + \underbrace{\phi_3}_{\sigma_2} + \underbrace{\phi_2}_{\sigma_3}\Big] $$

Collect like terms. There are two $\phi_1$, two $\phi_2$, and two $\phi_3$, so

$$ \hat{P}^{(A_1)}\phi_1 = \frac{1}{6}\cdot 2\left(\phi_1 + \phi_2 + \phi_3\right) = \frac{1}{3}\left(\phi_1 + \phi_2 + \phi_3\right) $$

The coefficient $\frac13$ has no meaning (see the caution in Section 9.3.2), so we extract only the form and normalize it. Assuming that the $\phi_i$ are orthonormal ($\langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$), the squared norm of $N(\phi_1+\phi_2+\phi_3)$ is

$$ N^2\left(\langle\phi_1|\phi_1\rangle + \langle\phi_2|\phi_2\rangle + \langle\phi_3|\phi_3\rangle\right) = 3N^2 \stackrel{!}{=} 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N = \frac{1}{\sqrt3} $$
$$ \begin{equation} \psi_{A_1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\left(\phi_1 + \phi_2 + \phi_3\right) \label{eq:9-salcA1} \end{equation} $$

∎ This is a nodeless, “totally symmetric” function in which all three orbitals are added with the same sign.

Example 9.3: Checking that $\hat{P}^{(A_2)}\phi_1 = 0$

The characters of $A_2$ are $\chi(E)=1$, $\chi(C_3)=1$, $\chi(\sigma_v)=-1$. Therefore

$$ \hat{P}^{(A_2)}\phi_1 = \frac{1}{6}\Big[\phi_1 + \phi_2 + \phi_3 - \phi_1 - \phi_3 - \phi_2\Big] = 0 $$

(The first three terms are $E, C_3, C_3^2$, and the last three are $\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3$, which are multiplied by the sign $-1$.)

The result is zero. This is consistent with $a_{A_2}=0$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:9-gamma1s}. ∎ The projection operator thus also serves as a means of independently rechecking the result of the reduction.

9.4.4 Constructing the SALCs of $E$ — the crux

Derivation: Obtaining $\psi_{E,1}$ from $\hat{P}^{(E)}\phi_1$

$E$ has $\ell = 2$ and characters $\chi(E) = 2$, $\chi(C_3)=\chi(C_3^2)=-1$, $\chi(\sigma_j)=0$. By Eq. \eqref{eq:9-projchi},

$$ \hat{P}^{(E)} = \frac{2}{6}\left[2\,\hat{P}_E - \hat{P}_{C_3} - \hat{P}_{C_3^2} + 0\cdot\hat{P}_{\sigma_1} + 0\cdot\hat{P}_{\sigma_2} + 0\cdot\hat{P}_{\sigma_3}\right] = \frac{1}{3}\left[2\hat{P}_E - \hat{P}_{C_3} - \hat{P}_{C_3^2}\right] $$

It is welcome that all the reflection terms drop out. Applying this to $\phi_1$, by Table 9.2 we obtain

$$ \hat{P}^{(E)}\phi_1 = \frac{1}{3}\left[2\phi_1 - \phi_2 - \phi_3\right] $$

Now we normalize. The coefficient vector is $(2,-1,-1)$, so the squared norm is $N^2(2^2 + (-1)^2 + (-1)^2) = 6N^2 = 1$, that is, $N = 1/\sqrt6$:

$$ \begin{equation} \psi_{E,1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}\left(2\phi_1 - \phi_2 - \phi_3\right) \label{eq:9-salcE1} \end{equation} $$

∎

Derivation: How to construct the second partner $\psi_{E,2}$

Since $E$ is two-dimensional, we need one more partner. Let us simply apply the same operator to $\phi_2$ as well:

$$ \hat{P}^{(E)}\phi_2 = \frac{1}{3}\left[2\phi_2 - \phi_3 - \phi_1\right], \qquad \hat{P}^{(E)}\phi_3 = \frac{1}{3}\left[2\phi_3 - \phi_1 - \phi_2\right] $$

Three functions have come out, but they are not independent. Indeed, adding the three gives

$$ (2\phi_1-\phi_2-\phi_3) + (2\phi_2-\phi_3-\phi_1) + (2\phi_3-\phi_1-\phi_2) = 0 $$

. Only two are independent, which is consistent with $a_E = 1$ ($E$ appears only once). ✓

However, $\hat{P}^{(E)}\phi_1$ and $\hat{P}^{(E)}\phi_2$ are not orthogonal. The inner product of the coefficient vectors $(2,-1,-1)$ and $(-1,2,-1)$ is

$$ 2\cdot(-1) + (-1)\cdot 2 + (-1)\cdot(-1) = -2 - 2 + 1 = -3 \ne 0 $$

So we construct an orthogonal pair. Method A (a clever subtraction) is the fastest. Subtracting $\hat{P}^{(E)}\phi_3$ from $\hat{P}^{(E)}\phi_2$ gives

$$ \hat{P}^{(E)}\phi_2 - \hat{P}^{(E)}\phi_3 = \frac13\left[(2\phi_2 - \phi_3 - \phi_1) - (2\phi_3 - \phi_1 - \phi_2)\right] = \frac13\left[3\phi_2 - 3\phi_3\right] = \phi_2 - \phi_3 $$

The coefficient vector is $(0,1,-1)$, and its inner product with $(2,-1,-1)$ is $0 + (-1) + 1 = 0$. They are orthogonal. ✓ Normalizing, $N^2(0+1+1)=1$ gives $N = 1/\sqrt2$:

$$ \begin{equation} \psi_{E,2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\phi_2 - \phi_3\right) \label{eq:9-salcE2} \end{equation} $$

Method B (Gram–Schmidt) gives the same answer. Let $g_1 = 2\phi_1-\phi_2-\phi_3$ ($\langle g_1|g_1\rangle = 6$) and $g_2 = 2\phi_2-\phi_3-\phi_1$ ($\langle g_1|g_2\rangle = -3$). Then

$$ g_2^{\perp} = g_2 - \frac{\langle g_1|g_2\rangle}{\langle g_1|g_1\rangle}g_1 = g_2 - \frac{-3}{6}g_1 = g_2 + \frac12 g_1 $$ $$ = (2\phi_2-\phi_3-\phi_1) + \frac12(2\phi_1-\phi_2-\phi_3) = \left(-1+1\right)\phi_1 + \left(2-\tfrac12\right)\phi_2 + \left(-1-\tfrac12\right)\phi_3 = \tfrac32\left(\phi_2 - \phi_3\right) $$

∎ Apart from a constant multiple, this agrees with Eq. \eqref{eq:9-salcE2}.

Supplement: The orthodox method using the transfer operator $\hat{P}^{(E)}_{21}$

According to Eq. \eqref{eq:9-projact}, $\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\nu}$ “transfers the $\nu$th partner to the $\mu$th partner.” So once $\psi_{E,1}$ is in hand, applying $\hat{P}^{(E)}_{21}$ to it yields $\psi_{E,2}$ automatically. This method, however, requires not only the characters but all the components of the representation matrices $D^{(E)}(R)$ (the six matrices constructed in Example 9.2).

In practice, the dimension $\ell_n$ is almost always small (2 or 3), so it is usual to get by with Method A or B above. However, when we want to match the partners in the correct order (for example, when we want to associate $\psi_{E,1}$ with $x$ and $\psi_{E,2}$ with $y$ properly), the transfer operator is the reliable choice.

Example 9.4: Checking that $(\psi_{E,1},\psi_{E,2})$ really transforms in the same way as $(x,y)$

According to Eq. \eqref{eq:9-c3xy}, $\hat{P}_{C_3}x = -\tfrac12 x + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}y$. Compute $\hat{P}_{C_3}\psi_{E,1}$ and check whether the same relation holds for the SALCs.

Solution. Using Table 9.2, the left-hand side is

$$ \hat{P}_{C_3}\psi_{E,1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}\left(2\hat{P}_{C_3}\phi_1 - \hat{P}_{C_3}\phi_2 - \hat{P}_{C_3}\phi_3\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}\left(2\phi_2 - \phi_3 - \phi_1\right) $$

The prediction for the right-hand side is $-\tfrac12\psi_{E,1} + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\psi_{E,2}$. Writing this out in terms of the $\phi$:

$$ -\frac12\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt6}\left(2\phi_1-\phi_2-\phi_3\right) + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\phi_2-\phi_3\right) $$

We put the coefficient of the second term over $\sqrt6$. Since $\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2\sqrt2} = \dfrac{\sqrt3\cdot\sqrt3}{2\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt3} = \dfrac{3}{2\sqrt6}$,

$$ = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}\left[-\phi_1 + \tfrac12\phi_2 + \tfrac12\phi_3 + \tfrac32\phi_2 - \tfrac32\phi_3\right] = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}\left[-\phi_1 + 2\phi_2 - \phi_3\right] $$

This agrees exactly with the left-hand side. ∎

Why does this happen? Compare the coefficients with the coordinates of the atoms. The $x$ coordinates of the atoms are $(x_1,x_2,x_3) = a(1,-\tfrac12,-\tfrac12) \propto (2,-1,-1)$, and the $y$ coordinates are $(y_1,y_2,y_3) = a(0,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}) \propto (0,1,-1)$. That is,

$$ \psi_{E,1} \propto \sum_i x_i\,\phi_i,\qquad \psi_{E,2} \propto \sum_i y_i\,\phi_i $$

Since the coefficients are the coordinates themselves, it is only natural that the functions transform in the same way as $(x,y)$. This also explains why $\psi_{E,1}$ in Figure 9.2 looks “left–right asymmetric” and $\psi_{E,2}$ looks “up–down asymmetric.”

Supplement: Normalization when the overlap integral $S$ is not neglected

Actual $1s$ orbitals are not orthogonal to one another. If $\langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle = S$ ($i \ne j$), the normalization constants change. Since the squared norm of $\psi = N\sum_i c_i\phi_i$ is $N^2\sum_{i,j}c_ic_j\langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle$,

  • $A_1$: $c=(1,1,1)$. From $\sum_{i,j}c_ic_jS_{ij} = 3 + 6S$ we get $N = 1/\sqrt{3+6S}$
  • $E,1$: $c=(2,-1,-1)$. The diagonal terms give $4+1+1=6$ and the off-diagonal terms give $2[(2)(-1)+(2)(-1)+(-1)(-1)]S = -6S$, so $N = 1/\sqrt{6-6S}$
  • $E,2$: $c=(0,1,-1)$. The diagonal terms give $2$ and the off-diagonal terms give $2(1)(-1)S = -2S$, so $N = 1/\sqrt{2-2S}$

What matters is that, even for $S \ne 0$, the orthogonality itself is not destroyed. Indeed, $\langle\psi_{E,1}|\psi_{E,2}\rangle \propto (2)(1)S + (2)(-1)S + (-1)(-1)S + (-1)(1)S + \left[-1+1\right] = 0$. Functions belonging to different irreducible representations, and different partners of the same irreducible representation, are always orthogonal for reasons of symmetry. However, the orthogonality of partners can be asserted only when the two sets being compared obey the same unitary irreducible representation matrix $D^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)$ and are labeled with the same row convention (if the partners of only one set are re-chosen by a different unitary transformation, the correspondence of rows shifts and they may cease to be orthogonal). As long as this condition is met, orthogonality is guaranteed regardless of the details of the basis overlap—this is the strength of group theory.

9.5 Block Diagonalization of the Secular Equation — The Hückel Triangle

What do we gain by constructing SALCs? To see this, we actually solve a $3\times3$ eigenvalue problem.

9.5.1 Solving it naively

In the Hückel approximation, we set up the Hamiltonian matrix as follows.

$$ \alpha \equiv \langle\phi_i|\Ham|\phi_i\rangle \quad(\text{Coulomb integral}), \qquad \beta \equiv \langle\phi_i|\Ham|\phi_j\rangle \quad(i\ne j,\ \text{resonance integral}) $$

We then neglect the overlap integrals and set $\langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$. Here, the three $\alpha$'s are all equal, and the three $\beta$'s are all equal, as a consequence of symmetry: atoms 1 and 2 are carried into each other by $C_3$, so there is no way to distinguish them. The matrix is

$$ H = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \beta & \beta \\ \beta & \alpha & \beta \\ \beta & \beta & \alpha \end{pmatrix} $$

Derivation: Expanding the $3\times3$ determinant directly

The secular equation is $\det(H - \varepsilon I) = 0$. Setting $u \equiv \alpha - \varepsilon$, we have

$$ \det\begin{pmatrix} u & \beta & \beta \\ \beta & u & \beta \\ \beta & \beta & u \end{pmatrix} = 0 $$

Expanding in cofactors along the first row:

$$ u\left(u^2 - \beta^2\right) - \beta\left(\beta u - \beta^2\right) + \beta\left(\beta^2 - u\beta\right) = u^3 - u\beta^2 - \beta^2 u + \beta^3 + \beta^3 - u\beta^2 $$ $$ = u^3 - 3u\beta^2 + 2\beta^3 $$

We factor this cubic. Substituting $u = \beta$ gives $\beta^3 - 3\beta^3 + 2\beta^3 = 0$, so $u-\beta$ is a factor. Dividing, we obtain

$$ u^3 - 3u\beta^2 + 2\beta^3 = (u - \beta)\left(u^2 + \beta u - 2\beta^2\right) = (u-\beta)(u-\beta)(u+2\beta) = (u-\beta)^2(u+2\beta) $$

Therefore $u = \beta$ (a double root) and $u = -2\beta$. Since $\varepsilon = \alpha - u$,

$$ \begin{equation} \varepsilon = \alpha + 2\beta \ \ (1\text{-fold}), \qquad \varepsilon = \alpha - \beta \ \ (2\text{-fold degenerate}) \label{eq:9-huckel} \end{equation} $$

∎ So far we have not used group theory at all. However, we had to expand a $3\times3$ determinant and factor a cubic equation. As the number of atoms grows to 6, then 10, this method quickly breaks down.

9.5.2 Solving it with SALCs

Derivation: Computing all the matrix elements in the SALC basis

We replace the basis $\{\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3\}$ by $\{\psi_{A_1},\psi_{E,1},\psi_{E,2}\}$. It is convenient first to let $H$ act on the coefficient vectors.

(i) The $A_1$ diagonal element. When $H$ acts on $(1,1,1)^{\!\top}$, every row gives $\alpha + \beta + \beta = \alpha+2\beta$, so

$$ H\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix} = (\alpha+2\beta)\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix} $$

Hence

$$ \langle\psi_{A_1}|\Ham|\psi_{A_1}\rangle = \frac{1}{3}(1,1,1)\cdot(\alpha+2\beta)(1,1,1)^{\!\top} = \frac{1}{3}\cdot 3(\alpha+2\beta) = \alpha + 2\beta $$

(ii) The $E$ diagonal elements. We let $H$ act on $(2,-1,-1)^{\!\top}$. The first component is $2\alpha - \beta - \beta = 2(\alpha-\beta)$, the second is $2\beta - \alpha - \beta = -(\alpha - \beta)$, and the third is likewise $-(\alpha-\beta)$. That is,

$$ H\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\-1\end{pmatrix} = (\alpha-\beta)\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\-1\end{pmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle\psi_{E,1}|\Ham|\psi_{E,1}\rangle = \frac{1}{6}(\alpha-\beta)(4+1+1) = \alpha - \beta $$

Similarly, for $(0,1,-1)^{\!\top}$ the first component is $0 + \beta - \beta = 0$, the second is $0 + \alpha - \beta$, and the third is $0 + \beta - \alpha$, so

$$ H\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix} = (\alpha-\beta)\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle\psi_{E,2}|\Ham|\psi_{E,2}\rangle = \frac{1}{2}(\alpha-\beta)(0+1+1) = \alpha - \beta $$

(iii) The off-diagonal elements. Using the eigenvector-like relations obtained above, we only have to take inner products:

$$ \langle\psi_{A_1}|\Ham|\psi_{E,1}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt3\sqrt6}(1,1,1)\cdot(\alpha-\beta)(2,-1,-1)^{\!\top} = \frac{\alpha-\beta}{\sqrt{18}}\left(2-1-1\right) = 0 $$ $$ \langle\psi_{A_1}|\Ham|\psi_{E,2}\rangle = \frac{\alpha-\beta}{\sqrt{6}}\left(0+1-1\right) = 0 $$ $$ \langle\psi_{E,1}|\Ham|\psi_{E,2}\rangle = \frac{\alpha-\beta}{\sqrt{12}}\left(2\cdot 0 + (-1)\cdot 1 + (-1)\cdot(-1)\right) = \frac{\alpha-\beta}{\sqrt{12}}\left(0 - 1 + 1\right) = 0 $$

Therefore, in the new basis the Hamiltonian matrix is

$$ \begin{equation} H' = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha+2\beta & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \alpha-\beta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \alpha-\beta \end{pmatrix} = \left(\begin{array}{c|cc} \alpha+2\beta & 0 & 0 \\ \hline 0 & \alpha-\beta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \alpha-\beta \end{array}\right) \label{eq:9-blockdiag} \end{equation} $$

∎ The matrix has been diagonalized completely. We solved neither a determinant nor a cubic equation. The result agrees with Eq. \eqref{eq:9-huckel}. ✓

Why? The three zeros are no accident — Schur's lemma is at work

In the calculation above we actually computed the off-diagonal elements and confirmed that they vanish, but with group theory we can know they are zero before computing anything.

(1) Elements between different irreducible representations vanish. Since $\Ham$ commutes with all the operations of the group, $\Ham\psi_{E,\mu}$ is again a function belonging to $E$. On the other hand, $\psi_{A_1}$ belongs to $A_1$. Functions belonging to different irreducible representations are orthogonal (the orthogonality of Chapters 7 and 8). Hence $\langle\psi_{A_1}|\Ham|\psi_{E,\mu}\rangle = 0$. There is no need to compute it.

(2) The $E$ block is a constant multiple of the identity matrix. The restriction of $\Ham$ to the two-dimensional subspace spanned by $E$ commutes with all the $D^{(E)}(R)$ on that space. A matrix that commutes with an irreducible representation must be a constant multiple of the identity matrix — this is Schur's first lemma (Chapter 7). Hence

$$ \Ham\Big|_{E} = \varepsilon_E \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} $$

The off-diagonal elements are zero, and it is even guaranteed that the two diagonal elements are equal. We do not even need to solve a $2\times2$ problem. That $\alpha-\beta$ became a double root in Eq. \eqref{eq:9-huckel} was a consequence of this theorem.

In general, an $N\times N$ secular equation splits into blocks, one for each irreducible representation, and the size of each block equals the number of times that irreducible representation appears, $a_n$. If $a_n = 1$, the block is $1\times1$, that is, no diagonalization is needed. In the present example $a_{A_1}=a_E=1$, so every block became $1\times1$.

Physical Meaning: Generalization to an $N$-membered ring — the seed of Bloch's theorem

Let us apply the same argument to a system of $N$ atoms arranged in a ring. The symmetry contains the cyclic group $C_N$, whose irreducible representations are all one-dimensional and can be written $\chi^{(k)}(C_N^{\,j}) = e^{2\pi i kj/N}$ ($k=0,1,\dots,N-1$). The projection operator is

$$ \hat{P}^{(k)} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} e^{-2\pi i kj/N}\,\hat{P}_{C_N^{\,j}} $$

Acting with this on $\phi_0$ and using $\hat{P}_{C_N^{\,j}}\phi_0 = \phi_j$, we obtain

$$ \psi_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} e^{-2\pi i kj/N}\,\phi_j $$

(The sign of the phase factor is a matter of convention. If we relabel $k \to N-k$, we still exhaust the same set of irreducible representations, so no generality is lost even if one uses the convention $e^{+2\pi ikj/N}$.) If only nearest neighbors are connected by $\beta$, then

$$ \varepsilon_k = \alpha + \beta\left(e^{2\pi i k/N} + e^{-2\pi i k/N}\right) = \alpha + 2\beta\cos\frac{2\pi k}{N} $$

For $N=3$, this gives $\alpha+2\beta$ for $k=0$ and $\alpha+2\beta\cos120^\circ = \alpha - \beta$ (twofold) for $k=1,2$, which reproduces Eq. \eqref{eq:9-huckel}. ✓

Look closely at the form of this $\psi_k$ — a sum of atomic orbitals multiplied by the phase factor $e^{ikj}$. It is precisely a Bloch function. Here $k$ is the label of an irreducible representation of the translation group (here, the cyclic group), and $\varepsilon_k = \alpha + 2\beta\cos(ka)$ is none other than the band dispersion in the tight-binding approximation. We derive Bloch's theorem group-theoretically in Chapter 11 and treat the tight-binding approximation in Chapter 18, but their essence is already contained entirely in this three-atom example.

9.6 Spherical Harmonics and $d$ Orbitals — Characters of the Rotation Group

So far we have dealt with point groups, which have finitely many elements. However, atomic orbitals are, by origin, bases of irreducible representations of the full rotation group (a continuous group). They are the $2\ell+1$ spherical harmonics $Y_{\ell m}$ belonging to the angular momentum quantum number $\ell$. In an isolated atom these orbitals are $2\ell+1$-fold degenerate; when the atom is placed in a crystal and the symmetry is lowered to a point group, they split. This is the crystal-field splitting.

9.6.1 Derivation of the characters $\chi^{(\ell)}(\alpha)$ of the rotation group

Derivation: $\hat{P}_{R}Y_{\ell m}$ and the closed formula for the characters

In polar coordinates the spherical harmonics have the form

$$ Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi) = \Theta_{\ell m}(\theta)\, e^{im\varphi},\qquad m = -\ell,\dots,\ell $$

($\Theta_{\ell m}$ is a function of $\theta$ alone, containing the associated Legendre function).

Step 1: Since the character is determined by the class alone, we may take the axis to be $z$. Rotations with the same rotation angle $\alpha$ are conjugate to one another (within the rotation group) and belong to the same class. Since the character is a function of the class, it suffices to compute it for the rotation $R_z(\alpha)$ about the $z$ axis, which is easy to handle.

Step 2: Apply $\hat{P}_{R_z(\alpha)}$. Since $R_z(\alpha)^{-1}$ is a rotation by $-\alpha$, in polar coordinates it is the transformation $\theta \to \theta$, $\varphi \to \varphi - \alpha$. From the definition \eqref{eq:9-PRdef},

$$ \hat{P}_{R_z(\alpha)}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi) = Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi-\alpha) = \Theta_{\ell m}(\theta)\,e^{im(\varphi-\alpha)} = e^{-im\alpha}\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi) $$

That is, the $Y_{\ell m}$ do not mix with one another; they merely acquire a phase factor. The representation matrix is the diagonal matrix

$$ D^{(\ell)}\!\left(R_z(\alpha)\right) = \mathrm{diag}\!\left(e^{i\ell\alpha},\,e^{i(\ell-1)\alpha},\,\dots,\,e^{-i\ell\alpha}\right) $$

Step 3: Take the trace, summing it as a geometric series.

$$ \chi^{(\ell)}(\alpha) = \sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell} e^{-im\alpha} = \sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell} e^{im\alpha} $$

(Replacing $m$ by $-m$ leaves the range of the sum unchanged, so these two are equal. Below we use the latter.) Letting $q \equiv e^{i\alpha}$, this is the sum of a geometric sequence with first term $q^{-\ell}$, common ratio $q$, and $2\ell+1$ terms:

$$ \chi^{(\ell)} = \sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}q^{m} = q^{-\ell}\,\frac{q^{2\ell+1}-1}{q-1} = \frac{q^{\ell+1}-q^{-\ell}}{q-1} $$

Here the standard trick is to multiply the denominator and numerator by $q^{-1/2}$:

$$ = \frac{q^{\ell+\frac12}-q^{-\ell-\frac12}}{q^{\frac12}-q^{-\frac12}} = \frac{e^{i(\ell+\frac12)\alpha}-e^{-i(\ell+\frac12)\alpha}}{e^{i\alpha/2}-e^{-i\alpha/2}} $$

Both the numerator and the denominator take the form $2i\sin(\cdot)$, so the factor $2i$ cancels and we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \chi^{(\ell)}(\alpha) = \frac{\sin\!\left[\left(\ell+\tfrac12\right)\alpha\right]}{\sin\!\left(\alpha/2\right)} \label{eq:9-chiell} \end{equation} $$

∎

Check 1 ($\alpha\to0$). The numerator and denominator both approach 0, so l'Hôpital's rule gives $\dfrac{(\ell+\frac12)\cos[(\ell+\frac12)\alpha]}{\frac12\cos(\alpha/2)}\to \dfrac{\ell+\frac12}{1/2} = 2\ell+1$. The character of the identity operation equals the dimension of the representation, $2\ell+1$. ✓

Check 2 ($\ell=0$). $\chi^{(0)} = \sin(\alpha/2)/\sin(\alpha/2) = 1$. An $s$ orbital is spherically symmetric, so it is invariant under any rotation. ✓

Check 3 ($\ell=1$). From the trigonometric identity $\sin(3\alpha/2) = \sin(\alpha/2)\left(2\cos\alpha+1\right)$ (or by substituting $x=\alpha/2$ into $\sin(3x)=\sin x(2\cos 2x+1)$), we obtain

$$ \chi^{(1)}(\alpha) = 1 + 2\cos\alpha $$

This is exactly the same as the trace of the rotation matrix found in Chapter 4, $\Tr C_n = 1+2\cos(2\pi/n)$. It reflects the fact that the $p$ orbitals $(p_x,p_y,p_z)$ transform in the same way as the vector $(x,y,z)$. ✓

Supplement: Characters of improper rotations ($\det = -1$)

Unless we can also handle reflections, inversion, and rotoreflections, the results cannot be used for point groups. Any improper rotation can be written as $\bar{R} = i\,R(\alpha)$ (the product of the inversion and a proper rotation by the angle $\alpha$) (Chapter 4). Since the parity of the spherical harmonics is $\hat{P}_i Y_{\ell m} = (-1)^{\ell}Y_{\ell m}$, $\hat{P}_i$ is $(-1)^\ell$ times the identity matrix. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \chi^{(\ell)}\!\left(i R(\alpha)\right) = (-1)^{\ell}\,\frac{\sin\!\left[\left(\ell+\tfrac12\right)\alpha\right]}{\sin\!\left(\alpha/2\right)} \label{eq:9-chiimproper} \end{equation} $$

In use, first factor out the inversion and read off the angle of the remaining proper rotation, as in $\sigma_h = iC_2$ ($\alpha=180^\circ$), $S_4 = iC_4^3$ ($\alpha=270^\circ$), and $S_6 = iC_3^2$ ($\alpha=240^\circ$). Since Eq. \eqref{eq:9-chiell} is an even function of $\alpha$, $270^\circ$ gives the same value as $-90^\circ$, that is, as $90^\circ$.

9.6.2 Decomposition of the $d$ orbitals in $O_h$

Derivation: Computing the characters for $\ell=2$ over the ten classes of $O_h$

Since $\ell=2$, we have $(-1)^\ell = +1$; that is, all $d$ orbitals are even (gerade). Putting $\ell=2$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:9-chiell} gives $\chi^{(2)}(\alpha) = \sin(5\alpha/2)/\sin(\alpha/2)$.

  • $\alpha = 0$: $2\ell+1 = 5$
  • $\alpha = 120^\circ$ ($C_3$): $\dfrac{\sin300^\circ}{\sin60^\circ} = \dfrac{-\sqrt3/2}{\sqrt3/2} = -1$
  • $\alpha = 90^\circ$ ($C_4$): $\dfrac{\sin225^\circ}{\sin45^\circ} = \dfrac{-\sqrt2/2}{\sqrt2/2} = -1$
  • $\alpha = 180^\circ$ ($C_2$, $C_2'$): $\dfrac{\sin450^\circ}{\sin90^\circ} = \dfrac{1}{1} = +1$

For improper rotations, Eq. \eqref{eq:9-chiimproper} gives a factor $(-1)^2=+1$, that is, the same values unchanged: $i$ has $5$ at $\alpha=0$, $S_4$ has $-1$ at $\alpha=270^\circ\equiv-90^\circ$, $S_6$ has $-1$ at $\alpha=240^\circ\equiv-120^\circ$, and $\sigma_h$ and $\sigma_d$ have $+1$ at $\alpha=180^\circ$.

∎ Counting dimensions, $2+3 = 5$ ✓. The $d$ orbitals, which were fivefold degenerate in the isolated atom, split in a regular octahedral field into a twofold ($e_g$) and a threefold ($t_{2g}$) level.

Physical Meaning: Which lies higher and which lower

All that group theory can tell us is that the levels split into “$2+3$”; which one has the higher energy is not determined by group theory alone. To decide this, one must actually calculate the interaction with the ligands.

A picture in real space makes it intuitively clear. Among the five real $d$ orbitals,

  • $e_g$ = $\left\{d_{z^2},\ d_{x^2-y^2}\right\}$: the lobes point along $\pm x,\pm y,\pm z$, that is, in the directions of the ligands
  • $t_{2g}$ = $\left\{d_{xy},\ d_{yz},\ d_{zx}\right\}$: the lobes point between the axes

If the ligands carry negative charge (or are electron-rich coordinating atoms), the $e_g$ orbitals, whose lobes point in those directions, are electrostatically disfavored and have the higher energy. We write the splitting as $\Delta_{\mathrm{o}} = 10Dq$. The magnitude of this $\Delta_\mathrm{o}$ governs the color of transition-metal complexes, the distinction between high-spin and low-spin states, and the presence or absence of Jahn–Teller distortion.

Incidentally, in a tetrahedral field ($T_d$) the ligands approach from between the axes, so the ordering is reversed: $\Gamma_d = E \oplus T_2$, with $e$ lower and $t_2$ higher ($\Delta_{\mathrm{t}} \approx \tfrac49\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}$). A complete calculation is carried out in Chapter 10.

Caution: The splitting in $O_h$ mixes different $m$

The two members of $E_g$, $\{d_{z^2},\ d_{x^2-y^2}\}$, are, in terms of $m$, a combination of $m=0$ and $m=\pm2$. The three members of $T_{2g}$, $\{d_{xy},d_{yz},d_{zx}\}$, are a combination of $m=\pm2$ and $m=\pm1$. That is, a crystal field of cubic symmetry mixes states with different $m$.

This is only natural: $m$ is the label of the symmetry “continuous rotation about the $z$ axis,” and $O_h$ does not have that symmetry (it has only $C_4$). When the symmetry is lowered, the original quantum number ceases to be a good quantum number. It is precisely group theory that tells us what remains as a good quantum number.

Example 9.5: Showing that $R_z$ belongs to $A_2$ in $C_{3v}$

$R_z$ represents “rotation about the $z$ axis”; we may think of it as an arrow indicating the sense of rotation in the $xy$ plane (counterclockwise taken as positive). For angular momentum, $L_z = xp_y - yp_x$.

(i) Under $C_3$. Rotating the arrow about the $z$ axis does not change its sense (counterclockwise). Thus $\hat{P}_{C_3}R_z = R_z$, that is, $\chi = +1$.

(ii) Under $\sigma_v$ (the $xz$ plane, $y\to-y$). In a mirror, a counterclockwise arrow looks clockwise. Thus $\hat{P}_{\sigma_v}R_z = -R_z$, that is, $\chi = -1$.

Let us check this with equations. Since $\sigma_1$ is $(x,y,z)\to(x,-y,z)$ and the momentum transforms likewise, $(p_x,p_y,p_z)\to(p_x,-p_y,p_z)$, we have

$$ L_z = xp_y - yp_x \;\longrightarrow\; x(-p_y) - (-y)p_x = -\left(xp_y - yp_x\right) = -L_z \quad ✓ $$

Therefore the characters of $R_z$ are $(\chi(E),\chi(C_3),\chi(\sigma_v)) = (1,1,-1)$, and comparing with Table 9.1 we see that this is $A_2$. ∎

This is the decisive difference from $z$. The component $z$ (a polar vector) is invariant under the reflection $\sigma_v$, whereas $R_z$ (an axial vector) changes sign. In general, an axial vector transforms according to the transformation law of a polar vector multiplied by one extra factor of $\det R$. This is why, in $C_{3v}$, they go to different places: $z \in A_1$ and $R_z \in A_2$. This difference is the root of the distinction between electrical and magnetic properties (Chapter 20).

Example 9.6: “Constructing” the rightmost column with a projection operator

In $C_{3v}$, compute $\hat{P}^{(A_1)}x^2$ and explain why $x^2+y^2$ is written in the $A_1$ row of Table 9.1.

Solution. First we compute the rotation part. By Eq. \eqref{eq:9-c3xy}, $\hat{P}_{C_3}x = -\tfrac12 x + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}y$, and $\hat{P}_R$ satisfies $\hat{P}_R(fg) = (\hat{P}_Rf)(\hat{P}_Rg)$ for a product of functions (since it merely replaces the arguments in the same way). Therefore

$$ \hat{P}_{C_3}x^2 = \left(-\tfrac12 x + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}y\right)^2 = \tfrac14 x^2 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}xy + \tfrac34 y^2 $$

Similarly, since $\hat{P}_{C_3^2}x = -\tfrac12x - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}y$,

$$ \hat{P}_{C_3^2}x^2 = \tfrac14 x^2 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}xy + \tfrac34 y^2 $$

Adding the three, the $xy$ terms cancel and we obtain

$$ \hat{P}_E x^2 + \hat{P}_{C_3}x^2 + \hat{P}_{C_3^2}x^2 = x^2 + \tfrac12 x^2 + \tfrac32 y^2 = \tfrac32\left(x^2+y^2\right) $$

Next, the reflection part. The three reflections can be written as $\{\sigma_1,\ \sigma_1C_3,\ \sigma_1C_3^2\}$ (the cosets of Chapter 6), so

$$ \sum_{j=1}^{3}\hat{P}_{\sigma_j}x^2 = \hat{P}_{\sigma_1}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{2}\hat{P}_{C_3^k}x^2\right] = \hat{P}_{\sigma_1}\left[\tfrac32(x^2+y^2)\right] = \tfrac32\left(x^2+y^2\right) $$

(Since $\sigma_1$ is $y\to-y$, $y^2$ is invariant.) Hence

$$ \hat{P}^{(A_1)}x^2 = \frac{1}{6}\left[\tfrac32(x^2+y^2) + \tfrac32(x^2+y^2)\right] = \frac{1}{2}\left(x^2+y^2\right) $$

∎ What emerges is precisely $x^2+y^2$. The rightmost column of a character table can thus be constructed mechanically with projection operators. $z^2$ (or $2z^2-x^2-y^2$) likewise falls into $A_1$.

Physical Meaning: Three uses of the rightmost column

(1) Determining whether a group is polar (connection with Chapter 4). If $z$ (or any of $x,y,z$) belongs to the totally symmetric representation, then placing a spontaneous polarization along that direction does not contradict the symmetry. In $C_{3v}$, $z\in A_1$, so it is a polar point group. Indeed, $\mathrm{NH_3}$ has a permanent dipole. On the other hand, in $D_{3h}$, $z \in A_2''$, which is not totally symmetric, so the group is nonpolar. This explains why $\mathrm{BF_3}$ (a planar triangle) has no dipole.

(2) Determining infrared and Raman activity (Chapter 10). A necessary condition for a normal mode to be infrared-active is that the irreducible representation of the vibration contain one of $x,\ y,\ z$. The condition for Raman activity is that it contain one of the quadratic forms. In $C_{3v}$, $A_1$ and $E$ are both infrared- and Raman-active, while $A_2$ is inactive in both.

(3) The mutual exclusion rule. In a centrosymmetric point group, $x,y,z$ always belong to odd ($u$) representations and the quadratic forms always belong to even ($g$) representations (because under $\rr\to-\rr$ a linear expression changes sign while a quadratic expression does not). Therefore infrared-active modes are Raman-inactive, and Raman-active modes are infrared-inactive, so no mode appears in both. Conversely, if a peak at the same frequency appears in both spectra, we can conclude that the molecule (crystal) is not centrosymmetric. This is a powerful experimental means of addressing the problem mentioned in Chapter 4, that it is difficult to decide whether a crystal has centrosymmetry.

9.8.1 Summary

  • The symmetry operation acting on a function is defined by $\hat{P}_R f(\rr) = f(R^{-1}\rr)$. The $R^{-1}$ appears because “moving the hump of a function in the positive direction” is the same as “moving the argument in the negative direction” (Figure 9.1). With any other definition, $\hat{P}_R\hat{P}_S = \hat{P}_{RS}$ would fail and the order of the product would be reversed.
  • Basis functions are a set of functions satisfying $\hat{P}_R f^{(\Gamma_n)}_\mu = \sum_\nu D^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)_{\nu\mu}f^{(\Gamma_n)}_\nu$. The sum runs over the first index (the row). If we choose $(x,y,z)$ as the basis, then $D(R) = R$; that is, the representation matrix is just the geometric rotation matrix itself (Eq. \eqref{eq:9-DequalsR}).
  • In $C_{3v}$, $z \in A_1$ and $(x,y)\in E$. As $\hat{P}_{C_3}x = -\tfrac12 x + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}y$ shows, $x$ does not close on itself but always mixes with $y$, so the two together span $E$. This is the meaning of $(x,y)$ being enclosed in parentheses in the $E$ row of the character table.
  • The projection operators $$ \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}_{\mu\nu} = \frac{\ell_n}{h}\sum_R D^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)^{*}_{\mu\nu}\hat{P}_R, \qquad \hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)} = \frac{\ell_n}{h}\sum_R \chi^{(\Gamma_n)}(R)^{*}\hat{P}_R $$ can be derived from the great orthogonality theorem in two lines. The coefficient $\ell_n/h$ is there to cancel the right-hand side $h/\ell_n$ of the great orthogonality theorem. Idempotency $\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_{n'})} = \delta_{nn'}\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}$ and completeness $\sum_n\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)} = \hat{1}$ hold, and the function space is decomposed into a direct sum over the irreducible representations.
  • The practical form can be built from characters alone. Even without knowing the representation matrices, the character table alone lets us write down $\hat{P}^{(\Gamma_n)}$. For irreducible representations of dimension two or higher, however, subtraction or Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization is needed to obtain two or more partners.
  • SALCs of the three atoms of an equilateral triangle. With $\Gamma_{1s} = A_1\oplus E$, $$ \psi_{A_1} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(\phi_1+\phi_2+\phi_3),\quad \psi_{E,1} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt6}(2\phi_1-\phi_2-\phi_3),\quad \psi_{E,2} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\phi_2-\phi_3) $$ the coefficients are proportional to “all 1,” “the $x$ coordinate of the atom,” and “the $y$ coordinate of the atom,” respectively.
  • Taking SALCs as the basis block-diagonalizes the secular equation. Matrix elements between different irreducible representations vanish by symmetry, and each block belonging to the same irreducible representation is a constant multiple of the identity matrix by Schur's lemma. For the three-atom Hückel problem, $\varepsilon = \alpha+2\beta$ ($a_1$) and $\alpha-\beta$ ($e$, twofold degenerate) are obtained, so the answer comes out without solving the $3\times3$ determinant. Generalized to an $N$-membered ring, it gives $\varepsilon_k = \alpha+2\beta\cos(2\pi k/N)$, which is precisely the Bloch function and the tight-binding band.
  • The character of the rotation group is $\chi^{(\ell)}(\alpha) = \sin[(\ell+\tfrac12)\alpha]/\sin(\alpha/2)$. It is derived as the sum of a geometric series from the fact that $Y_{\ell m}$ acquires $e^{-im\alpha}$ under rotation about the $z$ axis. For improper rotations, it is multiplied by $(-1)^\ell$. Using this, we can show in a few lines that $\ell=2$ splits into $E_g\oplus T_{2g}$ in $O_h$.
  • The right-hand columns of a character table list the basis functions. $x,y,z$ are polar vectors ($p$ orbitals, electric dipole, polarization), $R_x,R_y,R_z$ are axial vectors (angular momentum, magnetic moment), and the quadratic forms correspond to $d$ orbitals and the polarizability. They are used to decide infrared activity, magnetic properties, and Raman activity, respectively. In a centrosymmetric group, the former belong to $u$ and the latter to $g$, so the mutual exclusion rule holds.

9.8.2 Exercises

Exercise 9.1: Checking $\hat{P}_R\hat{P}_S = \hat{P}_{RS}$ in a concrete example

In $C_{4v}$, consider $C_4$ (rotation by $+90^\circ$ about the $z$ axis) and $\sigma_v$ (the $xz$ plane, $y\to-y$).

(1) Find $\hat{P}_{C_4}x$, $\hat{P}_{C_4}y$, $\hat{P}_{\sigma_v}x$, and $\hat{P}_{\sigma_v}y$.

(2) Compute $\hat{P}_{C_4}\left(\hat{P}_{\sigma_v}x\right)$ and $\hat{P}_{\sigma_v}\left(\hat{P}_{C_4}x\right)$, and verify that they are different. Explain how this corresponds to $C_4\sigma_v \ne \sigma_v C_4$.

(3) Verify that $\hat{P}_{C_4}\hat{P}_{\sigma_v} = \hat{P}_{C_4\sigma_v}$ by actually computing the matrix $C_4\sigma_v$.

Hint: (1) $\hat{P}_{C_4}x = y$, $\hat{P}_{C_4}y = -x$, $\hat{P}_{\sigma_v}x = x$, $\hat{P}_{\sigma_v}y = -y$. (2) The former gives $\hat{P}_{C_4}x = y$ and the latter gives $\hat{P}_{\sigma_v}y = -y$, so the signs differ. What matters here is that the order of the operators agrees with the order of the product of the group elements (Eq. \eqref{eq:9-homo}). (3) $C_4\sigma_v = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, that is, $(x,y)\to(y,x)$, which is one of the $\sigma_d$ (the plane $y=x$).

Exercise 9.2: Constructing SALCs from four atoms in $C_{4v}$

Instead of placing the vertices of the square in the diagonal directions $(\pm a, \pm a)$, place four atoms on the $\pm x$ and $\pm y$ axes (think of the F atoms of $\mathrm{XeF_4}$, or the ligands of a square-planar complex; the point group of these molecules as a whole is $D_{4h}$, but here we restrict attention to its subgroup $C_{4v}$). Number the atoms $1,2,3,4$ counterclockwise starting from $+x$, and let $\phi_i$ be the $s$ orbital of each. The character table of $C_{4v}$ is

$C_{4v}$$E$$2C_4$$C_2$$2\sigma_v$$2\sigma_d$Basis
$A_1$11111$z$; $x^2+y^2$, $z^2$
$A_2$111$-1$$-1$$R_z$
$B_1$1$-1$11$-1$$x^2-y^2$
$B_2$1$-1$1$-1$1$xy$

(1) Find the characters of the reducible representation by counting “the number of atoms that did not move.”

(2) Reduce it into irreducible representations.

(3) Using projection operators, write down all the normalized SALCs.

Hint: (1) $\chi = (4,\,0,\,0,\,2,\,0)$. $\sigma_v$ (the $xz$ and $yz$ planes) leaves two atoms each, and $\sigma_d$ (the diagonal planes) leaves none. (2) $a_{A_1}=\frac18(4+4)=1$, $a_{B_1}=\frac18(4+4)=1$, $a_E=\frac18(8)=1$, and the others are zero. Hence $\Gamma = A_1\oplus B_1\oplus E$. (3) $\psi_{A_1}=\frac12(\phi_1+\phi_2+\phi_3+\phi_4)$, $\psi_{B_1}=\frac12(\phi_1-\phi_2+\phi_3-\phi_4)$, and $\psi_{E}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\phi_1-\phi_3)$ and $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\phi_2-\phi_4)$. The last two transform in the same way as $x$ and $y$, respectively.

Exercise 9.3: Hückel levels of an $N$-membered ring and benzene

Suppose that $N$ equivalent atoms form a ring and that only nearest neighbors are connected by the resonance integral $\beta$.

(1) Apply the projection operator for the irreducible representation $\chi^{(k)}(C_N^{\,j}) = e^{2\pi ikj/N}$ of the cyclic group $C_N$ to $\phi_0$, and derive $\psi_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_j e^{-2\pi ikj/N}\phi_j$.

(2) Show that $\varepsilon_k = \langle\psi_k|\Ham|\psi_k\rangle = \alpha + 2\beta\cos(2\pi k/N)$.

(3) For $N=6$ (the $\pi$ system of benzene), find all the levels, and compute the total $\pi$ energy when six $\pi$ electrons are put in. Compare this with that of three ethylene molecules ($3\times2(\alpha+\beta)$) and find the delocalization energy.

Hint: (2) Substitute $\Ham\phi_j = \alpha\phi_j + \beta(\phi_{j-1}+\phi_{j+1})$ and factor out the phase factors as $e^{\pm 2\pi ik/N}$. (3) $k=0$: $\alpha+2\beta$; $k=\pm1$: $\alpha+\beta$ (twofold degenerate); $k=\pm2$: $\alpha-\beta$ (twofold degenerate); $k=3$: $\alpha-2\beta$. With six electrons, $2(\alpha+2\beta)+4(\alpha+\beta) = 6\alpha+8\beta$. The energy of three ethylene molecules is $6\alpha+6\beta$, so the difference is $2\beta$ (stabilization, since $\beta$ is negative). This is the Hückel explanation of the aromatic stabilization of benzene.

Exercise 9.4: Decomposition of the $f$ orbitals ($\ell=3$) in $O_h$

Using Eqs. \eqref{eq:9-chiell} and \eqref{eq:9-chiimproper}, find how the sevenfold degeneracy of $\ell=3$ splits in $O_h$.

(1) Compute $\chi^{(3)}$ for the 10 classes of $O_h$ and make a table.

(2) Reduce it into irreducible representations.

(3) Explain why only irreducible representations of $u$ (odd) type appear.

Hint: (1) From $\chi^{(3)}(\alpha) = \sin(7\alpha/2)/\sin(\alpha/2)$, for proper rotations $E:7$, $C_3:1$, $C_4:-1$, $C_2:-1$, $C_2':-1$. For improper rotations, multiply by $(-1)^3=-1$: $i:-7$, $S_4:+1$, $S_6:-1$, $\sigma_h:+1$, $\sigma_d:+1$. (2) $\Gamma_f = A_{2u}\oplus T_{1u}\oplus T_{2u}$ (dimensions $1+3+3=7$ ✓). (3) Because the parity of $Y_{3m}$ is $(-1)^3=-1$, and $\hat{P}_i$ therefore acts as $-\hat{1}$. The crystal-field splitting of the lanthanide $4f$ electrons corresponds to this decomposition.

Exercise 9.5: SALCs of $p$ orbitals — the non-spherical case

In Section 9.4 we dealt with the $1s$ orbitals (spherically symmetric), so it was enough to simply relabel the atoms, $\hat{P}_R\phi_i = \phi_j$. Now consider the case in which a $p_z$ orbital sits on each of the three atoms of an equilateral triangle ($z$ is the direction of the $C_3$ axis).

(1) Verify that the $p_z$ orbital does not change its orientation under any operation of $C_{3v}$, and show that $\hat{P}_R\phi^{(p_z)}_i$ can be written with exactly the same table as in the $1s$ case (Table 9.2).

(2) What happens, then, if a $p_x$ orbital sits on each atom? Explain in words what happens when $\hat{P}_{C_3}$ is applied.

(3) Find the characters of the nine-dimensional (three atoms $\times$ three components $= 9$) reducible representation as “the number of atoms that did not move $\times$ the contribution of the vector on that atom,” and reduce it into irreducible representations.

Hint: (1) $p_z \propto z\,f(r)$, and $z$ belongs to $A_1$ (Section 9.2.1). (2) As the atom moves from 1 to 2, the orientation of the orbital also turns by $120^\circ$, so the result is a mixture of $p_x$ and $p_y$ on atom 2. (3) The contribution per unmoved atom is the character $\chi_{\mathrm{vec}}(R)$ of the vector representation of that operation ($1+2\cos\alpha$ for a rotation, $1$ for a reflection). We obtain $\chi = (9,\,0,\,1)$, and $\Gamma = 2A_1\oplus A_2\oplus 3E$. This is also the starting point of the counting of molecular vibrations in Chapter 10.

Exercise 9.6: Predicting physical properties by reading the right-hand columns of a character table

(1) Water, $\mathrm{H_2O}$, is $C_{2v}$ ($mm2$). Write down the character table of $C_{2v}$, and determine to which irreducible representation each of $x,y,z$ and $R_x,R_y,R_z$ belongs, in the same way as in Section 9.2 (by applying each operation). From this, show that $\mathrm{H_2O}$ is a polar molecule.

(2) $\mathrm{BF_3}$ is $D_{3h}$. Show, by applying $\sigma_h$ (the molecular plane), that $z$ belongs not to the totally symmetric representation $A_1'$ but to $A_2''$, and conclude that $\mathrm{BF_3}$ is nonpolar. Where does the difference from $\mathrm{NH_3}$ ($C_{3v}$) come from?

(3) Show, by applying the inversion operation $\hat{P}_i$, that in a centrosymmetric point group $x,y,z$ always belong to $u$ and the quadratic forms always belong to $g$. Derive the mutual exclusion rule from this.

Hint: (1) $C_{2v} = \{E, C_2(z), \sigma(xz), \sigma(yz)\}$. $z\in A_1$, $x\in B_1$, $y\in B_2$, $R_z\in A_2$. Since $z$ is totally symmetric, the polar axis is along the $z$ direction, and this is the direction of the dipole of water. (2) Since $\hat{P}_{\sigma_h}z = -z$, $\chi(\sigma_h) = -1$, so it is not totally symmetric. $C_{3v}$ has no $\sigma_h$ whereas $D_{3h}$ does, and that is the whole difference (compare with the list of polar point groups in Chapter 4). (3) $\hat{P}_i x = -x$, $\hat{P}_i (xy) = (-x)(-y) = xy$.
