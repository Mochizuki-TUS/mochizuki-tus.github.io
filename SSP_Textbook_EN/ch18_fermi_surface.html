Chapter 18Fermi Surfaces and Metals

In Chapter 15 we treated the free-electron Fermi gas, in Chapter 16 we saw that a periodic potential opens band gaps, and in Chapter 17 we dealt with semiconductors, which use only the neighborhood of a band edge. The protagonist of this chapter is the metal. In a metal, a band is filled only part of the way. The boundary surface between the filled and the empty states—this is the Fermi surface.

Why is the Fermi surface so important? There is only one reason. Almost all the low-temperature properties of a metal are determined solely by the electrons in the immediate vicinity of the Fermi surface. Because of the Pauli exclusion principle, an electron lying deeper than $k_BT$ below the Fermi surface has no vacant state to move to, however much one tries to excite it. For $\mathrm{Cu}$ at room temperature, $k_BT/\epsilon_F = 0.026/7.00 = 0.0037$, so fewer than 0.4 % of all the electrons are able to move. Electrical conduction, heat conduction, the electronic specific heat, Pauli paramagnetism, and superconductivity are all carried by that 0.4 %. To understand a metal is to know the shape of its Fermi surface.

The shape of the Fermi surface, however, is not necessarily a sphere as for free electrons. In a periodic potential the bands bend at the boundaries of the Brillouin zone, the Fermi surface meets the zone boundary at right angles, and it splits into several sheets. The Fermi surface of $\mathrm{Cu}$ touches the boundary at the $L$ point and puts out a “neck,” and the Fermi surface of $\mathrm{Al}$ straddles the second and third zones. The method of assembling this complicated shape by hand, starting from the free-electron sphere, is the Harrison construction.

The other high point of this chapter is the method of seeing the Fermi surface experimentally. When a magnetic field is applied, an electron circulates on a constant-energy surface in $k$ space. From Onsager’s condition that this orbit be quantized, the magnetization oscillates as a periodic function of $1/B$—this is the de Haas–van Alphen effect. If one measures the period of the oscillation, the cross-sectional area of the Fermi surface is obtained directly. Ever since its discovery in 1930, this has been the royal road to precise measurement of the Fermi surface, and even today it is the most severe touchstone for checking the correctness of first-principles calculations.

18.1 What Is the Fermi Surface, and Why Does It Matter?

18.1.1 Definition

Definition: Fermi surface

At absolute zero, the surface in $\kk$ space that separates occupied orbitals from empty orbitals is called the Fermi surface. That is, for a band structure $\epsilon_n(\kk)$, it is the set of $\kk$ satisfying

$$ \epsilon_n(\kk) = \epsilon_F $$

Here $n$ is the band index, and if several bands cross $\epsilon_F$, the Fermi surface consists of several sheets.

Since $\epsilon_n(\kk)=\epsilon_n(\kk+\GG)$ (Chapter 11), the Fermi surface also has the periodicity of the reciprocal lattice. Therefore drawing only the inside of the first Brillouin zone exhausts the information (the reduced-zone scheme), but when we discuss the connectivity of orbits, a periodically repeated picture (the repeated-zone scheme) is easier to read.

For the free-electron gas (Chapter 15), $\epsilon(\kk)=\hbar^2k^2/2m$, so the Fermi surface is a sphere of radius

$$ \begin{equation} k_F = (3\pi^2 n)^{1/3} \label{eq:18-kf} \end{equation} $$

Here $n$ is the number of conduction electrons per unit volume. Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-kf}$ was derived in Chapter 15. Let us recall the route briefly. If periodic boundary conditions are imposed on a box of volume $V$, there is one $\kk$ point per $(2\pi)^3/V$; since the spin doubles this, the number of electrons in a sphere of radius $k_F$ is $N = 2\cdot\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3}k_F^3 = \frac{V k_F^3}{3\pi^2}$, which we then solve for $n=N/V$.

Example 18.1: Comparing the size of the Fermi surface of $\mathrm{Cu}$ with the Brillouin zone

Suppose that $\mathrm{Cu}$ is fcc with lattice constant $a=3.61\,\text{Å}$ and has one conduction electron per atom ($4s^1$). Find $k_F$ and compare it with the distance to the $L$ point (the center of a $\{111\}$ face).

Solution. The fcc cubic unit cell contains four atoms (Chapter 1). Therefore

$$ n = \frac{4}{a^3} = \frac{4}{(3.61\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{cm})^3} = \frac{4}{4.705\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{cm^3}} = 8.50\times10^{22}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}} $$

This agrees well with the measured value $8.45\times10^{22}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. From Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-kf}$,

$$ k_F = (3\pi^2 \times 8.50\times10^{22})^{1/3} = (2.517\times10^{24})^{1/3} = 1.36\times10^{8}\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}} = 1.36\,\text{Å}^{-1} $$

It is convenient to rewrite this in terms of the lattice constant. Substituting $n=4/a^3$,

$$ k_F = \left(\frac{12\pi^2}{a^3}\right)^{1/3} = \frac{(12\pi^2)^{1/3}}{a} = \frac{4.911}{a} $$

On the other hand, the $L$ point is the midpoint of $\GG_{111}=\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,1,1)$, so its distance from $\Gamma$ is

$$ k_L = \frac{1}{2}\abs{\GG_{111}} = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{a}\sqrt{3} = \frac{\sqrt3\,\pi}{a} = \frac{5.441}{a} $$

Hence

$$ \frac{k_F}{k_L} = \frac{4.911}{5.441} = 0.903 $$

A free-electron sphere reaches only 90 % of the way to the $L$ point. Yet the actual Fermi surface of $\mathrm{Cu}$ touches the zone boundary at the $L$ point. What makes up this 10 % difference is the periodic potential created by the filled $3d$ shell. We treat this in detail in Section 18.7.∎

18.1.2 Why does only the Fermi surface matter?

Why? The reason low-temperature properties are determined only near the Fermi surface

The occupation number at finite temperature is given by the Fermi–Dirac distribution

$$ f(\epsilon) = \frac{1}{\ee^{(\epsilon-\mu)/k_BT}+1} $$

Differentiating with respect to $\epsilon$ gives

$$ -\frac{\partial f}{\partial \epsilon} = \frac{1}{k_BT}\cdot\frac{\ee^{(\epsilon-\mu)/k_BT}}{\left(\ee^{(\epsilon-\mu)/k_BT}+1\right)^2} = \frac{1}{4k_BT}\,\mathrm{sech}^2\!\left(\frac{\epsilon-\mu}{2k_BT}\right) $$

Let us write out the intermediate step. Setting $x=(\epsilon-\mu)/k_BT$, we have $\ee^x/(\ee^x+1)^2$. Dividing numerator and denominator by $\ee^{x}$ gives $1/(\ee^{x/2}+\ee^{-x/2})^2 = 1/(2\cosh(x/2))^2$. Therefore $-\partial f/\partial\epsilon = \frac{1}{4k_BT}\mathrm{sech}^2(x/2)$.

This function has a sharp peak at $\epsilon=\mu$ with a width of $\sim 4k_BT$, and $\int(-\partial f/\partial\epsilon)\dd\epsilon = 1$. In other words, $-\partial f/\partial\epsilon$ is a delta-function-like object stuck to the Fermi level.

Now, both transport coefficients and thermodynamic responses always contain $-\partial f/\partial\epsilon$ as a factor in their expressions. For example, the electrical conductivity can be written as

$$ \sigma_{\alpha\beta} = e^2\int \frac{2\,\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\tau(\kk)\,v_\alpha(\kk)v_\beta(\kk)\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial\epsilon}\right) $$

Since $-\partial f/\partial\epsilon\to\delta(\epsilon-\epsilon_F)$ as $T\to0$, this integral collapses to a surface integral over the Fermi surface.

$$ \sigma_{\alpha\beta} \xrightarrow{T\to0} \frac{e^2}{4\pi^3}\oint_{\rm FS}\frac{\dd S_k}{\hbar\abs{\vv}}\,\tau\, v_\alpha v_\beta $$

(We decomposed $\dd^3k = \dd S_k\,\dd k_\perp$ and used $\dd\epsilon = \hbar\abs{\vv}\dd k_\perp$.) Electrons deeper than the Fermi surface and vacant seats shallower than it contribute nothing at all.

The same holds for the electronic specific heat. The conclusion of the Sommerfeld expansion in Chapter 15, $C_{el}=\frac{\pi^2}{3}k_B^2T\,g(\epsilon_F)$, contains only the value of the density of states at $\epsilon_F$. And $g(\epsilon_F)$ itself is “the integral of the area of the Fermi surface divided by $\abs{\vv}$”:

$$ g(\epsilon_F) = \frac{2}{(2\pi)^3}\oint_{\rm FS}\frac{\dd S_k}{\hbar\abs{\vv(\kk)}} $$

In the end, the quantities that characterize a low-temperature metal are only the shape of the Fermi surface (area, curvature, connectivity) and the velocity distribution on it. This is why it is said that “the Fermi surface is the fingerprint of a metal.”

Conversely, a slight change in the shape of the Fermi surface changes the physical properties greatly. When alloying fills the bands slightly and the Fermi surface touches the Brillouin zone boundary, the density of states jumps there and the phase stability changes. This is the origin of the Hume-Rothery rule (the alloy phase is determined by the electron concentration $e/a$).

Supplement: Luttinger's theorem — the volume enclosed by the Fermi surface does not change

The shape of the Fermi surface is changed by interactions, but the volume in $\kk$ space enclosed by the Fermi surface remains at its free-electron value no matter how strong the interactions are. This is called Luttinger's theorem (1960).

$$ \frac{2}{(2\pi)^3}\sum_n V_n^{\rm occ} = n \quad(\text{number of electrons per unit volume}) $$

Thanks to this theorem, the seemingly crude procedure of starting from a free-electron sphere in the Harrison construction (Section 18.2) is in fact exactly correct as far as the volume is concerned. The periodic potential and electron correlation deform the Fermi surface but neither inflate nor shrink it. This fact is also used in the check of whether the sum of the dHvA cross sections measured in experiments agrees with the number of electrons.

However, this theorem does not hold unconditionally for every system. The prerequisites are that translational symmetry is preserved, that the particle number is conserved ($U(1)$ symmetry), and that the system can be described as an ordinary Fermi liquid (or an appropriate generalization of it). It cannot be used in its original form for systems with topological order, or for systems in which ordering has doubled the unit cell. Also, in a crystal a completely filled band has no Fermi surface and merely supplies an integer number of electrons per unit cell. Therefore, when reading the number of electrons off the shape of the Fermi surface, we count modulo the filled bands. The convention is that electron pockets and hole pockets are counted with opposite signs.

18.2 Zone Schemes and the Harrison Construction

18.2.1 The three zone schemes

As we saw in Chapter 16, the energy of an electron in a periodic potential has the periodicity $\epsilon_n(\kk)=\epsilon_n(\kk+\GG)$. Because of this periodicity, there are three conventions for drawing the band structure. All of them express the same information, but we use them selectively depending on the purpose.

Definition: The three zone schemes

  • Extended-zone scheme: The $n$th band is drawn inside the $n$th Brillouin zone. In the free-electron limit, the parabola $\hbar^2k^2/2m$ appears as a single connected curve. The Harrison construction is carried out in this scheme.
  • Reduced-zone scheme: All bands are folded back and drawn inside the first Brillouin zone. A state is specified by the pair of $\kk$ and the band index $n$; this is the most standard way of drawing. The band diagram ($\Gamma$–$X$–$M$–$\cdots$) is of this kind.
  • Repeated-zone scheme: $\epsilon_n(\kk)$ is drawn repeated periodically over the whole of $\kk$ space. When we judge whether an orbit is closed or open, we must use this scheme.

Supplement: Every zone has the same volume

The $n$th Brillouin zone is the set of $\kk$ that can be reached from the origin by crossing exactly $n-1$ Bragg planes (a Bragg plane is the perpendicular bisecting plane of a reciprocal lattice vector $\GG$; Chapter 3).

An important fact is that every zone has the same volume, equal to the volume $(2\pi)^3/\Omega$ of the first Brillouin zone ($\Omega$ is the volume of the primitive cell in real space). This is because, if each piece of the $n$th zone is translated by a suitable reciprocal lattice vector, the pieces tile the first zone exactly once with no gaps (this operation is the “reduction”).

Therefore, the number of states in one band, including spin, is $2N$ ($N$ is the number of primitive cells in the crystal). With one valence electron per atom there are $N$ electrons—the band is only half filled. With two valence electrons there are $2N$ electrons—exactly one band's worth, but if the bands overlap the material is a metal, and if they do not overlap it is an insulator. This counting is the same as the one derived in Chapter 16.

18.2.2 The Harrison construction

When the periodic potential is weak (simple metals such as the alkali metals and $\mathrm{Al}$), the Fermi surface is very close to “the free-electron sphere cut into pieces by the Brillouin zones and folded back.” The Harrison construction is a prescription for carrying out this cutting mechanically.

Harrison construction

  1. From the electron density $n$, find the free-electron Fermi wavenumber $k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$ (three dimensions) or $k_F=\sqrt{2\pi n_{2D}}$ (two dimensions).
  2. Draw the reciprocal lattice, and draw a sphere of radius $k_F$ (a circle in two dimensions) centered at every reciprocal lattice point.
  3. For each point in $\kk$ space, count how many spheres cover it.
  4. The region covered $m$ or more times is the occupied region of the $m$th band (in the extended-zone scheme, the occupied part of the $m$th zone).
  5. Folding it back into the first Brillouin zone by reciprocal lattice vectors gives the Fermi surface in the reduced-zone scheme.

Why is “the region covered $m$ or more times” the $m$th band?

For free electrons the occupation condition is simply $\abs{\kk}<k_F$. In the reduced-zone scheme, however, the state of wave vector $\kk$ is represented as a set of $\kk+\GG$. That is, for a reduced wave vector $\kk$ (inside the first zone), the candidates for the free-electron energy are

$$ \epsilon_{\GG}(\kk) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\abs{\kk+\GG}^2 \qquad (\GG:\text{all reciprocal lattice vectors}) $$

infinitely many in number, and arranging them in increasing order gives the first band, the second band, and so on.

Now, to say that the $m$th band is occupied at wave vector $\kk$ means that there are at least $m$ vectors $\GG$ satisfying $\epsilon_\GG(\kk)<\epsilon_F$. The condition $\epsilon_\GG(\kk)<\epsilon_F$ is equivalent to $\abs{\kk+\GG}<k_F$, that is, to “$\kk$ lies inside the sphere of radius $k_F$ centered at $-\GG$.”

Therefore “$\kk$ is covered by $m$ or more spheres” $\iff$ “the states up to the $m$th band are occupied at $\kk$.” This is the trick behind the Harrison construction. Nothing difficult is being done.

18.2.3 Working it out for the two-dimensional square lattice

Words alone are hard to follow, so let us actually draw it. Consider a two-dimensional square lattice with lattice constant $a$, with two electrons per lattice point (divalent).

Derivation: $k_F$ in two dimensions, and why it exceeds the first-zone boundary

Step 1: Counting states in two dimensions. If periodic boundary conditions are imposed on a square of area $A=L^2$, the allowed $\kk$ points occur at a rate of one per area $(2\pi/L)^2$. That is, there are $A/(2\pi)^2$ of them per unit area of $\kk$ space. Multiplying by 2 for spin, the number of electrons inside a circle of radius $k_F$ is

$$ N = 2\cdot\frac{A}{(2\pi)^2}\cdot \pi k_F^2 = \frac{A k_F^2}{2\pi} $$

Step 2: Solving for $k_F$. Solving for the areal density $n_{2D}=N/A$ gives

$$ k_F = \sqrt{2\pi n_{2D}} $$

Step 3: Substituting the divalent case. There are two electrons per lattice point and the areal density of lattice points is $1/a^2$, so $n_{2D}=2/a^2$. Hence

$$ k_F = \frac{\sqrt{4\pi}}{a} = \frac{3.545}{a} $$

Step 4: Comparing with the Brillouin zone. The reciprocal lattice of a square lattice is also a square lattice, with spacing $2\pi/a$. The first Brillouin zone is a square of side $2\pi/a$, that is, $\abs{k_x},\abs{k_y}<\pi/a$.

  • Distance to the midpoint of an edge (the $X$ point): $\pi/a = 3.142/a$
  • Distance to a corner (the $M$ point): $\sqrt2\,\pi/a = 4.443/a$

$k_F=3.545/a$ lies between these two. In other words, the Fermi circle pushes through the edges but does not reach the corners.

This one remark settles the conclusion. Near the corners (around the $M$ point), the first band is unoccupied—a hole pocket. The part that has pushed through the edges is the occupied region of the second band—when folded back, it becomes an electron lens around the $X$ point. ∎

Example 18.2: Checking by area (that the metal is compensated)

For the divalent two-dimensional metal above, verify by an area calculation that the number of holes in the first band equals the number of electrons in the second band.

Solution. The area of the Fermi circle is $\pi k_F^2 = \pi\cdot 4\pi/a^2 = 4\pi^2/a^2$. The area of the first Brillouin zone is $(2\pi/a)^2 = 4\pi^2/a^2$. The two are equal.

In the Harrison construction, the area of the Fermi circle equals “the occupied area of the first band + the occupied area of the second band” (the area covered once + the area covered twice, counted with multiplicity). That is,

$$ S_1^{\rm occ} + S_2^{\rm occ} = \pi k_F^2 = S_{\rm BZ} $$

On the other hand, the hole area of the first band is $S_1^{\rm hole}=S_{\rm BZ}-S_1^{\rm occ}$, so

$$ S_1^{\rm hole} = S_{\rm BZ} - S_1^{\rm occ} = S_2^{\rm occ} $$

Hole area = electron area. This metal is compensated. In general, a metal with an even number of electrons per primitive cell is always a compensated metal. This becomes important in the discussion of magnetoresistance in Section 18.3. $\mathrm{Mg}$, $\mathrm{Zn}$, $\mathrm{Bi}$, $\mathrm{W}$, and others are compensated metals. ∎

18.2.4 What changes when we include the periodic potential

The Harrison construction merely cuts and pastes free-electron spheres, so it has sharp corners and crosses the zone boundaries obliquely. Real Fermi surfaces are not like that. When we include the periodic potential $U_\GG$, the following four things happen.

What the periodic potential does to the Fermi surface

  1. A gap opens at the zone boundary (Chapter 16). On the boundary the energy is split by $2\abs{U_\GG}$.
  2. The Fermi surface stands perpendicular to the zone boundary. (Proved below.)
  3. Sharp corners are rounded. Where surfaces belonging to different zones meet, the potential causes hybridization and joins them smoothly.
  4. The total volume enclosed by the Fermi surface does not change (Luttinger's theorem). The shape changes, but the volume is conserved.

Derivation: Why does the Fermi surface stand perpendicular to the zone boundary?

Recall the “nearly free electrons” model of Chapter 16. Near the Bragg plane of the reciprocal lattice vector $\GG$, only the two plane waves $\ket{\kk}$ and $\ket{\kk-\GG}$ matter, and we only have to solve the $2\times2$ secular equation

$$ \begin{vmatrix} \epsilon^0_{\kk}-\epsilon & U_\GG \\ U_\GG^{*} & \epsilon^0_{\kk-\GG}-\epsilon \end{vmatrix}=0, \qquad \epsilon^0_\kk = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} $$

Expanding, we get $\epsilon^2 - (\epsilon^0_\kk+\epsilon^0_{\kk-\GG})\epsilon + \epsilon^0_\kk\epsilon^0_{\kk-\GG}-\abs{U_\GG}^2=0$, and the quadratic formula gives

$$ \epsilon_\pm(\kk) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\epsilon^0_\kk+\epsilon^0_{\kk-\GG}\right) \pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}\left(\epsilon^0_\kk-\epsilon^0_{\kk-\GG}\right)^2+\abs{U_\GG}^2} $$

Now we come to the main point. Let $\kk_0$ be a point on the Bragg plane, and write the displacement measured from it as $\qq$: $\kk=\kk_0+\qq$. Since the Bragg plane is perpendicular to $\GG$, we split $\qq$ into the component $q_\parallel$ perpendicular to the plane (along $\GG$) and the in-plane component $\qq_\perp$.

Step 1: Calculate the difference of the two free-electron energies.

$$ \epsilon^0_\kk-\epsilon^0_{\kk-\GG} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(k^2-\abs{\kk-\GG}^2\right) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(2\kk\cdot\GG-G^2\right) = \frac{\hbar^2}{m}\left(\kk\cdot\GG-\frac{G^2}{2}\right) $$

Here the Bragg plane is defined by $\kk_0\cdot\GG=G^2/2$ (the perpendicular bisecting plane of $\GG$). Hence, substituting $\kk=\kk_0+\qq$, we get $\kk\cdot\GG-G^2/2=\qq\cdot\GG = q_\parallel G$. Therefore

$$ \epsilon^0_\kk-\epsilon^0_{\kk-\GG} = \frac{\hbar^2 G}{m}\,q_\parallel $$

The difference is proportional only to $q_\parallel$ and does not depend on the in-plane displacement $\qq_\perp$. This is the key.

Step 2: Calculate the sum.

$$ \frac{1}{2}\left(\epsilon^0_\kk+\epsilon^0_{\kk-\GG}\right) =\frac{\hbar^2}{4m}\left(k^2+\abs{\kk-\GG}^2\right) =\frac{\hbar^2}{4m}\left(2k^2-2\kk\cdot\GG+G^2\right) =\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(k^2-\kk\cdot\GG+\frac{G^2}{2}\right) $$

We substitute $\kk=\kk_0+\qq$, $\kk\cdot\GG = G^2/2+q_\parallel G$, and $k^2=k_0^2+2\kk_0\cdot\qq+q^2$. Since $\kk_0\cdot\qq = \kk_0\cdot\qq_\perp + (\kk_0\cdot\hat\GG)q_\parallel = \kk_0\cdot\qq_\perp + \frac{G}{2}q_\parallel$,

$$ \frac{1}{2}\left(\epsilon^0_\kk+\epsilon^0_{\kk-\GG}\right) =\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(k_0^2+2\kk_0\cdot\qq_\perp + Gq_\parallel + q^2 - \frac{G^2}{2}-q_\parallel G+\frac{G^2}{2}\right) $$ $$ =\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(k_0^2+2\kk_0\cdot\qq_\perp+q^2\right) $$

The term in $Gq_\parallel$ cancels neatly. Since $q^2=q_\parallel^2+q_\perp^2$, the variable $q_\parallel$ enters only at second order.

Step 3: Differentiate with respect to $q_\parallel$. Collecting the above, we have

$$ \epsilon_\pm = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(k_0^2+2\kk_0\cdot\qq_\perp+q_\perp^2+q_\parallel^2\right) \pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\hbar^2G}{2m}q_\parallel\right)^2+\abs{U_\GG}^2} $$ $$ \frac{\partial\epsilon_\pm}{\partial q_\parallel} =\frac{\hbar^2}{m}q_\parallel \pm \frac{\left(\hbar^2G/2m\right)^2q_\parallel}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{\hbar^2G}{2m}q_\parallel\right)^2+\abs{U_\GG}^2}} $$

Both terms are proportional to $q_\parallel$, so at $q_\parallel=0$, that is, at every point on the Bragg plane,

$$ \left.\frac{\partial\epsilon_\pm}{\partial q_\parallel}\right|_{q_\parallel=0}=0 $$

Step 4: Read off the conclusion. The velocity of the electron is $\vv=\frac{1}{\hbar}\nabla_\kk\epsilon$, so this result means that “on the Bragg plane the component of the velocity normal to the plane is zero.” That is, $\nabla_\kk\epsilon$ points within the Bragg plane.

However, a constant-energy surface (the Fermi surface) is perpendicular to $\nabla_\kk\epsilon$. If $\nabla_\kk\epsilon$ points within the Bragg plane, the Fermi surface contains the direction of the normal to the Bragg plane. In other words, the Fermi surface meets the Bragg plane (the zone boundary) at right angles. ∎

Note that this conclusion holds formally even in the limit $\abs{U_\GG}\to0$, but then the gap closes and the Fermi surface simply passes smoothly through (the free-electron sphere). It is the finiteness of $U_\GG$ that produces the “rise” at the boundary.

Caution: Limitations of the Harrison construction

The Harrison construction is only a starting point when the periodic potential is weak. It agrees surprisingly well for simple metals such as $\mathrm{Na}$, $\mathrm{K}$, $\mathrm{Al}$, and $\mathrm{Pb}$, but it cannot be relied on in the following cases.

  • Transition metals: Because the $d$ bands cross $\epsilon_F$, the free-electron picture itself does not hold. The tight-binding approach (Section 18.4) is more appropriate.
  • Noble metals ($\mathrm{Cu}$, $\mathrm{Ag}$, $\mathrm{Au}$): The $4s$ band is free-electron-like, but hybridization with the filled $3d$ band distorts the region near the $L$ point greatly. The free-electron sphere does not reach the $L$ point, yet the real Fermi surface does reach it and forms a “neck.”
  • Small pockets: The tiny pockets predicted by the Harrison construction have often disappeared in reality (for example, the pocket in the fourth zone of $\mathrm{Al}$). The presence or absence of a pocket is sensitive to the magnitude of $U_\GG$.

18.3 Orbits in a Magnetic Field — Electron Orbits, Hole Orbits, and Open Orbits

18.3.1 Deriving two conservation laws from the semiclassical equation of motion

In Chapter 17 we derived the semiclassical equation of motion of a Bloch electron,

$$ \begin{equation} \hbar\frac{\dd\kk}{\dd t} = -e\left(\bm{E}+\frac{1}{c}\vv\times\BB\right), \qquad \vv(\kk)=\frac{1}{\hbar}\nabla_\kk\,\epsilon(\kk) \label{eq:18-semiclassical} \end{equation} $$

(where $e>0$ is the elementary charge and the electron has charge $-e$; Gaussian units). Now we apply only a static magnetic field $\BB$ and set the electric field to $\bm{E}=0$.

Derivation: In a magnetic field, $\kk$ circulates along “the intersection of a constant-energy surface and a plane perpendicular to the magnetic field”

The equation of motion is

$$ \hbar\dot\kk = -\frac{e}{c}\,\vv\times\BB $$

Conservation law 1: the component of $\kk$ along $\BB$. Take the scalar product of both sides with $\BB$.

$$ \hbar\,\dot\kk\cdot\BB = -\frac{e}{c}(\vv\times\BB)\cdot\BB = 0 $$

The scalar triple product $(\vv\times\BB)\cdot\BB$ contains the same vector twice, so it vanishes. Therefore

$$ \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(\kk\cdot\hat{B}\right)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k_{\parallel}\equiv \kk\cdot\hat{B} = \text{constant} $$

$\kk$ stays on a plane perpendicular to the magnetic field.

Conservation law 2: the energy. Take the scalar product of both sides with $\vv$.

$$ \hbar\,\dot\kk\cdot\vv = -\frac{e}{c}(\vv\times\BB)\cdot\vv = 0 $$

Again the triple product vanishes. Since $\vv=\frac{1}{\hbar}\nabla_\kk\epsilon$, we have

$$ \frac{\dd\epsilon}{\dd t}=\nabla_\kk\epsilon\cdot\dot\kk = \hbar\,\vv\cdot\dot\kk = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \epsilon(\kk)=\text{constant} $$

$\kk$ stays on a constant-energy surface. (This is the $\kk$-space version of the fact from classical mechanics that a magnetic field does no work.)

Conclusion. Combining the two conservation laws, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \kk(t)\ \in\ \Bigl\{\text{constant-energy surface } \epsilon(\kk)=\epsilon\Bigr\}\cap\Bigl\{\text{plane } \kk\cdot\hat B = k_\parallel\Bigr\} \label{eq:18-orbit} \end{equation} $$

That is, $\kk$ circulates along “the outline of the cross section obtained by cutting a constant-energy surface with a plane perpendicular to the magnetic field”. This outline is called an orbit. For electrons on the Fermi surface we take $\epsilon=\epsilon_F$, so the orbit is the outline of a cross section of the Fermi surface.

The sense of rotation is determined by $\eqref{eq:18-semiclassical}$. Since $\vv$ points along the outward normal of the constant-energy surface (the direction in which the energy increases), $\dot\kk\propto-\vv\times\BB$ points along the tangent to the outline.

18.3.2 Three kinds of orbits

Definition: Electron orbits, hole orbits, and open orbits

  • Electron orbit: a closed orbit whose inside is occupied. The outline encloses electrons.
  • Hole orbit: a closed orbit whose outside is occupied. The outline encloses empty states (holes). The sense of rotation is opposite to that of an electron orbit.
  • Open orbit: an orbit that, viewed in the repeated-zone scheme, does not close but runs through $\kk$ space indefinitely. It appears when the Fermi surface passes through the zone boundary and is connected.

Whether an orbit is open cannot be decided in the reduced-zone scheme. One must always look in the repeated-zone scheme and check whether the orbit connects to the neighboring cell.

Why do hole orbits rotate in the opposite direction?

The sense of rotation is determined by $\dot\kk = -\frac{e}{\hbar c}\vv\times\BB$. The vector $\vv$ points “in the direction in which the energy increases,” that is, from the occupied region toward the empty region.

For an electron orbit the occupied region lies inside the outline, so $\vv$ is the outward normal of the outline. For a hole orbit the occupied region lies outside, so $\vv$ is the inward normal of the outline. When the direction of $\vv$ is reversed, $\vv\times\BB$ is reversed too, and so is the sense of rotation.

Experimentally, this shows up as the sign of the Hall coefficient. In a metal dominated by hole orbits, $R_H>0$. Positive Hall coefficients are indeed observed in $\mathrm{Al}$, $\mathrm{Be}$, $\mathrm{Zn}$, $\mathrm{Cd}$, and others. The long-standing puzzle that some metals have a positive measured Hall coefficient even though in Chapter 15 we derived $R_H=-1/nec$ (negative) from the Drude model is resolved in this way.

18.3.3 The period of an orbit and the cyclotron effective mass

Derivation: $m_c=\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2\pi}\dfrac{\partial S}{\partial\epsilon}$ (cyclotron effective mass)

We compute the time $T$ required to go once around the orbit.

Step 1: Find $\abs{\dot\kk}$. Take the magnitude of the equation of motion. Writing the component of $\vv$ perpendicular to $\BB$ as $\vv_\perp$, we have $\abs{\vv\times\BB}=v_\perp B$, so

$$ \abs{\dot\kk} = \frac{eB}{\hbar c}v_\perp = \frac{eB}{\hbar c}\cdot\frac{1}{\hbar}\abs{\nabla_\kk\epsilon}_\perp $$

Here $\abs{\nabla_\kk\epsilon}_\perp$ is the magnitude of the gradient within the plane in which the orbit lies.

Step 2: Write the period as a line integral. Let $\dd k$ be the line element along the orbit. Then $\dd t=\dd k/\abs{\dot\kk}$, so

$$ T=\oint\dd t = \oint\frac{\dd k}{\abs{\dot\kk}} = \frac{\hbar^2c}{eB}\oint\frac{\dd k}{\abs{\nabla_\kk\epsilon}_\perp} $$

Step 3: Read off the geometric meaning of this line integral. Now draw the orbit of energy $\epsilon$ and the orbit of energy $\epsilon+\Delta\epsilon$ in the same plane. The width of the gap between the two outlines, measured as the distance $\Delta k_\perp$ in the direction perpendicular to the outline, satisfies

$$ \Delta\epsilon = \abs{\nabla_\kk\epsilon}_\perp\,\Delta k_\perp \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta k_\perp = \frac{\Delta\epsilon}{\abs{\nabla_\kk\epsilon}_\perp} $$

Therefore the area between the two outlines is

$$ \Delta S = \oint \Delta k_\perp\,\dd k = \Delta\epsilon\oint\frac{\dd k}{\abs{\nabla_\kk\epsilon}_\perp} $$

Here $S(\epsilon,k_\parallel)$ is the area in $\kk$ space enclosed by the orbit. Hence

$$ \oint\frac{\dd k}{\abs{\nabla_\kk\epsilon}_\perp} = \frac{\partial S}{\partial\epsilon} $$

Step 4: Put it together. Substituting into Step 2, we obtain

$$ T = \frac{\hbar^2 c}{eB}\frac{\partial S}{\partial\epsilon} $$

If we define the effective mass so that the angular frequency $\omega_c=2\pi/T$ can be written in the same form as the cyclotron frequency of a free electron, $\omega_c=eB/m^*c$, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \omega_c=\frac{eB}{m_c c},\qquad m_c \equiv \frac{\hbar^2}{2\pi}\frac{\partial S}{\partial\epsilon} \label{eq:18-mc} \end{equation} $$

$m_c$ is called the cyclotron effective mass. It is a quantity averaged over the whole orbit, and it is different from the band effective mass $m^*=\hbar^2/(\partial^2\epsilon/\partial k^2)$, which is defined at a single point of $\kk$.

Example 18.3: Checking that $m_c=m$ for free electrons

Let $\epsilon=\hbar^2k^2/2m$ and $\BB\parallel\hat z$. Fixing $k_z$, the cross-sectional area is obtained from

$$ k_x^2+k_y^2 = \frac{2m\epsilon}{\hbar^2}-k_z^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S(\epsilon,k_z)=\pi\left(\frac{2m\epsilon}{\hbar^2}-k_z^2\right) $$

Differentiating with respect to $\epsilon$ gives

$$ \frac{\partial S}{\partial\epsilon}=\pi\cdot\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}=\frac{2\pi m}{\hbar^2} $$

Substituting into $\eqref{eq:18-mc}$,

$$ m_c=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{2\pi m}{\hbar^2}=m \qquad\checkmark $$

It does not depend on $k_z$ either. For free electrons, the cyclotron mass is $m$ for every cross section. ∎

Example 18.4: Numerical value: what are $\omega_c$ and $\hbar\omega_c$ for $B=10\,\mathrm{T}$?

Taking $m_c=m$ (calculated in SI units),

$$ \omega_c=\frac{eB}{m}=\frac{1.602\times10^{-19}\times10}{9.109\times10^{-31}}=1.759\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} $$ $$ \hbar\omega_c=1.055\times10^{-34}\times1.759\times10^{12}=1.855\times10^{-22}\,\mathrm{J}=1.16\ \mathrm{meV} $$ $$ \frac{\hbar\omega_c}{k_B}=\frac{1.855\times10^{-22}}{1.381\times10^{-23}}=13.4\ \mathrm{K} $$

The period is $T=2\pi/\omega_c=3.57\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{s}=3.6\,\mathrm{ps}$.

This determines the experimental conditions of the de Haas–van Alphen effect. For the quantization effects not to be blurred by thermal motion we need $\hbar\omega_c>k_BT$, that is, $T\lesssim 4\,\mathrm{K}$ even at $10\,\mathrm{T}$. Moreover, for an electron not to be scattered before it completes one revolution we need $\omega_c\tau>1$, that is, $\tau>0.57\,\mathrm{ps}$. At room temperature $\tau\approx0.027\,\mathrm{ps}$ for $\mathrm{Cu}$, which falls far short, but in high-purity $\mathrm{Cu}$ at $4\,\mathrm{K}$ it reaches as much as $\tau\approx 2\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}$, which is more than sufficient. Low temperature, strong magnetic field, and high purity—these are the three conditions for measuring a Fermi surface. ∎

18.3.4 The real-space orbit is the $k$-space orbit rotated by $90^\circ$

Derivation: The relation between $\rr_\perp$ and $\kk$

Writing $\vv=\dot\rr$ in the equation of motion $\hbar\dot\kk=-\frac{e}{c}\vv\times\BB$, we have

$$ \hbar\frac{\dd\kk}{\dd t}=-\frac{e}{c}\frac{\dd\rr}{\dd t}\times\BB $$

Since $\BB$ is constant, we can integrate directly with respect to time.

$$ \hbar(\kk-\kk_0) = -\frac{e}{c}(\rr-\rr_0)\times\BB $$

We want to solve this for $\rr$. Take the cross product of both sides with $\hat B$ from the left. Using the BAC–CAB formula $\hat B\times(\bm{X}\times\BB)=\bm{X}(\hat B\cdot\BB)-\BB(\hat B\cdot\bm{X})$ on the right-hand side, with $\bm X = \rr-\rr_0$,

$$ \hat B\times\left[(\rr-\rr_0)\times\BB\right] = B(\rr-\rr_0) - B\,\hat B\left[\hat B\cdot(\rr-\rr_0)\right] = B\,(\rr-\rr_0)_\perp $$

(The last equality simply says that a vector minus its component along $\hat B$ is its perpendicular component.) Therefore

$$ \hbar\,\hat B\times(\kk-\kk_0) = -\frac{eB}{c}(\rr-\rr_0)_\perp $$
$$ \begin{equation} (\rr-\rr_0)_\perp = -\frac{\hbar c}{eB}\,\hat B\times(\kk-\kk_0) \label{eq:18-realspace} \end{equation} $$

Physical Meaning: What $\eqref{eq:18-realspace}$ says

The operation $\hat B\times$ rotates a vector by $90^\circ$ within the plane perpendicular to $\BB$. Therefore $\eqref{eq:18-realspace}$ says that

the real-space orbit is a similar figure obtained by rotating the $\kk$-space orbit by $90^\circ$ and scaling its lengths by a factor of $\hbar c/eB$.

(The overall sign changes with the convention for the sign of the charge, so it only means that the sense of rotation is reversed. The point is that the real-space orbit is “a similar figure rotated by $90^\circ$.”)

Let us estimate the scale factor. For $B=10\,\mathrm{T}$ and $\Delta k=k_F=1.36\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ ($\mathrm{Cu}$), the scale factor in SI units is $\hbar/eB$, so

$$ \Delta r=\frac{\hbar}{eB}\Delta k = \frac{1.055\times10^{-34}}{1.602\times10^{-19}\times10}\times1.36\times10^{10} = 6.59\times10^{-17}\times1.36\times10^{10}=8.96\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} $$

This is about $0.9\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, several thousand times the interatomic distance. An electron that goes once around the Fermi surface in $\kk$ space traces a circle of micrometer scale in real space. This is precisely why a dirty sample scatters the electron before it completes one revolution. Conversely, when this orbit radius becomes comparable to the thickness of the sample, a size effect (the Gantmakher effect) appears, and this is also used for measuring Fermi surfaces.

18.3.5 Open orbits and magnetoresistance

Physical Meaning: Why magnetoresistance does not saturate when open orbits exist

Consider the high-field limit ($\omega_c\tau\gg1$).

Closed orbits. An electron goes around its orbit many times before it is scattered. By Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-realspace}$ the real-space orbit is also closed, so the electron merely circles in the same place and makes no net progress in the direction perpendicular to the magnetic field. The only progress it makes is the slow drift of the orbit center caused by the electric field. As a result, the conductivity perpendicular to the magnetic field becomes small, $\sigma_{xx}\sim\sigma_0/(\omega_c\tau)^2$. At the same time, however, the Hall conductivity $\sigma_{xy}\sim\sigma_0/(\omega_c\tau)$ remains, so inverting the conductivity tensor to obtain the resistivity gives

$$ \rho_{xx}=\frac{\sigma_{xx}}{\sigma_{xx}^2+\sigma_{xy}^2}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{constant} $$

The magnetoresistance saturates. Indeed, the transverse magnetoresistance of $\mathrm{Na}$ and $\mathrm{K}$ (closed spherical Fermi surfaces) hardly increases.

Compensated metals. When the number of electrons equals the number of holes (Example 18.2), the Hall conductivities of the electrons and the holes cancel and $\sigma_{xy}\to0$. Then $\sigma_{xy}$ drops out of the denominator of the expression above, and $\rho_{xx}\sim1/\sigma_{xx}\propto(\omega_c\tau)^2\propto B^2$. It does not saturate but keeps increasing as $B^2$. This is the origin of the giant magnetoresistance of $\mathrm{Bi}$, $\mathrm{W}$, and $\mathrm{Mg}$.

Open orbits. Suppose $\kk$ does not close but keeps running in one direction in $\kk$ space (say, the $k_x$ direction). By Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-realspace}$, the electron in real space keeps advancing forever in the $y$ direction. Conduction in this direction is maintained even in a strong magnetic field, so $\sigma_{yy}$ remains finite. In the perpendicular $x$ direction, however, conduction dies away. The conductivity tensor becomes extremely anisotropic, and as a result the magnetoresistance in the direction perpendicular to the real-space direction of the open orbit keeps increasing as $B^2$.

What is decisive is that whether an open orbit exists depends sensitively on the direction of the magnetic field. Tilting the field only slightly changes the cross section of the Fermi surface from “connected” to “disconnected.” Therefore, when the magnetoresistance is measured as a function of the field direction, a sharp peak appears only at the particular directions in which open orbits arise.

$\mathrm{Cu}$ is the textbook example. The Fermi surface of $\mathrm{Cu}$ touches the zone boundary at the $L$ points on the $\{111\}$ planes and forms “necks,” and in the repeated-zone scheme it connects to the neighboring cell through the necks. When the magnetic field is directed along a particular orientation such as $[100]$, an open orbit forms along the chain of necks. In the classic experiment of Klauder and Kunzler, when the magnetoresistance was measured while the field direction was rotated, a sharp petal-shaped anisotropy pattern appeared, corresponding to the directions of the open orbits. This is one of the most direct pieces of evidence that the Fermi surface of $\mathrm{Cu}$ has necks.

18.4 Complete Derivation of the Tight-Binding Approximation

Up to this point we have worked from the standpoint of “start from free electrons and introduce a weak potential as a perturbation” (the NFE approximation, Chapter 16). In this section we take the exact opposite starting point. We start from the orbitals of isolated atoms and watch how the levels broaden into bands as the atoms are brought closer together. This is the tight-binding approximation (TB).

These two approaches are merely climbing the same mountain from opposite sides. NFE is suited to $s$-$p$ electrons, and TB to $d$-$f$ electrons. In transition metals, both are needed at the same time.

18.4.1 Constructing the Bloch sum

Derivation: The TB wave function satisfies Bloch's theorem

Let $\phi(\rr)$ be the orbital of an isolated atom. The atoms are arranged at the lattice points $\RR_m$ ($m=1,\dots,N$). Consider the following linear combination.

$$ \begin{equation} \psi_\kk(\rr)=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{m}\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\RR_m}\,\phi(\rr-\RR_m) \label{eq:18-blochsum} \end{equation} $$

(Continued) Checking Bloch's theorem and normalization

Checking Bloch's theorem. Let us shift by a lattice translation $\TT$ (which is also a lattice vector).

$$ \psi_\kk(\rr+\TT)=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_m \ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\RR_m}\phi(\rr+\TT-\RR_m) $$

Here we relabel the summation variable as $\RR_{m'}=\RR_m-\TT$. If $\RR_m$ runs over all lattice points, then $\RR_{m'}$ also runs over all lattice points (the lattice is mapped onto itself by a translation). Since $\RR_m=\RR_{m'}+\TT$,

$$ \psi_\kk(\rr+\TT)=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{m'} \ee^{\ii\kk\cdot(\RR_{m'}+\TT)}\phi(\rr-\RR_{m'}) =\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\TT}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{m'}\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\RR_{m'}}\phi(\rr-\RR_{m'}) $$ $$ =\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\TT}\,\psi_\kk(\rr)\qquad\checkmark $$

This is precisely Bloch's theorem (Chapter 11). By the single simple operation of attaching the phase factor $\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\RR_m}$ and summing, we obtain a function belonging to the irreducible representation $\kk$ of the translation group. In the language of the projection operators of Chapter 9, Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-blochsum}$ is nothing but the projection operator onto the irreducible representation $\kk$ of the translation group acting on $\phi(\rr)$.

Normalization. We write the overlap integral between orbitals on different lattice points as

$$ S(\bm\rho)\equiv\int \phi^{*}(\rr)\,\phi(\rr-\bm\rho)\,\dd^3r $$

Here $S(0)=1$. For now we first approximate $S(\bm\rho\neq0)=0$ (we will remove this approximation later). Then

$$ \langle \psi_\kk|\psi_\kk\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m,m'}\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot(\RR_m-\RR_{m'})}S(\RR_m-\RR_{m'}) =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m}1=1\qquad\checkmark $$

Supplement: $N$ atomic orbitals give $N$ $\kk$ states

The values of $\kk$ allowed in $\eqref{eq:18-blochsum}$ are, by the periodic boundary conditions, the $N$ values inside the first Brillouin zone (Chapter 11). That is, we have merely recombined the $N$ atomic orbitals $\{\phi(\rr-\RR_m)\}$ linearly to make $N$ Bloch states $\{\psi_\kk\}$. The number of states does not change. Including spin, there are $2N$ states—in agreement with the “capacity of one band” counted in Section 18.2.

This is exactly what chemists call the molecular-orbital method (LCAO). In fact, another name for the tight-binding approximation is the LCAO method (linear combination of atomic orbitals). For $N=2$ we get the two levels of the bonding and antibonding orbitals, and for $N=\infty$ we get a band.

18.4.2 Calculating the expectation value of the energy

Derivation: $\epsilon(\kk)=-\alpha-\gamma\sum_{\rm nn}\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho}$

Let the Hamiltonian be

$$ \Ham = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + U(\rr),\qquad U(\rr)=\sum_n U_{\rm at}(\rr-\RR_n) $$

(the crystal potential is written as a superposition of atomic potentials). Focusing on the $m$th atom, we split it as

$$ \Ham = \underbrace{\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+U_{\rm at}(\rr-\RR_m)\right]}_{\Ham_{\rm at}^{(m)}} +\underbrace{\sum_{n\neq m}U_{\rm at}(\rr-\RR_n)}_{\Delta U_m} $$

We have $\Ham_{\rm at}^{(m)}\phi(\rr-\RR_m)=\epsilon_{\rm at}\,\phi(\rr-\RR_m)$. In what follows we take the origin of energy at $\epsilon_{\rm at}=0$ (we can restore it at the end).

Step 1: Write the expectation value as a double sum.

$$ \epsilon(\kk)=\bra{\psi_\kk}\Ham\ket{\psi_\kk} =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m,m'}\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot(\RR_m-\RR_{m'})} \int \phi^{*}(\rr-\RR_{m'})\,\Ham\,\phi(\rr-\RR_m)\,\dd^3r $$

Step 2: Show that the integral depends only on the difference. We shift the integration variable by $\rr\to\rr+\RR_{m'}$. Since $\Ham$ is invariant under lattice translations ($U(\rr+\RR_{m'})=U(\rr)$),

$$ \int \phi^{*}(\rr-\RR_{m'})\Ham\,\phi(\rr-\RR_m)\dd^3r =\int \phi^{*}(\rr)\,\Ham\,\phi\!\left(\rr-(\RR_m-\RR_{m'})\right)\dd^3r \equiv t(\bm\rho),\quad \bm\rho\equiv\RR_m-\RR_{m'} $$

This $t(\bm\rho)$ is called the hopping integral (also called the transfer integral or the resonance integral).

Step 3: Reduce the double sum to a single sum. If we fix $m'$ and let $m$ run, $\bm\rho$ runs over all lattice vectors. The sum over $m'$ merely adds $N$ identical values, so $(1/N)\sum_{m'}=1$. Hence

$$ \epsilon(\kk)=\sum_{\bm\rho}\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho}\,t(\bm\rho) $$

Step 4: Evaluate $t(\bm\rho)$.

The $\bm\rho=0$ term (on-site term):

$$ t(0)=\int\phi^{*}(\rr)\left[\Ham_{\rm at}+\Delta U\right]\phi(\rr)\dd^3r =\underbrace{\epsilon_{\rm at}}_{=0}+\int\abs{\phi(\rr)}^2\Delta U(\rr)\,\dd^3r \equiv -\alpha $$

$\Delta U$ is the potential created by the other atoms, and it is negative because it is the attraction produced by the atomic nuclei. Hence $\alpha>0$. This is the effect that “the level is lowered thanks to the presence of surrounding atoms” (the crystal-field shift).

The $\bm\rho\neq0$ terms:

$$ t(\bm\rho)=\underbrace{\epsilon_{\rm at}S(\bm\rho)}_{=0}+\int\phi^{*}(\rr)\,\Delta U(\rr)\,\phi(\rr-\bm\rho)\,\dd^3r $$

The orbitals $\phi$ overlap only in the intermediate region between $\RR=0$ and $\bm\rho$, where $\Delta U<0$ and $\phi^*\phi>0$ (for an $s$ orbital), so this integral is negative. Keeping only the nearest neighbors (nn), we write its value as

$$ t(\bm\rho_{\rm nn})\equiv-\gamma,\qquad \gamma>0 $$

We drop the second-nearest and farther neighbors because the overlap of $\phi$ becomes exponentially small.

Step 5: Conclusion.

$$ \begin{equation} \epsilon(\kk)=-\alpha-\gamma\sum_{\bm\rho\in{\rm nn}}\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho} =-\alpha-\gamma\sum_{\bm\rho\in{\rm nn}}\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho} \label{eq:18-tb} \end{equation} $$

Supplement: Why either sign, $\ee^{+\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho}$ or $\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho}$, will do

The set of nearest-neighbor vectors is always inversion symmetric (if $\bm\rho$ is a nearest-neighbor vector, so is $-\bm\rho$). Therefore, when we take the sum,

$$ \sum_{\rm nn}\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho}=\sum_{\rm nn}\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho}=\sum_{\rm nn}\cos(\kk\cdot\bm\rho) $$

and the imaginary parts cancel, leaving a real number. It is only natural that the energy is real, but here we can confirm it in the equation itself.

18.4.3 The simple cubic lattice

Derivation: The TB band, bandwidth, and effective mass for simple cubic

Nearest-neighbor vectors. For a simple cubic (sc) lattice with lattice constant $a$, there are six nearest neighbors:

$$ \bm\rho = (\pm a,0,0),\ (0,\pm a,0),\ (0,0,\pm a) $$

Calculating the sum.

$$ \sum_{\rm nn}\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho} =\ee^{-\ii k_xa}+\ee^{+\ii k_xa}+\ee^{-\ii k_ya}+\ee^{+\ii k_ya}+\ee^{-\ii k_za}+\ee^{+\ii k_za} $$

Using Euler's formula $\ee^{\ii\theta}+\ee^{-\ii\theta}=2\cos\theta$ three times, we obtain

$$ =2\left(\cos k_xa+\cos k_ya+\cos k_za\right) $$

Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \epsilon_{\rm sc}(\kk)=-\alpha-2\gamma\left(\cos k_xa+\cos k_ya+\cos k_za\right) \label{eq:18-sc} \end{equation} $$

(Continued) Bandwidth and effective mass

Bandwidth. The sum of the $\cos$ terms varies from $-3$ to $+3$.

  • Minimum: at $\kk=\Gamma=(0,0,0)$ the sum of the $\cos$ terms $=3$ $\Rightarrow$ $\epsilon_{\min}=-\alpha-6\gamma$
  • Maximum: at $\kk=R=\frac{\pi}{a}(1,1,1)$ the sum of the $\cos$ terms $=-3$ $\Rightarrow$ $\epsilon_{\max}=-\alpha+6\gamma$
$$ W = \epsilon_{\max}-\epsilon_{\min} = 12\gamma = 2z\gamma\quad (z=6:\text{number of nearest neighbors}) $$

The bandwidth is proportional to the hopping integral. This is the most important message of tight binding.

Effective mass at the band bottom. We make a Taylor expansion for $\kk\to0$. Using $\cos x = 1-\frac{x^2}{2}+O(x^4)$, we obtain

$$ \cos k_xa+\cos k_ya+\cos k_za \simeq 3-\frac{a^2}{2}\left(k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2\right)=3-\frac{a^2k^2}{2} $$

Substituting this into $\eqref{eq:18-sc}$ gives

$$ \epsilon(\kk)\simeq-\alpha-2\gamma\left(3-\frac{a^2k^2}{2}\right)=-\alpha-6\gamma+\gamma a^2k^2 $$

We compare this with the free-electron form $\epsilon=\epsilon_{\min}+\hbar^2k^2/2m^{*}$. Equating the coefficients of $k^2$,

$$ \frac{\hbar^2}{2m^{*}}=\gamma a^2 $$
$$ \begin{equation} m^{*}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\gamma a^2} \label{eq:18-mstar} \end{equation} $$

Physical Meaning: How to read $m^{*}=\hbar^2/2\gamma a^2$

This equation says that “the harder it is to hop, the heavier the electron.” When $\gamma$ is small (it is hard to hop to the neighboring atom), $m^{*}$ is large and the electron moves with difficulty. In the limit $\gamma\to0$ (the atoms infinitely far apart), $m^{*}\to\infty$; that is, the electron is completely localized on its atom. This is an entirely natural conclusion.

Let us insert numbers. For $a=3\,\text{Å}=3\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}$ and $\gamma=1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$,

$$ m^{*}=\frac{(1.055\times10^{-34})^2}{2\times1.602\times10^{-19}\times(3\times10^{-10})^2} =\frac{1.113\times10^{-68}}{2.884\times10^{-38}}=3.86\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg} $$ $$ \frac{m^{*}}{m}=\frac{3.86\times10^{-31}}{9.109\times10^{-31}}=0.42 $$

For $\gamma\sim1\,\mathrm{eV}$, $m^*$ is comparable to the free-electron mass. Conversely, for $\gamma=0.1\,\mathrm{eV}$ (a narrow $d$ band), $m^*/m=4.2$, and for $\gamma=0.01\,\mathrm{eV}$ (an $f$ band), $m^*/m=42$. This is the most naive understanding of why $m^*/m\sim100$–$1000$ in heavy-fermion systems.

Caution: The sign of the effective mass changes at the band edge

Let us expand $\eqref{eq:18-sc}$ at the top of the band (the $R$ point). Putting $\kk=\frac{\pi}{a}(1,1,1)+\qq$, we have $\cos(k_ia)=\cos(\pi+q_ia)=-\cos(q_ia)\simeq-1+\frac{q_i^2a^2}{2}$, so

$$ \epsilon\simeq-\alpha-2\gamma\left(-3+\frac{a^2q^2}{2}\right)=-\alpha+6\gamma-\gamma a^2q^2 $$

The coefficient of $q^2$ is negative. That is, $m^{*}=-\hbar^2/2\gamma a^2<0$. An electron at the top of the band has a negative effective mass and behaves as a hole (Chapter 17). This is a general property of band structure that holds in both TB and NFE.

18.4.4 bcc and fcc

Derivation: The TB band for body-centered cubic (bcc)

Nearest-neighbor vectors. Seen from an atom at a vertex of the cube, the nearest neighbors are the eight atoms at the body centers; letting $a$ be the edge of the cubic unit cell,

$$ \bm\rho = \frac{a}{2}\left(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1\right)\qquad(\text{signs chosen independently, total }2^3=8\text{ vectors}) $$

The nearest-neighbor distance is $\sqrt3\,a/2 = 0.866a$.

Calculating the sum. For the three signs $s_1,s_2,s_3=\pm1$,

$$ \sum_{\rm nn}\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho} =\sum_{s_1=\pm1}\sum_{s_2=\pm1}\sum_{s_3=\pm1} \exp\left[-\ii\frac{a}{2}\left(s_1k_x+s_2k_y+s_3k_z\right)\right] $$

Since the exponent is a sum of three independent terms, the exponential factorizes into a product.

$$ =\left(\sum_{s_1=\pm1}\ee^{-\ii s_1k_xa/2}\right) \left(\sum_{s_2=\pm1}\ee^{-\ii s_2k_ya/2}\right) \left(\sum_{s_3=\pm1}\ee^{-\ii s_3k_za/2}\right) $$

Since each factor is $\ee^{-\ii\theta}+\ee^{+\ii\theta}=2\cos\theta$,

$$ =2\cos\frac{k_xa}{2}\cdot2\cos\frac{k_ya}{2}\cdot2\cos\frac{k_za}{2} =8\cos\frac{k_xa}{2}\cos\frac{k_ya}{2}\cos\frac{k_za}{2} $$

Therefore

$$ \epsilon_{\rm bcc}(\kk)=-\alpha-8\gamma\,\cos\frac{k_xa}{2}\cos\frac{k_ya}{2}\cos\frac{k_za}{2} $$

Bandwidth. The product of the three $\cos$ factors varies from $-1$ to $+1$ (it is $+1$ at the $\Gamma$ point, and at $H=\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,0,0)$ it is $\cos\pi\cdot1\cdot1=-1$). Hence

$$ W = 16\gamma = 2z\gamma\quad(z=8) $$

Effective mass. For $\kk\to0$, $\cos(k_ia/2)\simeq1-k_i^2a^2/8$, so the product is

$$ \left(1-\frac{k_x^2a^2}{8}\right)\left(1-\frac{k_y^2a^2}{8}\right)\left(1-\frac{k_z^2a^2}{8}\right) \simeq 1-\frac{a^2}{8}\left(k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2\right)=1-\frac{a^2k^2}{8} $$

(Since we keep terms only up to second order, the cross terms are dropped.) Hence

$$ \epsilon\simeq-\alpha-8\gamma+\gamma a^2k^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m^{*}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\gamma a^2} $$

This has the same form as for simple cubic (except that $a$ is the edge of the cubic unit cell and $\gamma$ takes a different value). ∎

Derivation: The TB band for face-centered cubic (fcc)

Nearest-neighbor vectors. The fcc lattice has 12 nearest neighbors; letting $a$ be the edge of the cubic unit cell,

$$ \bm\rho=\frac{a}{2}(\pm1,\pm1,0),\quad \frac{a}{2}(0,\pm1,\pm1),\quad \frac{a}{2}(\pm1,0,\pm1) $$

Each of the three families has four vectors, for a total of 12. The nearest-neighbor distance is $a/\sqrt2=0.707a$.

Calculating the sum. First we add only the four vectors of the $(x,y)$ family.

$$ \sum_{s_1,s_2=\pm1}\exp\left[-\ii\frac{a}{2}(s_1k_x+s_2k_y)\right] =\left(2\cos\frac{k_xa}{2}\right)\left(2\cos\frac{k_ya}{2}\right) =4\cos\frac{k_xa}{2}\cos\frac{k_ya}{2} $$

We used the same “factorization into a product” as in the bcc case. The $(y,z)$ and $(z,x)$ families are handled in the same way, so adding all 12 vectors gives

$$ \sum_{\rm nn}\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho} =4\left(\cos\frac{k_xa}{2}\cos\frac{k_ya}{2} +\cos\frac{k_ya}{2}\cos\frac{k_za}{2} +\cos\frac{k_za}{2}\cos\frac{k_xa}{2}\right) $$
$$ \begin{equation} \epsilon_{\rm fcc}(\kk)=-\alpha-4\gamma\left( \cos\frac{k_xa}{2}\cos\frac{k_ya}{2} +\cos\frac{k_ya}{2}\cos\frac{k_za}{2} +\cos\frac{k_za}{2}\cos\frac{k_xa}{2}\right) \label{eq:18-fcc} \end{equation} $$

(Continued) The fcc bandwidth is not $2z\gamma$

Abbreviate $c_i\equiv\cos(k_ia/2)$ and examine the range of $f=c_xc_y+c_yc_z+c_zc_x$. Here $c_i\in[-1,1]$.

Maximum. At the $\Gamma$ point, $c_x=c_y=c_z=1$ and $f=3$. Hence $\epsilon_{\min}=-\alpha-12\gamma$.

Minimum. We use the identity

$$ f=\frac{1}{2}\left[(c_x+c_y+c_z)^2-(c_x^2+c_y^2+c_z^2)\right] $$

It is quicker to think directly than to make a rough estimate with the inequality $c_i^2\le1$. To make $f$ small, we want the first term $(c_x+c_y+c_z)^2$ to be as small as possible and $c_x^2+c_y^2+c_z^2$ to be as large as possible. When every $c_i$ is $\pm1$, $c_x^2+c_y^2+c_z^2$ takes its maximum value $3$, but then only $c_x+c_y+c_z=\pm1$ or $\pm3$ is possible (the sum of three $\pm1$'s is odd). The minimum occurs for $c_x+c_y+c_z=\pm1$:

$$ f_{\min}=\frac{1}{2}\left[1-3\right]=-1 $$

For example, at the $X$ point $\kk=\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,0,0)$ we have $c_x=\cos\pi=-1,\ c_y=c_z=1$, so $f=(-1)+(1)+(-1)=-1$ ✓. Hence $\epsilon_{\max}=-\alpha+4\gamma$.

Bandwidth.

$$ W_{\rm fcc}=\epsilon_{\max}-\epsilon_{\min}=(-\alpha+4\gamma)-(-\alpha-12\gamma)=16\gamma $$

However, $2z\gamma=2\times12\times\gamma=24\gamma$. The two do not agree.

Why? The relation $W=2z\gamma$ holds when the lattice is a bipartite graph, that is, when the lattice points can be divided into two sets such that every nearest-neighbor bond connects the two sets. The sc and bcc lattices are of this kind (for sc, the parity of $x+y+z$; for bcc, the corners and the body center). In that case, a suitable choice of $\kk$ aligns all the phases to $-1$, and the sum reaches $-z$. The fcc lattice, however, contains triangular loops, so it is not a bipartite graph, and the phases of all the bonds cannot be reversed simultaneously (frustration). As a result, the band is squashed at the top and becomes asymmetric, and $W<2z\gamma$.

Effective mass. Substituting $c_i\simeq1-k_i^2a^2/8$ for $\kk\to0$ gives

$$ c_xc_y\simeq1-\frac{a^2}{8}(k_x^2+k_y^2) $$

Adding the three terms, each $k_i^2$ appears twice, so

$$ f\simeq3-\frac{a^2}{8}\cdot2\left(k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2\right)=3-\frac{a^2k^2}{4} $$ $$ \epsilon\simeq-\alpha-4\gamma\left(3-\frac{a^2k^2}{4}\right)=-\alpha-12\gamma+\gamma a^2k^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m^{*}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\gamma a^2} $$

Again we obtain the same form. ∎

18.4.5 The one-dimensional chain and its density of states

With the simplest example, we carry the calculation of the relation between the band and the density of states through to the end. Consider a chain of atoms lined up in a row with spacing $a$. There are two nearest neighbors, $\bm\rho=\pm a$, so from Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-tb}$

$$ \begin{equation} \epsilon(k)=-\alpha-2\gamma\cos ka,\qquad -\frac{\pi}{a}<k\le\frac{\pi}{a} \label{eq:18-1d} \end{equation} $$

Derivation: Density of states of the one-dimensional tight-binding band

Step 1: Group velocity.

$$ v(k)=\frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{\dd\epsilon}{\dd k}=\frac{2\gamma a}{\hbar}\sin ka $$

At $k=0$ and $k=\pm\pi/a$, $v=0$. The group velocity vanishes at the zone boundary because the wave becomes a standing wave there (the same situation as for phonons in Chapter 13 and electrons in Chapter 16).

Step 2: Counting the states. If we impose periodic boundary conditions on a chain of length $L=Na$, the $k$ points are spaced by $2\pi/L$. The number of states with energy in $[\epsilon,\epsilon+\dd\epsilon]$ receives contributions from two places, $+k$ and $-k$, and is further doubled by spin, so

$$ \dd N = \underbrace{2}_{\rm spin}\times\underbrace{2}_{\pm k}\times\frac{L}{2\pi}\,\dd k=\frac{2L}{\pi}\dd k $$

Step 3: Converting $\dd k$ to $\dd\epsilon$.

$$ g(\epsilon)=\frac{\dd N}{\dd\epsilon}=\frac{2L}{\pi}\frac{\dd k}{\dd\epsilon} =\frac{2L}{\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{2\gamma a\sin ka} =\frac{L}{\pi\gamma a\,\sin ka} $$

Step 4: Expressing $\sin ka$ in terms of $\epsilon$. From Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-1d}$, $\cos ka=-\dfrac{\epsilon+\alpha}{2\gamma}$. Therefore

$$ \sin ka=\sqrt{1-\cos^2ka}=\sqrt{1-\frac{(\epsilon+\alpha)^2}{4\gamma^2}} =\frac{\sqrt{4\gamma^2-(\epsilon+\alpha)^2}}{2\gamma} $$

Step 5: Substituting.

$$ g(\epsilon)=\frac{L}{\pi\gamma a}\cdot\frac{2\gamma}{\sqrt{4\gamma^2-(\epsilon+\alpha)^2}} $$
$$ \begin{equation} g(\epsilon)=\frac{2L}{\pi a}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\gamma^2-(\epsilon+\alpha)^2}} =\frac{2N}{\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\gamma^2-(\epsilon+\alpha)^2}} \label{eq:18-1ddos} \end{equation} $$

Example 18.5: Checking the normalization of $\eqref{eq:18-1ddos}$

When integrated over the whole band, the result should be two states per atom (including spin). Verify this.

Solution. Substituting $u=\epsilon+\alpha$, the range of integration becomes $u\in[-2\gamma,2\gamma]$.

$$ \int g\,\dd\epsilon=\frac{2N}{\pi}\int_{-2\gamma}^{2\gamma}\frac{\dd u}{\sqrt{4\gamma^2-u^2}} $$

Using $\int\dd u/\sqrt{A^2-u^2}=\arcsin(u/A)$ (with $A=2\gamma$),

$$ =\frac{2N}{\pi}\left[\arcsin\frac{u}{2\gamma}\right]_{-2\gamma}^{2\gamma} =\frac{2N}{\pi}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right)=\frac{2N}{\pi}\cdot\pi=2N\qquad\checkmark $$

From $N$ atoms we obtain $2N$ states. This agrees exactly with the state counting for the TB model (Section 18.4.1). ∎

Physical Meaning: Divergence at the band edges (van Hove singularity)

Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-1ddos}$ diverges as $1/\sqrt{\cdot}$ for $\epsilon\to-\alpha\pm2\gamma$ (the top and bottom of the band). This is the same as the van Hove singularity that we met for phonons in Chapter 14, and the cause is the same. At the band edges the group velocity $v=\frac{1}{\hbar}\dd\epsilon/\dd k$ vanishes, so many $k$ states are packed into a narrow energy interval.

What is interesting is that the manner of divergence differs with the dimension.

  • One dimension: $g\propto(\epsilon-\epsilon_{\rm edge})^{-1/2}$—it diverges.
  • Two dimensions: at the band edges, $g\to$ a finite value (a step). However, at a saddle point (for a square lattice, the $X$ point $(\pi/a,0)$) a logarithmic divergence $g\propto\ln\dfrac{1}{\abs{\epsilon-\epsilon_X}}$ occurs. The energy of this saddle point coincides exactly with $\epsilon_F$ at half filling (Exercise 18.2). This is the singularity behind the “van Hove scenario” discussed for cuprate high-temperature superconductors.
  • Three dimensions: at the band edges, $g\propto\sqrt{\epsilon-\epsilon_{\rm edge}}$ (the same as for free electrons). At a saddle point, the derivative of $g$ diverges (a kink).

From the shape of the density of states, one can roughly tell the dimensionality of a band. This is a technique actually used to identify layered materials and one-dimensional-chain materials.

18.4.6 Why are the $d$ bands of transition metals narrow?

Why? The reason the $d$ bands are narrow, and its consequences

The reason can be stated in a word: the radial distribution of the $3d$ orbital is contracted close to the atomic nucleus.

A $3d$ electron has principal quantum number $n=3$ and azimuthal quantum number $\ell=2$. The centrifugal term $\ell(\ell+1)\hbar^2/2mr^2$ is a repulsive potential, so the larger $\ell$ is, the farther the electron is pushed away from the nucleus. One might therefore expect the $3d$ orbital to be extended, but in fact it is not. What matters is the number of radial nodes. The number of radial nodes is given by $n-\ell-1$, which for $3d$ is $3-2-1=0$; that is, it has no radial node. This is because there is no $d$ orbital inside the $3d$, so there is no need to be orthogonal to the core levels. Since there is no need to extend outward for the sake of orthogonality, the $3d$ orbital has no “reason to swell.” As a result, the radial maximum of the $3d$ orbital stays at only about $0.4\,\text{Å}$ for $\mathrm{Cu}$.

On the other hand, the nearest-neighbor distance in $\mathrm{Cu}$ is $a/\sqrt2=3.61/1.414=2.55\,\text{Å}$. This is more than six times the extent of the orbital. The hopping integral is determined by the overlap of the tails of the orbitals, and the tails decay as $\ee^{-\kappa r}$, so $\gamma$ decreases exponentially with distance.

In contrast, the $4s$ orbital has $n=4$ and $\ell=0$; it has three radial nodes and extends far outward (its maximum is around $1.5\,\text{Å}$). It overlaps sufficiently with the $4s$ orbital of the neighboring atom. That is why the $4s$-$4p$ band is wide, $10$–$20\,\mathrm{eV}$, while the $3d$ band is narrow, $3$–$5\,\mathrm{eV}$.

Consequence 1: The density of states is large. One band holds $2N$ states. Since the $d$ orbital is fivefold ($\ell=2$), $10N$ states are squeezed into a width $W_d\sim4\,\mathrm{eV}$. For an $s$ band, $2N$ states are spread over $15\,\mathrm{eV}$. The density of states per unit energy is about 20 times larger for $d$.

Consequence 2: The electronic specific heat is large. From $C_{el}=\gamma_{el}T$ and $\gamma_{el}=\frac{\pi^2}{3}k_B^2g(\epsilon_F)$ in Chapter 15, $\gamma_{el}$ is proportional to $g(\epsilon_F)$.

$\mathrm{Cu}$ and $\mathrm{Ni}$ are neighboring elements and differ by only one in the number of electrons, yet their $\gamma_{el}$ differ by a factor of 10. This is because in $\mathrm{Cu}$ the $3d$ band is full and $\epsilon_F$ lies in the wide $4s$ band, whereas in $\mathrm{Ni}$ $\epsilon_F$ lies in the narrow $3d$ band. Measuring $\gamma_{el}$ is measuring $g(\epsilon_F)$, and $g(\epsilon_F)$ was the area of the Fermi surface divided by the velocity (Section 18.1). Everything is connected.

Consequence 3: Magnetism appears. The Stoner criterion $I\,g(\epsilon_F)>1$ ($I$ is the strength of the exchange interaction, Chapter 20) is satisfied more easily the larger $g(\epsilon_F)$ is. It is no accident that the elemental metals showing ferromagnetism are limited to the $3d$ transition metals $\mathrm{Fe}$, $\mathrm{Co}$, and $\mathrm{Ni}$.

Consequence 4: Electron correlation matters. When the ratio $U/W$ of the cost $U$ of putting two electrons on the same site (the Hubbard $U$, $3$–$8\,\mathrm{eV}$ for $3d$) to the bandwidth $W$ exceeds 1, the electrons can no longer move and the system becomes a Mott insulator. $\mathrm{NiO}$, $\mathrm{CoO}$, and $\mathrm{La_2CuO_4}$ are examples. Band theory (that is, the framework of this chapter) predicts these to be metals, in disagreement with experiment. It is precisely because of this smallness of $W$ that DFT+$U$ and dynamical mean-field theory are needed when $3d$ oxides are treated by first-principles calculations.

Supplement: When the overlap integral is not neglected

In Section 18.4.1 we approximated $S(\bm\rho\neq0)=0$. If we drop this approximation, Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-tb}$ becomes

$$ \epsilon(\kk)=\frac{\displaystyle-\alpha-\gamma\sum_{\rm nn}\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho}} {\displaystyle 1+S\sum_{\rm nn}\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho}} \qquad\left(S\equiv S(\bm\rho_{\rm nn})\right) $$

A denominator appears because $\{\phi(\rr-\RR_m)\}$ is not an orthonormal basis, so the eigenvalue problem becomes a generalized eigenvalue problem $H\bm{c}=\epsilon\,S\bm{c}$ (the same situation as in the discussion of representations in Chapter 7, where things become troublesome if the basis is not orthogonal).

When $S$ is included, the band becomes asymmetric between top and bottom—the antibonding side (top) is raised more than the bonding side (bottom) is lowered. This is exactly the phenomenon learned in chemistry as “the destabilization of an antibonding orbital is larger than the stabilization of a bonding orbital.” In quantitative calculations, of course, $S$ is not dropped.

Supplement: The multi-orbital case and the connection to group theory

When there are several orbitals per atom (three for $p$, five for $d$), Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-blochsum}$ is constructed for each orbital, and the energies are the eigenvalues of the matrix at each $\kk$

$$ H_{\mu\nu}(\kk)=\sum_{\bm\rho}\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho}\,t_{\mu\nu}(\bm\rho) $$

($\mu,\nu$ are orbital indices). For a $d$ band this is a $5\times5$ matrix.

Here the group theory of Chapters 10 and 11 comes into play. At a high-symmetry point $\kk$, this matrix is block-diagonalized according to the irreducible representations of the little co-group. For example, at the $\Gamma$ point of cubic symmetry the $5\times5$ $d$ block splits into $E_g$ (two-dimensional) and $T_{2g}$ (three-dimensional), and $\Gamma_d=E_g\oplus T_{2g}$ appears (exactly the same decomposition as the crystal-field splitting of Chapter 10). Before a band calculation is actually run, where the bands are degenerate and with what degeneracy is already fixed by group theory alone. This is the reason we placed Part II before Part III.

The result of organizing the directional dependence of $t_{\mu\nu}(\bm\rho)$ in the two-center approximation is the Slater–Koster parameters ($V_{ss\sigma}$, $V_{sp\sigma}$, $V_{pp\pi}$, $V_{dd\delta}$, etc.), which remain a working tool even today for compressing the bands of a first-principles calculation into a small number of parameters (Wannierization).

18.5 The Wigner–Seitz Method and Pseudopotentials

18.5.1 The paradox — why can electrons remain “almost free”?

A serious contradiction

So far we have assumed that the Fermi surfaces of $\mathrm{Na}$ and $\mathrm{Al}$ are extremely close to the free-electron sphere. In fact, the deviation of the Fermi surface of $\mathrm{Na}$ from a sphere is less than 0.1 %. But on reflection, this is implausibly strange.

The nucleus of $\mathrm{Na}$ carries a positive charge of $Z=11$. When a conduction electron passes at a distance of $0.1\,\text{Å}$ from the nucleus, the Coulomb potential it feels is (using $e^2=14.4\,\mathrm{eV\cdot\text{Å}}$)

$$ U=-\frac{Ze^2}{r}=-\frac{11\times14.4}{0.1}=-1584\ \mathrm{eV} $$

Even if we make the most generous estimate of the screening by the core electrons and see only the valence charge $+e$, the potential is $-28.8\,\mathrm{eV}$ at $r=0.5\,\text{Å}$.

On the other hand, the Fermi energy of $\mathrm{Na}$ is only $\epsilon_F=3.24\,\mathrm{eV}$. Although the depth of the potential is tens to hundreds of times the kinetic energy, why can the electrons behave freely? Perturbation theory hardly seems usable at all.

What resolves this contradiction is the Wigner–Seitz method and the pseudopotential of this section. To state the answer first — orthogonality to the core orbitals acts as an effective repulsion that cancels the strong attraction.

18.5.2 The Wigner–Seitz cell method

The idea of the Wigner–Seitz method (1933)

Step 1: Divide the crystal into Wigner–Seitz cells. The WS cell introduced in Chapter 1 assigns to each lattice point the region “nearest” to it, and fills the crystal without gaps. In a metal, each WS cell contains one valence electron (for a monovalent metal).

Step 2: Solve for the state at the bottom of the band ($\kk=0$). The Bloch function at $\kk=0$ satisfies $\psi_0(\rr+\TT)=\psi_0(\rr)$, that is, it has the lattice periodicity. From this symmetry, the normal derivative must vanish at the boundary of the WS cell:

$$ \left.\frac{\partial\psi_0}{\partial n}\right|_{\text{boundary of the WS cell}}=0 $$

(For the function to join smoothly at the boundary, its derivative must agree with that of the identical function coming from the neighboring cell; by symmetry the derivatives on the two sides differ in sign, so they can only be zero.)

Step 3: Approximate the cell by a sphere of equal volume. Replace the WS cell by a sphere of radius $r_s$ (the Wigner–Seitz radius). Here $r_s$ is defined by

$$ \frac{4\pi}{3}r_s^3=\frac{1}{n}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_s=\left(\frac{3}{4\pi n}\right)^{1/3} $$

Since the potential can be regarded as spherically symmetric, solving the radial equation

$$ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\left(r^2\frac{\dd u}{\dd r}\right)+U(r)u=\epsilon_0 u, \qquad \left.\frac{\dd u}{\dd r}\right|_{r=r_s}=0 $$

numerically gives the band bottom $\epsilon_0$. This is almost the same as the problem of an isolated atom; only the boundary condition differs ($u(\infty)=0$ for an isolated atom, $u'(r_s)=0$ for a metal).

Step 4: Approximate $\kk\neq0$ by free electrons. The claim of Wigner and Seitz is

$$ \epsilon(\kk)\simeq\epsilon_0+\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} $$

That is, only the position of the bottom of the band is determined by the strong potential, and the $\kk$ dependence measured from it is the same as for free electrons. ∎

Why the $\kk$ dependence becomes free-electron-like

When we actually solve it, the wave function $u(r)$ at $\kk=0$ has the following form.

  • $r\lesssim0.5\,\text{Å}$ (core region): it oscillates violently in order to be orthogonal to the core orbitals.
  • $r\gtrsim0.5\,\text{Å}$ (most of the outer part of the cell): almost flat, close to a constant.

In terms of volume, we can see how small this “violently oscillating region” is. For $\mathrm{Na}$,

$$ r_s=\left(\frac{3}{4\pi\times2.65\times10^{22}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}}\right)^{1/3}=2.08\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{cm}=2.08\,\text{Å} $$

If we take the core radius to be $0.5\,\text{Å}$, the volume ratio occupied by the core is

$$ \left(\frac{0.5}{2.08}\right)^3=0.014=1.4\% $$

In 98.6 % of the cell the wave function is flat. Introducing $\kk$ means multiplying this flat part by a slowly varying modulation $\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\rr}$, and the potential there is nearly constant (so it only produces a constant shift). Hence the $\kk$ dependence remains that of free electrons, $\hbar^2k^2/2m$.

In other words — the deep potential well is too narrow to be “seen” by an electron wave with the Fermi wavelength $\lambda_F=2\pi/k_F\approx7\,\text{Å}$.

18.5.3 The Phillips–Kleinman cancellation

The argument above is qualitative. It was stated rigorously in the language of operators by Phillips and Kleinman (1959) in their cancellation theorem. This is the heart of this section, so we follow it carefully.

Derivation: The pseudopotential and the Phillips–Kleinman cancellation

Setup. For the true Hamiltonian $\Ham=T+U$ ($U$ is a strong attractive potential),

  • core states: $\Ham\ket{c}=E_c\ket{c}$ ($c=1s,2s,2p,\dots$)
  • valence states: $\Ham\ket{\psi}=E\ket{\psi}$, with $\langle c|\psi\rangle=0$ (orthogonal to the core)

Note that $E>E_c$ (the valence electrons lie above the core levels).

Step 1: The idea of the orthogonalized plane wave (OPW). The true $\ket{\psi}$ oscillates violently near the core and is hard to handle. So we regard $\ket{\psi}$ as a smooth function $\ket{\varphi}$ with its core components subtracted. Writing the projection operator onto the core as

$$ P\equiv\sum_c\ket{c}\bra{c} $$

we have

$$ \ket{\psi}=(1-P)\ket{\varphi}=\ket{\varphi}-\sum_c\ket{c}\langle c|\varphi\rangle $$

This $\ket{\varphi}$ is called the pseudo-wavefunction. By construction, $\langle c|\psi\rangle=\langle c|\varphi\rangle-\langle c|\varphi\rangle=0$ is automatically satisfied ✓.

Step 2: Substitute into the Schrödinger equation.

$$ \Ham(1-P)\ket{\varphi}=E(1-P)\ket{\varphi} $$

We compute $\Ham P$ on the left-hand side. Since $\Ham\ket{c}=E_c\ket{c}$,

$$ \Ham P\ket{\varphi}=\Ham\sum_c\ket{c}\langle c|\varphi\rangle=\sum_c E_c\ket{c}\langle c|\varphi\rangle $$

Therefore

$$ \Ham\ket{\varphi}-\sum_c E_c\ket{c}\langle c|\varphi\rangle=E\ket{\varphi}-E\sum_c\ket{c}\langle c|\varphi\rangle $$

Step 3: Transpose terms. Moving the second term on the right-hand side to the left-hand side gives

$$ \Ham\ket{\varphi}+\sum_c\left(E-E_c\right)\ket{c}\langle c|\varphi\rangle=E\ket{\varphi} $$

That is, defining $V_R\equiv\sum_c(E-E_c)\ket{c}\bra{c}$, we obtain

$$ \left(T+U+V_R\right)\ket{\varphi}=E\ket{\varphi} $$

Step 4: Reading this off. Three things can be said.

  1. The eigenvalue $E$ has not changed. Even if we solve the equation for the smooth $\ket{\varphi}$, the energy obtained is the true valence energy.
  2. $V_R$ is repulsive. For an arbitrary state $\ket{\chi}$, $$\bra{\chi}V_R\ket{\chi}=\sum_c(E-E_c)\abs{\langle c|\chi\rangle}^2>0$$ because $E>E_c$. The “geometrical constraint” of orthogonality to the core has appeared in the form of a repulsive potential.
  3. $U$ (strong attraction) and $V_R$ (repulsion) largely cancel each other. What remains, $$U_{\rm ps}\equiv U+V_R$$ is the pseudopotential. We have $\abs{U_{\rm ps}}\ll\abs{U}$, and perturbation theory can be used for $U_{\rm ps}$.

∎

Physical Meaning: The intuition behind the cancellation

Why do the attraction and the repulsion cancel so well? It is easy to understand from the standpoint of energy.

The true valence wave function oscillates violently near the core. The oscillation generates a large amount of kinetic energy $\propto\abs{\nabla\psi}^2$. This large positive kinetic energy nearly offsets the large negative potential energy. Only the net result appears in $E$.

The pseudopotential prescription carries out this offsetting from the start. Instead of removing the oscillation near the core to obtain a smooth $\varphi$, it makes up for the kinetic energy that has been removed in the form of a repulsive potential $V_R$. It is a rearrangement of the books; the physics is the same.

The practical benefit is enormous. A smooth $\varphi$ can be expanded in a small number of plane waves. Where expanding the true $\psi$ in plane waves would require $10^6$ of them, $\varphi$ needs only $10^2$–$10^3$. It is solely because of this that today’s plane-wave-basis first-principles calculations (VASP, Quantum ESPRESSO, etc.) are feasible. The PAW method (projector augmented wave) used by VASP also belongs to this lineage in that it handles the oscillation near the core with projection operators.

18.5.4 The empty-core model and its Fourier transform

The simplest model of a pseudopotential is Ashcroft’s empty-core model.

$$ \begin{equation} U_{\rm ps}(r)= \begin{cases} 0, & r<R_c\\[2pt] -\dfrac{e^2}{r}, & r>R_c \end{cases} \label{eq:18-emptycore} \end{equation} $$

It is a crude model: inside the core the attraction and repulsion cancel completely to give zero, and outside it is simply the naive Coulomb attraction of the screened ion. The only parameter is the core radius $R_c$, which is chosen to fit experiment (band gaps and spectra). For alkali metals, $R_c\approx1\,\text{Å}$ or so.

Derivation: The Fourier component $U_\GG$ of the empty-core model

The Fourier component of the periodic potential (the quantity that appeared in the $2\times2$ secular equation in Chapter 16) is

$$ U_\GG=\frac{1}{\Omega}\int U_{\rm ps}(r)\,\ee^{-\ii\GG\cdot\rr}\,\dd^3r $$

($\Omega$ is the volume of the primitive cell).

Step 1: Do the angular integral first. Since $U_{\rm ps}$ is spherically symmetric, we can take $\GG$ as the polar axis and write $\GG\cdot\rr=Gr\cos\theta$.

$$ \int\ee^{-\ii Gr\cos\theta}\,\dd\Omega =2\pi\int_0^{\pi}\ee^{-\ii Gr\cos\theta}\sin\theta\,\dd\theta $$

Substituting $t=\cos\theta$ gives $\dd t=-\sin\theta\,\dd\theta$, and the range of integration is $t:1\to-1$, so

$$ =2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}\ee^{-\ii Grt}\,\dd t =2\pi\left[\frac{\ee^{-\ii Grt}}{-\ii Gr}\right]_{-1}^{1} =2\pi\cdot\frac{\ee^{\ii Gr}-\ee^{-\ii Gr}}{\ii Gr} =\frac{4\pi\sin Gr}{Gr} $$

(The last step uses $\ee^{\ii x}-\ee^{-\ii x}=2\ii\sin x$.)

Step 2: Substitute into the radial integral. With $\dd^3r=r^2\dd r\,\dd\Omega$, and since the integrand is zero for $r<R_c$,

$$ U_\GG=\frac{1}{\Omega}\int_{R_c}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{e^2}{r}\right)\frac{4\pi\sin Gr}{Gr}\,r^2\,\dd r =-\frac{4\pi e^2}{\Omega G}\int_{R_c}^{\infty}\sin(Gr)\,\dd r $$

With $r$ squared and two factors of $1/r$, all the powers of $r$ cancel exactly.

Step 3: Treat the divergent integral with a convergence factor. The integral $\int_{R_c}^\infty\sin(Gr)\dd r$ does not converge as it stands. Physically there is screening, so we multiply by $\ee^{-\kappa r}$ and finally let $\kappa\to0$.

$$ I(\kappa)=\int_{R_c}^{\infty}\sin(Gr)\,\ee^{-\kappa r}\,\dd r $$

The indefinite integral is

$$ \int\sin(Gr)\ee^{-\kappa r}\dd r=-\frac{\ee^{-\kappa r}\left(\kappa\sin Gr+G\cos Gr\right)}{\kappa^2+G^2} $$

(This can be checked by differentiation.) It vanishes at the upper limit $r\to\infty$; substituting the lower limit $r=R_c$ gives

$$ I(\kappa)=\frac{\ee^{-\kappa R_c}\left(\kappa\sin GR_c+G\cos GR_c\right)}{\kappa^2+G^2} \xrightarrow{\ \kappa\to0\ }\frac{G\cos GR_c}{G^2}=\frac{\cos GR_c}{G} $$

Step 4: Summarize.

$$ \begin{equation} U_\GG=-\frac{4\pi e^2}{\Omega G^2}\cos\!\left(GR_c\right) \label{eq:18-UG} \end{equation} $$

Physical Meaning: Three points to read from Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-UG}$

  1. For $R_c=0$ we have $\cos=1$, and we return to the Fourier transform of the bare Coulomb potential, $-4\pi e^2/\Omega G^2$. The presence of $R_c$ attaches an oscillating factor $\cos(GR_c)$, which reduces $U_\GG$. This is how the cancellation looks in Fourier space.
  2. For $GR_c=\pi/2$, $U_\GG$ is exactly zero. For a particular $\GG$ the pseudopotential component can vanish by accident, and there the band gap hardly opens at all. The sign change of the pseudopotential form factors of $\mathrm{Si}$ and $\mathrm{Ge}$ near $G\sim1.5$–$2\,\text{Å}^{-1}$ is due to this node.
  3. Because of the factor $1/G^2$, the components with large $\GG$ become small rapidly. That is why considering only the first and second Bragg planes is roughly sufficient.

If the screening by conduction electrons is included in the Thomas–Fermi approximation, then $-e^2/r\to-(e^2/r)\ee^{-k_{\rm TF}r}$, and as a result the denominator of $\eqref{eq:18-UG}$ is replaced as $G^2\to G^2+k_{\rm TF}^2$. Since $k_{\rm TF}$ is about $1$–$2\,\text{Å}^{-1}$ in metals, this reduces it further by several tens of percent.

Example 18.6: Evaluating $U_\GG$ of $\mathrm{Na}$ numerically

$\mathrm{Na}$ is bcc with $a=4.225\,\text{Å}$. Evaluate $U_\GG$ for the shortest reciprocal lattice vector $\GG_{110}$, and compare it with $\epsilon_F=3.24\,\mathrm{eV}$.

Solution. Step 1: $\Omega$ and $G$. The volume of the bcc primitive cell is half that of the cubic cell, so

$$ \Omega=\frac{a^3}{2}=\frac{(4.225)^3}{2}=\frac{75.43}{2}=37.72\ \text{Å}^3 $$

The reciprocal lattice of bcc is fcc, and the shortest reciprocal lattice vectors are of the type $\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,1,0)$. Their magnitude is

$$ G_{110}=\frac{2\pi}{a}\sqrt2=\frac{2\pi\times1.4142}{4.225}=2.103\ \text{Å}^{-1} $$

Step 2: Prefactor. Using $e^2=14.4\,\mathrm{eV\cdot\text{Å}}$, we obtain

$$ \frac{4\pi e^2}{\Omega G^2}=\frac{4\pi\times14.4}{37.72\times(2.103)^2}=\frac{180.96}{37.72\times4.423}=\frac{180.96}{166.8}=1.085\ \mathrm{eV} $$

Step 3: $\cos(GR_c)$. For $R_c=1.0\,\text{Å}$, $GR_c=2.103\,\mathrm{rad}=120.5^\circ$ and $\cos=-0.506$. Hence

$$ U_{\GG_{110}}=-1.085\times(-0.506)=+0.55\ \mathrm{eV} $$

Step 4: Including screening. The Thomas–Fermi wavenumber is $k_{\rm TF}^2=4\pi e^2g(\epsilon_F)=6\pi ne^2/\epsilon_F$ (we used $g=3n/2\epsilon_F$ for free electrons). Inserting $n=0.0265\,\text{Å}^{-3}$ gives

$$ k_{\rm TF}^2=\frac{6\pi\times0.0265\times14.4}{3.24}=2.22\ \text{Å}^{-2} $$ $$ \frac{G^2}{G^2+k_{\rm TF}^2}=\frac{4.423}{4.423+2.22}=0.666 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad U_{\GG_{110}}\approx0.37\ \mathrm{eV} $$

Step 5: Decision.

$$ \frac{\abs{U_\GG}}{\epsilon_F}=\frac{0.37}{3.24}=0.11 $$

About 10%. Perturbation theory is perfectly applicable. This is the quantitative basis for the statement “the Fermi surface of $\mathrm{Na}$ is a sphere.” The number $-1584\,\mathrm{eV}$ at the beginning has thus shrunk to $0.37\,\mathrm{eV}$. This is a cancellation of about four orders of magnitude.

Note that $R_c$ is an empirical parameter, so this estimate is quite sensitive to $R_c$ (for $R_c=1.7\,\text{Å}$, $\abs{U_\GG}=0.65\,\mathrm{eV}$). Please take it as an order-of-magnitude argument. ∎

18.6 The de Haas–van Alphen Effect and the Onsager Quantization Condition

So far we have constructed the Fermi surface theoretically. This section is about how to measure it. In 1930, de Haas and van Alphen discovered that the magnetic susceptibility of $\mathrm{Bi}$ oscillates with the magnetic field. At the time it was a curious phenomenon of unknown origin, but in 1952 Onsager showed that the cross-sectional area of the Fermi surface appears directly in its period, and since then this has been the standard means of measuring Fermi surfaces.

18.6.1 Landau levels — in a magnetic field, $\kk$ space splits into tubes

Derivation: Landau levels of free electrons (checking the result)

Solving quantum mechanically for a free electron in a magnetic field $\BB=B\hat z$ (Landau, 1930), the energy eigenvalues are

$$ \epsilon_{n}(k_z)=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega_c+\frac{\hbar^2k_z^2}{2m}, \qquad \omega_c=\frac{eB}{mc},\quad n=0,1,2,\dots $$

That is, the continuous kinetic energy in the $xy$ plane is quantized into harmonic-oscillator levels (because the motion in the $xy$ plane is circular motion, which is equivalent to a two-dimensional harmonic oscillator).

What happened in $\kk$ space. Before the magnetic field was applied, the states in the $k_x k_y$ plane were distributed uniformly. When the field is applied, the states

$$ \frac{\hbar^2(k_x^2+k_y^2)}{2m}=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega_c \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k_\perp^2 = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\frac{eB}{mc}=\left(2n+1\right)\frac{eB}{\hbar c} $$

are gathered onto circles of discrete radii. In three dimensions, the states are concentrated on concentric cylinders extending in the $k_z$ direction—Landau tubes.

The area enclosed by a tube. The cross-sectional area of the $n$th tube is

$$ S_n=\pi k_\perp^2=\pi(2n+1)\frac{eB}{\hbar c}=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{2\pi eB}{\hbar c} $$

The difference in area between adjacent tubes is

$$ \Delta S = S_{n+1}-S_n=\frac{2\pi eB}{\hbar c} $$

It is a constant value independent of $n$. In other words, the Landau tubes divide $\kk$ space into “rings of equal area.” ∎

18.6.2 The Onsager quantization condition — to a general Fermi surface

The above concerns free electrons. Can the same be said for an arbitrary band structure? It can. That is Onsager's result. We start from the semiclassical Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization condition.

Derivation: $S_n=(n+\gamma)\dfrac{2\pi eB}{\hbar c}$ (Onsager, 1952)

Step 0: Starting point. The Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization condition for a system undergoing periodic motion is

$$ \oint \pp\cdot\dd\rr = (n+\gamma)\,2\pi\hbar $$

Here $\pp$ is the canonical momentum and $\gamma$ is a phase correction ($\gamma=1/2$ for free electrons). Please do not confuse this $\gamma$ with the hopping integral $\gamma$ of Section 18.4 or with the electronic specific heat coefficient $\gamma_{el}$; it is unrelated to either. For a Bloch electron of charge $q$ in a magnetic field, we can write

$$ \pp = \hbar\kk+\frac{q}{c}\bm{A} $$

($\bm{A}$ is the vector potential and $\hbar\kk$ is the crystal momentum). For the electron, $q=-e$. For free electrons $\hbar\kk=m\vv$, but in a general band the velocity is given by $\vv=\frac{1}{\hbar}\nabla_\kk\epsilon_n(\kk)$, and $m\vv=\hbar\kk$ does not hold. All we use below is that “the mechanical part of the canonical momentum is the crystal momentum $\hbar\kk$,” so the argument proceeds independently of the shape of the band.

Step 1: Split the integral into two.

$$ \oint\pp\cdot\dd\rr = \underbrace{\hbar\oint\kk\cdot\dd\rr}_{(\mathrm{I})} +\underbrace{\frac{q}{c}\oint\bm{A}\cdot\dd\rr}_{(\mathrm{II})} $$

Step 2: Write (II) in terms of the magnetic flux. By Stokes' theorem,

$$ \oint\bm{A}\cdot\dd\rr=\int(\nabla\times\bm{A})\cdot\dd\bm{S}=\int\BB\cdot\dd\bm{S}=\Phi $$

$\Phi$ is the magnetic flux enclosed by the real-space orbit. Hence $(\mathrm{II})=\dfrac{q}{c}\Phi$.

Step 3: Calculate (I). We use the relation

$$ \hbar\kk = \frac{q}{c}\,\rr\times\BB $$

from which $\eqref{eq:18-realspace}$ of Section 18.3.4 was derived (we took the origin at the center of the orbit to eliminate the integration constant). Substituting this gives

$$ (\mathrm{I})=\frac{q}{c}\oint(\rr\times\BB)\cdot\dd\rr $$

Using the cyclic property of the scalar triple product, $(\bm{a}\times\bm{b})\cdot\bm{c}=\bm{a}\cdot(\bm{b}\times\bm{c})$, we have $(\rr\times\BB)\cdot\dd\rr = \rr\cdot(\BB\times\dd\rr) = -\BB\cdot(\rr\times\dd\rr)$. Therefore

$$ (\mathrm{I})=-\frac{q}{c}\,\BB\cdot\oint\rr\times\dd\rr $$

Here we can use the geometric formula for a closed curve,

$$ \oint\rr\times\dd\rr = 2\bm{S}\qquad(\bm{S}:\text{enclosed area vector}) $$

(this just says that summing up the triangles $\frac12\abs{\rr\times\dd\rr}$ gives the area). Hence

$$ (\mathrm{I})=-\frac{q}{c}\cdot2\,\BB\cdot\bm{S}=-\frac{2q}{c}\Phi $$

Step 4: Add them up.

$$ \oint\pp\cdot\dd\rr=-\frac{2q}{c}\Phi+\frac{q}{c}\Phi=-\frac{q}{c}\Phi=\frac{e}{c}\Phi \qquad(q=-e) $$

Substituting into the quantization condition, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \Phi_n=(n+\gamma)\,\frac{2\pi\hbar c}{e}=(n+\gamma)\,\Phi_0, \qquad \Phi_0\equiv\frac{2\pi\hbar c}{e}=\frac{hc}{e}=4.14\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{G\cdot cm^2} \label{eq:18-flux} \end{equation} $$

(Continued) Step 5: Translating the magnetic flux into an area in $\kk$ space

$\eqref{eq:18-flux}$ says that “the magnetic flux enclosed by the real-space orbit is quantized.” We now restate this in the language of $\kk$ space. By $\eqref{eq:18-realspace}$, the real-space orbit is the $\kk$-space orbit scaled by a factor of $\hbar c/eB$. If lengths are multiplied by $L$, areas are multiplied by $L^2$, so

$$ A_{\rm real}=\left(\frac{\hbar c}{eB}\right)^2 S $$

($S$ is the area enclosed by the $\kk$-space orbit.) The magnetic flux is therefore

$$ \Phi = B\,A_{\rm real}=B\left(\frac{\hbar c}{eB}\right)^2S=\frac{\hbar^2c^2}{e^2B}\,S $$

Equating this with $\eqref{eq:18-flux}$, we get

$$ \frac{\hbar^2c^2}{e^2B}S_n=(n+\gamma)\frac{2\pi\hbar c}{e} $$

Multiplying both sides by $\dfrac{e^2B}{\hbar^2c^2}$ gives

$$ S_n=(n+\gamma)\frac{2\pi\hbar c}{e}\cdot\frac{e^2B}{\hbar^2c^2}=(n+\gamma)\frac{2\pi eB}{\hbar c} $$

∎

$$ \begin{equation} \boxed{\ S_n=(n+\gamma)\,\frac{2\pi eB}{\hbar c}\ } \qquad\left(\text{in SI units, } S_n=(n+\gamma)\frac{2\pi eB}{\hbar}\right) \label{eq:18-onsager} \end{equation} $$

Supplement: Checking with free electrons

The result $S_n=(n+\frac12)\frac{2\pi eB}{\hbar c}$ obtained directly for free electrons in Section 18.6.1 agrees exactly with the result of $\eqref{eq:18-onsager}$ with $\gamma=1/2$ ✓. The semiclassical argument reproduces the exact quantum-mechanical solution.

In general, the value of $\gamma$ can deviate from $1/2$. For Dirac electrons (graphene and Weyl semimetals), $\gamma=0$, and this is used as experimental evidence for a Berry phase of $\pi$. That is, by reading the phase of the dHvA oscillations precisely, one can learn the topology of the bands.

18.6.3 The cross-sectional area of the Fermi surface can be read from the period in $1/B$

Derivation: $\Delta\left(\dfrac{1}{B}\right)=\dfrac{2\pi e}{\hbar c\,S}$

As the magnetic field is increased, the right-hand side of $\eqref{eq:18-onsager}$ grows in proportion to $B$, so the Landau tubes swell. The swollen tubes pass through the Fermi surface one after another.

Let $B_n$ be the field at which the cross-sectional area of the $n$th tube coincides exactly with the cross-sectional area $S$ of the Fermi surface. Then

$$ S=(n+\gamma)\frac{2\pi eB_n}{\hbar c} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{B_n}=(n+\gamma)\frac{2\pi e}{\hbar c\,S} $$

Taking the difference between adjacent $n$ and $n+1$, both $\gamma$ and $S$ drop out of the result:

$$ \frac{1}{B_{n+1}}-\frac{1}{B_n}=\frac{2\pi e}{\hbar c\,S} $$

∎

$$ \begin{equation} \Delta\!\left(\frac{1}{B}\right)=\frac{2\pi e}{\hbar c\,S} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad F\equiv\frac{1}{\Delta(1/B)}=\frac{\hbar c\,S}{2\pi e} \label{eq:18-dhva} \end{equation} $$

Physical Meaning: This is the “ruler of the Fermi surface”

Each time a Landau tube passes through the Fermi surface, the density of states at the Fermi level jumps up (because the states are concentrated at the edge of the tube). If the density of states oscillates, the free energy oscillates accordingly, and therefore

  • the magnetization $M=-\partial F/\partial B$ oscillates—the de Haas–van Alphen effect
  • the electrical resistance oscillates—the Shubnikov–de Haas effect
  • in addition, almost all physical quantities oscillate, including magnetostriction, thermoelectric power, and the speed of sound

And by $\eqref{eq:18-dhva}$, just by measuring the $1/B$ period of the oscillations we obtain the cross-sectional area $S$ of the Fermi surface in absolute value. No fitting parameter is needed at all; it is determined solely by physical constants and the measured value. Such a direct measurement is rare even in solid-state physics.

Furthermore, by rotating the sample and changing the direction of the magnetic field, we can measure cross-sectional areas in various directions. Collecting the entire orientation dependence of the cross-sectional area, we can reconstruct the three-dimensional shape of the Fermi surface. This is what “fermiology” in the 1950s–70s was all about.

Why do only “extremal” cross-sections matter?

The $S$ in $\eqref{eq:18-dhva}$ is that of the extremal cross-section of the Fermi surface, that is, a cross-section satisfying

$$ \left.\frac{\partial S(\epsilon_F,k_\parallel)}{\partial k_\parallel}\right|_{k_\parallel=k_\parallel^{*}}=0 $$

Why do only the extrema survive?

The observed oscillation is the sum of the contributions from all cross-sections with different $k_\parallel$. Each cross-section oscillates like $\cos\left[2\pi F(k_\parallel)/B\right]$, and its phase depends on $k_\parallel$ through $F(k_\parallel)\propto S(k_\parallel)$. Therefore

$$ M_{\rm osc}\propto\int \dd k_\parallel\,\cos\left[\frac{2\pi F(k_\parallel)}{B}+\phi\right] $$

Since $F/B$ is an enormous number, $10^3$–$10^5$, even a slight change in $k_\parallel$ makes the phase rotate many times. The contributions from adjacent cross-sections cancel each other.

What is not canceled is only the neighborhood where the phase is stationary with respect to $k_\parallel$, that is, $\partial S/\partial k_\parallel=0$. This is the stationary phase approximation. Expanding around the stationary point as $S\simeq S_{\rm ext}+\frac12 S''(k_\parallel-k^*)^2$ and carrying out the Fresnel integral, we also find that the amplitude acquires a factor $\abs{S''}^{-1/2}$.

Geometrically speaking, only the “thickest part” and the “thinnest part” of the Fermi surface are visible. For $\mathrm{Cu}$ with $\BB\parallel[111]$, there are two extremal cross-sections, the thick part (belly) and the thin part (neck), and indeed two oscillation periods are observed simultaneously.

18.6.4 dHvA in numbers

Example 18.7: Calculating the dHvA frequency of the belly orbit of $\mathrm{Cu}$

Treating $\mathrm{Cu}$ as free-electron-like ($k_F=1.36\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$), find the dHvA frequency $F$ arising from the maximum cross-sectional area when $\BB\parallel[111]$. Carry out the calculation in SI units.

Solution. Step 1: Cross-sectional area. For a sphere the maximum cross section is the equatorial plane, so

$$ S=\pi k_F^2=\pi\times(1.36\times10^{10})^2=\pi\times1.850\times10^{20}=5.81\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}} $$

Step 2: Substitute into the SI version of Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-dhva}$.

$$ F=\frac{\hbar S}{2\pi e}=\frac{1.055\times10^{-34}\times5.81\times10^{20}}{2\pi\times1.602\times10^{-19}} =\frac{6.13\times10^{-14}}{1.007\times10^{-18}}=6.1\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{T} $$

Step 3: Convert to a period.

$$ \Delta\!\left(\frac1B\right)=\frac{1}{F}=1.6\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{T^{-1}} $$

Looking at the oscillation near $B=10\,\mathrm{T}$, we have $1/B=0.1\,\mathrm{T^{-1}}$, so $1/B$ needs to change by only $1.6\times10^{-5}$ to advance one period. In terms of $B$, $\Delta B\approx B^2\Delta(1/B)=100\times1.6\times10^{-5}=1.6\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T}$. Changing the magnetic field by just 1.6 mT gives one period. This shows why precise field sweeping and high-sensitivity detection are necessary.

Step 4: Compare with experiment. The measured belly frequency of $\mathrm{Cu}$ is about $5.8\times10^4\,\mathrm{T}$, in good agreement with the estimate above. The neck frequency, on the other hand, is about $2\times10^3\,\mathrm{T}$. Taking the ratio,

$$ \frac{S_{\rm neck}}{S_{\rm belly}}=\frac{F_{\rm neck}}{F_{\rm belly}}=\frac{2\times10^3}{5.8\times10^4}=0.034 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{k_{\rm neck}}{k_{\rm belly}}=\sqrt{0.034}=0.19 $$

The radius of the neck is about one fifth that of the belly—in perfect agreement with the picture of the actual Fermi surface of $\mathrm{Cu}$. ∎

Example 18.8: How many Landau tubes are occupied?

For $\mathrm{Cu}$ at $B=10\,\mathrm{T}$, find the number $\nu$ of Landau tubes that lie inside the Fermi surface.

Solution. By Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-onsager}$, the area of the $n$th tube reaches $S$ when $n\simeq S\hbar/(2\pi eB)=F/B$, so

$$ \nu\simeq\frac{F}{B}=\frac{6.1\times10^4}{10}=6.1\times10^3 $$

About 6000 tubes. Even when one tube drops out, the total number of electrons changes by only $1/6000$. The dHvA oscillation is therefore an extremely small ripple riding on a huge nonoscillatory component (the relative amplitude is typically $10^{-4}$–$10^{-6}$). High-sensitivity techniques such as torque magnetometry and the field-modulation method are required.

Conversely, in the semimetal $\mathrm{Bi}$ the carrier density is only $3\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, so $F\sim10\,\mathrm{T}$, and at $B=10\,\mathrm{T}$ we get $\nu\sim1$. The quantum limit (all electrons in the lowest Landau level) is within reach. It was no accident that de Haas and van Alphen first found the oscillation in $\mathrm{Bi}$. ∎

Supplement: The Lifshitz–Kosevich formula — what the amplitude tells us

The period of the oscillation gives $S$. What, then, does the amplitude tell us? According to the theory of Lifshitz and Kosevich, the oscillation amplitude is damped by the following two factors.

$$ R_T=\frac{X}{\sinh X},\qquad X=\frac{2\pi^2k_BT\,m_c}{\hbar eB} \qquad\text{(damping by temperature)} $$ $$ R_D=\exp\left(-\frac{2\pi^2k_BT_D\,m_c}{\hbar eB}\right) \qquad\text{(damping by impurity scattering, Dingle factor)} $$
  • The temperature dependence determines the cyclotron mass $m_c$. If we measure the amplitude as a function of $T$ and fit it to the form of $R_T$, $m_c$ is determined uniquely. This amounts to measuring experimentally the quantity $m_c=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\pi}\partial S/\partial\epsilon$ in Eq. $\eqref{eq:18-mc}$.
  • The Dingle temperature $T_D$ determines the relaxation time $\tau=\hbar/2\pi k_BT_D$. It serves as a measure of sample purity.

In short, a single dHvA measurement yields the shape of the Fermi surface ($S$), the band dispersion ($m_c$), the quality of the sample ($T_D$), and even, from the phase $\gamma$, the Berry phase. There is no better check of a first-principles calculation.

18.7 Fermi Surfaces of Real Metals

18.7.1 Alkali metals — nearly perfect spheres

Example 18.9: Does the Fermi sphere of an alkali metal touch the Brillouin zone?

An alkali metal is bcc with one conduction electron per atom. Express $k_F$ in terms of the lattice constant $a$, and compare it with the distance to the nearest Brillouin-zone boundary (the $N$ point).

Solution. Step 1: $k_F$. The cubic unit cell of bcc contains two atoms, so $n=2/a^3$.

$$ k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}=\left(\frac{6\pi^2}{a^3}\right)^{1/3}=\frac{(6\pi^2)^{1/3}}{a}=\frac{3.898}{a} $$

Step 2: Distance to the $N$ point. The reciprocal lattice of bcc is fcc, and the shortest reciprocal lattice vectors are of the type $\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,1,0)$ (twelve of them). The $N$ point is the midpoint of such a vector, so

$$ k_N=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{a}\sqrt2=\frac{\sqrt2\,\pi}{a}=\frac{4.443}{a} $$

Step 3: The ratio.

$$ \frac{k_F}{k_N}=\frac{3.898}{4.443}=0.877 $$

The Fermi sphere stops at 88 % of the distance to the boundary and does not touch it. Therefore the Fermi surface of an alkali metal is a single, simply connected, closed sphere, and only the first band is partially filled. ∎

Experimentally, the deviation of the Fermi surfaces of $\mathrm{Na}$ and $\mathrm{K}$ from a sphere is 0.1 % or less, and they are the closest to free electrons among all metals. $\mathrm{Li}$ and $\mathrm{Cs}$ are distorted somewhat more ($\mathrm{Li}$ has no $2p$ core level, so its pseudopotential is relatively strong, and $\mathrm{Cs}$ has a shallow $5p$ core level and nearby $d$ states). Indeed, the thermal effective mass of $\mathrm{Li}$ is large, $m^*/m\approx2.2$, which departs from the simple free-electron picture.

Physical Meaning: Consequences of being a closed sphere

That the Fermi surface is only a single closed sphere means that there are no open orbits. Therefore (from Section 18.3.5)

  • the transverse magnetoresistance saturates with the magnetic field;
  • the Hall coefficient is close to $R_H=-1/nec$, in agreement with the value expected for one valence electron;
  • the magnetoresistance has almost no dependence on orientation.

Experiment shows exactly this. The alkali metals are “the textbook realization of the free-electron gas.”

18.7.2 Noble metals — the necks appear

$\mathrm{Cu}$, $\mathrm{Ag}$, and $\mathrm{Au}$ are also monovalent, but they are fcc and, moreover, have filled $d$ shells. As we saw in Example 18.1, the free-electron sphere should reach only to 90 % of the distance to the $L$ point. In reality, however, it touches the boundary and develops necks.

Why do necks appear in noble metals?

The $3d$ band of $\mathrm{Cu}$ lies $2$–$5\,\mathrm{eV}$ below $\epsilon_F$ and is completely filled. It is a mistake to think that, because it is filled, it does not matter for conduction. The $4s$ band and the $3d$ band hybridize at $\kk$ points where they have the same symmetry, and they repel each other.

The $L$ point lies on the boundary in the $\{111\}$ directions, where a band of $4s$ origin and a band of $3d$ origin can couple by symmetry (this is the matter of the little co-group and the compatibility relations in Chapter 11). This hybridization widens the effective gap at the $\{111\}$ planes, so the lower (occupied) band is pushed down at the $L$ point and falls below $\epsilon_F$. As a result, the Fermi surface bulges out in the $[111]$ direction and touches the boundary. The volume enclosed by the Fermi surface does not change, by Luttinger's theorem, so the other directions are slightly indented by the amount of the bulge.

Once it touches, the Fermi surface connects with that of the neighboring cell in the repeated-zone scheme. A neck forms at each of the eight $\{111\}$ faces, and the Fermi surface becomes multiply connected. This is the origin of the open orbits and the magnetoresistance anisotropy described in Section 18.3.5.

The thickness of the neck differs from metal to metal. For $\mathrm{Cu}$, $S_{\rm neck}/S_{\rm belly}\approx0.034$ (Example 18.7). In $\mathrm{Ag}$ the neck is thinner, and in $\mathrm{Au}$ it is comparable to that of $\mathrm{Cu}$. The differences in the position of the $d$ band (its depth below $\epsilon_F$) and in the strength of the hybridization appear directly in the thickness of the neck.

18.7.3 $\mathrm{Al}$ — the hole surface in the second zone and the electron surface in the third zone

$\mathrm{Al}$ is fcc and trivalent. Being trivalent means three electrons per primitive cell (one atom). Since one band holds $2$ electrons, the first band is full, and the remaining one electron is distributed between the second and third bands.

Derivation: The high-field Hall coefficient of $\mathrm{Al}$ indicates “one hole per atom”

We count the electrons per atom. The first band is completely filled, so it does not contribute to conduction (a filled band carries no net current; Chapter 16).

Let $n_2$ be the number of electrons in the second band and $n_3$ the number of electrons in the third band (both per atom). In total,

$$ n_2+n_3=3-2=1 $$

The second band can accommodate two electrons (including spin), so the number of holes in the second band is $p_2=2-n_2$. Hence the net “holes minus electrons” is

$$ p_2-n_3=(2-n_2)-n_3=2-(n_2+n_3)=2-1=1 $$

As a count of carriers, there is a net one hole per atom. The Hall coefficient in the high-field limit (when all orbits are closed) is determined by the net carrier density, so

$$ R_H=+\frac{1}{n_{\rm atom}\,ec}\qquad(n_{\rm atom}=\text{atomic number density}) $$

which is positive. This is indeed what is found experimentally: the high-field Hall coefficient of $\mathrm{Al}$ is positive, and its value agrees well with that expected from the atomic density. ∎

Caution: At low fields the contribution of the electron orbits of the third band matters, so $R_H$ is negative. The sign reverses as the field is raised. This is a phenomenon that the Drude model of Chapter 15 can never explain, and it is direct evidence for the multi-sheet structure of the Fermi surface.

When the Fermi surface of $\mathrm{Al}$ is constructed by the Harrison construction, a complicated hole surface along the boundary (often called the “monster”) appears in the second zone, and thin electron “rings” or “tubes” appear around the $W$ and $K$ points in the third zone. In the free electron model a small pocket also appears in the fourth zone, but in real $\mathrm{Al}$ it disappears owing to the effect of the pseudopotential. This is a concrete example of the warning “do not trust small pockets” in Section 18.2.4.

18.7.4 $\mathrm{Bi}$ — a semimetal

Physical Meaning: A “metal” with only $10^{-5}$ as many carriers

$\mathrm{Bi}$ is rhombohedral, with two atoms in the primitive cell and five valence electrons per atom ($6s^26p^3$), so ten in total — an even number. If the bands did not overlap, it should be an insulator.

In reality, the top of the valence band (at the $T$ point) is higher than the bottom of the conduction band (at the $L$ point) by only $\sim30\,\mathrm{meV}$. Because of this slight overlap, a small electron pocket forms at the $L$ point and a small hole pocket at the $T$ point. The numbers of electrons and holes are necessarily equal (a compensated metal).

The numbers are dramatic.

18.8 How to Measure the Fermi Surface

18.8.1 Cyclotron resonance (Azbel–Kaner geometry)

In a semiconductor, we can measure $m^*$ directly by applying microwaves and looking for the resonance at $\omega=\omega_c=eB/m^*c$ (Chapter 17). In a metal, however, the electromagnetic wave penetrates only a skin depth $\delta\sim10^{-5}\,\mathrm{cm}$, so the same method cannot be used.

The idea of Azbel and Kaner is to apply the magnetic field parallel to the sample surface. The electrons then trace out helices in planes parallel to the surface and return to the skin layer once every period $T=2\pi/\omega_c$. If the phase of the electric field is the same when they return, they are accelerated repeatedly. The condition is

$$ \omega = n\,\omega_c\qquad(n=1,2,3,\dots) $$

that is, the resonances are equally spaced in $1/B$. The spacing in $1/B$ gives $\omega_c$, and hence $m_c$. Here too the extremal orbits dominate (the phases line up because the period of the orbit is stationary at the extremal cross-section). Whereas dHvA measures the area of the Fermi surface, cyclotron resonance measures the energy derivative of the area ($m_c=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\pi}\partial S/\partial\epsilon$). The two are complementary.

18.8.2 ARPES: “seeing” the Fermi surface

Derivation: Kinematics of ARPES

Angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) shines light of energy $\hbar\omega$ on a sample and measures the kinetic energy and the emission angle of the electrons that are ejected.

Energy conservation. Let $\phi$ be the work function and $E_B\ge0$ the binding energy (the depth measured from $\epsilon_F$). Then

$$ E_{\rm kin}=\hbar\omega-\phi-E_B $$

Momentum conservation. The crystal surface breaks the translational symmetry in the direction normal to the surface, but the translational symmetry in the in-plane directions is preserved. Therefore the in-plane wavenumber $k_\parallel$ is conserved (up to a reciprocal lattice vector). Since a photoelectron in vacuum is a free electron,

$$ E_{\rm kin}=\frac{\hbar^2K^2}{2m},\qquad K_\parallel=K\sin\theta $$

Hence

$$ k_\parallel = K_\parallel=\frac{\sqrt{2mE_{\rm kin}}}{\hbar}\sin\theta $$

Converting to practical units. Since $\sqrt{2m\times1\,\mathrm{eV}}/\hbar=0.5123\,\text{Å}^{-1}$,

$$ \begin{equation} k_\parallel\,[\text{Å}^{-1}]=0.5123\,\sqrt{E_{\rm kin}\,[\mathrm{eV}]}\;\sin\theta \label{eq:18-arpes} \end{equation} $$

Example 18.10: Does a He I source reach the Fermi surface of $\mathrm{Cu}$?

Take the He I resonance line $\hbar\omega=21.2\,\mathrm{eV}$ and the work function of $\mathrm{Cu}$, $\phi=4.6\,\mathrm{eV}$. For an electron at $\epsilon_F$ ($E_B=0$), find the largest $k_\parallel$ that can be reached, and compare it with $k_F=1.36\,\text{Å}^{-1}$.

Solution.

$$ E_{\rm kin}=21.2-4.6-0=16.6\ \mathrm{eV} $$ $$ k_\parallel^{\max}=0.5123\times\sqrt{16.6}\times\sin90^\circ=0.5123\times4.074=2.09\ \text{Å}^{-1} $$

This comfortably reaches $k_F=1.36\,\text{Å}^{-1}$. In fact, the Fermi surface is crossed at $\sin\theta=1.36/2.09=0.651$, that is, at $\theta=40.6^\circ$.

In an experiment, we record the $E_{\rm kin}$ spectrum while sweeping $\theta$ and plot the intensity at $E_B=0$ in the $(k_x,k_y)$ plane. The locus of strong intensity is directly a photograph of the Fermi surface. ∎

Caution: The weak point of ARPES is $k_\perp$

The in-plane $k_\parallel$ is strictly conserved, but the normal component $k_\perp$ is not conserved (because the surface breaks the translational symmetry in that direction). To determine $k_\perp$ one needs an approximation: “assume that the final states form a free-electron-like parabola and fit the inner potential $V_0$.” This introduces a systematic error.

Therefore ARPES is

  • strong for layered materials and two-dimensional materials (where the $k_\perp$ dependence is small to begin with). The studies of cuprate high-temperature superconductors, iron-based superconductors, graphene, transition-metal dichalcogenides, and topological insulators have advanced almost entirely through ARPES.
  • at a disadvantage for three-dimensional bulk metals. If one wants to determine the thickness of the neck of $\mathrm{Cu}$ to 1 % accuracy, dHvA is still the more reliable method.

Moreover, ARPES is surface sensitive (escape depth $5$–$20\,\text{Å}$), so preparing a clean surface (cleaving in ultrahigh vacuum) is essential. dHvA, on the other hand, probes the bulk. The two are not competitors but stand in a complementary relationship.

18.8.4 Comparison with first-principles calculations

Physical Meaning: The Fermi surface is the most stringent test of band theory

First-principles calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) are by now an everyday tool. If we compute $\epsilon_n(\kk)$ and draw the isosurface $\epsilon_n(\kk)=\epsilon_F$, we obtain the Fermi surface. We then compute its cross-sectional areas to obtain the dHvA frequencies and compare them with experiment—this is the most stringent test of band theory.

The reason is simple: dHvA frequencies can be measured to a precision of $10^{-3}$. For “integrated quantities” such as the total energy or the lattice constant, small errors are averaged out and hidden, but the Fermi surface is a fine geometric feature at the single point $\epsilon_F$, so a shift of the bands by only $0.05\,\mathrm{eV}$ changes a cross-sectional area by several percent. There is no room for fudging.

What is the result?

  • Simple metals and noble metals: DFT (LDA/GGA) reproduces the Fermi surface to within a few percent. The ratio of the neck to the belly of $\mathrm{Cu}$ also agrees. This is a great triumph of band theory.
  • Transition metals: The agreement is generally good, but $m_c$ often comes out smaller than in experiment. The difference is explained by the renormalization due to the electron–phonon interaction and electron correlation ($m^*=m_{\rm band}(1+\lambda)$). Extracting $\lambda$ from the ratio of the measured $m_c$ to the calculated $m_{\rm band}$ is a standard technique in superconductivity research.
  • Strongly correlated systems: In heavy-fermion systems ($\mathrm{CeCu_6}$, $\mathrm{UPt_3}$, etc.), $m_c/m$ reaches $100$–$1000$, which disagrees with the DFT prediction by orders of magnitude. However, the shape and volume of the Fermi surface often agree—this is because Luttinger's theorem (Section 18.1) is at work. Methods such as dynamical mean-field theory were developed to explain the situation in which “the shape agrees but the mass does not.”

VASP, which the Mochizuki Research Group uses on a daily basis, can likewise compute the band structure, find the bands that cross $\epsilon_F$, and draw the Fermi surface. In doing so, how to choose a $\kk$ path such as $\Gamma$–$X$–$M$–$R$ is determined by the representation theory of space groups in Chapter 11. The crystal structure in Part I, the symmetry in Part II, and the physical properties in Part III—these three meet on the single surface that is the Fermi surface.

18.9 Summary and Exercises

18.9.1 Summary

  • The Fermi surface $\epsilon_n(\kk)=\epsilon_F$ determines everything about a metal. Because $-\partial f/\partial\epsilon$ is sharply localized at $\epsilon_F$, the conductivity, the specific heat, and the density of states all collapse into surface integrals over the Fermi surface. In $\mathrm{Cu}$ at room temperature, fewer than 0.4 % of all electrons are able to move.
  • The Harrison construction: draw a sphere of radius $k_F$ about every reciprocal lattice point; the region covered $m$ or more times is the occupied region of the $m$th band. The logic is simply that we are counting “the number of $\GG$ satisfying $\abs{\kk+\GG}<k_F$.” For a two-dimensional square lattice with valence two, the first band acquires a hole pocket at the $M$ point and the second band an electron lens at the $X$ point, and the two areas are exactly equal (a compensated metal).
  • A periodic potential makes the Fermi surface meet the zone boundary perpendicularly. The essential point is that in the $2\times2$ secular equation, $\epsilon^0_\kk-\epsilon^0_{\kk-\GG}=\frac{\hbar^2G}{m}q_\parallel$ is a function of $q_\parallel$ alone. The total volume is conserved by Luttinger's theorem.
  • In a magnetic field, $\kk$ goes around the intersection of “the constant-energy surface with a plane perpendicular to $\BB$.” This follows from the two triple products $\dot\kk\cdot\BB=0$ and $\dot\kk\cdot\vv=0$. There are three kinds of orbits: electron orbits, hole orbits, and open orbits. When an open orbit exists, the magnetoresistance does not saturate and depends sharply on the direction of the magnetic field.
  • The real-space orbit is the $\kk$-space orbit rotated by $90^\circ$ and multiplied by $\hbar c/eB$: $\rr_\perp=-\frac{\hbar c}{eB}\hat B\times\kk$. For $\mathrm{Cu}$ at $10\,\mathrm{T}$ it is about $0.9\,\mu\mathrm{m}$.
  • The tight-binding approximation: from $\psi_\kk=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_m\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\RR_m}\phi(\rr-\RR_m)$ we obtain $\epsilon(\kk)=-\alpha-\gamma\sum_{\rm nn}\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\bm\rho}$. For simple cubic, $-2\gamma\sum\cos k_ia$ ($W=12\gamma$); for bcc, $-8\gamma\,c_xc_yc_z$ ($W=16\gamma$); for fcc, $-4\gamma(c_xc_y+c_yc_z+c_zc_x)$ ($W=16\gamma\neq2z\gamma$, because of frustration). In every case $m^*=\hbar^2/2\gamma a^2$ at the band bottom.
  • The $d$ bands are narrow because the $3d$ orbitals are contracted close to the atomic nucleus and the overlap is small. Narrow $\Rightarrow$ $g(\epsilon_F)$ is large $\Rightarrow$ the electronic specific heat is large, the Stoner criterion is easily satisfied, and $U/W$ is large so that correlations matter. This is why $\gamma_{el}$ of $\mathrm{Cu}$ and $\mathrm{Ni}$ differ by a factor of 10.
  • The pseudopotential: orthogonality to the core states acts as a repulsion $V_R=\sum_c(E-E_c)\ket{c}\bra{c}$ and cancels the strong attraction $U$ (Phillips–Kleinman). The Fourier component of the empty-core model is $U_\GG=-\frac{4\pi e^2}{\Omega G^2}\cos(GR_c)$. For $\mathrm{Na}$, $-1584\,\mathrm{eV}$ becomes $0.4\,\mathrm{eV}$.
  • The Onsager quantization condition: from $\oint\pp\cdot\dd\rr=(n+\gamma)2\pi\hbar$ (this $\gamma$ is a phase correction and is distinct from both the hopping integral $\gamma$ above and the electronic specific heat coefficient $\gamma_{el}$), the magnetic flux through the real-space orbit is quantized as $\Phi_n=(n+\gamma)hc/e$, and translated into $\kk$ space this gives $S_n=(n+\gamma)\frac{2\pi eB}{\hbar c}$. Therefore $\Delta(1/B)=\frac{2\pi e}{\hbar cS}$. Merely by measuring the period of the oscillations, the extremal cross-sectional area of the Fermi surface is determined in absolute value.
  • Real metals: the alkali metals are almost perfect spheres ($k_F/k_N=0.877$); the noble metals have eight necks along $\{111\}$; $\mathrm{Al}$ has a second-zone hole surface plus a third-zone electron ring, giving $R_H>0$ in high fields (one hole per atom); and $\mathrm{Bi}$ is a semimetal with $n\sim3\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$.
  • Measurement methods: dHvA/SdH (cross-sectional area and $m_c$), cyclotron resonance ($m_c$), ARPES ($k_\parallel=0.512\sqrt{E_{\rm kin}}\sin\theta$, especially suited to two-dimensional systems), and positron annihilation. As a test of first-principles calculations, dHvA is the most stringent.

Answer the following for $\epsilon(\kk)=-\alpha-2\gamma(\cos k_xa+\cos k_ya)$.

(2) Show that $\epsilon_F=-\alpha$ at half filling (one electron per lattice point), and find the shape of the Fermi surface.

Perfect nesting and the van Hove singularity give rise to instabilities toward antiferromagnetism, charge-density waves, and superconductivity.
