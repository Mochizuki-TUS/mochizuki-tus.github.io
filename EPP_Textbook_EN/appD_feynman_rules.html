Appendix DSummary of the Feynman Rules (Tree Level)

This appendix collects the Feynman rules scattered through Chapters 8 to 14 of this book in a form that can be looked up on a single page. When, in the middle of a calculation, you wonder “what was the numerator of the $W$ propagator?” or “was the outgoing antiparticle $v$ or $\bar v$?”, you can simply come back here. Everything is given in tables and figures, and proofs are limited to pointers to the relevant places in the main text.

The essence of the Feynman rules is that they are a dictionary from pictures to formulas. Done honestly, the perturbative expansion of a field theory is a long job: one contracts the $n$th-order term of the Dyson series with Wick’s theorem and sorts out the result. The Feynman rules are nothing other than the outcome of that job, extracted and packaged as a mechanical correspondence: “a propagator for each line, a coupling constant for each point, a spinor at each end.” Once we have this dictionary, no field operator ever appears when we write down a $2\to2$ scattering amplitude. We draw the diagram, multiply the factors, and read off the result.

This “mechanical” character tends to be underestimated, but it is in fact of decisive importance. Precisely because the calculation is mechanical, it can be left to a computer. In today’s high-energy experiments, programs such as FeynArts / FormCalc, MadGraph, and CalcHEP implement exactly the tables of this appendix, automatically generating hundreds of diagrams and integrating them numerically. A human writes by hand only the few tree-level diagrams, but being able to write those few by hand is the surest basis for judging whether the computer’s output can be trusted. This appendix is the toolbox for “writing those few by hand.”

There is one more point we want to stress. This dictionary is not the exclusive property of particle physics. The propagator is a Green function, the vertex factor is a matrix element of the coupling, and a closed loop is a susceptibility. When one draws diagrams in condensed matter physics, one uses the same dictionary. The only differences are that a Fermi sea takes the place of the vacuum, and lattice translation symmetry takes the place of Lorentz invariance. In the “Connection to Materials Science” boxes placed in Sections D.3 through D.5, we show, with equations rather than analogies, that the rules of this appendix appear with the same structure in the Lindhard function, the RPA dielectric function, the electron–phonon coupling, and the Bogoliubov–de Gennes equation (we also state the range in which the correspondence holds).

Finally, in Section D.7 we take the most basic process, $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$, and work it through from beginning to end, following the procedure of Section D.1 strictly. Starting from drawing the diagram, we write down every step, skipping none, until we reach a number that can be compared with experiment, $\sigma = 86.9\ \mathrm{nb}/s[\mathrm{GeV^2}]$. If you can reproduce this one example yourself, you should be able to follow every calculation in this book on your own.

D.1 Procedure — Nine Steps from the Diagram to $\mathcal{M}$

We first show the overall flow. A calculation with the Feynman rules consists, without exception, of the following nine steps. However complicated the process and whatever the interaction, this order does not change. All that changes is the content of the factors assigned in Steps 3–5.

Definition: Procedure for applying the Feynman rules (tree level)

  1. Draw the diagram. Draw all topologically distinct diagrams connecting the given initial and final states. Decide in advance the order (the number of vertices) that you require. A diagram with $n$ vertices contributes to the amplitude as $g^n$.
  2. Assign momenta. Label the external lines $p_1,p_2,\dots$ (the convention of this book is to take the incoming ones as $1,2$ and the outgoing ones as $3,4$) and the internal lines $q_1,q_2,\dots$, and draw on each line an arrow showing its direction. On each fermion line, always put an arrow representing the flow of particle number.
  3. External-line factors. Following the table in Section D.2, assign to each external line $1$ (spin 0), $u,\bar u,v,\bar v$ (spin $1/2$), or $\epsilon_\mu,\epsilon_\mu^*$ (spin 1).
  4. Vertex factors. Following the table in Section D.4, place a factor at each vertex. For QED it is $-\ii g_eQ_f\gamma^\mu$ (for the electron, $\ii g_e\gamma^\mu$), for QCD it is $-\ii g_s\gamma^\mu(\lambda^a/2)_{ij}$, and so on.
  5. Propagators. Following the table in Section D.3, place a factor on each internal line. The denominator is always $q^2-m^2$ ($+\ii\epsilon$).
  6. Momentum conservation. Place $(2\pi)^4\delta^4(k_1+k_2+\cdots)$ at each vertex (take the sum with all momenta oriented inward).
  7. Integration over internal momenta. For each internal line, multiply by $\displaystyle\int\frac{\dd^4q}{(2\pi)^4}$.
  8. Overall factors and signs. Multiply by $(-1)$ and a trace for each closed fermion loop, by a relative sign $(-1)$ between diagrams obtained by exchanging the external lines of identical fermions, and by a symmetry factor $1/S$ for diagrams in which identical internal lines can be interchanged.
  9. Read off $\mathcal{M}$. The $\delta^4$ functions of Step 6 are eaten by the integrals of Step 7, and finally only a single $(2\pi)^4\delta^4(p_1+p_2-p_3-p_4)$, expressing overall momentum conservation, remains. What is left after removing it is $-\ii\mathcal{M}$.

The notation “$-\ii\mathcal{M}$” in Step 9 may be puzzling. It is a historical convention: to match the fact that the first-order term in the expansion of the $S$ matrix is $-\ii\int\dd^4x\,\Ham_{\rm int}$, this book (in the style of Griffiths and of Bjorken–Drell) defines $S = 1 - \ii\,(2\pi)^4\delta^4(\textstyle\sum p)\,\mathcal{M}$. If we multiply the factors according to the rules, what comes out is $-\ii\mathcal{M}$. Finally, we just multiply by $\ii$ to reverse the sign. In the other style, which defines $S = 1 + \ii T$ and $\braket{f|\ii T|i} = (2\pi)^4\delta^4(\textstyle\sum p)\,\ii\mathcal{M}$ (Peskin–Schroeder), exactly the same product of factors is read as $+\ii\mathcal{M}$. Only the overall sign of $\mathcal{M}$ is reversed, and the observable $\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2$ is the same in both. Note also that the overall phase of $\mathcal{M}$ does not enter observations, so for a process with only one diagram there is no need to worry about this sign. For a process in which two or more diagrams interfere, only the relative sign matters, so it is essential to keep to the same convention to the end.

D.1.1 A pitfall in Step 1 — what does “all diagrams” mean?

The instruction of Step 1 to “draw all topologically distinct diagrams” is in practice the easiest place to make mistakes. The criteria for judging are as follows.

Caution: Same diagram or different diagram?

(1) Reconnecting the external lines gives a different diagram. For example, in $e^-e^-\to e^-e^-$ (Møller scattering) there are two possibilities, depending on which outgoing electron comes from which incoming electron (the $t$-channel and the $u$-channel). Both must be added. Moreover, since this is an exchange of identical fermions, the relative sign is $-1$ (Section D.5.3).

(2) Reordering the vertices gives a different diagram. In Compton scattering $\gamma e^-\to\gamma e^-$, there are two diagrams: one in which the photon is absorbed first and emitted later (the $s$-channel), and one in which it is emitted first and absorbed later (the $u$-channel).

(3) A diagram merely deformed by bending the lines is the same diagram. A Feynman diagram is a topological object, and the lengths and curvature of the lines have no meaning. All that matters is which line is connected to which vertex.

(4) A diagram with only the direction of an internal line reversed is the same diagram. However, the direction of the arrow on a fermion line carries physical information (particle or antiparticle), so it must not be reversed.

If in doubt, it suffices to judge whether the connectivity of vertices and lines (the graph) is isomorphic, keeping the labels of the external lines fixed.

D.1.2 When does Step 8 matter?

In a tree-level (loop-free) calculation, the only part of Step 8 that actually matters is “the relative sign from exchanging identical fermions.” Closed fermion loops appear only in loop diagrams, and the symmetry factor is always $S=1$ for a tree diagram with fixed external lines, because such a diagram has no automorphisms (Section D.5.4). Therefore as long as we stay at tree level, we may remember Step 8 simply as “multiply by $-1$ the diagram in which identical fermions are exchanged”. When you go on to loops, just return to Section D.5.

Why this order?

There is a reason for the order of the procedure. The factors attached in Steps 3–5 are all determined by local information (information about that line or that point alone). Steps 6–8, on the other hand, require global information (the topology of the whole diagram). The order goes from local to global.

This structure is no accident. The interactions of a field theory are local (products of fields at the same spacetime point), and each term of the perturbative expansion is “local interaction points glued together with propagators as the glue.” How the glue is applied is determined locally, and how the pieces are joined matters globally. That the rules for Feynman diagrams can be written in such simple language as “lines and points” is thanks to the locality of the theory.

D.2 External Lines

An external line is a line that enters or leaves at the edge of a diagram and represents a real particle. “Real” means that the momentum lies on the mass shell ($p^2=m^2$); hence no propagator is attached to an external line. What is attached is the wave function of that particle itself.

D.2.1 Spin 0

The plane-wave solution for a spin-0 particle (Higgs, the $\pi$ meson, A, B, and C of the ABC model of Chapter 8, etc.) is just the phase factor $\ee^{-\ii p\cdot x}$, and since there is no spin degree of freedom, the external-line factor is

$$ \begin{equation} \text{external line of spin 0 (incoming and outgoing)}\;:\qquad 1 \label{eq:D-ext-scalar} \end{equation} $$

That is, “no factor is multiplied,” and this is why the Feynman rules were particularly simple in the ABC model of Chapter 8.

D.2.2 Spin $1/2$ — distinguishing the four cases carefully

This is where mistakes are most easily made. The factor differs in each of the $2\times2=4$ cases: incoming or outgoing, particle or antiparticle. The rules are as follows.

Definition: External-line factors for spin $1/2$

$$ \begin{equation} \begin{array}{ll} \text{incoming particle} & u(p,s)\\[2pt] \text{outgoing particle} & \bar u(p,s)\\[2pt] \text{incoming antiparticle} & \bar v(p,s)\\[2pt] \text{outgoing antiparticle} & v(p,s) \end{array} \label{eq:D-ext-fermion} \end{equation} $$

Here $u$ is the positive-energy solution of the Dirac equation $(\slashed p-m)u=0$, and $v$ is the negative-energy solution of $(\slashed p+m)v=0$ (that is, the antiparticle solution) (Chapter 9). We define $\bar u\equiv u^\dagger\gamma^0$ and $\bar v\equiv v^\dagger\gamma^0$. The symbol $s$ is a label specifying the spin direction, and there are two independent solutions, $s=1,2$.

Caution: The arrow is not the momentum

This is a point on which every beginner gets confused at least once. The arrow attached to a fermion line shows the flow of fermion number (particles minus antiparticles). The direction of the momentum $p$, on the other hand, has to be specified separately, and it is usually taken as “incoming particles go to the right, and outgoing particles also go to the right.” That is, on an external line of $e^+$, the momentum arrow and the fermion-number arrow point in opposite directions.

When drawing a diagram, it is safest to keep the two apart: draw the direction of the momentum with a small arrow outside the line, and the direction of fermion number with a triangle on the line. If they are confused, you will get the sign of $\slashed q$ in the propagator wrong.

The physical basis of this rule is in Chapter 9. If we expand the Dirac field as $\psi = \sum_s\int(a_s u\,\ee^{-\ii px} + b_s^\dagger v\,\ee^{+\ii px})$, then $\psi$ either “annihilates a particle” or “creates an antiparticle.” Both are operations that decrease the fermion number by $1$, and the place where $\psi$ is written (the right end) corresponds to the side of the diagram where the fermion-number arrow comes in.

D.2.3 The direction in which a fermion line is traced

Spinors and $\gamma$ matrices are matrices, so the order of multiplication matters. The rule can be stated in one sentence.

Theorem: Order of writing down a fermion line

The factors that appear along a single continuous fermion line (external-line spinors, vertex matrices, propagators) are arranged in the order obtained by following the line against the arrow, from left to right.

As a result, a single fermion line always takes the form

$$ \bigl[\bar u \text{ or } \bar v\bigr]\; \bigl(\text{vertex}\bigr)\bigl(\text{propagator}\bigr)\cdots\bigl(\text{vertex}\bigr)\; \bigl[u \text{ or } v\bigr] $$

and as a whole it becomes a $1\times4$ row vector $\times$ a $4\times4$ matrix $\times$ a $4\times1$ column vector $=$ a single complex number. No spinor index survives to the end.

Why do we trace the line against the arrow?

The reason is that in the interaction Hamiltonian (for QED, $\Ham_{\rm int}=-\Lag_{\rm int}=g_eQ_f\,\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi A_\mu$; for the electron $Q_e=-1$, so this is $-g_e\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi A_\mu$), $\bar\psi$ is written on the left and $\psi$ on the right. The operator $\psi$ “annihilates a particle / creates an antiparticle,” and $\bar\psi$ “creates a particle / annihilates an antiparticle”; in matrix multiplication the factor on the right acts first. Therefore what “came in first” is on the right, and what “goes out later” is on the left.

Each time we pass through a vertex, this $\bar\psi\cdots\psi$ pattern is stacked up once more, so the direction in which we trace the line is necessarily against the flow of fermion number. This is less a rule than a natural consequence of the fact that $\gamma$ matrices are matrices.

D.2.4 Spin 1

An external line of a spin-1 vector particle (photon, gluon, $W$, $Z$) carries a polarization vector $\epsilon^\mu$.

Definition: External-line factors for spin 1

$$ \begin{equation} \text{incoming vector particle}\;:\;\epsilon_\mu(p,\lambda), \qquad \text{outgoing vector particle}\;:\;\epsilon_\mu^{*}(p,\lambda) \label{eq:D-ext-vector} \end{equation} $$

Here $\lambda$ is the polarization (helicity) label. The polarization vector satisfies the transversality condition

$$ p^\mu\epsilon_\mu = 0 $$

and the normalization $\epsilon^*\cdot\epsilon=-1$. The number of independent polarizations is 2 for a massless particle ($\lambda=\pm1$) and 3 for a massive one ($\lambda=0,\pm1$).

As with the spinors $u,\bar u$, the complex conjugate is attached on the outgoing side. The index $\mu$ of $\epsilon$ is contracted with the index of the vertex to which that particle is attached.

Math Note: The sum over polarization vectors

When summing $\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2$ over polarizations, the following replacements can be used. For a vector particle of mass $m\neq0$, we have exactly

$$ \begin{equation} \sum_{\lambda=1}^{3}\epsilon_\mu(p,\lambda)\,\epsilon_\nu^{*}(p,\lambda) = -g_{\mu\nu}+\frac{p_\mu p_\nu}{m^2} \label{eq:D-polsum-massive} \end{equation} $$

For a massless photon there are only two independent polarizations, so the $m\to0$ limit of this expression cannot be taken. However, thanks to current conservation (the Ward identity, Section D.6.2) $p_\mu\mathcal{M}^\mu=0$, the terms proportional to $p_\mu p_\nu$ do not contribute to $\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2$. Therefore in practice we may make the replacement

$$ \begin{equation} \sum_{\lambda=1}^{2}\epsilon_\mu\epsilon_\nu^{*}\;\longrightarrow\;-g_{\mu\nu} \label{eq:D-polsum-massless} \end{equation} $$

Whether this replacement is allowed must always be judged after adding up all the diagrams. For a single diagram alone, the Ward identity does not hold.

Example: Writing the external-line factors for four processes

(1) $e^-\mu^-\to e^-\mu^-$: The electron line has $u(p_1)$ and $\bar u(p_3)$, and the muon line has $u(p_2)$ and $\bar u(p_4)$. There are no antiparticles.

(2) $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$: On the incoming side, $e^-$ gives $u(p_1)$ and $e^+$ gives $\bar v(p_2)$. On the outgoing side, $\mu^-$ gives $\bar u(p_3)$ and $\mu^+$ gives $v(p_4)$. The electron line is arranged as $\bar v(p_2)\cdots u(p_1)$ and the muon line as $\bar u(p_3)\cdots v(p_4)$.

(3) Compton scattering $\gamma e^-\to\gamma e^-$: The electron line has $u(p_1),\bar u(p_3)$. The photons give $\epsilon_\mu(p_2)$ (incoming) and $\epsilon_\nu^*(p_4)$ (outgoing).

(4) Pair annihilation $e^+e^-\to\gamma\gamma$: $u(p_1)$, $\bar v(p_2)$, and $\epsilon^*_\mu(p_3),\epsilon^*_\nu(p_4)$. There is only a single fermion line, $\bar v(p_2)\cdots u(p_1)$.

D.3 Propagators

The factor assigned to an internal line (a virtual particle) is the propagator. Every propagator has the common form

$$ \begin{equation} \text{propagator} \;=\; \frac{\ii\times(\text{numerator from the spin sum})}{q^2-m^2+\ii\epsilon} \label{eq:D-prop-general} \end{equation} $$

The denominator $q^2-m^2$ is the same for any spin, and it measures “how far the virtual particle is off the mass shell.” The smaller the deviation, the smaller the denominator and the larger the amplitude. The divergence at $q^2\to m^2$ is the resonance (Chapter 8, Section 8.7.4; Appendix B, Section B.4). The numerator is the part that counts the spin degrees of freedom: it is $1$ for spin 0, $\slashed q+m$ for spin $1/2$, and $-(g^{\mu\nu}-q^\mu q^\nu/m^2)$ for spin 1. That is, the numerator is the sum over the polarization states of that spin.

D.3.1 The individual propagators

Definition: List of propagators

(1) Scalar (spin 0, mass $m$):

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{\ii}{q^2-m^2+\ii\epsilon} \label{eq:D-prop-scalar} \end{equation} $$

(2) Fermion (spin $1/2$, mass $m$):

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{\ii(\slashed q+m)}{q^2-m^2+\ii\epsilon} = \frac{\ii}{\slashed q-m+\ii\epsilon} \label{eq:D-prop-fermion} \end{equation} $$

(3) Massive vector ($W^\pm$, $Z$, mass $m$, unitary gauge):

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{-\ii\left(g_{\mu\nu}-\dfrac{q_\mu q_\nu}{m^2}\right)}{q^2-m^2+\ii\epsilon} \label{eq:D-prop-massive} \end{equation} $$

(4) Photon (mass 0, Feynman gauge):

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{-\ii g_{\mu\nu}}{q^2+\ii\epsilon} \label{eq:D-prop-photon} \end{equation} $$

(5) Gluon (mass 0, Feynman gauge, colors $a,b$):

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{-\ii g_{\mu\nu}\,\delta^{ab}}{q^2+\ii\epsilon} \label{eq:D-prop-gluon} \end{equation} $$

The second expression for the fermion propagator is obtained simply by rationalizing the denominator with $(\slashed q-m)(\slashed q+m)=q^2-m^2$ (which follows from the Clifford algebra of Appendix C). This form shows the propagator’s origin as the “inverse matrix of the Dirac operator,” so it is worth remembering as well.

D.3.2 The meaning of the numerator — the spin sum is hiding there

Derivation: Why is the numerator of the fermion propagator $\slashed q+m$?

Step 1: Definition of the propagator. The propagator is the vacuum expectation value (two-point function) for creating a field at the spacetime point $x$ and annihilating it at $y$:

$$ S_F(x-y)=\braket{0|T\{\psi(x)\bar\psi(y)\}|0} $$

Here $T$ denotes the time-ordered product.

Step 2: Expand the fields and contract. Inserting the plane-wave expansion of $\psi$, the particle contribution survives when $x^0>y^0$ and the antiparticle contribution survives when $x^0<y^0$. The former gives $\sum_s u(p,s)\bar u(p,s)$ and the latter gives $\sum_s v(p,s)\bar v(p,s)$.

Step 3: Use the completeness relation. By the completeness relations of Appendix C,

$$ \sum_{s=1}^{2}u(p,s)\bar u(p,s)=\slashed p+m, \qquad \sum_{s=1}^{2}v(p,s)\bar v(p,s)=\slashed p-m $$

Step 4: Fourier transform and add. Combining the two contributions and moving to four-momentum space, Feynman’s $\ii\epsilon$ prescription (the choice of how to go around the poles in the $q^0$ plane; Appendix A, Section A.4.3) combines the two contributions into exactly one covariant expression:

$$ \tilde S_F(q)=\frac{\ii(\slashed q+m)}{q^2-m^2+\ii\epsilon} $$

Step 5: Read off the meaning. The numerator $\slashed q+m$ expresses “the sum over the two spin states of the particle, if $q$ is on the mass shell.” That is, the numerator of a propagator is the sum over the internal states the particle can take. For spin 0 there is one state, so the numerator is $1$; for spin 1 (massive) there are three states, so the numerator is $-(g_{\mu\nu}-q_\mu q_\nu/m^2)$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:D-polsum-massive}. The three rules come from one and the same principle.

D.3.3 Gauge dependence of the photon propagator

Only the photon propagator has a value that depends on the choice of gauge. In a general $R_\xi$ gauge,

$$ \begin{equation} D_{\mu\nu}(q)=\frac{-\ii}{q^2+\ii\epsilon}\left[g_{\mu\nu}-(1-\xi)\frac{q_\mu q_\nu}{q^2}\right] \label{eq:D-prop-rxi} \end{equation} $$

and $\xi=1$ is the Feynman gauge (the standard in this book), while $\xi=0$ is the Landau gauge.

Caution: Never mix gauges

The $\xi$-dependent term $q_\mu q_\nu/q^2$ never contributes to a physical amplitude. This is because the photon couples to the conserved current $j^\mu=\bar u\gamma^\mu u$, and $q_\mu j^\mu=0$ (the Ward identity, Section D.6.2). Therefore the final result is the same in any gauge.

However, this holds only “after adding all the diagrams”; each individual diagram depends on $\xi$. Computing one diagram and switching gauges partway through leads to errors. In one calculation, use a single $\xi$ from start to finish.

Conversely, this property provides a powerful check. If separate calculations in the Feynman gauge and the Landau gauge give the same answer, the calculation is very likely correct.

D.3.4 The low-energy limit — eliminating the heavy boson

In the exchange of a $W$ or $Z$, when the momentum transferred satisfies $\abs{q^2}\ll m_W^2$, the propagator Eq. \eqref{eq:D-prop-massive} becomes

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{-\ii\left(g_{\mu\nu}-q_\mu q_\nu/m_W^2\right)}{q^2-m_W^2} \;\xrightarrow{\;\abs{q^2}\ll m_W^2\;}\; \frac{+\ii g_{\mu\nu}}{m_W^2} \label{eq:D-prop-fermi} \end{equation} $$

a constant. The disappearance of the momentum dependence means that in position space the interaction is a $\delta$ function, that is, a contact interaction. This is Fermi’s four-fermion theory of Chapter 12, and the origin of the relation $G_F/\sqrt2=g_w^2/(8m_W^2)$. The $q_\mu q_\nu/m_W^2$ term produces one mass factor at each end of the current when the Dirac equation is used there, so relative to the $g_{\mu\nu}$ term it is suppressed by $m_fm_{f^\prime}/m_W^2$ (the product of the masses at the two ends). When the masses of the fermions it couples to are small, it may be dropped. (Conversely, helicity-suppressed decays such as $\pi\to\mu\nu$ are the cases in which this term plays the leading role.)

Connection to Materials Science: The propagator is the single-particle Green function itself

Let us take the nonrelativistic limit of the scalar propagator Eq. \eqref{eq:D-prop-scalar}. Measuring the energy from the rest mass, write $q^0=m+E$, and suppose $\abs{\bm q}\ll m$ and $\abs{E}\ll m$. Then

$$ q^2-m^2=(m+E)^2-\bm q^2-m^2=2mE+E^2-\bm q^2 \simeq 2m\left(E-\frac{\bm q^2}{2m}\right) $$

(the term $E^2$ is smaller than $2mE$ by a factor $E/2m$). Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{\ii}{q^2-m^2+\ii\epsilon} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{2m}\cdot\frac{\ii}{E-\dfrac{\bm q^2}{2m}+\ii\epsilon} \label{eq:D-nonrel-green} \end{equation} $$

The factor $\dfrac{1}{E-\varepsilon_{\bm q}+\ii\epsilon}$ on the right-hand side is precisely the retarded Green function of the Schrödinger equation. It is the same expression as $G^R(\bm k,\omega)=\left[\omega-\varepsilon_{\bm k}+\ii\eta\right]^{-1}$, which we used in “Introduction to Linear Response Theory,” Appendix C, “How to Read Feynman Diagrams,” and in the Green-function chapter of “Introduction to Density Functional Theory,” differing only in the letters.

The prefactor $1/2m$ is the factor that bridges the difference between the relativistic normalization (Appendix A, Section A.5) $\braket{p|p'}=2E(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\bm p-\bm p')$ and the nonrelativistic normalization $\braket{\bm p|\bm p'}=(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\bm p-\bm p')$; it is a product of convention, not of physics.

The same holds for the fermion propagator: in the nonrelativistic limit of $\dfrac{\ii(\slashed q+m)}{q^2-m^2}$ we have $\slashed q+m\to 2m\,\dfrac{1+\gamma^0}{2}$ (a projection onto the upper two components); the $2m$ cancels the $2m$ in the denominator, and what remains is the Green function $\left[E-\varepsilon_{\bm q}+\ii\eta\right]^{-1}\mathbb{1}_2$ for a two-component Pauli spinor. The only difference is that the place where the band structure $\varepsilon_{\bm k}$ enters is, in particle physics, $\sqrt{\bm q^2+m^2}$.

Because of this correspondence, the diagram technique of condensed matter (Abrikosov–Gor’kov–Dzyaloshinski) and the Feynman rules of particle physics become the same rules. There are only three differences. (i) Because there is a Fermi sea instead of the vacuum, the way the poles of the propagator are taken (the sign of $\ii\epsilon$) changes depending on whether $\varepsilon_{\bm k}$ is larger or smaller than $\varepsilon_F$. (ii) Because the symmetry is lattice translation instead of Lorentz invariance, momentum is conserved within the Brillouin zone (up to a reciprocal lattice vector $\bm G$). (iii) At finite temperature, one uses imaginary time $\tau\in[0,\beta]$ instead of real time $t$, and $\int\dd q^0/2\pi$ becomes the Matsubara sum $T\sum_n$. The skeleton of the rules does not change at all.

Connection to Materials Science: The Nambu-space propagator and the BdG equation

The numerator $\slashed q+m$ of the fermion propagator is a $4\times4$ matrix written with the $\gamma$ matrices. Written out in the Dirac–Pauli representation,

$$ \slashed q+m=\begin{pmatrix} q^0+m & -\bm q\cdot\bm\sigma\\ \bm q\cdot\bm\sigma & -q^0+m\end{pmatrix} $$

it has the structure “energies $\pm$ on the diagonal and coupling off the diagonal.” The Green function of a superconductor (the Gor’kov equation), for the Nambu spinor $\Psi_{\bm k}=(c_{\bm k\uparrow},\,c^\dagger_{-\bm k\downarrow})^{\mathsf T}$, is

$$ \begin{equation} \hat G(\bm k,\ii\omega_n) =\frac{\ii\omega_n\,\tau_0+\xi_{\bm k}\,\tau_3+\Delta\,\tau_1}{(\ii\omega_n)^2-\xi_{\bm k}^2-\abs{\Delta}^2} \label{eq:D-gorkov} \end{equation} $$

(where $\tau_i$ are the Pauli matrices in Nambu space and $\xi_{\bm k}=\varepsilon_{\bm k}-\mu$). Compare it with Eq. \eqref{eq:D-prop-fermion}. In both, the denominator has the same form, “energy squared $-$ (momentum squared $+$ mass squared)”. However, the matrix structure of the numerators does not correspond one to one. In the Dirac numerator $\slashed q+m$, the mass $m$ multiplies the identity matrix and the energy $q^0$ multiplies $\gamma^0$, whereas in the numerator of Eq. \eqref{eq:D-gorkov}, $\ii\omega_n$ multiplies the identity matrix $\tau_0$, $\xi_{\bm k}$ multiplies $\tau_3$, and $\Delta$ multiplies $\tau_1$. What lines up cleanly is that both have the form of “the inverse matrix of $(\text{energy})-(\text{linear Hamiltonian})$,” that is, $\hat G=(\ii\omega_n-H_{\rm BdG})^{-1}$ and $(\slashed q-m)^{-1}$. Placing the Hamiltonians side by side makes the structural agreement easy to see: the BdG Hamiltonian $H_{\rm BdG}=\xi_{\bm k}\tau_3+\Delta\tau_1$ and the Dirac Hamiltonian $H_D=\bm\alpha\cdot\bm p+\beta m$ are both a sum of two mutually anticommuting matrices, and that is exactly why the cross terms vanish when they are squared, giving $E^2=\xi_{\bm k}^2+\abs{\Delta}^2$ and $E^2=\bm p^2+m^2$. The correspondence is

$$ q^0\leftrightarrow\ii\omega_n,\qquad \abs{\bm q}\leftrightarrow\xi_{\bm k},\qquad m\leftrightarrow\Delta $$

and the superconducting gap $\Delta$ plays the role of the Dirac mass $m$. This is why the quasiparticle dispersion $E_{\bm k}=\sqrt{\xi_{\bm k}^2+\abs{\Delta}^2}$ has exactly the same form as the relativistic dispersion $E=\sqrt{\bm p^2+m^2}$.

This correspondence is not merely formal. As we saw in Chapter 13, superconductivity is spontaneous breaking of the $\mathrm{U}(1)$ symmetry, and it has the same structure as the Higgs mechanism in that the gauge field (the photon) acquires a mass (the Anderson–Higgs mechanism). $\Delta$ is the order parameter and at the same time the “mass acquired by the quasiparticle.” Moreover, the component of Eq. \eqref{eq:D-gorkov} proportional to $\tau_1$ (the anomalous Green function $F=\braket{cc}$) is a propagation that does not conserve particle number, and it has the same structure as the propagator of the Majorana neutrino (of the $\braket{\psi\psi}$ type), in which the arrows point toward each other in Figure 14.6(a) of Chapter 14, Section 14.7.2. A materials scientist solving the BdG equation is, without knowing it, handling a Majorana-type propagator.

Math Note: Unstable particles need a width

When a short-lived particle such as the $Z$ boson is exchanged in the $s$ channel and $q^2$ comes close to $m_Z^2$ (as in $e^+e^-\to Z\to f\bar f$), the denominator of the propagator vanishes and the expression diverges. To avoid this, we use the Breit–Wigner form, which puts the decay width $\Gamma$ into the denominator:

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{1}{q^2-m^2}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{1}{q^2-m^2+\ii m\Gamma} \label{eq:D-breitwigner} \end{equation} $$

(Chapter 8, Section 8.7.4; Appendix B, Section B.4). This is the result of adding in the imaginary part of the self-energy, and the $\ii$ in $\ii m\Gamma$ expresses that “the particle is disappearing.” Taking $\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2$ gives

$$ \abs{\mathcal{M}}^2\propto\frac{1}{(q^2-m^2)^2+m^2\Gamma^2} $$

and a resonance curve appears, with a peak at $\sqrt{q^2}=m$ and a full width at half maximum $\Gamma$. For the $Z$, $\Gamma_Z=2.4952\ \mathrm{GeV}$. It is exactly the same prescription as expressing the lifetime of a quasiparticle in condensed matter by $\omega-\varepsilon_{\bm k}+\ii/2\tau$ in the Green function.

D.4 Vertex Factors

The vertex factors are the content of the theory itself. The external-line factors and the propagators are fixed only by “what kind of particle it is,” whereas the vertex factors are fixed by “what kind of interaction it is.” The task of writing down the Lagrangian of the Standard Model is, in practice, nothing other than writing the tables of this section.

The common construction is as follows. From each term $\Lag_{\rm int}$ of the interaction Lagrangian, remove the fields that appear in it, replace each derivative $\partial_\mu$ by $-\ii k_\mu$ (the incoming momentum), and multiply the whole by $\ii$. In a term in which the same field appears $n$ times, there are $n!$ ways of assigning the fields, so this factor is also included.

$$ \begin{equation} \text{vertex factor}=\ii\times\left.\frac{\delta^n \Lag_{\rm int}}{\delta\phi_1\cdots\delta\phi_n}\right|_{\partial_\mu\to-\ii k_\mu} \label{eq:D-vertex-recipe} \end{equation} $$

D.4.1 QED

Definition: The QED vertex

The vertex factor corresponding to the interaction $\Lag_{\rm int}=-g_e Q_f\,\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi\,A_\mu$ is

$$ \begin{equation} -\ii\,g_e\,Q_f\,\gamma^\mu \label{eq:D-vertex-qed} \end{equation} $$

(this is the result of applying the recipe of Eq. \eqref{eq:D-vertex-recipe} directly, and it is consistent with the $\Lag_{\rm int}$ above, including the overall sign). Here $Q_f$ is the charge of the fermion measured in units of the elementary charge ($-1$ for $e^-,\mu^-,\tau^-$, $+2/3$ for $u,c,t$, and $-1/3$ for $d,s,b$). For the electron, substituting $Q_e=-1$ gives $\ii g_e\gamma^\mu$, which agrees with the convention of Table 10.2 in Chapter 10. The coupling constant is

$$ g_e=\sqrt{4\pi\alpha}=\sqrt{4\pi/137.036}=0.30282, \qquad \alpha=\frac{g_e^2}{4\pi}=\frac{1}{137.036} $$

(Heaviside–Lorentz units). Each additional vertex multiplies the amplitude by $g_e$ and the probability by $\alpha$.

The QED vertex is of only one kind: “two fermion lines and one photon line.” Since the photon has no charge, there is no $\gamma\gamma\gamma$ vertex. This is the main reason why QED is a computable theory.

D.4.2 QCD

Definition: The three QCD vertices

(1) Quark–gluon vertex (quark color $j\to i$, gluon color $a$):

$$ \begin{equation} -\ii\,g_s\,\gamma^\mu\left(\frac{\lambda^a}{2}\right)_{ij} \label{eq:D-vertex-qcd} \end{equation} $$

Here $\lambda^a$ ($a=1,\dots,8$) are the Gell-Mann matrices, and $T^a=\lambda^a/2$ are the generators of $\mathrm{SU}(3)$. With $\alpha_s\equiv g_s^2/4\pi$ and $\alpha_s(m_Z)=0.118$, we obtain $g_s=\sqrt{4\pi\times0.118}=1.218$.

(2) Three-gluon vertex (all momenta $k_1,k_2,k_3$ taken as incoming, with $k_1+k_2+k_3=0$):

$$ \begin{equation} -g_s f^{abc}\Bigl[g^{\mu\nu}(k_1-k_2)^\rho+g^{\nu\rho}(k_2-k_3)^\mu+g^{\rho\mu}(k_3-k_1)^\nu\Bigr] \label{eq:D-vertex-3g} \end{equation} $$

Here $f^{abc}$ are the structure constants of $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ ($[T^a,T^b]=\ii f^{abc}T^c$). Note the cyclic symmetry of the indices: the expression is invariant under the cyclic permutation $(a\mu k_1)\to(b\nu k_2)\to(c\rho k_3)\to(a\mu k_1)$.

(3) Four-gluon vertex:

$$ \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} -\ii g_s^2\Bigl[&f^{abe}f^{cde}\left(g^{\mu\rho}g^{\nu\sigma}-g^{\mu\sigma}g^{\nu\rho}\right)\\ +&f^{ace}f^{bde}\left(g^{\mu\nu}g^{\rho\sigma}-g^{\mu\sigma}g^{\nu\rho}\right)\\ +&f^{ade}f^{bce}\left(g^{\mu\nu}g^{\rho\sigma}-g^{\mu\rho}g^{\nu\sigma}\right)\Bigr] \end{aligned} \label{eq:D-vertex-4g} \end{equation} $$

This vertex does not depend on the momenta (it contains no derivatives).

Why do only gluons couple to themselves?

The photon has no charge, but the gluon carries color charge. More precisely, $\mathrm{U}(1)$ is an Abelian group, so its structure constants vanish ($f^{abc}=0$), whereas $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ is non-Abelian, so $f^{abc}\neq0$. The fact that Eq. \eqref{eq:D-vertex-3g} and Eq. \eqref{eq:D-vertex-4g} are proportional to $f^{abc}$ directly expresses this difference.

These two vertices are the origin of the asymptotic freedom of Chapter 11. Of the coefficient $\beta_0=11-\tfrac23 n_f$ of the $\beta$ function, the $11$ comes from the gluon self-coupling (antiscreening), and the $-\tfrac23 n_f$ comes from quark loops (screening). If $n_f\le16$, then $\beta_0>0$ and $\alpha_s$ becomes small at high energy. Indeed, for $n_f=6$ we have $\beta_0=7$.

D.4.3 Charged weak interaction

Definition: The charged-current ($W^\pm$) vertex

(1) Leptons (connecting $\ell=e,\mu,\tau$ with their partner $\nu_\ell$):

$$ \begin{equation} -\ii\,\frac{g_w}{2\sqrt2}\,\gamma^\mu\left(1-\gamma^5\right) =-\ii\,\frac{g_w}{\sqrt2}\,\gamma^\mu P_L \label{eq:D-vertex-cc-lepton} \end{equation} $$

Here $P_L=(1-\gamma^5)/2$ is the left-handed projection operator. All three generations have the same $g_w$ (lepton universality). Vertices that cross generations do not exist (apart from $\nu$ oscillations; see Chapter 14).

(2) Quarks (connecting an up-type quark $u_i=(u,c,t)$ with a down-type quark $d_j=(d,s,b)$):

$$ \begin{equation} -\ii\,\frac{g_w}{2\sqrt2}\,V_{ij}\,\gamma^\mu\left(1-\gamma^5\right) \label{eq:D-vertex-cc-quark} \end{equation} $$

Here $V_{ij}$ is the CKM matrix (Chapter 12, Section 12.5.3). Transitions that cross generations occur only here.

The coupling constant is $g_w=0.6529$ (determined from $G_F$ and $m_W$; see Chapter 12). We have $g_w/g_e=2.156$, so the weak coupling is more than twice as strong as the electromagnetic one. It looks weak only because the $W$ is heavy.

D.4.4 Neutral weak interaction

Definition: The neutral-current ($Z^0$) vertex

$$ \begin{equation} -\ii\,\frac{g_z}{2}\,\gamma^\mu\left(c_V^f-c_A^f\gamma^5\right), \qquad g_z\equiv\frac{g_w}{\cos\theta_W} \label{eq:D-vertex-nc} \end{equation} $$

The coefficients are given by

$$ \begin{equation} c_V^f=T^3_f-2Q_f\sin^2\theta_W, \qquad c_A^f=T^3_f \label{eq:D-cvca} \end{equation} $$

Here $T^3_f$ is the third component of weak isospin ($+1/2$ for the upper member and $-1/2$ for the lower member of a left-handed doublet), and $Q_f$ is the electric charge. In this appendix we use the effective mixing angle $\sin^2\theta_W=0.2312$ ($\overline{\rm MS}$) to evaluate $c_V^f$, and we take $g_z=g_w/\cos\theta_W=0.6529/0.87681=0.7446$. Note that $g_w=0.6529$ here is the value determined from $G_F$ and $m_W$, so different conventions for the definition of the mixing angle are mixed. If we align everything with the on-shell definition $\cos\theta_W=m_W/m_Z=0.8814$ ($\sin^2\theta_W=0.2231$), we obtain $g_z=\sqrt{4\sqrt2G_Fm_Z^2}=0.7407$, which is smaller by about $0.5\%$. In a tree-level estimate, this difference of less than $1\%$ falls within the electroweak radiative corrections.

The $Z$ vertex does not change the flavor (absence of FCNC, the GIM mechanism). This is why neutral-current transitions such as $s\to d$ do not occur at tree level.

In Table D.4, $c_V^e=-0.0376$ is smaller than the others by about an order of magnitude. This is because $\sin^2\theta_W$ happens to be close to $1/4$ ($0.2312\simeq0.25$), a situation close to the “coincidence” in which $c_V^e=-1/2+2\times0.25=0$.

This smallness has two experimental consequences. First, on the $Z$ pole ($\sqrt s=m_Z$, where the $\gamma$–$Z$ interference vanishes), the forward–backward asymmetry of $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$ is $A_{FB}^0=\tfrac34A_eA_\mu$ with $A_f=2c_V^fc_A^f/[(c_V^f)^2+(c_A^f)^2]$, so the smallness of $c_V^\ell$ translates directly into the smallness of $A_{FB}$. As a result, the determination of $\sin^2\theta_W$ becomes sharply sensitive (because $c_V$ is sensitive to changes in $\sin^2\theta_W$). At energies below the pole, on the other hand, the interference term survives, and $A_{FB}\simeq\tfrac32\chi\,c_A^ec_A^\mu$ is determined by $c_A$ (Section D.7.6). This is why LEP was able to determine $\sin^2\theta_W$ to a precision of $10^{-4}$. Second, in measurements of atomic parity nonconservation (in Cs atoms and others), the same smallness of $c_V$ enhances the sensitivity to “deviations from the Standard Model.”

Incidentally, the contribution of each channel to the total decay width of the $Z$ is proportional to $c_V^2+c_A^2$ (times the color factor $N_c=3$ for quarks). Using the rightmost column of the table, $\Gamma(Z\to\nu\bar\nu)$ per generation is $0.5$ and $\Gamma(Z\to e^+e^-)$ is $0.2514$, and the ratio is $1.99$. Dividing the experimental value $\Gamma_{\rm inv}/\Gamma_{\ell\ell}=5.943$ by $0.5/0.2514=1.989$ gives $2.99$, that is, the famous conclusion that there are three generations of neutrinos (Chapter 12).

D.4.5 Higgs couplings

Definition: The Higgs vertices

(1) Yukawa coupling ($H f\bar f$):

$$ \begin{equation} -\ii\,\frac{m_f}{v} =-\ii\,\frac{g_w\,m_f}{2m_W} \qquad(v=246.2\ \mathrm{GeV}) \label{eq:D-vertex-yukawa} \end{equation} $$

No $\gamma$ matrix appears (a scalar coupling). The important point is that it is proportional to the mass of the particle it couples to; as we derived in Chapter 13, this is because mass itself arises from the coupling to the Higgs.

(2) Coupling to gauge bosons ($HVV$):

$$ \begin{equation} \ii\,\frac{2m_V^2}{v}\,g^{\mu\nu} =\begin{cases} \ii\,g_w\,m_W\,g^{\mu\nu} & (HW^+W^-)\\[3pt] \ii\,g_z\,m_Z\,g^{\mu\nu} & (HZZ) \end{cases} \label{eq:D-vertex-hvv} \end{equation} $$

This one is proportional to the square of the mass. The $H\to\gamma\gamma$ vertex does not exist at tree level, and arises only through $W$ loops and top-quark loops (Chapter 13).

(3) Higgs self-coupling: The $HHH$ vertex is $-\ii\,3m_H^2/v$, and the $HHHH$ vertex is $-\ii\,3m_H^2/v^2$. Attempts to measure the former are being made at the HL-LHC.

Connection to Materials Science: The vertex factor is the electron–phonon coupling matrix element

The QED vertex $\ii g_e\gamma^\mu$ is “the amplitude for an electron to absorb or emit one photon.” The quantity that plays the same role in condensed matter is the electron–phonon coupling matrix element

$$ \begin{equation} g^{\nu}_{\bm k+\bm q,\bm k} =\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2M\omega_{\bm q\nu}}}\; \Bigl\langle \psi_{\bm k+\bm q}\Bigm| \bm e_{\bm q\nu}\cdot\frac{\partial V_{\rm SCF}}{\partial\bm u}\Bigm|\psi_{\bm k}\Bigr\rangle \label{eq:D-elph} \end{equation} $$

where $\bm u$ is the atomic displacement and $\bm e_{\bm q\nu}$ is the phonon eigenvector. (The contraction of $\partial V_{\rm SCF}/\partial\bm u$ with $\bm e_{\bm q\nu}$ is written $\partial_{\bm q\nu}V$ in Giustino’s notation. Note that this is not a derivative with respect to $\bm q$.) Setting the structures side by side, the correspondence is complete.

  • Incoming state: $u(p_1)$ in particle physics, $\ket{\psi_{\bm k}}$ in condensed matter.
  • Outgoing state: $\bar u(p_3)$ in particle physics, $\bra{\psi_{\bm k+\bm q}}$ in condensed matter.
  • Coupling operator: $g_e\gamma^\mu$ in particle physics, $\partial V_{\rm SCF}/\partial\bm u$ in condensed matter (the derivative of the self-consistent potential with respect to the atomic displacement).
  • External-line factor of a boson: $\epsilon_\mu$ in particle physics; in condensed matter, the eigenvector $\bm e_{\bm q\nu}$ and the zero-point amplitude $\sqrt{\hbar/2M\omega}$. This $1/\sqrt{2\omega}$ is exactly the same thing as the $1/\sqrt{2E}$ of relativistic normalization (Appendix A, Section A.5).
  • Momentum conservation: $\delta^4(\sum k)$ at the vertex in particle physics; in condensed matter, the subscript $\bm k+\bm q$ (conservation up to a reciprocal lattice vector).

Because of this correspondence, the Eliashberg theory of superconductivity can be written directly in terms of the Feynman rules. The Eliashberg function

$$ \alpha^2F(\omega)=\frac{1}{N(\varepsilon_F)}\sum_{\bm k\bm q\nu} \abs{g^\nu_{\bm k+\bm q,\bm k}}^2 \delta(\varepsilon_{\bm k})\delta(\varepsilon_{\bm k+\bm q})\delta(\omega-\omega_{\bm q\nu}) $$

is precisely “the square of the vertex factor $\times$ the density of states,” which has the same structure as the golden rule $\Gamma=2\pi\abs{M}^2\rho$ of Chapter 8. The coupling constant $\lambda=2\int\dd\omega\,\alpha^2F(\omega)/\omega$ is the dimensionless expansion parameter that corresponds to $\alpha$ in QED.

The correspondence even extends to the theorem that guarantees the validity of perturbation theory. In QED, $\alpha=1/137\ll1$ suppresses the higher-order terms. In the electron–phonon system, Migdal’s theorem guarantees that vertex corrections are suppressed to $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{m/M})\sim10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$ ($m$ is the electron mass and $M$ the ion mass; it is $2.9\times10^{-3}$ for Cu and $4.5\times10^{-3}$ for Al, and only light atoms such as hydrogen reach $10^{-2}$). Both rest on the same argument: “because there is a small dimensionless quantity, we may stop at tree level.” Conversely, just as perturbation theory breaks down in QCD with $\alpha_s\sim1$, the accuracy of the Migdal–Eliashberg framework becomes an issue for strong-coupling superconductors with $\lambda\gtrsim1$ (Pb, Nb$_3$Sn, hydrides under high pressure).

D.5 Details of the Rules — Integrals, Signs and Symmetry Factors

What remains after pasting in the factors of Sections D.2–D.4 is the “fine print” of this section. It hardly matters in tree-level calculations, but once we move on to loops, a mistake here makes the answer off by a factor of $2$ or by a sign.

D.5.1 The momentum-conserving delta function and loop integrals

Definition: Rules for delta functions and integrals

(1) At each vertex, place

$$ \begin{equation} (2\pi)^4\,\delta^4\!\left(k_1+k_2+\cdots+k_n\right) \label{eq:D-vertex-delta} \end{equation} $$

for all the momenta meeting there. As for signs, all momenta are added with the orientation chosen as the direction flowing into the vertex (an outflowing momentum is counted with a minus sign).

(2) For each internal line, integrate its momentum $q$ with

$$ \begin{equation} \int\frac{\dd^4q}{(2\pi)^4} \label{eq:D-loop-measure} \end{equation} $$

The placement of the $2\pi$ comes from the Fourier-transform convention (Appendix A, Section A.3): just remember that each $\delta$ function carries $(2\pi)^4$ and each integral carries $1/(2\pi)^4$.

Derivation: The number of remaining integrals (the loop-number formula)

Step 1: Fix what is to be counted. Suppose a connected diagram consists of $V$ vertices and $I$ internal lines. Let there be $E$ external lines.

Step 2: The number of integrals. There is one four-dimensional integral for each internal line, so there are $I$ integrals.

Step 3: The number of delta functions. There is one $\delta^4$ for each vertex, so there are $V$ of them.

Step 4: One of them becomes the overall conservation law. Of the $V$ factors of $\delta^4$, one becomes the “overall momentum conservation” $\delta^4(\sum p_{\rm in}-\sum p_{\rm out})$, which contains only external lines and consumes no integral (in Step 9 we strip it off to read $\mathcal{M}$). The remaining $V-1$ consume integrals.

Step 5: Conclusion. Therefore the number of independent integrals that remain, that is, the loop number, is

$$ \begin{equation} L = I - (V-1) = I - V + 1 \label{eq:D-loopcount} \end{equation} $$

Step 6: Check. The tree diagram of $e^-\mu^-\to e^-\mu^-$ has $V=2$ and $I=1$, so $L=1-2+1=0$: no integral remains. The QED vacuum polarization (the photon self-energy) has $V=2$ and $I=2$, so $L=1$: one integral remains. The bubble diagram of $\phi^4$ theory has $V=2$ and $I=2$, so $L=1$. In every case this agrees with the number of loops counted by looking at the diagram.

Tree level means $L=0$, that is, $I=V-1$. The graph-theoretic term “tree” is used just as it is.

D.5.2 Closed fermion loops

Theorem: The rule for closed fermion loops

When a fermion line forms a closed loop, multiply by

$$ \begin{equation} (-1)\times\Tr\left[\;\cdots\;\right] \label{eq:D-fermionloop} \end{equation} $$

for each such loop. That is, attach a factor $(-1)$ and take the trace of the product of $4\times4$ matrices arranged along the loop.

The $(-1)$ originates from Fermi statistics. When the field operators are contracted by Wick’s theorem, forming a closed loop requires rearranging $\psi$ and $\bar\psi$ into a “ring,” and the anticommutation of fermion operators produces the sign $(-1)$ of an odd permutation. The trace appears because the two ends of the loop are connected, so that the spinor indices close up ($\sum_a M_{aa}=\Tr M$).

Example: QED vacuum polarization

Following the procedure of Section D.1, the photon self-energy (vacuum polarization) is written

$$ \begin{equation} \ii\Pi^{\mu\nu}(q)=(-1)\int\frac{\dd^4k}{(2\pi)^4} \Tr\!\left[\left(\ii g_e\gamma^\mu\right)\frac{\ii(\slashed k+m)}{k^2-m^2} \left(\ii g_e\gamma^\nu\right)\frac{\ii(\slashed k+\slashed q+m)}{(k+q)^2-m^2}\right] \label{eq:D-vacpol} \end{equation} $$

The $(-1)$ at the front and the trace are the rule of this subsection. This integral is ultraviolet divergent, but renormalization extracts the physical effect, namely the running of the coupling constant (Chapter 10, Section 10.6):

$$ \alpha_{\rm eff}(q^2)=\frac{\alpha}{1-\Pi(q^2)} \simeq\frac{\alpha}{1-\dfrac{\alpha}{3\pi}\ln\dfrac{\abs{q^2}}{m_e^2}} $$

The larger $q^2$ is (that is, the shorter the distance), the larger $\alpha$ becomes. At the $m_Z$ scale, $\alpha(m_Z)\simeq1/129$ (a round number in the on-shell definition; precisely, $\alpha^{-1}(m_Z)=128.95$, Chapter 10, Section 10.6.4), an increase of about $6\%$ over $1/137$.

D.5.3 The relative sign from exchanging identical fermions

Theorem: The relative sign of exchange diagrams

When two diagrams are related by exchanging the external lines of identical fermions, a relative sign $(-1)$ is attached between them. In general, the relative sign is the sign of the permutation that rearranges the labels of the external fermions from the order in one diagram to the order in the other.

This rule, too, is a direct consequence of Fermi statistics. When there are two fermions of the same kind in the final state, the total state must be antisymmetric under exchange of the two particles. For the amplitude to have that antisymmetry, the exchanged diagram must enter with a minus sign.

Example: Møller scattering $e^-e^-\to e^-e^-$

Let the incoming momenta be $p_1,p_2$ and the outgoing momenta $p_3,p_4$. There are two diagrams.

Diagram 1 ($t$ channel): A photon connects the line $p_1\to p_3$ and the line $p_2\to p_4$. We have $q=p_1-p_3$ and $t=q^2$.

$$ \mathcal{M}_t=-\frac{g_e^2}{t}\left[\bar u(3)\gamma^\mu u(1)\right]\left[\bar u(4)\gamma_\mu u(2)\right] $$

Diagram 2 ($u$ channel): The connections are $p_1\to p_4$ and $p_2\to p_3$. We have $u=(p_1-p_4)^2$.

$$ \mathcal{M}_u=-\frac{g_e^2}{u}\left[\bar u(4)\gamma^\mu u(1)\right]\left[\bar u(3)\gamma_\mu u(2)\right] $$

Combining them:

$$ \begin{equation} \mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_t-\mathcal{M}_u \label{eq:D-moller} \end{equation} $$

The key point is that the sign is minus. Exchanging $3\leftrightarrow4$ interchanges $\mathcal{M}_t\leftrightarrow\mathcal{M}_u$, so $\mathcal{M}$ as a whole is antisymmetric. This is how the Pauli exclusion principle shows up at the level of the amplitude.

As a result, the cross section contains the negative interference term $\abs{\mathcal{M}_t}^2+\abs{\mathcal{M}_u}^2-2\,\mathrm{Re}(\mathcal{M}_t\mathcal{M}_u^*)$. At $\theta=90^\circ$ ($t=u$) the interference is at its maximum, and the cross section is smaller than the classical expectation (twice the Rutherford value). (The expression above is the form for fixed spins; averaging over unpolarized spins, the coefficient of the interference term becomes $-1$: the triplet has weight $3/4$ and gives $-2$, and the singlet has weight $1/4$ and gives $+2$.) This is an effect that genuinely matters in analyzing measurements that directly observe electron–electron scattering—(e,2e) spectroscopy, electron-impact ionization, secondary-electron emission, and Møller polarimeters. By contrast, elastic electron diffraction and ordinary electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) are described by one-electron scattering from the crystal potential, and this exchange interference is incorporated only approximately, in the form of an exchange potential, and does not play the leading role.

Caution: The sign in Bhabha scattering

$e^+e^-\to e^+e^-$ (Bhabha scattering) also has two diagrams (the pair annihilation in the $s$ channel and the scattering in the $t$ channel), and here too the relative sign is $(-1)$. The reason is that, to go from one diagram to the other, the external line of the $e^-$ and that of the $e^+$ must be exchanged “as a way of connecting the fermion lines.”

A practical test when in doubt: for each diagram, follow the fermion lines and make a list of the external lines in the order of appearance (for example, $(3,1)(4,2)$ for Diagram 1 and $(4,1)(3,2)$ for Diagram 2). The sign of the permutation that takes one of these two lists into the other is the relative sign. In the Møller example above it is a single transposition $3\leftrightarrow4$, so the sign is $(-1)$.

D.5.4 Symmetry factors

Definition: Symmetry factor

If a diagram contains a set of internal lines or vertices that can be interchanged, with the external lines held fixed, without changing the diagram, divide by the total number $S$ of such interchanges. That is, multiply by $1/S$. $S$ is the order of the automorphism group of the graph with the external lines fixed.

Here are some typical examples. In scalar theory with $\Lag_{\rm int}=-\tfrac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4$:

  • The “bubble,” in which two internal lines connect two vertices: swapping the two internal lines gives the same diagram, so $S=2$.
  • The “tadpole,” which leaves one vertex and returns to the same vertex: reversing the direction of the loop gives the same diagram, so $S=2$.
  • The “sunset,” in which three internal lines connect two vertices: there are $3!=6$ interchanges, so $S=6$.

On the other hand, in diagrams with fermion lines, the symmetry factor is $1$ in most cases. This is because a fermion line has a direction, given by its arrow, and the direction distinguishes the internal lines. In tree diagrams of QED, QCD, and electroweak theory, $S\neq1$ never occurs. Therefore, in practice, this subsection matters only in scalar field theory and in loop calculations.

Caution: The symmetry factor and the statistical factor are different things

It is confusing, but the following two are different matters.

(1) Symmetry factor $1/S$: a factor multiplied onto a diagram at the stage of computing the amplitude $\mathcal{M}$. It is determined by the topology of the diagram.

(2) Statistical factor $S$ (Appendix B): when there are $n$ identical particles in the final state, the factor $1/n!$ that prevents the same state from being counted $n!$ times in the phase-space integral. It multiplies the formulas for decay rates and cross sections.

For example, in $\pi^0\to\gamma\gamma$ the two photons in the final state are identical, so the statistical factor $1/2!$ is attached. This is (2), not (1). In this book the symbols collide, so distinguish them by context.

Connection to Materials Science: The closed fermion loop is the Lindhard function

The rule of Section D.5.2, “a factor $(-1)$ and a trace for each closed fermion loop,” has a much more familiar name in condensed matter. It is the susceptibility (polarization function).

Let us write down the nonrelativistic version of the vacuum polarization in Eq. \eqref{eq:D-vacpol}. Replace the propagator by Eq. \eqref{eq:D-nonrel-green} of Section D.3, replace $\int\dd^4k/(2\pi)^4$ by $\displaystyle\sum_{\bm k}\int\frac{\dd\varepsilon}{2\pi}$, and take the spin trace $\Tr[\mathbb{1}_2]=2$. The $\varepsilon$ integral (the positions of the poles switch at the Fermi surface) can then be carried out, and we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \chi_0(\bm q,\omega) =2\sum_{\bm k}\frac{f(\varepsilon_{\bm k})-f(\varepsilon_{\bm k+\bm q})} {\omega-\left(\varepsilon_{\bm k+\bm q}-\varepsilon_{\bm k}\right)+\ii\eta} \label{eq:D-lindhard} \end{equation} $$

This is the Lindhard function, which appeared in “Introduction to Linear Response Theory,” Chapter 10, “Screening — Thomas–Fermi and Lindhard Functions,” and in the RPA chapter of “Introduction to Density Functional Theory.” The factor $2$ comes from the spin trace, and the overall sign comes from the $(-1)$ of the loop. The fine print of the Feynman rules directly fixes the prefactor of the Lindhard function.

The correspondence goes further. In QED, when the vacuum polarization is summed with the Dyson equation, the photon propagator is “renormalized” into

$$ D=\frac{D_0}{1-\Pi D_0} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha_{\rm eff}=\frac{\alpha}{1-\Pi} $$

The same geometric series summed in condensed matter is precisely the RPA dielectric function

$$ \begin{equation} \epsilon(\bm q,\omega)=1-v_{\bm q}\,\chi_0(\bm q,\omega), \qquad v_{\rm eff}(\bm q,\omega)=\frac{v_{\bm q}}{\epsilon(\bm q,\omega)} \label{eq:D-rpa} \end{equation} $$

Simply reading $\Pi\leftrightarrow v_{\bm q}\chi_0$ and $\alpha_{\rm eff}\leftrightarrow v_{\rm eff}$ makes the two agree completely. The screening of charge by vacuum polarization and the screening of the Coulomb interaction by electrons in a metal are the same sum of the same diagrams.

Finally, the result of the screening returns to Section D.3.4. In the long-wavelength, static limit, $\chi_0(\bm q\to0,0)=-N(\varepsilon_F)$ ($N$ is the density of states at the Fermi surface), so inserting $v_{\bm q}=4\pi e^2/q^2$ (Gaussian units) gives

$$ \epsilon(\bm q,0)=1+\frac{4\pi e^2 N(\varepsilon_F)}{q^2}=1+\frac{k_{\rm TF}^2}{q^2}, \qquad v_{\rm eff}(\bm q)=\frac{4\pi e^2}{q^2+k_{\rm TF}^2} $$

This has the same form as the propagator, Eq. \eqref{eq:D-prop-massive}, of a vector particle of mass $k_{\rm TF}$. Returning to position space, we obtain the Yukawa potential $\dfrac{e^2}{r}\ee^{-k_{\rm TF}r}$—Thomas–Fermi screening. That is, inside a metal the photon acquires a mass $k_{\rm TF}$ (but this holds only for the static longitudinal response; the static transverse magnetic field is not screened in a normal metal). For a typical metal ($r_s\simeq2$), $k_{\rm TF}^{-1}\simeq0.5\ \mathrm{\AA}$, and this is precisely the reason why band calculations work well in the one-body approximation: “in a metal the Coulomb interaction is cut off at the scale of the interatomic distance.”

The fact that the weak interaction looks like a contact interaction because the $W$ boson is heavy (Eq. \eqref{eq:D-prop-fermi}), and the fact that the electron–electron interaction looks short-ranged in a metal, have the same structure at the level of the equations (on the metal side, only for the static longitudinal response). The only difference is the origin: the mass of the $W$ comes from the vacuum expectation value of the Higgs field, whereas the effective mass of the photon comes from the polarization of the Fermi sea (the latter becomes a true mass in a superconductor as the Meissner effect; Chapter 13).

D.6 Checklist for Checking Calculations

Calculations with the Feynman rules are mechanical, but long. A single mistake in the order of the $\gamma$ matrices changes the answer. Therefore whether or not you have a method of verification determines the reliability of the calculation. In this section we give four checks that can be done in a few minutes on paper. None of them “guarantees that the answer is correct,” but each is very powerful at “discovering that it is wrong.”

Theorem: Dimension of the amplitude

The mass dimension of the amplitude $\mathcal{M}$ of a process with a total of $n$ external lines is

$$ \begin{equation} \left[\mathcal{M}\right]=4-n \label{eq:D-dim-M} \end{equation} $$

Therefore, for $2\to2$ scattering ($n=4$), $\mathcal{M}$ is dimensionless, and for a $1\to2$ decay ($n=3$) it has the dimension of mass.

Example: Checking three calculations by dimensions

(1) $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$ ($n=4$): From Section D.7, $\mathcal{M}=-\dfrac{g_e^2}{s}\left[\bar v\gamma^\mu u\right]\left[\bar u\gamma_\mu v\right]$. We have $[g_e^2]=0$, $[1/s]=-2$, and each spinor bilinear has $[\bar u\gamma u]=2\times\tfrac12=1$, so the two of them contribute $+2$. The total is $0-2+2=0$, which agrees with $[\mathcal{M}]=4-4=0$.

(2) $\pi^-\to\mu^-\bar\nu_\mu$ ($n=3$): From Chapter 12, $\mathcal{M}=\ii\dfrac{G_F}{\sqrt2}V_{ud}f_\pi m_\mu\left[\bar u(1-\gamma^5)v\right]$. We have $[G_F]=-2$, $[f_\pi]=1$, $[m_\mu]=1$, and the bilinear $=1$. The total is $-2+1+1+1=1$, which agrees with $[\mathcal{M}]=4-3=1$.

(3) The formula side: In Appendix B, $\dfrac{\dd\sigma}{\dd\Omega}=\dfrac{\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2}{64\pi^2 s}\dfrac{\abs{\bm p_f}}{\abs{\bm p_i}}$ gives $0-2+0=-2$. For the $1\to2$ decay, $\Gamma=\dfrac{S\abs{\bm p}}{8\pi m^2}\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2$ gives $1-2+2=1$. Both are correct.

When the dimensions do not match, in most cases the cause is not a forgotten $2\pi$ but a more essential mistake (an omitted propagator, a wrong number of spinors). This is the first check to perform.

D.6.2 Gauge invariance (Ward identity)

For a process with a photon (or gluon) among the external lines, the amplitude can be written as $\mathcal{M}=\epsilon_\mu(q)\,\mathcal{M}^\mu$ (incoming photon) or $\mathcal{M}=\epsilon_\mu^*(q)\,\mathcal{M}^\mu$ (outgoing photon). Then $\mathcal{M}^\mu$, summed over all diagrams, must satisfy

$$ \begin{equation} q_\mu\,\mathcal{M}^\mu=0 \label{eq:D-ward} \end{equation} $$

This is the Ward identity (this is the form for amplitudes with the external lines on the mass shell; the generalization to off-shell Green functions, $q_\mu\Gamma^\mu=S^{-1}(p+q)-S^{-1}(p)$, is distinguished by the name Ward–Takahashi identity). Physically it is a consequence of current conservation $\partial_\mu j^\mu=0$, and it means that the amplitude does not change under the gauge transformation $\epsilon_\mu\to\epsilon_\mu+c\,q_\mu$.

Example: Using the Ward identity in Compton scattering

Compton scattering $\gamma(q)+e^-(p)\to\gamma(q')+e^-(p')$ has two diagrams: the $s$-channel (absorption first) and the $u$-channel (emission first). Taking $\epsilon_\mu(q)$ as the polarization of the incoming photon,

$$ \mathcal{M}^\mu_s=\bar u(p')\,\slashed\epsilon'^{\,*}\frac{\slashed p+\slashed q+m}{(p+q)^2-m^2}\gamma^\mu\,u(p)\,g_e^2, \qquad \mathcal{M}^\mu_u=\bar u(p')\,\gamma^\mu\frac{\slashed p-\slashed q'+m}{(p-q')^2-m^2}\slashed\epsilon'^{\,*}\,u(p)\,g_e^2 $$

(the overall $\ii$ is omitted). When we multiply by $q_\mu$, we decompose $\slashed q=(\slashed p+\slashed q-m)-(\slashed p-m)$ and use $(\slashed p-m)u(p)=0$ (the Dirac equation); the numerator and denominator of the first term then cancel. Doing the same for the second term, the two contributions cancel exactly:

$$ q_\mu\left(\mathcal{M}^\mu_s+\mathcal{M}^\mu_u\right)=0 $$

The crucial point here is that $q_\mu\mathcal{M}^\mu_s\neq0$ for $\mathcal{M}^\mu_s$ alone. That is, the Ward identity serves as a test that “not a single diagram has been omitted.” If even one diagram is forgotten, it is always violated. This is the most powerful check in this section.

The same argument holds for external gluons, but in a non-Abelian gauge theory the Ward identity is generalized to the Slavnov–Taylor identity, and the contribution of ghost fields is added (at loop level). At tree level, $q_\mu\mathcal{M}^\mu=0$ holds if we sum up to and including the three-gluon vertex. However, with two or more external gluons a condition is needed: $q_\mu\mathcal{M}^{\mu\nu\cdots}=0$ holds only when the remaining gluons are contracted with physical (transverse) polarizations. Therefore, in processes such as $q\bar q\to gg$ or $gg\to gg$, using the replacement $\sum\epsilon_\mu\epsilon_\nu^*\to-g_{\mu\nu}$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:D-polsum-massless} as it stands lets unphysical polarizations contaminate the result and gives a wrong $\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2$ even at tree level. One must do one of the following: sum only the physical polarizations explicitly; use the light-cone-gauge polarization sum $-g_{\mu\nu}+(q_\mu n_\nu+n_\mu q_\nu)/(q\cdot n)$; or use $-g_{\mu\nu}$ and subtract the ghost contribution.

D.6.3 Crossing symmetry

Theorem: Crossing symmetry

In the amplitude of a process, if we move an external particle to the other side, replacing that particle by its antiparticle and its momentum by $p\to-p$ at the same time, we obtain the amplitude of another process. That is,

$$ \begin{equation} \mathcal{M}\bigl(A(p_A)+B(p_B)\to C(p_C)+D(p_D)\bigr) =\mathcal{M}\bigl(A(p_A)+\bar C(-p_C)\to \bar B(-p_B)+D(p_D)\bigr) \label{eq:D-crossing} \end{equation} $$

holds. The right-hand side is read as “the amplitude of the process in which $C$ is changed into the antiparticle $\bar C$ and placed in the initial state with momentum $-p_C$, and $B$ is changed into the antiparticle $\bar B$ and placed in the final state with momentum $-p_B$.” In terms of the Mandelstam variables (Chapter 4), the roles of $s,t,u$ are interchanged.

Example: Relating $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$ and $e^-\mu^-\to e^-\mu^-$ by crossing

In the massless limit, the results of Chapter 10 are

$$ \braket{\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2}_{e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-}=2g_e^4\,\frac{t^2+u^2}{s^2}, \qquad \braket{\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2}_{e^-\mu^-\to e^-\mu^-}=2g_e^4\,\frac{s^2+u^2}{t^2} $$

They are indeed interchanged by $s\leftrightarrow t$. If we follow the momenta as well, what crossing connects directly is $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$ and $e^-\mu^+\to e^-\mu^+$; but at tree level in QED the sign of the charge does not affect the spin-averaged $\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2$, so the same expression holds for $e^-\mu^-\to e^-\mu^-$ as well (Figure D.7).

Let us also confirm this numerically. In the center-of-mass frame of $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$, $t=-\tfrac{s}{2}(1-\cos\theta)$ and $u=-\tfrac{s}{2}(1+\cos\theta)$, so

$$ 2g_e^4\frac{t^2+u^2}{s^2} =2g_e^4\cdot\frac{1}{4}\left[(1-\cos\theta)^2+(1+\cos\theta)^2\right] =g_e^4\left(1+\cos^2\theta\right) $$

which agrees with the result obtained directly from the trace in Section D.7. Using crossing, we avoid doing the trace calculation again.

D.6.4 Nonrelativistic limit

As the scattering energy is lowered, the answer must reduce to known classical, nonrelativistic results. Concretely, it is good to remember the following three.

Theorem: Three nonrelativistic limits

(1) The Yukawa potential. Reinterpreting the amplitude for the exchange of a boson of mass $m$, $\mathcal{M}\simeq \dfrac{g^2}{\bm q^2+m^2}$ ($q^0\to0$), in the Born approximation gives

$$ \begin{equation} V(r)=-\frac{g^2}{4\pi}\frac{\ee^{-mr}}{r} \label{eq:D-yukawa} \end{equation} $$

(Appendix A, Section A.6). For $m\to0$ this becomes the Coulomb potential $-g^2/4\pi r$.

(2) Rutherford scattering. Taking the amplitude of $e^-\mu^-\to e^-\mu^-$ to the nonrelativistic limit (velocity $v\ll1$), we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{\dd\sigma}{\dd\Omega}=\frac{\alpha^2}{4m^2v^4\sin^4(\theta/2)} \label{eq:D-rutherford} \end{equation} $$

This is the well-known Rutherford formula. Here $m$ is the reduced mass $\mu=m_em_\mu/(m_e+m_\mu)$ (since $m_\mu\gg m_e$, it is effectively the electron mass $m_e$), $v$ is the relative velocity, and $\theta$ is the scattering angle in the center-of-mass frame. At the intermediate relativistic stage (Mott scattering, Chapter 10, Section 10.5), a spin correction $1-\beta^2\sin^2(\theta/2)$ appears, which vanishes as $\beta\to0$.

(3) The Fermi contact interaction. The $\abs{q^2}\ll m_W^2$ limit of $W$ exchange becomes, as in Eq. \eqref{eq:D-prop-fermi}, the four-fermion interaction with $G_F$, which correctly gives the lifetime of neutron $\beta$ decay (Chapter 12).

Why insist so much on checking?

Because in a Feynman-rule calculation there is almost no moment at which one “notices along the way.” When solving a differential equation, one notices an anomaly midway, when the solution diverges or a negative probability appears. But the Feynman rules proceed mechanically to the very end, and even with a wrong order of $\gamma$ matrices a “plausible” answer comes out.

That is exactly why one needs several independent tests. Item 3 (the Ward identity) in particular reliably catches the most frequent error, “forgetting one diagram.” It is good to decide which checks to use before starting the calculation.

This situation closely resembles first-principles calculations. The total energy in DFT also comes out “plausible” with a wrong cutoff or number of $k$ points. It becomes trustworthy only when checks are combined, such as convergence tests, reproduction of known systems, and verification of sum rules—the same structure.

D.7 Working Through an Actual Example — $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$

In this section we carry out the nine steps of Section D.1 without skipping a single one, and obtain the cross section of $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$ all the way to a number. We choose this process because it has only one diagram, its final state has two bodies, and it is the yardstick for every $e^+e^-$ collision experiment.

D.7.1 Steps 1–2: Draw the diagram and assign momenta

The $e^+e^-$ pair annihilates into a virtual photon, which then produces a $\mu^+\mu^-$ pair. This is the only diagram at lowest order ($g_e^2$). Since $e$ and $\mu$ are different particles, there is no exchange diagram like that of Møller scattering. There is also a diagram in which a $Z$ is exchanged instead of the $\gamma$, but for $\sqrt s\ll m_Z$ the denominator of the propagator becomes $s-m_Z^2\simeq-m_Z^2$, so the contribution is suppressed by $s/m_Z^2$. For $\sqrt s=10\ \mathrm{GeV}$ we have $s/m_Z^2=100/8315=1.2\times10^{-2}$, so we may ignore it for now (we estimate it in Section D.7.6).

We denote the momentum of the incoming $e^-$ by $p_1$, that of the incoming $e^+$ by $p_2$, that of the outgoing $\mu^-$ by $p_3$, and that of the outgoing $\mu^+$ by $p_4$. Momentum conservation at the left vertex gives the internal-line momentum $q=p_1+p_2$, and in terms of the Mandelstam variables we have

$$ q^2=(p_1+p_2)^2\equiv s $$

Here $s$ is nothing but the square of the total energy in the center-of-mass frame.

D.7.2 Steps 3–9: Assemble the amplitude

Derivation: Steps 3–9 — up to reading off $\mathcal{M}$

Step 3 (external-line factors). From Table D.1: $u(p_1)$ for the incoming $e^-$, $\bar v(p_2)$ for the incoming $e^+$, $\bar u(p_3)$ for the outgoing $\mu^-$, and $v(p_4)$ for the outgoing $\mu^+$.

Step 4 (vertex factors). Both vertices are the QED vertex of Eq. \eqref{eq:D-vertex-qed}. Substituting $Q_e=Q_\mu=-1$ gives $-\ii g_eQ_f\gamma^\mu\to\ii g_e\gamma^\mu$, so we take $\ii g_e\gamma^\mu$ for the left vertex and $\ii g_e\gamma^\nu$ for the right one (only $Q_eQ_\mu=1$ matters in the product).

Step 5 (propagators). The internal photon line gets $-\ii g_{\mu\nu}/q^2$ (Feynman gauge, Eq. \eqref{eq:D-prop-photon}).

Write down the fermion line. Following the rule of Section D.2.3, we trace against the arrows. The electron line enters from the external $e^+$ side and ends at the external $e^-$ side, so

$$ \bar v(p_2)\left(\ii g_e\gamma^\mu\right)u(p_1) $$

The muon line enters from the external $\mu^-$ side and ends at the external $\mu^+$ side, so

$$ \bar u(p_3)\left(\ii g_e\gamma^\nu\right)v(p_4) $$

Steps 6–7 (delta functions and integrals). We attach $(2\pi)^4\delta^4(p_1+p_2-q)$ to the left vertex, $(2\pi)^4\delta^4(q-p_3-p_4)$ to the right vertex, and $\int\dd^4q/(2\pi)^4$ to the internal line. Writing everything out,

$$ \begin{aligned} &\int\frac{\dd^4q}{(2\pi)^4} \left[\bar v(2)\left(\ii g_e\gamma^\mu\right)u(1)\right] (2\pi)^4\delta^4(p_1+p_2-q)\\ &\qquad\times\frac{-\ii g_{\mu\nu}}{q^2} \left[\bar u(3)\left(\ii g_e\gamma^\nu\right)v(4)\right] (2\pi)^4\delta^4(q-p_3-p_4) \end{aligned} $$

The first $\delta^4$ eats the $q$ integral, and $q=p_1+p_2$ is substituted. The remaining second $\delta^4$ becomes the overall momentum conservation $(2\pi)^4\delta^4(p_1+p_2-p_3-p_4)$.

Step 8 (sign). There is one diagram, no loops, and no exchange of identical fermions. Hence there is no additional sign and no symmetry factor.

Step 9 (reading off the result). Stripping off the overall $\delta^4$ gives

$$ -\ii\mathcal{M} =\left[\bar v(2)\left(\ii g_e\gamma^\mu\right)u(1)\right] \frac{-\ii g_{\mu\nu}}{s} \left[\bar u(3)\left(\ii g_e\gamma^\nu\right)v(4)\right] $$

We tidy up the coefficient. Since $\ii\times\ii\times(-\ii)=(-1)\times(-\ii)=+\ii$,

$$ -\ii\mathcal{M}=\ii\,\frac{g_e^2}{s}\left[\bar v(2)\gamma^\mu u(1)\right]\left[\bar u(3)\gamma_\mu v(4)\right] $$

Dividing both sides by $-\ii$,

$$ \begin{equation} \mathcal{M}=-\frac{g_e^2}{s}\left[\bar v(2)\gamma^\mu u(1)\right]\left[\bar u(3)\gamma_\mu v(4)\right] \label{eq:D-M-eemumu} \end{equation} $$

This is the desired amplitude. Dimension check of Section D.6.1: $[g_e^2]=0$, $[1/s]=-2$, each spinor bilinear contributes $+1$ for a total of $+2$, so the total is $0$. This agrees with $[\mathcal{M}]=4-4=0$.

D.7.3 Spin sums and traces

In an experiment, one usually neither controls the polarization of the incident beams nor measures the spins of the outgoing particles. We therefore take the average over initial spins and the sum over final spins. The initial state has $2\times2=4$ spin states, so we multiply by $1/4$.

Derivation: Expressing $\braket{\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2}$ in terms of traces

Step 1: Write $\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2$. From Eq. \eqref{eq:D-M-eemumu}, naming the index on the conjugate side $\nu$,

$$ \abs{\mathcal{M}}^2=\frac{g_e^4}{s^2} \left[\bar v(2)\gamma^\mu u(1)\right]\left[\bar v(2)\gamma^\nu u(1)\right]^{*} \left[\bar u(3)\gamma_\mu v(4)\right]\left[\bar u(3)\gamma_\nu v(4)\right]^{*} $$

Step 2: Casimir’s trick. Using the spinor completeness relations of Appendix C

$$ \sum_s u(p,s)\bar u(p,s)=\slashed p+m, \qquad \sum_s v(p,s)\bar v(p,s)=\slashed p-m $$

the spin sums turn into traces. On the electron side (keeping $m_e$) we have

$$ L^{\mu\nu}\equiv\sum_{s_1,s_2}\left[\bar v(2)\gamma^\mu u(1)\right]\left[\bar v(2)\gamma^\nu u(1)\right]^{*} =\Tr\!\left[\gamma^\mu(\slashed p_1+m_e)\gamma^\nu(\slashed p_2-m_e)\right] $$

and on the muon side

$$ M_{\mu\nu}\equiv\sum_{s_3,s_4}\left[\bar u(3)\gamma_\mu v(4)\right]\left[\bar u(3)\gamma_\nu v(4)\right]^{*} =\Tr\!\left[\gamma_\mu(\slashed p_4-m_\mu)\gamma_\nu(\slashed p_3+m_\mu)\right] $$

Step 3: Put it together. Including the initial-spin average $1/4$,

$$ \begin{equation} \braket{\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2}=\frac{g_e^4}{4s^2}\,L^{\mu\nu}M_{\mu\nu} \label{eq:D-M2-tensor} \end{equation} $$

No spinors are left. All that remains is to take the traces of the $\gamma$ matrices.

Derivation: Carrying out the traces

Step 1: Drop the terms with an odd number of γ matrices. By “the trace of a product of an odd number of $\gamma$ matrices is zero” (Appendix C, Table C.5 and Eq. (C-trace-odd)), expanding $L^{\mu\nu}$ yields four terms, but the term carrying only one factor of $m_e$ (three $\gamma$ matrices) vanishes. The term carrying $m_e^2$ survives.

In what follows we set $m_e\to0$ since $m_e\ll\sqrt s$ ($m_e/\sqrt s=5\times10^{-5}$ at $\sqrt s=10\ \mathrm{GeV}$).

Step 2: The lepton tensor. From the formula for the product of four matrices in Appendix C (Eq. (C-trace4): $\Tr[\gamma^\alpha\gamma^\beta\gamma^\gamma\gamma^\delta]=4(g^{\alpha\beta}g^{\gamma\delta}-g^{\alpha\gamma}g^{\beta\delta}+g^{\alpha\delta}g^{\beta\gamma})$),

$$ L^{\mu\nu}=\Tr\!\left[\gamma^\mu\slashed p_1\gamma^\nu\slashed p_2\right] =4\left[p_1^\mu p_2^\nu+p_1^\nu p_2^\mu-g^{\mu\nu}(p_1\!\cdot\!p_2)\right] $$

Step 3: The muon tensor. The same calculation, keeping the $m_\mu$ term, gives

$$ M_{\mu\nu}=4\left[p_{3\mu}p_{4\nu}+p_{3\nu}p_{4\mu}-g_{\mu\nu}\left(p_3\!\cdot\!p_4+m_\mu^2\right)\right] $$

Step 4: Contract. Nine terms appear, but, noting that $g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\nu}=4$ and tidying up, the terms containing only momenta leave $2\left[(p_1\!\cdot\!p_3)(p_2\!\cdot\!p_4)+(p_1\!\cdot\!p_4)(p_2\!\cdot\!p_3)\right]$, and the $m_\mu^2$ terms leave $+2m_\mu^2(p_1\!\cdot\!p_2)$. Therefore

$$ L^{\mu\nu}M_{\mu\nu}=32\left[(p_1\!\cdot\!p_3)(p_2\!\cdot\!p_4)+(p_1\!\cdot\!p_4)(p_2\!\cdot\!p_3)+m_\mu^2\,(p_1\!\cdot\!p_2)\right] $$

Step 5: Substitute back into Eq. \eqref{eq:D-M2-tensor}.

$$ \begin{equation} \braket{\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2}=\frac{8g_e^4}{s^2} \left[(p_1\!\cdot\!p_3)(p_2\!\cdot\!p_4)+(p_1\!\cdot\!p_4)(p_2\!\cdot\!p_3)+m_\mu^2(p_1\!\cdot\!p_2)\right] \label{eq:D-M2-invariant} \end{equation} $$

Up to this point the expression is invariant, independent of the coordinate system.

D.7.4 Kinematics in the center-of-mass frame

Derivation: Writing the inner products in terms of the angle

Step 1: Write down the four-momenta. In the center-of-mass frame, let $E=\sqrt s/2$ and take the $e^-$ along the positive $z$ axis ($m_e=0$):

$$ p_1=(E,0,0,E),\qquad p_2=(E,0,0,-E) $$

On the outgoing side $\abs{\bm p_3}=\abs{\bm p_4}=\beta E$, where

$$ \begin{equation} \beta\equiv\sqrt{1-\frac{4m_\mu^2}{s}} \label{eq:D-beta} \end{equation} $$

(the velocity of the $\mu$). Taking the $\mu^-$ in a direction at angle $\theta$ from the $z$ axis,

$$ p_3=\left(E,\;\beta E\sin\theta,\;0,\;\beta E\cos\theta\right),\qquad p_4=\left(E,\;-\beta E\sin\theta,\;0,\;-\beta E\cos\theta\right) $$

Step 2: Compute the inner products. Using $a\cdot b=a^0b^0-\bm a\cdot\bm b$,

$$ p_1\!\cdot\!p_2=E^2+E^2=2E^2=\frac{s}{2} $$ $$ p_1\!\cdot\!p_3=E^2-\beta E^2\cos\theta=E^2(1-\beta\cos\theta)=p_2\!\cdot\!p_4 $$ $$ p_1\!\cdot\!p_4=E^2+\beta E^2\cos\theta=E^2(1+\beta\cos\theta)=p_2\!\cdot\!p_3 $$

Step 3: Substitute into Eq. \eqref{eq:D-M2-invariant}.

$$ \left[\;\right]=E^4\left[(1-\beta\cos\theta)^2+(1+\beta\cos\theta)^2\right]+2m_\mu^2E^2 =2E^4\left(1+\beta^2\cos^2\theta\right)+2m_\mu^2E^2 $$

Inserting $E^2=s/4$,

$$ \left[\;\right]=\frac{s^2}{8}\left(1+\beta^2\cos^2\theta\right)+\frac{m_\mu^2 s}{2} $$

Step 4: Tidy up. From Eq. \eqref{eq:D-beta}, $4m_\mu^2/s=1-\beta^2$, so

$$ \begin{equation} \braket{\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2} =g_e^4\left[1+\beta^2\cos^2\theta+\left(1-\beta^2\right)\right] =g_e^4\left[2-\beta^2+\beta^2\cos^2\theta\right] \label{eq:D-M2-angle} \end{equation} $$

Step 5: Check in two limits.

  • Ultrarelativistic limit ($\beta\to1$, that is, $\sqrt s\gg 2m_\mu$): $\braket{\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2}=g_e^4(1+\cos^2\theta)$. This is the characteristic forward–backward symmetric angular distribution.
  • Threshold ($\beta\to0$, $\sqrt s\to2m_\mu$): $\braket{\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2}=2g_e^4$. The angular dependence disappears and the distribution becomes isotropic. This is natural, since the produced $\mu^+\mu^-$ pair is at rest and no direction can be specified.

D.7.5 On to the cross section

Derivation: The differential and total cross sections

Step 1: The formula from Appendix B. The differential cross section for two-body $\to$ two-body scattering in the center-of-mass frame is

$$ \frac{\dd\sigma}{\dd\Omega}=\frac{1}{64\pi^2 s}\,\frac{\abs{\bm p_f}}{\abs{\bm p_i}}\,\braket{\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2} $$

Here $\abs{\bm p_i}=E$ and $\abs{\bm p_f}=\beta E$, so $\abs{\bm p_f}/\abs{\bm p_i}=\beta$.

Step 2: Substitute. Inserting Eq. \eqref{eq:D-M2-angle},

$$ \frac{\dd\sigma}{\dd\Omega}=\frac{g_e^4\,\beta}{64\pi^2 s}\left[2-\beta^2+\beta^2\cos^2\theta\right] $$

Step 3: Rewrite in terms of $\alpha$. From $g_e^2=4\pi\alpha$ we have $g_e^4=16\pi^2\alpha^2$. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{\dd\sigma}{\dd\Omega} =\frac{\alpha^2\beta}{4s}\left[2-\beta^2+\beta^2\cos^2\theta\right] =\frac{\alpha^2\beta}{4s}\left[1+\cos^2\theta+\left(1-\beta^2\right)\sin^2\theta\right] \label{eq:D-dsigma} \end{equation} $$

The second equality is a rewriting that uses $\sin^2\theta=1-\cos^2\theta$. In the ultrarelativistic limit the second term vanishes, giving

$$ \frac{\dd\sigma}{\dd\Omega}=\frac{\alpha^2}{4s}\left(1+\cos^2\theta\right) $$

which is the famous formula.

Step 4: Integrate over the solid angle. Since $\int\dd\Omega=4\pi$ and $\int\cos^2\theta\,\dd\Omega=4\pi/3$,

$$ \int\left[2-\beta^2+\beta^2\cos^2\theta\right]\dd\Omega =4\pi\left(2-\beta^2\right)+\frac{4\pi}{3}\beta^2 =4\pi\left(2-\frac{2\beta^2}{3}\right)=\frac{8\pi}{3}\left(3-\beta^2\right) $$

Step 5: Total cross section.

$$ \begin{equation} \sigma\left(e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-\right) =\frac{\alpha^2\beta}{4s}\cdot\frac{8\pi}{3}\left(3-\beta^2\right) =\frac{4\pi\alpha^2}{3s}\cdot\frac{\beta\left(3-\beta^2\right)}{2} \label{eq:D-sigma-full} \end{equation} $$

As $\beta\to1$ the last factor becomes $1$, and we obtain the textbook formula

$$ \begin{equation} \sigma=\frac{4\pi\alpha^2}{3s} \label{eq:D-sigma-point} \end{equation} $$

This is the benchmark formula for “pair production of pointlike spin $1/2$ particles.”

D.7.6 Putting in numbers

Example: The cross section at $\sqrt s=10\ \mathrm{GeV}$

(1) Evaluate the coefficient numerically.

$$ \frac{4\pi\alpha^2}{3}=\frac{4\pi}{3}\times\left(\frac{1}{137.036}\right)^2 =4.18879\times5.32513\times10^{-5}=2.2306\times10^{-4} $$

(2) Restore the units. Multiply by $(\hbar c)^2=0.3894\ \mathrm{GeV^2\,mb}$ (Chapter 1, Section 1.7.2).

$$ \sigma\,s=2.2306\times10^{-4}\times0.3894\ \mathrm{GeV^2\,mb} =8.686\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{GeV^2\,mb} =86.9\ \mathrm{GeV^2\,nb} $$

That is,

$$ \begin{equation} \sigma\left(e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-\right)=\frac{86.9\ \mathrm{nb}}{s\,[\mathrm{GeV^2}]} \label{eq:D-sigma-nb} \end{equation} $$

(3) Insert $\sqrt s=10\ \mathrm{GeV}$. Since $s=100\ \mathrm{GeV^2}$, $\sigma=0.869\ \mathrm{nb}$.

(4) Correction due to $\beta$. Since $m_\mu=0.105658\ \mathrm{GeV}$,

$$ \beta=\sqrt{1-\frac{4\times0.105658^2}{100}}=\sqrt{1-4.465\times10^{-4}}=0.999777 $$ $$ \frac{\beta(3-\beta^2)}{2}=\frac{0.999777\times(3-0.999554)}{2}=\frac{0.999777\times2.000446}{2}=1.0000000 $$

Expanded in $\delta\equiv1-\beta^2=4m_\mu^2/s$, this factor is $1-\tfrac38\delta^2=1-7.5\times10^{-8}$ (the multiplication above suffers from loss of significant digits, so evaluating the expansion is more reliable). The correction is below $10^{-7}$ and can be completely ignored. Just above threshold the story is different ($\sqrt s=0.25\ \mathrm{GeV}$ against $2m_\mu=0.2113\ \mathrm{GeV}$). Since $4m_\mu^2/s=4\times0.011164/0.0625=0.71447$, we get $\beta=\sqrt{1-0.71447}=\sqrt{0.28553}=0.5343$, and this factor is $\beta(3-\beta^2)/2=0.5343\times2.71447/2=0.7252$, that is, a suppression of $27\%$.

(5) Contribution of $Z$ exchange. The ratio of the $\gamma$-exchange and $Z$-exchange amplitudes is roughly

$$ \frac{\mathcal{M}_Z}{\mathcal{M}_\gamma}\sim \frac{\left(g_z/2\right)^2}{g_e^2}\cdot\frac{s}{s-m_Z^2} \simeq\frac{0.7446^2/4}{0.30282^2}\times\frac{100}{100-8315} =1.512\times(-0.01217)=-1.84\times10^{-2}$$

The value $g_z^2/(4g_e^2)=1.512$ here requires care. If the tree-level relation $g_e=g_w\sin\theta_W$ held exactly, this ratio would have to be $1/(4\sin^2\theta_W\cos^2\theta_W)=1.406$. The $7\%$ discrepancy arises because the conventions are mixed: $g_w$ is taken from $G_F$ and $m_W$, $g_e$ from $\alpha(0)$, and $\sin^2\theta_W$ from $\overline{\rm MS}$ (see the caution in Chapter 12, Section 12.1.4). The estimates below should be read as accurate to within $\pm10\%$.

However, we must not read this $-1.8\times10^{-2}$ directly as the size of the interference, because the $Z$ vertex carries $c_V^f,c_A^f$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:D-vertex-nc}). After the spin sum, the interference term that affects the total cross section is proportional to $c_V^ec_V^\mu$, and the interference term that affects the forward–backward asymmetry is proportional to $c_A^ec_A^\mu$. From Table D.4, $c_V^ec_V^\mu=(-0.0376)^2=1.41\times10^{-3}$ and $c_A^ec_A^\mu=(-0.5)^2=0.25$, so

$$ \frac{\Delta\sigma}{\sigma}=2\chi\,c_V^ec_V^\mu=2\times(-1.84\times10^{-2})\times1.41\times10^{-3}=-5.2\times10^{-5}, \qquad A_{FB}=\frac{3}{2}\chi\,c_A^ec_A^\mu=-6.9\times10^{-3} $$

(where $\chi$ is the amplitude ratio above). That is, at $\sqrt s=10\ \mathrm{GeV}$, $Z$ exchange has no effect at all on the magnitude of the cross section, and first appears as $-0.7\%$ in the forward–backward bias of the angular distribution. That it does not affect the magnitude of the cross section is a direct consequence of the smallness of $c_V$ for charged leptons (Section D.4.4), and the asymmetry appears first because $c_A=-1/2$ is not small. At LEP, running at $\sqrt s=m_Z$, the denominator of the propagator is replaced by $\ii m_Z\Gamma_Z$, so $\abs{s/(s-m_Z^2)}\to m_Z/\Gamma_Z=36.5$ and $\abs{\mathcal{M}_Z/\mathcal{M}_\gamma}^2=(1.512\times36.5)^2\times0.2514^2\simeq190$, and the cross section jumps to $\sigma\simeq190\times(86.9/8315)\ \mathrm{nb}\simeq2.0\ \mathrm{nb}$ (the Breit–Wigner peak, Eq. \eqref{eq:D-breitwigner}; this agrees well with the PDG pole cross section $\sigma^0_{\ell\ell}=1.999\ \mathrm{nb}$).

(6) Realism of the experiment. For a $B$ factory with luminosity $\mathcal{L}=10^{33}\ \mathrm{cm^{-2}s^{-1}}$, since $0.869\ \mathrm{nb}=0.869\times10^{-33}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, the event rate is $0.87\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, or $7.5\times10^4$ events per day. Since $\mu^+\mu^-$ pairs are the events most easily identified in a detector, they are used for the absolute calibration of the luminosity.

Caution: When comparing a tree-level answer with experiment

If we compare \eqref{eq:D-sigma-nb} directly with the experimental value, we find a discrepancy of a few percent. There are three reasons.

(1) Radiative corrections. In reality, $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-\gamma$ (bremsstrahlung) is always mixed in. Unless we define how far we count something as a “$\mu^+\mu^-$ event,” we cannot compare with the theoretical value. Typically the effect is of order $\mathcal{O}(\alpha)$, that is, a few percent.

(2) Running coupling constant. We should evaluate $\alpha$ not as $1/137.036$ but as $\alpha(s)$. At $\sqrt s=10\ \mathrm{GeV}$, the lepton loop alone gives $\Delta\alpha_{\rm lep}=0.021$ and $\alpha\simeq1/134$; including the hadronic contribution $\Delta\alpha_{\rm had}\simeq0.013$ as well, $\alpha\simeq1/132$. Since $\sigma\propto\alpha^2$, the cross section becomes $(137.036/132)^2=1.08$ times larger, that is, larger by about $7\sim8\%$.

(3) $Z$ interference. As we saw in (5) above, at $\sqrt s=10\ \mathrm{GeV}$ the contribution to the total cross section is below $10^{-4}$ and can be neglected. It is the angular distribution that is affected, and it appears as a forward–backward asymmetry of $-0.7\%$. As $\sqrt s$ is raised, this grows rapidly, and at $\sqrt s=m_Z$ the cross section itself becomes $190$ times larger.

A tree-level calculation “gives the order of magnitude and the angular distribution,” and if one aims at $1\%$ accuracy, a loop calculation is needed. Even so, the angular distribution $1+\cos^2\theta$ is hardly changed by radiative corrections (apart from the forward–backward asymmetry of $-0.7\%$), so this is a strong prediction that can be compared directly with experiment.

D.8 Summary and Exercises

D.8.1 Summary

  • The Feynman rules are a dictionary from pictures to formulas. The nine steps (draw the diagram, assign momenta, external-line factors, vertex factors, propagators, a delta function at each vertex, an integral for each internal line, sign and symmetry factor, reading off $\mathcal{M}$) do not change, whatever the theory or the process. Only the content of the factors assigned changes.
  • Four kinds of external-line factors are distinguished. Incoming particle $u$, outgoing particle $\bar u$, incoming antiparticle $\bar v$, outgoing antiparticle $v$. For spin 1, $\epsilon_\mu$ for incoming and $\epsilon_\mu^*$ for outgoing. A fermion line is written down from left to right following the line against the arrow, so the barred spinor comes at the left end and the unbarred spinor at the right end.
  • Every propagator is “$\ii\times$(numerator from the spin sum)$/(q^2-m^2)$.” The denominator is common to all spins and measures how far the virtual particle is off the mass shell. The numerator is the sum over the internal states of that spin: it is $1$ for spin 0, $\slashed q+m$ for spin $1/2$, and $-(g_{\mu\nu}-q_\mu q_\nu/m^2)$ for spin 1.
  • The vertex factors determine the content of the theory. QED has $-\ii g_eQ_f\gamma^\mu$ (for the electron, substituting $Q_e=-1$ gives $\ii g_e\gamma^\mu$); QCD has $-\ii g_s\gamma^\mu(\lambda^a/2)_{ij}$ together with the three- and four-gluon vertices; the charged weak interaction has $-\ii(g_w/2\sqrt2)\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)$, the neutral weak interaction has $-\ii(g_z/2)\gamma^\mu(c_V-c_A\gamma^5)$, and the Yukawa vertex is $-\ii m_f/v$. The “form” of the vertex directly expresses the parity character of that interaction.
  • The details come from Fermi statistics and the counting of graphs. A closed fermion loop carries $(-1)$ and a trace; a diagram in which identical fermions are exchanged carries a relative sign $(-1)$; a diagram with an automorphism carries a symmetry factor $1/S$. The number of remaining integrals is fixed by the number of loops $L=I-V+1$, and “tree” means $L=0$.
  • Keep four checks at hand. Dimension ($[\mathcal{M}]=4-n$), the Ward identity ($q_\mu\mathcal{M}^\mu=0$, which reliably catches an omitted diagram), crossing symmetry ($s\leftrightarrow t$), and the nonrelativistic limit (Rutherford, Yukawa, Fermi). Decide which of them to use before starting the calculation.
  • $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$ is the yardstick for everything. Carrying it through the procedure gives $\mathcal{M}=-(g_e^2/s)[\bar v_2\gamma^\mu u_1][\bar u_3\gamma_\mu v_4]$; averaging over spins, $\braket{\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2}=g_e^4(2-\beta^2+\beta^2\cos^2\theta)$; the cross section is $\dd\sigma/\dd\Omega=(\alpha^2\beta/4s)(2-\beta^2+\beta^2\cos^2\theta)$; and the total cross section is $\sigma=(4\pi\alpha^2/3s)\cdot\beta(3-\beta^2)/2=86.9\ \mathrm{nb}/s[\mathrm{GeV^2}]$.
  • This dictionary works unchanged in condensed matter. The propagator is the one-particle Green function (in the nonrelativistic limit, $1/(E-\varepsilon_{\bm q}+\ii\eta)$); the closed fermion loop is the Lindhard function (the $(-1)$ and the trace fix the prefactor); the Dyson summation gives the RPA dielectric function $\epsilon=1-v_q\chi_0$; and the Thomas–Fermi-screened Coulomb interaction, as far as the static longitudinal response is concerned, has the same form as the propagator of a massive vector particle. The vertex factor is the electron–phonon coupling matrix element, and Migdal’s theorem corresponds to $\alpha\ll1$ in QED.
  • The Gor’kov propagator of a superconductor has the same structure as the Dirac propagator. $\Delta$ plays the role of $m$, and the quasiparticle dispersion $\sqrt{\xi^2+\abs{\Delta}^2}$ corresponds to the relativistic dispersion $\sqrt{\bm p^2+m^2}$. The anomalous Green function is a Majorana-type propagator.

($N_c$ is $3$ for quarks and $1$ for leptons).

(3) With $m_H=125.25\ \mathrm{GeV}$, $v=246.2\ \mathrm{GeV}$, $m_b=4.18\ \mathrm{GeV}$, and $N_c=3$, find $\Gamma(H\to b\bar b)$ in MeV. Also, with $m_\tau=1.77686\ \mathrm{GeV}$ and $N_c=1$, find $\Gamma(H\to\tau^+\tau^-)$, and confirm that the ratio $\Gamma_{bb}/\Gamma_{\tau\tau}$ equals $N_c(m_b/m_\tau)^2$.

Hint: (1) Since $n=3$, $[\mathcal{M}]=1$. With $[m_f/v]=0$ and $[\bar u v]=1$, the total is $1$. (2) The right-hand side has $1+2-2=1$, which matches $[\Gamma]=1$. (3) $\beta_b^2=1-4\times17.472/15687.6=0.99555$, $\beta_b^3=0.99333$. $\Gamma_{bb}=\dfrac{3\times125.25\times17.4724}{8\pi\times60614.4}\times0.99333=4.28\ \mathrm{MeV}$. $\beta_\tau^2=1-4\times3.1572/15687.6=0.99919$, $\beta_\tau^3=0.99879$, so $\Gamma_{\tau\tau}=\dfrac{1\times125.25\times3.1572}{8\pi\times60614.4}\times0.99879=0.2593\ \mathrm{MeV}$. The ratio is $4.281/0.2593=16.51$, and this agrees with $N_c(m_b/m_\tau)^2=3\times(4.18/1.77686)^2=3\times5.534=16.60$, differing only by the difference in $\beta^3$ ($0.5\%$). Note that to obtain a value close to the measured one, one must take for $m_b$ not the pole mass $4.18$ but the running mass $\overline{m}_b(m_H)\simeq2.79\ \mathrm{GeV}$; this gives $\Gamma_{bb}\simeq1.9\ \mathrm{MeV}$, which approaches the Standard Model prediction including QCD corrections ($\simeq2.4\ \mathrm{MeV}$, which is $58\%$ of the total width $\Gamma_H=4.1\ \mathrm{MeV}$). Note also that the partial widths of the Higgs have not been measured directly (even the total width is constrained only indirectly), and the LHC compares with this prediction through the signal strength $\mu_{bb}$.

Exercise D.3: From the closed fermion loop to Thomas–Fermi screening

(1) Using the loop-number formula $L=I-V+1$, find the number of loops of the following diagrams: (a) the tree diagram of $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$, (b) the electron self-energy (one loop), (c) the photon self-energy (one loop), (d) the two-loop electron self-energy ($V=4$, $I=5$).

(2) Using the rules of Section D.5.2, write down the photon self-energy $\ii\Pi^{\mu\nu}(q)$. Do not forget the leading $(-1)$ and the trace.

(3) Show that the static, long-wavelength limit of the Lindhard function in Eq. \eqref{eq:D-lindhard} is $\chi_0(\bm q\to0,0)=-N(\varepsilon_F)$ ($N$ is the density of states per unit volume, including both spins).

(4) For the free electron gas, use $N(\varepsilon_F)=3n/2\varepsilon_F$ to calculate the Thomas–Fermi screening wavenumber $k_{\rm TF}^2=4\pi e^2N(\varepsilon_F)$ (Gaussian units) for copper. You may use $k_F=1.36\ \mathrm{\AA^{-1}}$ and $a_0=0.529\ \mathrm{\AA}$ (it can be rewritten as $k_{\rm TF}^2=4k_F/\pi a_0$). Find the screening length $1/k_{\rm TF}$ in $\mathrm{\AA}$ and compare it with the interatomic distance $2.56\ \mathrm{\AA}$.

Hint: (1) (a) $I=1,V=2\Rightarrow L=0$. (b) $I=2,V=2\Rightarrow L=1$. (c) $I=2,V=2\Rightarrow L=1$. (d) $L=5-4+1=2$. (3) As $\bm q\to0$, put $f(\varepsilon_{\bm k})-f(\varepsilon_{\bm k+\bm q})\to-\bm q\cdot\nabla_{\bm k}f$; then, setting $\omega=0$, the integrand becomes $\partial f/\partial\varepsilon$, which for $T\to0$ is $-\delta(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_F)$. (4) $k_{\rm TF}^2=4\times1.36/(\pi\times0.529)=3.27\ \mathrm{\AA^{-2}}$, $k_{\rm TF}=1.81\ \mathrm{\AA^{-1}}$, $1/k_{\rm TF}=0.553\ \mathrm{\AA}$. This is $1/4.6$ of the interatomic distance, and in a metal the Coulomb interaction is so strongly screened that it does not even reach the nearest-neighbor atoms. This is the main reason that band calculations in the one-body approximation work well.

Exercise D.4: Using crossing symmetry

Consider the limit in which all masses are neglected. The Mandelstam variables are $s=(p_1+p_2)^2$, $t=(p_1-p_3)^2$, $u=(p_1-p_4)^2$, with $s+t+u=0$.

(1) In \eqref{eq:D-M2-invariant} set $m_\mu\to0$, rewrite the dot products in terms of the Mandelstam variables, and show that

$$ \braket{\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2}_{e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-}=2g_e^4\,\frac{t^2+u^2}{s^2} $$

holds.

(2) Using crossing ($s\leftrightarrow t$), state that the spin-averaged squared amplitude of $e^-\mu^-\to e^-\mu^-$ is $2g_e^4(s^2+u^2)/t^2$, and explain that it diverges as $t\to0$ (forward scattering) and what the physical reason for this is.

(3) In the center-of-mass frame, use $t=-\tfrac{s}{2}(1-\cos\theta)$ and $u=-\tfrac{s}{2}(1+\cos\theta)$ to confirm that the result of (1) equals $g_e^4(1+\cos^2\theta)$. Find its value at $\theta=90^\circ$ in units of $g_e^4$.

Hint: (1) For massless particles, $t=-2p_1\!\cdot\!p_3$, $u=-2p_1\!\cdot\!p_4$, $s=2p_1\!\cdot\!p_2$, and $p_2\!\cdot\!p_4=p_1\!\cdot\!p_3$, $p_2\!\cdot\!p_3=p_1\!\cdot\!p_4$. Hence the bracket is $t^2/4+u^2/4$. (2) $t\to0$ corresponds to the long-distance Coulomb force, and is the same thing as the $1/\sin^4(\theta/2)$ divergence of the Rutherford cross section. It is cut off if there is screening (a finite interaction range). (3) At $\theta=90^\circ$, $1+\cos^2\theta=1$, that is, $\braket{\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2}=g_e^4=8.41\times10^{-3}$.

Exercise D.5: Obtaining the $Z$ boson decay width from the vertex factors

The partial decay width of the $Z$ can be written, using $c_V,c_A$ of Table D.4, as

$$ \Gamma\left(Z\to f\bar f\right)=\frac{g_z^2\,m_Z}{48\pi}\left(c_V^2+c_A^2\right)N_c $$

(Chapter 12). Let $g_z=0.7446$ and $m_Z=91.188\ \mathrm{GeV}$.

(1) Find the common factor $g_z^2m_Z/48\pi$ in GeV.

(2) Find $\Gamma(Z\to e^+e^-)$ and $\Gamma(Z\to\nu\bar\nu)$ (per generation) in MeV, and compare them with the measured values $83.98\ \mathrm{MeV}$ and $\Gamma_{\rm inv}/3=166.3\ \mathrm{MeV}$.

(3) Add up all the channels (three charged leptons, three neutrinos, two up-type quarks ($u,c$; the $t$ is too heavy to be produced), and three down-type quarks ($d,s,b$)) to find $\Gamma_Z$, and compare it with the measured value $2.4952\ \mathrm{GeV}$. What is the main cause of the discrepancy?

Hint: (1) $0.7446^2\times91.188/(48\pi)=50.557/150.796=0.33527\ \mathrm{GeV}$. (2) $e$: $0.33527\times0.2514=84.3\ \mathrm{MeV}$. $\nu$: $0.33527\times0.5=167.6\ \mathrm{MeV}$. The deviations from the measurements are $0.4\%$ for $e$ and $0.8\%$ for $\nu$, so both agree to within $1\%$. Note that Chapter 12 evaluates the same quantity in the $G_F$ form $\Gamma_{ee}=G_Fm_Z^3(c_V^2+c_A^2)/(6\sqrt2\pi)$ and obtains $83.4\ \mathrm{MeV}$. The $1\%$ difference is due to a difference of convention, namely whether $g_z$ is taken from $G_F$ or from $g_w/\cos\theta_W$ (Section D.4.4), and lies within the accuracy of tree level. (3) Leptons $3\times0.0843=0.2529$, neutrinos $3\times0.1676=0.5029$, up-type $2\times3\times0.33527\times0.2868=0.5769$, down-type $3\times3\times0.33527\times0.3696=1.1153$. The total is $2.448\ \mathrm{GeV}$, which is $1.9\%$ smaller than the measured value. The main cause is the QCD correction to the hadronic final state ($1+\alpha_s/\pi\simeq1.038$); multiplying by it gives $2.512\ \mathrm{GeV}$, and the gap shrinks to $0.7\%$. The remainder comes from higher-order electroweak corrections.

Exercise D.6: The $R$ ratio—counting quark colors from cross sections

The process by which hadrons are produced in $e^+e^-$ collisions is, at lowest order, $e^+e^-\to q\bar q$. The diagram is identical to that of $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$, with the final-state muons simply replaced by quarks, so the ratio of the cross sections is determined solely by the ratio of the squares of the vertex factors.

(1) For a quark of charge $Q_q$, use the fact that the vertex factor is $-\ii g_eQ_q\gamma^\mu$ to derive

$$ R\equiv\frac{\sigma\left(e^+e^-\to\text{hadrons}\right)}{\sigma\left(e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-\right)}=N_c\sum_{q}Q_q^2 $$

(the sum is taken over the quarks that can be produced at that energy).

(2) With $N_c=3$, find the value of $R$ in the three regions (a) $\sqrt s<2m_c$ ($u,d,s$), (b) $2m_c<\sqrt s<2m_b$ ($u,d,s,c$), and (c) $\sqrt s>2m_b$ ($u,d,s,c,b$). What would happen if $N_c=1$?

(3) Find the hadron production cross section at $\sqrt s=10\ \mathrm{GeV}$ in nb (use Eq. \eqref{eq:D-sigma-nb}).

Hint: (1) $\mathcal{M}$ becomes $-Q_q$ times its value (taking the $\mu^+\mu^-$ case as the reference), so $\abs{\mathcal{M}}^2$ becomes $Q_q^2$ times. Colors are distinguished as final states, so we add up the $N_c$ possibilities. (2) (a) $3\times\left(\tfrac49+\tfrac19+\tfrac19\right)=2$, (b) $3\times\tfrac{10}{9}=3.33$, (c) $3\times\tfrac{11}{9}=3.67$. For $N_c=1$ these are $2/3,\,10/9,\,11/9$ respectively, which do not agree at all with the experimental values ($\simeq2,\,3.3,\,3.6$). The $R$ ratio is a measurement that directly counts the color degrees of freedom. (3) $\sigma_{\rm had}=3.67\times0.869=3.19\ \mathrm{nb}$.
