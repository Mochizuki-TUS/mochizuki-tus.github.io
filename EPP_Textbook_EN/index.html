Introduction to Elementary Particle Physics for Materials Scientists— From Electrons and Holes to the Higgs Mechanism and Nuclear Fusion —

Particle physics seems remote to materials scientists. Many people think so. Quarks and neutrinos have nothing to do with the samples in our laboratories—or so it appears.

And yet, a considerable part of the concepts we use every day is written in the same mathematics as particle physics. The electrons and holes of a semiconductor have the same structure as the particles and antiparticles that Dirac arrived at after struggling with negative-energy solutions (where the line falls between what is the same and what is merely an analogy is drawn in Chapter 1). A superconductor spontaneously breaks a (global) $U(1)$ symmetry, and the mechanism by which the photon acquires a mass there has the same structure as the Higgs mechanism, by which the $W$ boson acquires a mass (the photon mass in vacuum remains zero. What is meant here is the effective mass that the photon acquires inside the medium that is the superconductor, whose inverse corresponds to the London penetration depth $\lambda_L$. For lead, $m_\gamma c^2=\hbar c/\lambda_L\approx5\ \mathrm{eV}$. This is the same kind of care as distinguishing the free-electron mass $m_e$ from the effective mass $m^*$ of a band. Chapter 13, Section 13.9)—or rather, the order is the reverse. It was Yoichiro Nambu who brought the idea of spontaneous symmetry breaking (the breaking of chiral symmetry and the accompanying Nambu–Goldstone boson) from BCS theory into particle physics (1960–61), and as for the mechanism by which a gauge field acquires a mass, what Anderson first saw through in the plasmons of a superconductor (1963) was formulated relativistically by Higgs and others (1964). The Majorana zero mode that appears in the vortex core of a topological superconductor satisfies the same condition $\gamma=\gamma^\dagger$ as one of the greatest unsolved problems of particle physics, whether the neutrino is a Majorana particle. And to estimate the process by which the first wall of a fusion reactor is destroyed by neutrons, we need the kinematics of two-body collisions (treated in Chapter 4 in a relativistically generalized form) and nuclear reaction cross sections.

Particle physics and materials science do not stand in an upstream–downstream relationship.
They speak the same language of symmetry, fields, renormalization, and scattering, at different energy scales.
This book shows, with equations, what the two have in common and where the boundary between the common and the non-common lies.

This book is an introduction to particle physics written from this standpoint. Its structure follows the table of contents of D. Griffiths’ Introduction to Elementary Particles (Japanese translation: “Particle Physics”) closely, but each chapter contains a section or box titled “Connection to Materials Science,” which shows, with equations rather than analogies, where the tools learned there appear in condensed matter physics and materials engineering. The final chapter (Chapter 16) is original to this book; it deals in one go, with the tools assembled so far, with nuclear fusion, irradiation damage, $\mu$SR, positron annihilation spectroscopy, neutron sources, and detector materials.

Equations are not omitted. This is the policy common to the companion volumes in this series. The lifetime of the muon ($\mu$), $\tau=192\pi^3/(G_F^2m_\mu^5)$, the cross section $\sigma=4\pi\alpha^2/(3s)$ of $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$, and the color factor $-4/3$ are all calculated to the end without skipping steps. If you work through them with your own hands, you can always follow them.

How to read this book
  • Reading straight through: from Chapter 1 in order. Chapter 1 is motivation, so even those in a hurry should not skip it.
  • Superconductivity and symmetry breaking as the goal: Chapter 5 → Chapter 6 → Chapter 13. Section 13.9 of Chapter 13 has a term-by-term correspondence table between Ginzburg–Landau theory and the Abelian Higgs model.
  • Majorana particles and topological superconductivity as the goal: Chapter 9 (the Dirac equation and BdG) → Chapter 13 → Chapter 14.
  • Nuclear fusion and irradiation damage as the goal: Chapter 3 → Chapter 4 (kinematics of the PKA) → Chapter 12 ($\beta$ decay and tritium) → Chapter 16.
  • Wanting above all to be able to calculate cross sections: Chapter 4 → Chapter 8 → Chapter 9 → Chapter 10. Appendices B, C, and D are the reference tables for calculations.
  • The Dirac equation for graphene and Weyl semimetals as the goal: Chapter 9 alone can be read by itself. It is helpful to look first at Section 5.3 of Chapter 5 ($SU(2)$ and spinors).
  • If you are unsure about group theory: first read “Introduction to Solid-State Physics” (in Japanese), Chapters 6–9 (finite groups and irreducible representations). Chapter 5 of this book is the continuous-group version of that material.
