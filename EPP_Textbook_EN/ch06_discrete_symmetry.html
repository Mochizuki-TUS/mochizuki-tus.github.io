Chapter 6Discrete Symmetries — P, C, T and the CPT Theorem

In Chapter 5 we saw that continuous symmetries give rise to conserved quantities. Spatial translation conserves momentum, time translation conserves energy, and rotation conserves angular momentum. Noether’s theorem is a beautiful theorem that gathers all of these under a single principle, and the quantum numbers it yields are additive (the momentum of a two-particle system is the sum of the momenta of the individual particles).

In nature, however, there are symmetries that cannot be reached by continuous deformation: reflecting in a mirror, replacing a particle by its antiparticle, and running a film backward. These three are called, in order, parity $\mathcal{P}$ (space inversion), charge conjugation $\mathcal{C}$, and time reversal $\mathcal{T}$. Of these, $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ return to the original state when applied twice in succession ($\mathcal{P}^2=\mathcal{C}^2=1$), so their eigenvalues can only be $\pm1$. $\mathcal{T}$ is antiunitary and the situation is different: it has no eigenvalue in the naive sense (no $\mathcal{T}$ quantum number), and, as we will see in Section 6.5, $\mathcal{T}^2=-1$ for half-integer spin. Moreover, the quantum numbers of $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ are multiplicative—the parity of a composite system is the product of the parities of its constituents. This one point alone shows that they are of a completely different character from continuous symmetries.

Some of the most thrilling episodes of twentieth-century physics took place around these three symmetries. Until 1956, it was an unquestioned premise that the mirror-image world obeys the same laws as our own. Lee and Yang pointed out that “no one has verified it experimentally for the weak interaction,” proposed concrete experimental tests, and the following year the Wu experiment shattered that premise. Nature distinguishes left from right. Then, in 1964, Cronin and Fitch found that even $\mathcal{CP}$ is violated. What remains is only $\mathcal{CPT}$, the product of all three. From the general principles of field theory (Lorentz invariance, locality, and spectral positivity), one can prove that this one cannot be violated:

$$ \left[\,\Theta,\ \Ham\,\right]=0,\qquad \Theta \equiv \mathcal{CPT} $$

And from here on comes the main subject for materials scientists. Whether the point group of a crystal has inversion symmetry $\bar{1}$ directly determines whether ferroelectricity, piezoelectricity, and second-harmonic generation (SHG) are possible. Whether time-reversal symmetry is preserved determines the presence or absence of Kramers degeneracy, and whether a $\mathbb{Z}_2$ topological insulator can exist. And the particle-physics style of argument, “a response that appears only when both $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are broken,” predicts the nonreciprocal optical response of multiferroics. What you will acquire in this chapter is the skill of dissecting elementary particles and crystals with the same tools.

6.1 Parity $\mathcal{P}$ — The World in the Mirror

6.1.1 Definition of space inversion

First we fix our terminology. Space inversion is the operation that changes the sign of all spatial coordinates:

$$ \begin{equation} \mathcal{P}:\quad \bm{x}\ \longmapsto\ -\bm{x},\qquad t\ \longmapsto\ t \label{eq:6-parity-def} \end{equation} $$

In three dimensions this amounts to multiplication by the matrix $-\mathbb{1}_3 = \mathrm{diag}(-1,-1,-1)$, whose determinant is $(-1)^3=-1$. Because the determinant is $-1$, $\mathcal{P}$ is not an element of the rotation group $SO(3)$. No matter how continuously we rotate, we can never arrive at space inversion. This is why it is called a “discrete symmetry.”

Caution: Space inversion and reflection are different, but physically amount to the same thing

The everyday phrase “reflect in a mirror” means a reflection that changes the sign along only one axis, $\sigma_z:(x,y,z)\to(x,y,-z)$. This too has determinant $-1$. The relation between space inversion $\mathcal{P}$ and the reflection $\sigma_z$ is

$$ \mathcal{P} = \sigma_z \cdot R_z(\pi) $$

that is, they are related simply by inserting a “$180^\circ$ rotation about the $z$ axis.” In a theory in which rotational symmetry holds, violation of $\mathcal{P}$ and violation of reflection symmetry mean the same thing. So we may rephrase “parity violation” as “the laws of physics differ in the world in the mirror.”

When dealing with crystals, however, care is needed. The point group of a crystal does not contain all continuous rotations, so whether it contains $\bar{1}$ (inversion) or contains $m$ (reflection) are separate conditions. For example, the point group $mm2$ (the orthorhombic phase of $\mathrm{BaTiO_3}$, in which the spontaneous polarization points along the $\langle110\rangle$ direction of the pseudocubic cell) has mirror planes but no inversion center. When carrying arguments from particle physics directly over to crystals, be conscious of this difference.

In quantum mechanics, we define the unitary operator $\hat{\mathcal{P}}$ corresponding to $\mathcal{P}$ through its action on wave functions:

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{\mathcal{P}}\,\psi(\bm{x},t) = \psi(-\bm{x},t) \label{eq:6-parity-op} \end{equation} $$

Applying it twice in succession returns the original, so $\hat{\mathcal{P}}^2=\mathbb{1}$. Therefore $\hat{\mathcal{P}}$ has only two eigenvalues, $\pm1$. $\hat{\mathcal{P}}$ is both unitary and Hermitian ($\hat{\mathcal{P}}^\dagger=\hat{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}$).

If the Hamiltonian is invariant under space inversion, that is, if $[\hat{\mathcal{P}},\Ham]=0$, then parity is a conserved quantity, and the eigenstates can be chosen to be parity eigenstates. The fact that the spherical harmonics of a particle in a central-force potential $V(\abs{\bm{x}})$ satisfy

$$ \hat{\mathcal{P}}\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi) = Y_{\ell m}(\pi-\theta,\phi+\pi) = (-1)^{\ell}\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi) $$

is the most familiar manifestation of this situation. The parity of a state of orbital angular momentum $\ell$ is $(-1)^\ell$: $s$ orbitals are $+$, $p$ orbitals are $-$, and $d$ orbitals are $+$. This is the familiar fact from chemistry that “$s,d$ are $g$ (gerade, even) and $p,f$ are $u$ (ungerade, odd).”

6.1.2 Polar vectors and axial vectors

This is the most practical part of this chapter. Although we lump them all together as “vectors,” there are two kinds of behavior under space inversion.

Definition: Polar vectors and axial vectors

Among quantities $\bm{V}$ that transform as vectors under rotations,

  • those with $\hat{\mathcal{P}}\bm{V}\hat{\mathcal{P}}^{-1} = -\bm{V}$ are called polar vectors (true vectors, $\mathcal{P}$-odd),
  • those with $\hat{\mathcal{P}}\bm{V}\hat{\mathcal{P}}^{-1} = +\bm{V}$ are called axial vectors (pseudovectors, $\mathcal{P}$-even).

Similarly, among scalar quantities $s$, those with $\hat{\mathcal{P}}s\hat{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}=+s$ are called scalars, and those with $=-s$ are called pseudoscalars.

The origin of the distinction is quite simple: it comes down to the fact that each time we take one cross product, the sign under $\mathcal{P}$ changes once. The cross product of two polar vectors, $\bm{a}\times\bm{b}$, has its sign restored, $(-\bm{a})\times(-\bm{b})=+\bm{a}\times\bm{b}$, and becomes an axial vector. This is why the angular momentum $\bm{L}=\bm{x}\times\bm{p}$ is an axial vector. And since the spin $\bm{S}$ is an angular momentum, it too is an axial vector.

Why?: The Maxwell equations require the magnetic field to be axial

That “$\bm{B}$ is an axial vector” is not an arbitrary convention handed down from above; it is a necessary consequence of the requirement that the Maxwell equations be invariant under space inversion. Let us check. Consider Faraday’s law

$$ \nabla\times\bm{E} = -\frac{\partial\bm{B}}{\partial t} $$

The electric field $\bm{E}$ is polar (because the force $\bm{F}=q\bm{E}$ is polar). $\nabla=\partial/\partial\bm{x}$ is also polar. The left-hand side is “polar $\times$ polar,” hence axial. For the right-hand side to have the same transformation property, $\bm{B}$ must be axial.

The same can be checked with Ampère’s law $\nabla\times\bm{B}=\mu_0\bm{j}$. The left-hand side is “polar $\times$ axial,” hence polar, and the current density $\bm{j}=\rho\bm{v}$ on the right-hand side is indeed polar. They are consistent.

This consistency check can be used directly in discussions of crystal properties. The question “Is this response tensor allowed in this point group?” ultimately reduces to checking “whether the transformation properties of the two sides agree.” We will do this thoroughly in Section 6.6.

6.1.3 Scalars and pseudoscalars, and the universal criterion

For scalars, too, there are two kinds. The dot product $\bm{a}\cdot\bm{b}$ of two polar vectors is invariant under $\mathcal{P}$ (a true scalar), but the dot product of a polar and an axial vector changes sign:

$$ \hat{\mathcal{P}}\,(\bm{S}\cdot\bm{p})\,\hat{\mathcal{P}}^{-1} = (+\bm{S})\cdot(-\bm{p}) = -\,\bm{S}\cdot\bm{p} $$

This $\bm{S}\cdot\bm{p}$ is one of the protagonists of this chapter, the helicity. To be precise, we use the normalized projection of the spin onto the momentum direction,

$$ \begin{equation} h \equiv \frac{\bm{S}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}}{s\hbar}, \qquad \hat{\bm{p}}\equiv\frac{\bm{p}}{\abs{\bm{p}}} \label{eq:6-helicity} \end{equation} $$

(where $s$ is the magnitude of the spin). It is conventional to take $s\hbar$ for the denominator rather than the quantum-mechanical $\abs{\bm{S}}=\hbar\sqrt{s(s+1)}$—with this normalization, for a spin-$1/2$ particle we get $h=2\bm{S}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}/\hbar=\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}=\pm1$ (right-handed and left-handed), neatly equal to $\pm1$. Since $h$ is a pseudoscalar, in a theory in which parity is conserved it can never be biased toward one side. The theorem stated next expresses exactly this content.

Theorem 6.1: Pseudoscalar criterion

Suppose that the state $\ket{\psi}$ of a system is a parity eigenstate ($\hat{\mathcal{P}}\ket{\psi}=\eta\ket{\psi}$, $\eta=\pm1$). Then the expectation value of any $\mathcal{P}$-odd operator $\hat{O}$ (a pseudoscalar, or one component of a polar vector) is zero:

$$ \braket{\hat{O}} = \bra{\psi}\hat{O}\ket{\psi} = 0 $$

Proof. Insert $\hat{\mathcal{P}}^\dagger\hat{\mathcal{P}}=\mathbb{1}$ into the expectation value.

$$ \braket{\hat{O}} =\bra{\psi}\hat{\mathcal{P}}^\dagger\,\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{O}\hat{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}\,\hat{\mathcal{P}}\ket{\psi} =\eta^{*}\eta\,\bra{\psi}\left(\hat{\mathcal{P}}\hat{O}\hat{\mathcal{P}}^{-1}\right)\ket{\psi} =1\cdot\bra{\psi}(-\hat{O})\ket{\psi}=-\braket{\hat{O}} $$

Therefore $2\braket{\hat{O}}=0$, that is, $\braket{\hat{O}}=0$. ∎

Conversely, if an experiment shows that the expectation value of a pseudoscalar quantity is nonzero, this is direct evidence that parity is violated (or that the state is not a parity eigenstate). This is the logic of the Wu experiment (Section 6.2), and at the same time the logic of using SHG to determine whether “a crystal has an inversion center” (Section 6.6). For elementary particles and crystals alike, this one theorem is the only one we use.

Connection to Materials Science: All odd-rank polar tensors vanish in centrosymmetric crystals

Theorem 6.1 is a statement about expectation values of operators, but it translates directly into response tensors of crystals. That a crystal has an inversion center (its point group contains $\bar{1}$) means that the state of the crystal is an eigenstate of $\hat{\mathcal{P}}$. Then the expectation values of $\mathcal{P}$-odd physical quantities must all vanish.

Concretely, consider the power-series expansion of the nonlinear polarization in the electric field:

$$ P_i = \varepsilon_0\left(\chi^{(1)}_{ij}E_j + \chi^{(2)}_{ijk}E_jE_k + \chi^{(3)}_{ijkl}E_jE_kE_l+\cdots\right) $$

Since both $\bm{P}$ (the polarization) and $\bm{E}$ (the electric field) are polar vectors, under space inversion $P_i\to-P_i$ and $E_j\to-E_j$. If the crystal is inversion symmetric, $\chi^{(n)}$ is unchanged by the transformation, so for the second-order term

$$ -P_i = \varepsilon_0\,\chi^{(2)}_{ijk}(-E_j)(-E_k) = +\varepsilon_0\,\chi^{(2)}_{ijk}E_jE_k = +P_i^{(2)} $$

that is, $\chi^{(2)}_{ijk}=-\chi^{(2)}_{ijk}$, and therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \text{with an inversion center}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \chi^{(2)}_{ijk}=0\ \ (\text{SHG forbidden}) \label{eq:6-chi2} \end{equation} $$

Whether second-harmonic generation (SHG) occurs directly reflects the presence or absence of inversion symmetry. This is the principle by which SHG microscopy can selectively visualize ferroelectric domains, surfaces, and interfaces (even if the bulk is centrosymmetric, inversion symmetry is always broken at the surface, so only the surface lights up). By the same argument, the piezoelectric tensor $d_{ijk}$ (third rank, polar) also vanishes if there is an inversion center. On the other hand, the fourth-rank $\chi^{(3)}$ survives because $(-1)^4=+1$—third harmonic is emitted by any crystal. Only odd-rank polar tensors serve as fingerprints of inversion symmetry.

6.1.4 Intrinsic parity of particles

So far we have considered only the parity $(-1)^\ell$ of the orbital motion. Elementary particles, however, have an intrinsic parity attached to the particle itself, independent of its state of motion. This quantity is rooted in the Dirac equation, which we treat in Chapter 9, and the essential consequence is that a fermion and an antifermion have intrinsic parities of opposite sign.

Definition: Intrinsic parity and the composition law

We write the intrinsic parity of a particle $a$ as $\eta_a=\pm1$. The total parity of a system consisting of several particles is the product of the intrinsic parities of the individual particles, multiplied by the orbital parity of the relative motion:

$$ \begin{equation} \eta_{\text{total}} = \left(\prod_a \eta_a\right)\times(-1)^{\ell} \label{eq:6-parity-composition} \end{equation} $$

Here $\ell$ is the relative orbital angular momentum (for three or more bodies, $(-1)^{\ell_1+\ell_2+\cdots}$ over the relative coordinates). To say that parity is a multiplicative quantum number is to refer to this product structure.

Some of the intrinsic parities are conventions. In the strong interaction, three quantities are conserved: the baryon number $B$, the charge $Q$, and the strangeness $S$. Corresponding to these three additive quantum numbers, three phases of the intrinsic parities can be chosen freely. Therefore the parities of the proton, the neutron, and the $\Lambda$ cannot be measured individually, and by convention we set

$$ \eta_p = \eta_n = \eta_\Lambda = +1 $$

This is not an assumption that determines the physics; it is an act of fixing the origin of the measuring stick. On the other hand, the intrinsic parity of an antiparticle cannot be chosen freely: from Dirac theory,

$$ \begin{equation} \eta_{f}\,\eta_{\bar f} = -1\qquad(\text{fermion and antifermion}) \label{eq:6-fermion-antiparity} \end{equation} $$

is required (this is a consequence of the fact that the Dirac field transforms under parity as $\psi(\bm{x},t)\to\gamma^0\psi(-\bm{x},t)$. We treat $\gamma^0$ and the transformation properties of bilinear forms in Chapter 9; for the derivation itself, in which the intrinsic parities of the $u$ spinor and the $v$ spinor come out with opposite signs, see Griffiths, Chapter 4, Section 4.4, and Peskin–Schroeder, §3.6). Hence $\eta_{\bar p}=-1$ and $\eta_{e^+}=-1$. For bosons, in contrast, $\eta_b=\eta_{\bar b}$, and $\pi^+$ and $\pi^-$ both have parity $-1$.

The intrinsic parity of the photon is $\eta_\gamma=-1$. This follows immediately from the fact that the vector potential $\bm{A}$ is a polar vector: the photon is a quantum of $\bm{A}$, and $\bm{A}\to-\bm{A}$. One says that “the photon is a vector particle with $J^P=1^-$.”

6.1.5 The $\pi$ meson is a pseudoscalar — the logic of $\pi^-d\to nn$

So how do we determine an intrinsic parity that can be measured? The most famous and most beautiful case is that of the $\pi$ meson. To state the conclusion first, $\eta_\pi=-1$; that is, the $\pi$ meson is a pseudoscalar particle ($J^P=0^-$) with spin $0$ and parity $-1$. The argument goes as follows.

Derivation: Determining $\eta_\pi=-1$ from $\pi^-d\to nn$

Step 1: Choose the reaction. When a slow $\pi^-$ is fired into deuterium ($d$), the strong interaction causes

$$ \pi^- + d \longrightarrow n + n $$

to occur. Since parity is conserved in the strong interaction (this is an experimental fact), the parities of the initial state and the final state must coincide. We use this.

Step 2: Fix the angular momentum of the initial state. Since the $\pi^-$ is negatively charged, it is captured by the deuterium nucleus and forms a pionic atom. The $\pi$ is $273$ times heavier than the electron, and the Bohr radius is inversely proportional to the reduced mass (the reduced mass of the $\pi$–$d$ system is $129.9\ \mathrm{MeV}$, $254$ times that of the electron), so the orbit is smaller by a factor of about $250$; that is, it circles very close to the nucleus. From there it cascades and finally drops into the $1s$ state ($\ell=0$), where the nuclear reaction takes place. Therefore

$$ \ell_{\text{initial}} = 0 $$

The spin of the $\pi$ is $0$ (this is known independently, by comparing the cross sections of $pp\to\pi^+d$ and the inverse reaction $\pi^+d\to pp$ through detailed balance and noting that the ratio is proportional to $(2s_\pi+1)$), and the spin of the $d$ is $1$, so the total angular momentum of the initial state is uniquely determined to be

$$ J = \underbrace{0}_{J_\pi}\ \oplus\ \underbrace{1}_{J_d}\ \oplus\ \underbrace{0}_{\ell} = 1 $$

This is the crux of the argument.

Step 3: Write down the parity of the initial state. Deuterium is a proton and a neutron bound with $\ell=0$ (mainly $^3S_1$), so $\eta_d=\eta_p\eta_n(-1)^0=+1$. Therefore

$$ \eta_{\text{initial}} = \eta_\pi\cdot\eta_d\cdot(-1)^{\ell=0} = \eta_\pi $$

The parity of the initial state is simply the intrinsic parity of the $\pi$.

Step 4: Impose the Pauli exclusion principle on the final state. The final state consists of two identical fermions ($nn$), so the total wave function must change sign when the two particles are interchanged (antisymmetric). Writing the state of a two-nucleon system with the spectroscopic notation $^{2S+1}L_J$, the sign under particle exchange is the product

$$ \underbrace{(-1)^{L}}_{\text{spatial part}}\times\underbrace{(-1)^{S+1}}_{\text{spin part}} = (-1)^{L+S+1} $$

Let us check the factor for the spin part. The triplet $S=1$ is symmetric under exchange (obvious from $\uparrow\uparrow$), giving $(-1)^{1+1}=+1$, and the singlet $S=0$ is antisymmetric, $(\uparrow\downarrow-\downarrow\uparrow)/\sqrt2$, giving $(-1)^{0+1}=-1$. This is consistent. Requiring that the whole be antisymmetric, $(-1)^{L+S+1}=-1$, gives

$$ \begin{equation} L+S=\text{even}\qquad(\text{generalized Pauli principle for two identical nucleons}) \label{eq:6-nn-pauli} \end{equation} $$

Step 5: Combine with the condition $J=1$ to narrow down the state uniquely. There are only four candidates for a two-nucleon state with $J=1$. We put them in a table and eliminate them one by one.

Step 6: Match the parities. By the composition law, Eq. \eqref{eq:6-parity-composition}, the parity of the final state is

$$ \eta_{\text{final}} = \eta_n\,\eta_n\,(-1)^{L} = (+1)(+1)(-1)^{1} = -1 $$

Since parity is conserved in the strong interaction, $\eta_{\text{initial}}=\eta_{\text{final}}$, and from Step 3, $\eta_{\text{initial}}=\eta_\pi$. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \eta_\pi = -1 \label{eq:6-pion-parity} \end{equation} $$

The $\pi$ meson has spin $0$ and parity $-1$; that is, it is a pseudoscalar particle ($J^P=0^-$). ∎

Physical Meaning: Where this derivation did the work

We used only five assumptions. (i) Parity is conserved in the strong interaction, (ii) the $\pi^-$ is captured from the $1s$ orbit ($\ell=0$), (iii) the spin of the $\pi$ is $0$, (iv) the deuteron has $J^P=1^+$, and (v) the neutron is an identical fermion. Of these, (ii) is a fact of atomic physics, namely that “because the $\pi$ is heavy, the orbital radius is small and the cascade is fast,” and (v) is a statement about statistics. We used no details of the interaction at all (neither the coupling constant nor the shape of the potential). The quantum number is fixed by symmetry alone—a good example of the power of group-theoretic arguments.

The same kind of argument is used routinely in crystalline condensed-matter physics as well. For example, the proof that “the $d$–$d$ transition at an $O_h$-symmetric $\mathrm{Ni}^{2+}$ site is electric-dipole forbidden (the Laporte rule)” amounts to this: in $\bra{d}\bm{r}\ket{d}$ the $d$ orbital is $g$ and $\bm{r}$ is $u$, so the product is $u$, and the totally symmetric component of $g\times u\times g=u$ is zero. Transition-metal oxides have pale colors only because this prohibition is slightly broken by vibrations (odd-parity phonons).

Caution: Different books word the counting differently

Some books carry out the selection for the $nn$ system with the isospin made explicit. Since $nn$ has $T=1$ (an isospin triplet, symmetric under exchange), the requirement that the total wave function (space $\times$ spin $\times$ isospin) be antisymmetric reads

$$ (-1)^{L}\times(-1)^{S+1}\times(+1) = -1 $$

and again $L+S$ must be even. The conclusion is the same $^3P_1$ as in the main text. The difference in style is only whether the isospin factor is written explicitly; the physics is identical.

The correctness of the conclusion has also been confirmed by an independent experiment. That the planes of polarization of the two photons emitted in $\pi^0\to\gamma\gamma$ tend to be orthogonal is a characteristic of a pseudoscalar decay (if the $\pi^0$ were a scalar $0^+$, the planes of polarization would be aligned in parallel), and it has actually been measured from the distribution of the angle between the two planes in $\pi^0\to e^+e^-e^+e^-$ (two Dalitz pairs).

Worked Example 6.1: Is $\rho^0\to\pi^+\pi^-$ allowed?

The $\rho^0$ has $J^P=1^-$. Verify that the decay into $\pi^+\pi^-$ is allowed by the strong interaction (which conserves parity).

Solution. The final state consists of two $\pi$ mesons, each of spin $0$, so the total angular momentum is determined by the relative orbital angular momentum alone: $J=\ell$. To match $J=1$ of the $\rho^0$, we need $\ell=1$. By the composition law, Eq. \eqref{eq:6-parity-composition}, the parity of the final state is

$$ \eta_{\pi\pi} = \eta_{\pi^+}\eta_{\pi^-}(-1)^{\ell} = (-1)(-1)(-1)^1 = -1 $$

This agrees with the parity $-1$ of the initial state $\rho^0$. Hence the decay is allowed. Indeed, almost $100\%$ of $\rho^0$ decays are to $\pi\pi$, and with width $\Gamma\approx147\ \mathrm{MeV}$ (lifetime $\sim4.5\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{s}$) it is a typical strong decay. ∎

Incidentally, consider $\rho^0\to\pi^0\pi^0$. Since this has two identical bosons, Bose statistics requires $\ell$ to be even, which is incompatible with $\ell=1$. Therefore it is completely forbidden. It is not observed in experiments either. A prohibition by symmetry works “in one line” like this.

6.1.6 Summary of the parity composition law

In practice, what we use is, in the end, the following three lines.

The three principles of parity calculation

  1. Multiplicative: The parity of a composite system is the product of the intrinsic parities of its constituent particles.
  2. Orbital factor: Multiply by $(-1)^\ell$ for each relative orbital angular momentum $\ell$.
  3. Conservation law: Parity is conserved in the strong and electromagnetic interactions. In the weak interaction it is not conserved (Section 6.2).

From these three principles, selection rules such as “$\eta\to\pi^0\pi^0$ is forbidden” (Worked Example 6.3) and “$\eta\to4\pi^0$ is forbidden” (from parity and Bose statistics for four identical $\pi^0$) follow mechanically. Conversely, $\eta\to3\pi^0$ is allowed by parity ($(-1)^3=-1$ agrees with the $\eta$), so these three principles say nothing about it. What actually suppresses this decay is not parity but isospin ($G$ parity; see the Math Note at the end of Section 6.3), and it proceeds mainly through isospin breaking due to $m_u\neq m_d$ (see the second half of Worked Example 6.3).

6.2 Parity Violation — Nature Is Left-Handed

6.2.1 The $\theta$–$\tau$ puzzle

In the early 1950s, experiments with cosmic rays and accelerators found two troublesome particles. They were named $\theta^+$ and $\tau^+$ at the time (they have nothing to do with the present-day $\tau$ lepton). The trouble was that the two had

  • the same mass (about $494\ \mathrm{MeV}$ within the precision of the time, in agreement)
  • the same lifetime ($\sim1.2\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s}$, in agreement)
  • the same electric charge and production cross section

and yet they decayed differently.

$$ \theta^+ \to \pi^+\pi^0,\qquad \tau^+ \to \pi^+\pi^+\pi^- $$

Let us compute the parities. Every $\pi$ in the final state has $\eta_\pi=-1$. Since $\theta,\tau$ both were known to have spin $0$, angular-momentum conservation fixes $\ell=0$ strictly for $\pi\pi$, and for $\pi\pi\pi$ the two relative angular momenta satisfy $\ell_1=\ell_2$, so the orbital parity $(-1)^{\ell_1+\ell_2}$ is $+1$ regardless of $\ell$. Therefore

$$ \eta(\pi\pi) = (-1)^2(-1)^0 = +1,\qquad \eta(\pi\pi\pi) = (-1)^3(-1)^0 = -1 $$

That is, the parity of $\theta^+$ is $+1$ and the parity of $\tau^+$ is $-1$. As long as parity is conserved, these two must be different particles. But there is no reason for nature to provide two different particles with exactly the same mass and lifetime. This is the $\theta$–$\tau$ puzzle.

Why?: The Dalitz plot indicated “spin $0$”

In the three-body decay $\tau^+\to\pi^+\pi^+\pi^-$, if we take two independent kinematic variables (the Dalitz variables $m_{12}^2, m_{23}^2$ of Chapter 4) as axes and plot the events, the spin and parity of the parent particle can be read off from the density distribution. In 1953, Dalitz showed from this analysis that $\tau^+$ has $J^P=0^-$. On the other hand, the two-body decay $\theta^+\to\pi^+\pi^0$ gives $J^P=0^+$. The absurdity of “a $0^+$ particle and a $0^-$ particle coexisting with the same mass and the same lifetime” was thus presented quantitatively.

We now know that both $\theta^+$ and $\tau^+$ are the same $K^+$ ($J^P=0^-$, mass $493.677\ \mathrm{MeV}$, lifetime $1.238\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s}$). The $K^+$ decays through the weak interaction, and since the weak interaction does not conserve parity, final states of both parities, $+$ and $-$, emerge from the same parent. The branching ratios are $20.7\%$ for $\pi^+\pi^0$ and $5.6\%$ for $\pi^+\pi^+\pi^-$.

6.2.2 Lee and Yang’s observation (1956)

In 1956, Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang thoroughly re-examined past experimental data and reported a startling fact.

Lee–Yang observation (1956)

“Parity conservation has been verified to high precision for the strong and electromagnetic interactions. But for the weak interaction, there is not a single experiment that has tested it.”

This is not an inference but the conclusion of a literature survey. At the time, parity conservation was considered so self-evident that no one had tried to check it in the weak interaction. Lee and Yang went one step further and listed concrete proposals of the form “if parity is violated, this is what such-and-such an experiment should show.” Foremost among them was the $\beta$ decay of polarized nuclei.

The logic is exactly Theorem 6.1 of Section 6.1.3. The dot product of the nuclear spin $\bm{J}$ (axial) and the momentum $\bm{p}_e$ of the emitted electron (polar)

$$ \begin{equation} \braket{\bm{J}\cdot\bm{p}_e}\ \ \text{is a pseudoscalar}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathcal{P}\ \text{preserved requires it to vanish} \label{eq:6-wu-observable} \end{equation} $$

Conversely, if an experiment shows that this is nonzero, it is direct evidence of parity violation.

6.2.3 The Wu experiment (1957) — $\beta$ decay of $^{60}\mathrm{Co}$

Chien-Shiung Wu teamed up with the low-temperature group at the U.S. National Bureau of Standards (NBS) and accomplished this experiment in half a year. What they measured was

$$ {}^{60}\mathrm{Co}\ (J^P=5^+)\ \longrightarrow\ {}^{60}\mathrm{Ni}^{*}\ (J^P=4^+) + e^- + \bar\nu_e $$

That is the decay they measured. The essential points of the experiment are three: polarizing the nuclei, monitoring the degree of polarization independently, and measuring the front–back asymmetry of the $\beta$ rays.

Derivation: Following the logic of the Wu experiment from start to finish

Step 1: Align (polarize) the nuclear spins. The nuclear spin of $^{60}\mathrm{Co}$ is as large as $J=5$. To align it in one direction, it suffices that the interaction energy $\mu B$ between the nuclear magnetic moment $\mu$ and the external magnetic field $B$ exceed the thermal energy $k_BT$. The nuclear magneton, the unit of nuclear magnetic moment, is extremely small, $\mu_N=3.15\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{eV/T}$, and the nuclear magnetic moment of $^{60}\mathrm{Co}$ is only a few times that ($\mu=3.80\,\mu_N$), so for $B=1\ \mathrm{T}$ or so we need

$$ T \ll \frac{\mu B}{k_B} \approx \frac{3.80\times3.15\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{eV}}{8.62\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/K}} \approx 1.4\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{K} $$

This is an outrageously low temperature. So Wu and her collaborators deposited a thin layer of $^{60}\mathrm{Co}$ on the surface of the paramagnetic salt $\mathrm{Ce\,Mg}$ nitrate, cooled it to $\sim10\ \mathrm{mK}$ by adiabatic demagnetization, and furthermore polarized the nuclei using the hyperfine field ($\sim10\ \mathrm{T}$ class) created by the paramagnetic ions. This trick of using a “strong internal field” is the crux of the experiment.

Step 2: Monitor the degree of polarization. The nuclear polarization is confirmed not with the $\beta$ rays but with the angular anisotropy of the $\gamma$ rays emitted at the same time (the $^{60}\mathrm{Ni}^*$ cascade, $1.17,\ 1.33\ \mathrm{MeV}$). Since $\gamma$ emission is an electromagnetic interaction, it conserves parity, and only even powers of $\cos\theta$ appear ($\braket{\cos\theta}=0$). That is, only the intensity ratio between the equatorial and polar directions changes. This serves as a thermometer of the degree of polarization. When the sample warms up, the anisotropy disappears, so one can tell in real time “when the polarization was lost.”

Step 3: Measure the angular distribution of the $\beta$ rays. We compare the counts of $\beta$ rays forward and backward with respect to the direction of the nuclear spin (the direction of the magnetic field). In general the angular distribution can be written as

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{\dd N}{\dd\Omega} \propto 1 + \alpha\,P\,\frac{v_e}{c}\,\cos\theta, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{\bm{J}\cdot\bm{p}_e}{\abs{\bm{J}}\abs{\bm{p}_e}} \label{eq:6-wu-dist} \end{equation} $$

Here $\alpha$ is the asymmetry parameter and $P=\braket{J_z}/J$ is the nuclear polarization ($P=1$ for complete polarization, $P=0$ for no polarization). If parity is conserved, Theorem 6.1 requires $\alpha=0$.

Step 4: The result. Wu and her collaborators observed that reversing the direction of the magnetic field clearly changes the counting rate of the $\beta$ rays. The asymmetry reached $\sim40\%$, far larger than the statistical error. The sign is

$$ \alpha \simeq -1 $$

(electrons tend to be emitted in the direction opposite to the spin). Moreover, the $\beta$-ray asymmetry disappeared at the same time as the $\gamma$-ray anisotropy vanished when the sample warmed up. This is decisive evidence that it is caused by the nuclear polarization and not by a systematic error of the apparatus.

Step 5: Conclusion. $\braket{\bm{J}\cdot\bm{p}_e}\neq0$. The expectation value of a pseudoscalar is nonzero. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \text{parity is not conserved in the weak interaction} \label{eq:6-parity-violation} \end{equation} $$

Moreover, $\alpha\simeq-1$ means that parity is not “slightly violated” but maximally violated. ∎

Physical Meaning: “Absolute left and right” can be conveyed by telephone

Before the Wu experiment, it was thought that there was no way to convey the definition of “right” to a distant alien by radio waves alone (the Ozma problem). Even if we say “hold out your right hand,” there is no guarantee that the other party calls the same hand “right” as we do.

Now we can say the following. “Prepare cobalt-60 and align the nuclear spins with a magnetic field. Call the direction in which more electrons come out your feet and the direction of the spin your head; then, as seen from you, $\cdots$.” The weak interaction has given us a definition of left and right common to the universe. This is the meaning of parity violation.

However, there is a pitfall in this explanation. If the other party is made of antimatter, the answer is reversed by the effect of $\mathcal{C}$. A correct definition needs the proviso “if you are made of matter.” The $\mathcal{CP}$ violation of Section 6.4 makes even this proviso unnecessary (since $\mathcal{CP}$ is violated, matter and antimatter can be distinguished absolutely).

6.2.4 The helicity of the neutrino — the Goldhaber experiment (1958)

The Wu experiment showed that “parity is violated,” but it does not determine the form of the violation. What determined it was a measurement by Goldhaber, Grodzins, and Sunyar, which is called a masterpiece of experimental physics. They measured the helicity of the neutrino directly.

Derivation: The Goldhaber experiment — transferring helicity by angular-momentum conservation alone

Step 1: Choose the reaction. We use orbital electron capture of $^{152}\mathrm{Eu}^{m}$ ($J=0$).

$$ e^- + {}^{152}\mathrm{Eu}^{m}\,(J=0)\ \longrightarrow\ {}^{152}\mathrm{Sm}^{*}\,(J=1) + \nu_e $$

This is followed by $^{152}\mathrm{Sm}^*\to{}^{152}\mathrm{Sm}\,(J=0)+\gamma$ ($961\ \mathrm{keV}$).

Step 2: Count the angular momentum. The parent $^{152}\mathrm{Eu}^{m}$ has $J=0$, and the captured electron has spin $1/2$. The total angular momentum of the initial state is therefore $1/2$. The final state consists of $^{152}\mathrm{Sm}^*$ ($J=1$) and $\nu_e$ ($1/2$), and for the total to be $1/2$ their spins must point in opposite directions.

Step 3: Count the momentum. Since the parent is at rest, $^{152}\mathrm{Sm}^*$ and $\nu_e$ fly off in opposite directions: $\bm{p}_{\mathrm{Sm}}=-\bm{p}_\nu$.

Step 4: Transfer the helicity. Since the spins are opposite by Step 2 and the momenta are opposite by Step 3, the relative orientation of spin and momentum, that is, the helicity, is the same for both:

$$ h(\,{}^{152}\mathrm{Sm}^*) = h(\nu_e) $$

We cannot catch the neutrino, but we can measure the helicity of the recoil nucleus $^{152}\mathrm{Sm}^*$—this is the heart of the idea of the experiment.

Step 5: Transfer the helicity of the recoil nucleus to the $\gamma$ ray. In $^{152}\mathrm{Sm}^*(J=1)\to{}^{152}\mathrm{Sm}(J=0)+\gamma$, if we select only the $\gamma$ rays emitted in the direction opposite to the neutrino (that is, in the direction of motion of the recoil nucleus), angular-momentum conservation makes the helicity of the $\gamma$ ray equal to that of the recoil nucleus. “Selecting only the forward $\gamma$ rays” can be achieved by resonant scattering: only the $\gamma$ rays Doppler-shifted by the recoil are resonantly reabsorbed and reemitted by the $^{152}\mathrm{Sm}$ target. This is a truly ingenious kinematic filter.

Step 6: Measure the circular polarization of the $\gamma$ rays. When they are transmitted through magnetized iron, the transmission probabilities of left and right circularly polarized light differ because of the interaction with the electron spins (magnetic Compton scattering). By reversing the direction of the magnetization and looking at the difference in counting rates, the sign of the circular polarization, that is, the helicity, is determined.

Step 7: The result.

$$ \begin{equation} h(\nu_e) = -1\qquad(\text{measured value}\ -1.0\pm0.3) \label{eq:6-nu-helicity} \end{equation} $$

The neutrino is left-handed only. The antineutrino is (by $\mathcal{CPT}$) right-handed only. ∎

Physical Meaning: A preview of the V$-$A structure

The fact that “neutrinos are only left-handed” fixes the form of the weak-interaction current. We treat this in detail in Chapter 12, but to anticipate just the conclusion, the weak-interaction vertex takes the form

$$ \bar{u}\,\gamma^\mu\frac{1-\gamma^5}{2}\,u \;=\;\frac{1}{2}\Big(\underbrace{\bar u\gamma^\mu u}_{\text{vector V}}\;-\;\underbrace{\bar u\gamma^\mu\gamma^5 u}_{\text{axial vector A}}\Big) $$

Here $\gamma^\mu$ (the vector current, whose spatial components are $\mathcal{P}$-odd) and $\gamma^\mu\gamma^5$ (the axial-vector current, whose spatial components are $\mathcal{P}$-even) enter in equal amounts, and their interference term is $\mathcal{P}$-odd, so parity is maximally violated (note that the transformation of the four-current differs from component to component: $\bar u\gamma^0u$ is $\mathcal{P}$-even and its spatial components are odd; the axial current is the opposite). $(1-\gamma^5)/2$ is the projection operator that picks out only the left-handed component, and this is the mathematical identity of “nature is left-handed.” Note that what $\alpha\simeq-1$ in the Wu experiment means is that, because $^{60}\mathrm{Co}$ ($5^+\to4^+$) is a pure Gamow–Teller transition, the violation in the axial coupling is maximal ($C_A'=C_A$); the ratio of $V$ to $A$ being $1:1$ is not determined by the Wu experiment alone. That ratio is determined from decays in which Fermi and Gamow–Teller transitions are mixed (such as neutron decay) (Chapter 12, Section 12.3.2).

Note also that $\gamma^5$ is a pseudoscalar matrix that changes sign under parity, and it will be a protagonist of Chapters 9 and 12. It is enough to remember: “whenever $\gamma^5$ appears, suspect parity violation.”

Worked Example 6.2: Why $\pi^+\to\mu^+\nu_\mu$ is overwhelmingly more frequent than $e^+\nu_e$

The decay branching ratios of $\pi^+$ are $99.99\%$ for $\pi^+\to\mu^+\nu_\mu$ and only $1.23\times10^{-4}$ for $\pi^+\to e^+\nu_e$. Considering phase space alone, the lighter $e^+$ should be favored; why is it suppressed?

Solution. Suppose the $\pi^+$ has spin $0$ and is at rest. The $\ell^+$ and $\nu$ fly off in opposite directions. For the total spin to be $0$, their spins must point in opposite directions. The momenta are also opposite, so the helicity is the same for both. The neutrino is always left-handed ($h=-1$), so the $\ell^+$ must also be left-handed.

However, the $V-A$ interaction couples only to the right-handed component for antiparticles. The probability that a left-handed $\ell^+$ appears is proportional to the “helicity mixing” caused by the mass, and its amplitude is $\propto m_\ell$. Therefore the decay rate is proportional to $m_\ell^2$:

$$ \frac{\Gamma(\pi\to e\nu)}{\Gamma(\pi\to\mu\nu)} =\left(\frac{m_e}{m_\mu}\right)^2\left(\frac{m_\pi^2-m_e^2}{m_\pi^2-m_\mu^2}\right)^2 $$

We insert numbers: $m_e=0.511$, $m_\mu=105.658$, $m_\pi=139.570\ \mathrm{MeV}$.

$$ \left(\frac{0.511}{105.658}\right)^2=2.339\times10^{-5},\qquad \left(\frac{139.570^2-0.511^2}{139.570^2-105.658^2}\right)^2=\left(\frac{19479.5}{8316.2}\right)^2=5.487 $$ $$ \Longrightarrow\quad 2.339\times10^{-5}\times5.487 = 1.283\times10^{-4} $$

This agrees well with the measured value $1.230\times10^{-4}$ (the remaining $4\%$ difference is a radiative correction). ∎

This “suppression by $m_\ell^2$” is called helicity suppression, and it is one of the most striking consequences of angular-momentum conservation and the $V$/$A$ structure of the lepton current. Note that this suppression follows only from the current being of vector type ($V$) or axial-vector type ($A$), so the same $m_\ell^2$ suppression appears even for a pure $A$ coupling that conserves parity (it is not in itself evidence of parity violation). The suppression disappears if the current is of pseudoscalar type ($P$); in that case the ratio becomes $\approx5.5$, from the phase-space factor above alone. Historically, this ratio provided decisive evidence for excluding a $P$ coupling.

6.3 Charge Conjugation $\mathcal{C}$ — Exchanging Particles and Antiparticles

6.3.1 Definition, and which particles can be eigenstates

Definition: Charge conjugation $\mathcal{C}$

The charge conjugation transformation $\hat{\mathcal{C}}$ is the operation that replaces a particle by its antiparticle. It leaves the momentum and spin unchanged and reverses the sign of all internal quantum numbers, such as electric charge, baryon number, lepton number, and strangeness:

$$ \hat{\mathcal{C}}\ket{p,\ \bm{p},\ \bm{s}} = \ket{\bar p,\ \bm{p},\ \bm{s}} $$

Since $\hat{\mathcal{C}}^2=\mathbb{1}$, the eigenvalues are $\pm1$.

There is an important restriction here. Because $\hat{\mathcal{C}}$ reverses the charge, a charged particle cannot be an eigenstate of $\hat{\mathcal{C}}$: $\hat{\mathcal{C}}\ket{\pi^+}=\ket{\pi^-}$, which is not a constant multiple of $\ket{\pi^+}$. A $\mathcal{C}$ eigenvalue ($C$ parity) can be defined only for

  • neutral particles whose charge, baryon number, lepton number, strangeness, and so on all vanish ($\gamma,\ \pi^0,\ \eta,\ \rho^0,\ \omega,\ \phi,\ J/\psi$, etc.), and
  • systems consisting of a particle–antiparticle pair (positronium $e^+e^-$, quarkonium $q\bar q$, the $\pi^+\pi^-$ system, etc.).

The neutron is not an eigenstate, since $\hat{\mathcal{C}}\ket{n}=\ket{\bar n}$ (the baryon number goes from $+1\to-1$). Note that being neutral does not by itself guarantee being an eigenstate.

6.3.2 The $C$ parity of the photon is $-1$

This follows from the first principles of electromagnetism. Charge conjugation reverses all charges, so the electromagnetic fields created by the charges are reversed as well:

$$ \hat{\mathcal{C}}:\quad \rho\to-\rho,\quad \bm{j}\to-\bm{j} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bm{E}\to-\bm{E},\quad \bm{B}\to-\bm{B},\quad A^\mu\to-A^\mu $$

The photon is the quantum of $A^\mu$, so a one-photon state changes sign under $\hat{\mathcal{C}}$:

$$ \begin{equation} C(\gamma) = -1,\qquad\text{hence}\quad C(n\gamma) = (-1)^{n} \label{eq:6-photon-C} \end{equation} $$

The $C$ parity of a state of $n$ photons is $(-1)^n$ because $C$ is a multiplicative quantum number. This single line generates all of the selection rules that follow.

6.3.3 The $C$ parity of the $\pi^0$ is $+1$

The $\pi^0$ decays into $2\gamma$ (branching ratio $98.82\%$, lifetime $8.43\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm{s}$). This decay proceeds by the electromagnetic interaction, so $C$ is conserved. Therefore

$$ C(\pi^0) = C(2\gamma) = (-1)^2 = +1 $$

That is, the $\pi^0$ has $J^{PC}=0^{-+}$. And the following prediction follows immediately.

Theorem 6.2: $\pi^0\to3\gamma$ is forbidden by $\mathcal{C}$

Whereas $C(3\gamma)=(-1)^3=-1$, we have $C(\pi^0)=+1$. This decay cannot occur through the electromagnetic interaction, which conserves $C$.

Experimental value: $\mathrm{BR}(\pi^0\to3\gamma) < 3.1\times10^{-8}$ ($90\%$ confidence upper limit). This is suppressed by more than $7$ orders of magnitude relative to the $2\gamma$ decay (branching ratio $98.82\%$). If it were only a matter of one extra factor of $\alpha$, a suppression of $1/137$ should suffice, yet the decay is suppressed far more strongly. This shows that “it is not a matter of phase space or coupling constants, but a strict prohibition due to symmetry.”

6.3.4 Furry’s theorem

The prohibition of the $3\gamma$ decay can also be understood in the language of diagrams. In general, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 6.3 (Furry’s theorem, 1937)

In QED, the sum of the contributions of all diagrams in which an odd number of photon lines are attached to a closed fermion loop is zero.

Sketch of proof. For a closed loop there always exist two diagrams as a pair: one in which the fermion circulates clockwise and one in which it circulates counterclockwise. These two are transformed into each other by charge conjugation $\hat{\mathcal{C}}$. Under $\hat{\mathcal{C}}$ the electromagnetic current changes sign, $\hat{\mathcal{C}}j^\mu\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{-1}=-j^\mu$, so the amplitude of a loop with $n$ photons attached is multiplied by $(-1)^n$. If $n$ is odd, the amplitude equals $-1$ times itself, and is therefore zero. ∎

Math Note: Furry’s theorem seen through Dirac traces

The amplitude for $n$ photons attached to a closed fermion loop takes the form of a trace of $\gamma$ matrices,

$$ \mathcal{M}_n \propto \Tr\left[\gamma^{\mu_1}\frac{1}{\slashed{k}_1-m}\gamma^{\mu_2}\frac{1}{\slashed{k}_2-m}\cdots\gamma^{\mu_n}\frac{1}{\slashed{k}_n-m}\right] $$

The charge conjugation matrix $C=\ii\gamma^2\gamma^0$ satisfies $C^{-1}\gamma^\mu C = -(\gamma^\mu)^{T}$ (Chapter 9 and Appendix C). Inserting this into the trace and using $\Tr[A^T]=\Tr[A]$ together with the reversal of the order of the matrices, we obtain a factor $(-1)^n$ from the $n$ matrices $\gamma^\mu$, so that

$$ \mathcal{M}_n = (-1)^n\,\mathcal{M}_n $$

If $n$ is odd, then $\mathcal{M}_n=0$. This is not an abstract argument: it is a statement that can be verified using only the algebra of the $\gamma$ matrices.

This theorem has a practical benefit. In higher-order QED calculations there is no need to count “triangle loops,” which greatly reduces the amount of computation. It also guarantees that effective interactions with three external photon lines (of the $F^3$ type) are not generated.

6.3.5 Positronium — $\mathcal{C}$ alone changes the lifetime by three orders of magnitude

The power of $\mathcal{C}$ shows up most clearly in the bound state of an electron and a positron, positronium (Ps). It is obtained by replacing the proton of a hydrogen atom with a positron; since the reduced mass is $m_e/2$, the binding energy is half that of hydrogen, $-6.8\ \mathrm{eV}$ in the ground state.

Derivation: The $C$ parity of a fermion–antifermion pair

Step 1: Decomposing the exchange. Acting with $\hat{\mathcal{C}}$ on an $e^+e^-$ state interchanges the labels of particle and antiparticle. This is the same as “swapping the two constituents entirely,” and it can be decomposed into three exchanges: spatial, spin, and intrinsic (as fermions).

Step 2: The spatial part. The relative coordinate $\bm{r}=\bm{r}_{e^-}-\bm{r}_{e^+}$ goes to $\bm{r}\to-\bm{r}$, which gives a factor $(-1)^{L}$.

Step 3: The spin part. The triplet ($S=1$) is symmetric and the singlet ($S=0$) is antisymmetric, so the factor is $(-1)^{S+1}$.

Step 4: The fermion factor. Exchanging a fermion and an antifermion produces an additional factor $(-1)$ from the anticommutation of the field operators.

Step 5: Multiplying together.

$$ \begin{equation} C(f\bar f) = (-1)^{L}\cdot(-1)^{S+1}\cdot(-1) = (-1)^{L+S} \label{eq:6-C-fermion-pair} \end{equation} $$

Incidentally, the parity can be obtained by the same reasoning. From $\eta_f\eta_{\bar f}=-1$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:6-fermion-antiparity}) and the orbital factor,

$$ \begin{equation} P(f\bar f) = (-1)^{L+1} \label{eq:6-P-fermion-pair} \end{equation} $$

These two equations are used as they stand when we build the table of $J^{PC}$ for quarkonium ($c\bar c$, $b\bar b$). ∎

Let us apply Eq. \eqref{eq:6-C-fermion-pair} to the ground state ($L=0$).

The ratio of the lifetimes is $142.05\ \mathrm{ns}/125.14\ \mathrm{ps} = 1135$, that is, three orders of magnitude. This enormous difference comes from a single $\pm1$ quantum number, the $C$ parity.

Derivation: Obtaining the lifetime ratio $\sim1100$ from formulas

Step 1: The lowest-order decay rates. From QED calculations (Chapter 7, Section 7.3.3; the annihilation cross section itself is in Chapter 10, Section 10.7.3), the decay rates of para-Ps and ortho-Ps are

$$ \Gamma_{\text{para}} = \frac{1}{2}\,m_e\,\alpha^5, \qquad \Gamma_{\text{ortho}} = \frac{2(\pi^2-9)}{9\pi}\,m_e\,\alpha^6 $$

The powers of $\alpha$ differ by one because one more photon means one more vertex.

Step 2: Turning the para lifetime into a number. With $m_e=0.511\ \mathrm{MeV}$ and $\alpha^5 = 137.036^{-5} = 2.070\times10^{-11}$,

$$ \Gamma_{\text{para}} = 0.5\times0.511\times2.070\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{MeV} = 5.288\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{MeV} $$

Dividing by $\hbar = 6.582\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{MeV\,s}$ gives

$$ \tau_{\text{para}} = \frac{6.582\times10^{-22}}{5.288\times10^{-12}}\ \mathrm{s} = 1.245\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{s} = 124.5\ \mathrm{ps} $$

This agrees with the measured $125.14\ \mathrm{ps}$ to $0.5\%$ (the theoretical value through $O(\alpha^2)$ is $125.16\ \mathrm{ps}$).

Step 3: The ortho lifetime. With $\dfrac{2(\pi^2-9)}{9\pi} = \dfrac{2\times0.8696}{28.274} = 0.06152$ and $\alpha^6 = 1.510\times10^{-13}$,

$$ \Gamma_{\text{ortho}} = 0.06152\times0.511\times1.510\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{MeV} = 4.748\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{MeV} $$ $$ \tau_{\text{ortho}} = \frac{6.582\times10^{-22}}{4.748\times10^{-15}}\ \mathrm{s} = 1.386\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{s} = 138.6\ \mathrm{ns} $$

This differs from the measured $142.05\ \mathrm{ns}$ by $2.4\%$. The difference is accounted for by the $O(\alpha)$ radiative correction (which has a coefficient $-10.3\,\alpha/\pi$).

Step 4: Evaluating the ratio.

$$ \frac{\tau_{\text{ortho}}}{\tau_{\text{para}}} =\frac{\Gamma_{\text{para}}}{\Gamma_{\text{ortho}}} =\frac{1/2}{\dfrac{2(\pi^2-9)}{9\pi}\,\alpha} =\frac{9\pi}{4(\pi^2-9)}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha} = 8.128\times137.036 = 1114 $$

This agrees well with the measured ratio $1135$. The true origin of the three-orders-of-magnitude lifetime difference is, in the end, just “one factor of the fine-structure constant $\alpha$.” And the reason there is one more photon lies in a single point: since the $C$ parity is $-1$, the state cannot fall into an even number of photons. ∎

Connection to Materials Science: Positron annihilation spectroscopy (PAS) is a tool for measuring vacancies and free volume

The physics of positronium is used routinely as a method for materials characterization. In positron annihilation spectroscopy (PAS), positrons emitted from a $^{22}\mathrm{Na}$ source are implanted into a sample, and one measures the time until annihilation and the angular correlation of the $511\ \mathrm{keV}$ photons. The correspondence between the measured quantities and the information about the material is as follows.

  • Positron lifetime: Since the positron has positive charge, it feels repulsion from the positive ions and is readily trapped in a vacancy, where an ion is missing. Inside a vacancy the surrounding electron density $n_e$ is lower, so the annihilation rate $\Gamma\propto n_e$ decreases and the lifetime increases. It is $100$–$200\ \mathrm{ps}$ in the bulk of a metal, $\sim1.5$ times longer for a monovacancy, and longer still for a void. It is used for the nondestructive evaluation of irradiation defects, fatigue, and precipitates.
  • Lifetime of ortho-Ps (PALS): In substances with free volume, such as polymers and zeolites, ortho-Ps is formed. In vacuum its lifetime is $142\ \mathrm{ns}$, but it undergoes pick-off annihilation with electrons of the vacancy wall (the $e^+$ swaps partners and disappears into $2\gamma$), so the lifetime is shortened to $1$–$5\ \mathrm{ns}$. From this shortening one can determine the size of a free volume with a radius of roughly $0.2$–$1\ \mathrm{nm}$ (Tao–Eldrup model; the resolution in radius is about $0.01\ \mathrm{nm}$). It is a standard method for analyzing the glass transition of polymers and the permeation properties of membranes.
  • Angular correlation and Doppler broadening of $2\gamma$: Since these reflect the momentum distribution of the annihilating electrons, they can measure the Fermi surface (the ACAR method) and the chemical environment around defects (the coincidence Doppler broadening method).

What is at work here is the selection rule from $C$ parity, “$2\gamma$ or $3\gamma$.” The symmetry of elementary particles directly provides the resolution of materials measurement. The PET of medicine rests on the same physics (the coincidence counting of $2\gamma$).

Math Note: $G$ parity — combining $\mathcal C$ with an isospin rotation

Only neutral particles can be eigenstates of $\mathcal C$ (Section 6.3.1). The $\pi^\pm$ are interchanged by $\mathcal C$, so they have no $C$ parity. We therefore combine charge conjugation with a rotation in isospin space (Chapter 5, Section 5.4) to construct a quantum number that can also be used for charged $\pi$:

$$ \mathcal G \equiv \mathcal C\,\ee^{\ii\pi I_2} $$

$\ee^{\ii\pi I_2}$ is a $180^\circ$ rotation about the second axis in isospin space, which reverses $I_3\to-I_3$ and hence the sign of the charge. Since $\mathcal C$ undoes this, $\mathcal G$ maps each component of a multiplet to itself.

  • The $\pi$ meson: For $\ket{1,0}$ with $I=1$ we have $\ee^{\ii\pi I_2}\ket{1,0}=-\ket{1,0}$, and the $\pi^0$ has $C=+1$, so $G_\pi=-1$. Since $\mathcal G$ commutes with isospin rotations, the whole triplet has $G=-1$, and the $G$ parity of $n$ $\pi$ mesons is $(-1)^n$.
  • Neutral mesons with $I=0$: The rotation does nothing, so $G=C$. The $\eta$ ($C=+1$) has $G=+1$, and the $\omega$ ($C=-1$) has $G=-1$.

In the strong interaction, in which both $\mathcal C$ and isospin are conserved, $G$ is conserved as well. Then

  • $\eta\to3\pi$: $G=+1\to(-1)^3=-1$, so the decay is forbidden. The actual branching ratio ($32.6\%$ for $\pi^0\pi^0\pi^0$) arises from isospin violation due to $m_u\neq m_d$. This is why the total width of the $\eta$, $1.3\ \mathrm{keV}$, is extremely narrow for a strong decay.
  • $\omega\to\pi^+\pi^-$: $G=-1\to(-1)^2=+1$, so the decay is forbidden (the branching ratio of $1.5\%$ comes from isospin violation through $\rho$–$\omega$ mixing). On the other hand, $\omega\to\pi^+\pi^-\pi^0$ conserves $G$ and is allowed; it is the main decay, with a branching ratio of $89\%$.

The key point is that there are decays that are allowed by parity but forbidden by $G$ parity. In terms of the connection to materials science, this has the same structure as a transition that is allowed by the point-group selection rule but further forbidden by the time-reversal or spin selection rule (a spin-forbidden transition): the selection rule is determined by the product of several symmetries.

Worked Example 6.3: Show in two ways why $\eta\to\pi^0\pi^0$ is forbidden

Solution. The $\eta$ has $J^{PC}=0^{-+}$, and the $\pi^0$ has $0^{-+}$.

(i) Prohibition by parity. The final state consists of two spin-$0$ particles, so $J=\ell$. Since the $\eta$ has $J=0$, $\ell=0$. The parity is $\eta_{\pi}^2(-1)^0=(+1)(+1)=+1$, but the parity of the $\eta$ is $-1$. They do not match.

(ii) Prohibition by Bose statistics (a different route). Because the two bosons are identical, $\ell$ is even. The case $\ell=0$ does not match the parity, as shown above, and $\ell\geq2$ is incompatible with $J=0$. Either way, the decay is impossible.

The experimental value is $\mathrm{BR}(\eta\to\pi^0\pi^0)<3.5\times10^{-4}$. Note that $\eta\to3\pi^0$ ($\mathrm{BR}=32.6\%$) has $(-1)^3=-1$ in terms of parity, which matches the $\eta$, so it is allowed. However, it violates isospin ($G$ parity) (the $\eta$ has $I=0$, whereas $3\pi^0$ contains an $I=1$ component), so it is suppressed as a strong-interaction process and occurs mainly through the mass difference of the $u,d$ quarks (the electromagnetic contribution is small by Sutherland’s theorem). There is an unexpected benefit: the quark mass ratio can be determined from this decay rate. ∎

6.4 $\mathcal{CP}$ and $\mathcal{CP}$ Violation

6.4.1 The $\mathcal{CP}$ rescue

After parity fell in 1957, physicists soon found a way out. The weak interaction violates $\mathcal{P}$, but it also violates $\mathcal{C}$ at the same time. Perhaps applying both in succession restores the original, they suggested.

Indeed, for neutrinos this idea works beautifully.

$$ \nu_L \ \xrightarrow{\ \mathcal{P}\ }\ \nu_R\ (\text{does not exist}) \qquad \nu_L \ \xrightarrow{\ \mathcal{C}\ }\ \bar\nu_L\ (\text{does not exist}) $$ $$ \nu_L \ \xrightarrow{\ \mathcal{CP}\ }\ \bar\nu_R\ (\text{exists!}) $$

Whether $\mathcal{P}$ alone or $\mathcal{C}$ alone is applied, the particle is sent to one that “does not exist,” yet applying $\mathcal{CP}$ lands on a real particle. The $\mathcal{CP}$ transform of a left-handed neutrino is a right-handed antineutrino, and this one does exist in nature. Seeing this, Landau declared that “$\mathcal{CP}$ is the true mirror.” For the seven years from 1957 to 1964, physicists were satisfied with this conclusion.

6.4.2 The neutral $K$ meson system — a precision interferometer prepared by nature

However, a single system destroyed this comfort: the neutral $K$ meson.

The quark content is $K^0=d\bar s$ (strangeness $S=+1$) and $\bar K^0=s\bar d$ ($S=-1$). Since the strong interaction conserves strangeness, these two are produced as different particles. The weak interaction, however, does not conserve strangeness, so transitions $K^0\leftrightarrow\bar K^0$ occur through a second-order process (a box diagram using two $W$ bosons; it has the same form as the diagram for $K^0\to\mu^+\mu^-$ in Chapter 12, Section 12.5.2). Therefore the eigenstates of time evolution are neither $K^0$ nor $\bar K^0$, but superpositions of them.

Derivation: The $\mathcal{CP}$ eigenstates $K_1,K_2$ and their decay modes

Step 1: Write down the action of $\mathcal{CP}$. The $K$ meson is a pseudoscalar ($J^P=0^-$), so $\hat{\mathcal{P}}\ket{K^0}=-\ket{K^0}$. Charge conjugation is $\hat{\mathcal{C}}\ket{K^0}=\ket{\bar K^0}$ (we adopt this as the convention for the choice of phase). Therefore

$$ \hat{\mathcal{CP}}\ket{K^0}=-\ket{\bar K^0},\qquad \hat{\mathcal{CP}}\ket{\bar K^0}=-\ket{K^0} $$

Step 2: Construct the eigenstates. It suffices to construct the eigenvectors of the $2\times2$ matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$.

$$ \begin{equation} \ket{K_1}=\frac{\ket{K^0}-\ket{\bar K^0}}{\sqrt2}\ \ (\mathcal{CP}=+1), \qquad \ket{K_2}=\frac{\ket{K^0}+\ket{\bar K^0}}{\sqrt2}\ \ (\mathcal{CP}=-1) \label{eq:6-K1K2} \end{equation} $$

Let us check. $\hat{\mathcal{CP}}\ket{K_1}=(-\ket{\bar K^0}+\ket{K^0})/\sqrt2=+\ket{K_1}$. $\hat{\mathcal{CP}}\ket{K_2}=(-\ket{\bar K^0}-\ket{K^0})/\sqrt2=-\ket{K_2}$. Good.

Step 3: Compute the $\mathcal{CP}$ of the final states. The $2\pi$ state ($\ell=0$) has

$$ P(2\pi)=(-1)^2(-1)^0=+1,\qquad C(\pi^+\pi^-)=(-1)^{\ell}=+1,\qquad C(\pi^0\pi^0)=+1 $$

so $\mathcal{CP}(2\pi)=+1$. The $3\pi$ state (if all the angular momenta are $\ell=0$) has

$$ P(3\pi)=(-1)^3=-1,\qquad C(3\pi^0)=+1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathcal{CP}(3\pi)=-1 $$

Step 4: Read off the selection rule. If $\mathcal{CP}$ is strictly conserved, then

$$ K_1\ (\mathcal{CP}=+1)\ \to\ 2\pi\ \ \text{only}, \qquad K_2\ (\mathcal{CP}=-1)\ \to\ 3\pi\ \ \text{only} $$

Step 5: Predict that the lifetimes differ greatly. Compared with $m_K=497.6\ \mathrm{MeV}$, the mass sum of $\pi^+\pi^-\pi^0$ is $m_{\pi^+}+m_{\pi^-}+m_{\pi^0}\approx414\ \mathrm{MeV}$, so only $Q\approx83\ \mathrm{MeV}$ of energy is available for the $3\pi$ decay (for $3\pi^0$, the mass sum is $405\ \mathrm{MeV}$ and $Q\approx93\ \mathrm{MeV}$). For the $2\pi$ decay, on the other hand, $Q=m_K-2m_\pi\approx218\ \mathrm{MeV}$. Since the phase spaces differ by orders of magnitude, $K_2$ should live far longer. The measured values are

$$ \tau_S = 8.954\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{s}\ (=89.5\ \mathrm{ps}), \qquad \tau_L = 5.116\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s}\ (=51.2\ \mathrm{ns}) $$

and the ratio is about $570$. This agrees with the prediction. The short-lived state is called $K_S$ (S = short), and the long-lived state is called $K_L$ (L = long). ∎

Physical Meaning: Just letting the beam run gives a pure $K_L$

This lifetime difference is an enormously powerful experimental tool. Neutral $K$ mesons produced by hitting a target with a proton beam are mainly $K^0$, because of associated production ($pN\to K^0\Lambda X$ and so on). However, since $\ket{K^0}=(\ket{K_1}+\ket{K_2})/\sqrt2$, they contain $K_S$ and $K_L$ in equal parts. But $K_S$ disappears with the lifetime $\tau_S$, so if the beam is allowed to travel a sufficient distance, only the $K_S$ component dies out, and a pure $K_L$ beam remains.

Let us look at numbers. For a $K$ with momentum $1\ \mathrm{GeV}$, the value of $\gamma$ is $E/m=\sqrt{1000^2+497.6^2}/497.6=2.245$. The decay length of $K_S$ in the laboratory frame is

$$ \lambda_S = \gamma\beta c\tau_S = 2.245\times0.8953\times(3.00\times10^8)\times8.954\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m} = 5.4\ \mathrm{cm} $$

The decay length of $K_L$, on the other hand, is $\lambda_L=\gamma\beta c\tau_L\approx31\ \mathrm{m}$. Therefore, after $17\ \mathrm{m}$ of travel the $K_S$ has dwindled to the quantity $\ee^{-17/0.054}$, which hardly needs evaluating, while most of the $K_L$ survives. Cronin and Fitch used this. A “pure $\mathcal{CP}=-1$ state” can be made by distance alone, without doing anything—an experimental apparatus prepared by nature.

6.4.3 The Cronin–Fitch experiment (1964)

James Cronin and Val Fitch carried out the following experiment at the Brookhaven AGS. The logic is remarkably simple.

Derivation: The logic of Cronin–Fitch

Step 1: Make a pure $K_L$ beam. Place the detector at a position $57\ \mathrm{ft}$ ($17.4\ \mathrm{m}$) from the target. The $K_S$ has disappeared completely.

Step 2: Bring the decay region close to a vacuum. Let the decays occur inside a helium bag ($1.7\ \mathrm{m}$). If the beam interacts with matter in air, the $\bar K^0$ component is preferentially absorbed and $K_S$ is regenerated (regeneration), so this must be avoided. This care determined the quality of the experiment.

Step 3: Search for $\pi^+\pi^-$ with two arms. Two magnetic spectrometers measure the momenta of the two charged particles; assuming they are $\pi$, one reconstructs the invariant mass $m_{\pi\pi}$ and the angle $\theta$ between $\bm{p}_{\pi\pi}$ and the beam axis.

Step 4: How to recognize the signal. If $K_L\to\pi^+\pi^-$ (a two-body decay) is occurring, events concentrate at $m_{\pi\pi}=m_K$ and $\theta=0$ (coinciding with the direction of the parent’s momentum). On the other hand, in the three-body background decays $K_L\to\pi^+\pi^-\pi^0$ and $K_L\to\pi^\pm\mu^\mp\nu$, the one unseen particle carries away momentum, so $\theta$ spreads out and $m_{\pi\pi}$ also becomes smaller than $m_K$.

Step 5: Result. Plotting the $\cos\theta$ distribution for events with $m_{\pi\pi}$ near $m_K$, a sharp peak stood up at $\cos\theta=1$. The number of events was $45\pm9$ out of $22\,700$ $K_L$ decays. That is,

$$ \begin{equation} \mathrm{BR}(K_L\to\pi^+\pi^-) = (2.0\pm0.4)\times10^{-3} \label{eq:6-cronin-fitch} \end{equation} $$

(The current precise value is $1.967\times10^{-3}$.) A $K_L$ with $\mathcal{CP}=-1$ decayed into a $2\pi$ state with $\mathcal{CP}=+1$. $\mathcal{CP}$ is not conserved. ∎

6.4.4 The magnitude of $\mathcal{CP}$ violation

The physical $K_L$ and $K_S$ deviate slightly from the $\mathcal{CP}$ eigenstates. Introducing a small parameter $\varepsilon$, we write

$$ \begin{equation} \ket{K_S}=\frac{\ket{K_1}+\varepsilon\ket{K_2}}{\sqrt{1+\abs{\varepsilon}^2}}, \qquad \ket{K_L}=\frac{\ket{K_2}+\varepsilon\ket{K_1}}{\sqrt{1+\abs{\varepsilon}^2}} \label{eq:6-KL-KS} \end{equation} $$

The decay of $K_L$ into $2\pi$ is due to the admixed $\varepsilon\ket{K_1}$ component. The observables are the amplitude ratios

$$ \eta_{+-}\equiv\frac{A(K_L\to\pi^+\pi^-)}{A(K_S\to\pi^+\pi^-)}, \qquad \eta_{00}\equiv\frac{A(K_L\to\pi^0\pi^0)}{A(K_S\to\pi^0\pi^0)} $$

and the measured value is $\abs{\eta_{+-}}=2.232\times10^{-3}$ (phase $\phi_{+-}=43.5^\circ$). That is, $\mathcal{CP}$ violation is a very small effect, $0.2\%$ in amplitude. This contrasts with the maximal violation of $\mathcal{P}$, and “why it is violated only partially” is still a fundamental question today.

This completely solves the alien problem of Section 6.2.3. “Make $K_L$ and measure the semileptonic decays. The charged lepton that appears more often is the positron of your world.” The proviso “if you are made of matter” is no longer needed. Nature has a yardstick that distinguishes matter from antimatter in an absolute way.

This asymmetry also carries weight in cosmology. Sakharov (1967) listed three conditions for generating the matter–antimatter asymmetry of the universe: (i) baryon number nonconservation, (ii) violation of $\mathcal{C}$ and $\mathcal{CP}$, and (iii) departure from thermal equilibrium. Since $\mathcal{CP}$ violation really exists, (ii) is satisfied. However, the magnitude of $\mathcal{CP}$ violation in the Standard Model falls short, by about $10$ orders of magnitude, of explaining the observed baryon-to-photon ratio $\eta_B\approx6\times10^{-10}$. This is one of the most certain pieces of evidence that the Standard Model is incomplete (Chapter 15).

6.4.5 The $B$ meson and the CKM phase

Whether $\mathcal{CP}$ violation is a special circumstance of the $K$ meson alone, or a universal property built into the structure of the theory, remained unknown for a long time. The answer was given by the 1973 paper of Makoto Kobayashi and Toshihide Maskawa.

Theorem 6.4 (Kobayashi–Maskawa)

When there are $n$ generations of quarks, the number of physical phases contained in the flavor mixing matrix (the CKM matrix) is

$$ N_{\text{phase}}=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2} $$

For $n=2$ this is $0$; that is, there is no way to have a $\mathcal{CP}$-violating phase. To explain $\mathcal{CP}$ violation within the weak interaction, at least three generations of quarks are necessary.

In 1973, only three quarks, $u,d,s$, had been confirmed experimentally, and the second-generation $c$ had been predicted theoretically by the GIM mechanism (1970; Chapter 12, Section 12.5.2). Kobayashi and Maskawa showed that even the four-quark model including that $c$ admits no $\mathcal{CP}$-violating phase, and thus, from the experimental fact of $\mathcal{CP}$ violation alone, they demanded the existence of the third generation, $b,t$. The $c$ was discovered in 1974, the $b$ in 1977, and the $t$ in 1995. It was the Nobel Prize of 2008.

The CKM matrix for three generations can be written with three real mixing angles and one complex phase $\delta$. The magnitude of $\mathcal{CP}$ violation is measured by the Jarlskog invariant, an invariant that does not depend on the choice of phases,

$$ \begin{equation} J = \mathrm{Im}\left(V_{us}V_{cb}V_{ub}^{*}V_{cs}^{*}\right) = (3.08\pm0.15)\times10^{-5} \label{eq:6-jarlskog} \end{equation} $$

As long as the masses of quarks of the same type are not degenerate, $J\neq0$ is a necessary and sufficient condition for $\mathcal{CP}$ violation (to be precise, the condition is that both $J$ and the product of mass-squared differences $\prod(m_i^2-m_j^2)$ are nonzero), and the measured value is clearly away from zero.

In the $B$ meson system, $\mathcal{CP}$ violation appears far larger than in the $K$ system. The $B$ factories (the Belle experiment at KEKB and the BaBar experiment at SLAC) measured

$$ \sin2\beta = 0.699\pm0.017 $$

from the particle–antiparticle asymmetry in the time-dependent decay rate of $B^0\to J/\psi\,K_S$ (this is the PDG 2022 average; this book uses this value to match the numbers in Chapter 12 and Appendix E. The subsequent HFLAV average has been updated to around $0.708\pm0.011$). This is an $O(1)$ asymmetry. In this way the Kobayashi–Maskawa framework was established as correct, which led to the 2008 prize.

Connection to Materials Science: The $K^0$–$\bar K^0$ system is a textbook non-Hermitian two-level system

The time evolution of the neutral $K$ does not conserve probability, because of decay. Hence, in the Weisskopf–Wigner approximation, it is described by a $2\times2$ non-Hermitian effective Hamiltonian

$$ \mathcal{H}_{\text{eff}} = M - \frac{\ii}{2}\Gamma, \qquad \ii\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{pmatrix}a_{K^0}\\ a_{\bar K^0}\end{pmatrix} =\left(M-\frac{\ii}{2}\Gamma\right)\begin{pmatrix}a_{K^0}\\ a_{\bar K^0}\end{pmatrix} $$

Both $M$ (the mass matrix) and $\Gamma$ (the decay matrix) are Hermitian, but their combination is non-Hermitian. The difference of the real parts of the eigenvalues gives $\Delta m$, and the difference of the imaginary parts gives the lifetime difference $\Delta\Gamma$. If $\mathcal{CPT}$ holds, $M_{11}=M_{22}$ and $\Gamma_{11}=\Gamma_{22}$ are required, and this is the foundation of precision tests of $\mathcal{CPT}$.

This mathematics is reused almost verbatim in materials science.

  • Dissipative two-level systems: exciton polaritons, cavity QED, and the de-excitation of excitons in quantum dots. The eigenvalues become complex; the real part gives the “level repulsion” and the imaginary part gives the “linewidth.”
  • Exceptional point (EP): a non-Hermitian matrix has points at which, as a parameter is varied, two eigenvalues and eigenvectors become degenerate at the same time. This never happens for a Hermitian matrix. EPs have been realized in optical resonators, acoustic metamaterials, and electric circuits, and sensors have been proposed that exploit the $\sqrt{\ }$-type enhancement of sensitivity near an EP.
  • Flavor oscillation and quantum beats: the oscillation of the $\bar K^0$ component as $\cos(\Delta m\,t)$ in a $K^0$ beam is an interference produced by the phase difference between two eigenstates, and it is exactly the same mathematics as quantum-beat spectroscopy in solids and the Larmor precession of a spin. The “frequency” $\Delta m=5.29\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ can be read off directly in a time-resolved measurement.

When one studies non-Hermitian physics, the neutral $K$ meson system is the oldest and most precise example. It connects to the discussion of $\mathcal{PT}$ symmetry in Section 6.6(d).

6.5 Time Reversal $\mathcal{T}$ and the $\mathcal{CPT}$ Theorem

6.5.1 Definition of time reversal

Definition: Time reversal $\mathcal{T}$

Time reversal is the operation that reverses the direction of time:

$$ \mathcal{T}:\quad t\ \longmapsto\ -t,\qquad \bm{x}\ \longmapsto\ \bm{x} $$

As a result, velocity, momentum, angular momentum, and spin all change direction:

$$ \bm{v}\to-\bm{v},\quad \bm{p}\to-\bm{p},\quad \bm{L}\to-\bm{L},\quad \bm{S}\to-\bm{S} $$

The name “time reversal” is easily misleading. It does not mean “rewinding time”; it is an operation that asks “whether the motion played backward is also a solution obeying the same laws.” More precisely, it should be called motion reversal. Newton’s equation of motion $m\ddot{\bm{x}}=\bm{F}(\bm{x})$ contains the second derivative in $t$ and is therefore invariant under $t\to-t$, so classical mechanics is $\mathcal{T}$-symmetric. On the other hand, the equation with friction, $m\ddot{\bm{x}}=-\gamma\dot{\bm{x}}$, contains a first derivative and therefore violates $\mathcal{T}$. Note that the irreversibility expressed by the second law of thermodynamics is not in contradiction with microscopic $\mathcal{T}$ symmetry (it is a matter of statistical initial conditions).

6.5.2 Why must $\mathcal{T}$ be antiunitary?

This is the most important point of this section, and one on which beginners always stumble. The operators $\hat{\mathcal{P}}$ and $\hat{\mathcal{C}}$ were unitary operators, but $\hat{\mathcal{T}}$ must be antiunitary. We show the reason in two ways.

Derivation 1: Required by the canonical commutation relation

Step 1: Write down the requirement. Time reversal leaves the position unchanged and reverses the momentum:

$$ \hat{\mathcal{T}}\,\hat{x}\,\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}=\hat{x}, \qquad \hat{\mathcal{T}}\,\hat{p}\,\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}=-\hat{p} $$

Step 2: Act on the commutation relation. Sandwich both sides of the canonical commutation relation $[\hat x,\hat p]=\ii\hbar$ between $\hat{\mathcal{T}}\cdots\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}$. The left-hand side is

$$ \hat{\mathcal{T}}[\hat x,\hat p]\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1} =[\hat{\mathcal{T}}\hat x\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1},\ \hat{\mathcal{T}}\hat p\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}] =[\hat x,-\hat p]=-\ii\hbar $$

Step 3: Evaluate the right-hand side. If $\hat{\mathcal{T}}$ is linear, the number $\ii\hbar$ passes straight through, and

$$ \hat{\mathcal{T}}(\ii\hbar)\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}=\ii\hbar $$

Step 4: See the contradiction. The left-hand side $=-\ii\hbar$ and the right-hand side $=+\ii\hbar$. This cannot hold.

Step 5: The way out. Suppose $\hat{\mathcal{T}}$ is antilinear; that is, for any complex number $c$,

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{\mathcal{T}}\,(c\ket{\psi}) = c^{*}\,\hat{\mathcal{T}}\ket{\psi} \label{eq:6-antilinear} \end{equation} $$

Then $\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\ii\hbar)\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}=-\ii\hbar$, which agrees with the left-hand side. The contradiction disappears. ∎

Derivation 2: So that the energy spectrum is bounded below

Step 1: Write down the time evolution. The evolution of a state is $\ket{\psi(t)}=\ee^{-\ii\Ham t/\hbar}\ket{\psi(0)}$.

Step 2: The requirement of time reversal. We want “evolving to time $t$ and then reversing time” to be the same as “reversing time and then evolving for $-t$”:

$$ \hat{\mathcal{T}}\,\ee^{-\ii\Ham t/\hbar} = \ee^{+\ii\Ham t/\hbar}\,\hat{\mathcal{T}} $$

Step 3: Assume that $\hat{\mathcal{T}}$ is linear. Expanding the exponentials and comparing the terms of first order in $t$ gives $\hat{\mathcal{T}}(-\ii\Ham)=(+\ii\Ham)\hat{\mathcal{T}}$. If the operator is linear, $\ii$ passes through, and

$$ \hat{\mathcal{T}}\Ham\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}=-\Ham $$

Step 4: Disaster strikes. This means that for an eigenstate $\ket{E}$ of energy $E$, $\hat{\mathcal{T}}\ket{E}$ is an eigenstate of energy $-E$. In other words, the spectrum becomes symmetric under $E\to-E$. But the kinetic energy $\bm{p}^2/2m$ can become arbitrarily large (the spectrum is unbounded above), so by the symmetry the spectrum would also be unbounded below, and no ground state exists. Matter would decay without limit, and neither stable atoms nor crystals could exist.

Step 5: If it is antilinear, we are saved. If $\hat{\mathcal{T}}$ is antilinear, then $\hat{\mathcal{T}}(-\ii\Ham)=(-\ii)^{*}\hat{\mathcal{T}}\Ham=+\ii\hat{\mathcal{T}}\Ham$, and the relation of Step 3 changes to

$$ \hat{\mathcal{T}}\Ham\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}=+\Ham $$

The energy spectrum remains bounded below, and time reversal gives a state of the same energy. This is what later gives rise to Kramers degeneracy. ∎

Math Note: Properties of antiunitary operators and $\mathcal{T}^2=\pm1$

An antiunitary operator is an operator that is antilinear and preserves the norm. For the inner product,

$$ \braket{\hat{\mathcal{T}}\phi\,\vert\,\hat{\mathcal{T}}\psi} = \braket{\phi\,\vert\,\psi}^{*} = \braket{\psi\,\vert\,\phi} $$

holds (the difference from a unitary operator is that a complex conjugate appears). Any antiunitary operator can be written as $\hat{\mathcal{T}}=UK$ using some unitary $U$ and the complex-conjugation operator $K$ ($K\,c\,K^{-1}=c^*$, $K^2=1$).

For a spinless particle, $\hat{\mathcal{T}}=K$, which simply complex-conjugates the wave function: $\psi(\bm{x})\to\psi^{*}(\bm{x})$. This shows that the sign of $\bm{p}=-\ii\hbar\nabla$ changes. $\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=K^2=+1$.

For spin $1/2$, an additional rotation is needed to realize $\bm{S}\to-\bm{S}$. Since $\sigma_x,\sigma_z$ are real matrices, they are invariant under $K$, but $\sigma_y$ is purely imaginary and changes sign under $K$. To reverse all three components, it suffices to multiply by a $\pi$ rotation generated by $\sigma_y$:

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{\mathcal{T}} = -\ii\sigma_y K = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\ 1&0\end{pmatrix}K \label{eq:6-T-spinhalf} \end{equation} $$

We compute the square. Passing $K$ through to the left of the right-hand factor complex-conjugates the coefficients, so

$$ \hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=(-\ii\sigma_y K)(-\ii\sigma_y K) =(-\ii\sigma_y)(+\ii\sigma_y^{*})K^2 =(-\ii)(+\ii)\,\sigma_y(-\sigma_y) =-\sigma_y^2=-\mathbb{1} $$

(We used $\sigma_y^{*}=-\sigma_y$.) That is,

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{\mathcal{T}}^2 = (-1)^{2S} =\begin{cases} +1 & (\text{integer spin})\\ -1 & (\text{half-integer spin}) \end{cases} \label{eq:6-T-squared} \end{equation} $$

$\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=-1$ is the strange property that “reversing time twice changes the sign,” but it arises for the same reason that a spinor changes sign under a $2\pi$ rotation. And this $-1$ is precisely the reason for the existence of the Kramers degeneracy in the next subsection and of the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ topological insulator in Section 6.6.

6.5.3 Kramers degeneracy

Theorem 6.5 (Kramers, 1930)

If a system with half-integer spin (a system consisting of an odd number of electrons) is time-reversal symmetric ($[\hat{\mathcal{T}},\Ham]=0$), then every energy level is at least twofold degenerate.

Proof. Suppose $\Ham\ket{\psi}=E\ket{\psi}$.

(i) $\hat{\mathcal{T}}\ket{\psi}$ is also an eigenstate of the same energy. Since $[\hat{\mathcal{T}},\Ham]=0$,

$$ \Ham\,(\hat{\mathcal{T}}\ket{\psi}) = \hat{\mathcal{T}}\Ham\ket{\psi} = \hat{\mathcal{T}}(E\ket{\psi}) = E\,\hat{\mathcal{T}}\ket{\psi} $$

(Since $E$ is real, taking the complex conjugate through antilinearity changes nothing.)

(ii) $\hat{\mathcal{T}}\ket{\psi}$ is orthogonal to $\ket{\psi}$. In the inner-product rule for antiunitary operators

$$ \braket{\hat{\mathcal{T}}\phi\,\vert\,\hat{\mathcal{T}}\chi} = \braket{\chi\,\vert\,\phi} $$

choose $\phi=\psi$ and $\chi=\hat{\mathcal{T}}\psi$. Using $\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=-1$ (half-integer spin), the left-hand side is

$$ \braket{\hat{\mathcal{T}}\psi\,\vert\,\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}}\psi)} =\braket{\hat{\mathcal{T}}\psi\,\vert\,\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2\psi} =\braket{\hat{\mathcal{T}}\psi\,\vert\,-\psi} =-\braket{\hat{\mathcal{T}}\psi\,\vert\,\psi} $$

The right-hand side is simply $\braket{\chi\vert\phi}=\braket{\hat{\mathcal{T}}\psi\,\vert\,\psi}$. Equating the two gives

$$ -\braket{\hat{\mathcal{T}}\psi\,\vert\,\psi} = \braket{\hat{\mathcal{T}}\psi\,\vert\,\psi} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \braket{\hat{\mathcal{T}}\psi\,\vert\,\psi}=0 $$

The use of $\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=-1$ here is decisive. For integer spin ($\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=+1$), the sign of the left-hand side is not reversed and no conclusion follows.

(iii) Conclusion. $\ket{\psi}$ and $\hat{\mathcal{T}}\ket{\psi}$ are two mutually orthogonal states with the same energy. Therefore the level is at least twofold degenerate. ∎

For integer spin, $\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=+1$, so (ii) does not hold, and $\hat{\mathcal{T}}\ket{\psi}$ may turn out to be proportional to $\ket{\psi}$. Kramers degeneracy is a phenomenon specific to half-integer spin.

6.5.4 The $\mathcal{CPT}$ theorem

$\mathcal{P}$ is violated. $\mathcal{C}$ is violated. $\mathcal{CP}$ is also violated (though only slightly). What about $\mathcal{T}$? And what about the product of all three?

Theorem 6.6 ($\mathcal{CPT}$ theorem; Lüders, Pauli, Bell, Jost, 1954–1957)

Any quantum field theory satisfying the following three conditions is necessarily invariant under $\Theta\equiv\mathcal{CPT}$:

  1. Lorentz invariance (being a relativistic field theory)
  2. Locality (interactions occur at a single point of spacetime, and fields at spacelike separation commute (anticommute for fermion fields))
  3. Hermiticity and spectral positivity (the Hamiltonian is Hermitian, and the energy is bounded below)

Since these are the very skeleton of field theory, a violation of $\mathcal{CPT}$ would mean not merely something “outside the Standard Model” but the breakdown of “the framework of field theory itself.”

Why?: Why $\mathcal{CPT}$ is special — the connection with the Lorentz group

The intuitive understanding is as follows. Among Lorentz transformations, the part connected continuously to the identity (the proper orthochronous Lorentz transformations $L_+^\uparrow$) consists of those with determinant $+1$ that preserve the direction of time. $\mathcal{P}$ has determinant $-1$ and $\mathcal{T}$ reverses the direction of time, so neither belongs to $L_+^\uparrow$ by itself. However, $\mathcal{PT}=-\mathbb{1}_4$ has determinant $+1$, and in the complexified Lorentz group it is connected continuously to the identity through a rotation by an imaginary angle (within the real Lorentz group it is not connected, because it reverses the direction of time). Concretely, performing a $\pi$ rotation in two planes in $4$-dimensional Euclidean space and analytically continuing to Minkowski space gives $\mathcal{PT}$.

In field theory, by the relation between spin and statistics (the spin–statistics theorem), this analytic continuation is automatically accompanied by $\mathcal{C}$. As a result, $\mathcal{CPT}$ is built into the theory as “part of a continuous symmetry.” This is why the separate conservation of $\mathcal{P}$, $\mathcal{C}$, and $\mathcal{T}$ is merely an accident, whereas $\mathcal{CPT}$ alone is guaranteed as a theorem.

The $\mathcal{CPT}$ theorem has strong consequences that can be tested directly by experiment.

Consequences of $\mathcal{CPT}$

  1. The masses of a particle and its antiparticle are exactly equal: $m_a = m_{\bar a}$
  2. The total decay widths (lifetimes) are also exactly equal: $\Gamma_a=\Gamma_{\bar a}$, $\tau_a=\tau_{\bar a}$ (the individual branching ratios are not necessarily equal)
  3. The electric charges have opposite signs and equal magnitudes: $q_a=-q_{\bar a}$
  4. The magnetic moments have opposite signs and equal magnitudes: $\mu_a=-\mu_{\bar a}$
  5. If $\mathcal{CP}$ is violated, $\mathcal{T}$ is violated by the same amount

6.5.5 Searching for $\mathcal{T}$ violation — electric dipole moments (EDM)

Since $\mathcal{CP}$ is violated, the $\mathcal{CPT}$ theorem implies that $\mathcal{T}$ must also be violated. Is there a way to see $\mathcal{T}$ violation directly? The most powerful is the search for the electric dipole moment (EDM) of elementary particles.

Derivation: Why the EDM violates both $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$

Step 1: The direction of the EDM is fixed. For a spin-$1/2$ particle, there is no vector specifying a direction other than the spin $\bm{S}$. Therefore, if a permanent electric dipole moment $\bm{d}$ exists, it must be parallel to the spin:

$$ \bm{d} = d\,\frac{\bm{S}}{\abs{\bm{S}}} $$

This is a consequence of the Wigner–Eckart theorem (Chapter 5).

Step 2: Write down the interaction energy. The energy in an electric field $\bm{E}$, written alongside the magnetic-moment case, is

$$ H = -\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{B}-\bm{d}\cdot\bm{E} = -\mu\,\frac{\bm{S}\cdot\bm{B}}{\abs{\bm{S}}}-d\,\frac{\bm{S}\cdot\bm{E}}{\abs{\bm{S}}} $$

Step 3: Examine it under $\mathcal{P}$. Since $\bm{S}$ is axial (invariant) and $\bm{E}$ is polar (reversed),

$$ \bm{S}\cdot\bm{E}\ \xrightarrow{\ \mathcal{P}\ }\ -\bm{S}\cdot\bm{E} $$

That is, the EDM term is $\mathcal{P}$-odd. For comparison, the magnetic-moment term is $\bm{S}\cdot\bm{B}$ (axial $\times$ axial), which is $\mathcal{P}$-even and poses no problem.

Step 4: Examine it under $\mathcal{T}$. Since $\bm{S}\to-\bm{S}$ and $\bm{E}\to+\bm{E}$ (the electric field is produced by the charge distribution, and electric charge does not change under time reversal),

$$ \bm{S}\cdot\bm{E}\ \xrightarrow{\ \mathcal{T}\ }\ -\bm{S}\cdot\bm{E} $$

The EDM term is also $\mathcal{T}$-odd. By contrast, $\bm{S}\cdot\bm{B}$ is invariant, because the sign changes twice, through $\bm{S}\to-\bm{S}$ and $\bm{B}\to-\bm{B}$.

Step 5: Conclusion.

$$ \begin{equation} \bm{d}\neq0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathcal{P}\ \text{and}\ \mathcal{T}\ \text{are both violated} \label{eq:6-edm} \end{equation} $$

The converse does not hold as it stands. If both $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are violated, there is no longer any necessity for $\bm{d}$ to vanish, but its magnitude is determined by the dynamics (although the Standard Model violates both, the predicted $d_n$ is extremely small, of order $10^{-31}\ e\,\mathrm{cm}$, as in Table 6.6). Moreover, if $\mathcal{CPT}$ holds, a violation of $\mathcal{T}$ is equivalent to a violation of $\mathcal{CP}$. Therefore EDM measurements are the most sensitive experiments in the search for new sources of $\mathcal{CP}$ violation. ∎

Physical Meaning: How small the EDM scale is

Let us get a feel for the neutron EDM upper limit of $1.8\times10^{-26}\ e\,\mathrm{cm}$. This corresponds to “the charges $e$ and $-e$ being displaced by only $1.8\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{cm}=1.8\times10^{-28}\ \mathrm{m}$.” Since the radius of the neutron is $\sim0.8\ \mathrm{fm}=8\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{m}$, the relative displacement is

$$ \frac{1.8\times10^{-28}}{8\times10^{-16}} = 2.3\times10^{-13} $$

Even if the neutron were enlarged to the size of the Earth (radius $6.4\times10^6\ \mathrm{m}$), the displacement between the positive and negative charges would be only $1.4\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m}$, that is, about one micrometer. It has been confirmed with this precision that there is “no deviation from spherical symmetry.”

This upper limit imposes an extraordinarily tight constraint, $\bar\theta < 10^{-10}$, on the coefficient of the QCD $\theta$ term (Chapter 15, Section 15.5.5). Why $\bar\theta$ is $10^{-10}$ or smaller rather than $O(1)$ is called the strong CP problem, and it remains unsolved. The axion is a particle proposed as a solution to this problem, and it is also a leading candidate for dark matter (Chapter 15, Section 15.5.5).

At the same time, this means that the EDM is $\mathcal{CP}$-odd (an indicator of $\mathcal{CP}$ violation).

Derivation: Vanishing of the piezoelectric tensor in centrosymmetric crystals

Step 1: Write the constitutive equation. The direct piezoelectric effect is the phenomenon in which a stress $\sigma_{jk}$ generates a polarization $P_i$:

$$ P_i = d_{ijk}\,\sigma_{jk} $$

Step 2: Examine the $\mathcal{P}$ transformation property of each quantity. The polarization $\bm{P}$ is a polar vector ($\mathcal{P}$-odd). The stress tensor $\sigma_{jk}$ is the “force per area element,” and since it is the tensor product of the force $\bm{F}$ (polar) and the surface normal $\bm{n}$ (polar),

$$ \sigma_{jk}\ \xrightarrow{\ \mathcal{P}\ }\ (-1)(-1)\sigma_{jk}=+\sigma_{jk}\qquad(\mathcal{P}\text{-even}) $$

Step 3: Impose inversion symmetry. If the crystal has an inversion center, $d_{ijk}$ must be invariant under the transformation. Then

$$ -P_i = d_{ijk}(+\sigma_{jk}) = +P_i $$

Step 4: Conclusion. $P_i=0$, that is, $d_{ijk}=0$.

$$ \begin{equation} \text{with an inversion center}\ \Longrightarrow\ d_{ijk}=0\quad(\text{no piezoelectricity}) \label{eq:6-piezo} \end{equation} $$

This is the most basic constraint in materials design: “a piezoelectric material must necessarily be noncentrosymmetric.” $\mathrm{PbZr}_x\mathrm{Ti}_{1-x}\mathrm{O_3}$ (PZT) is an excellent piezoelectric because it adopts a polar point group such as $4mm$ or $3m$. On the other hand, once it enters the inversion-symmetric phase (cubic, $m\bar3m$), the piezoelectricity disappears completely. The fact that a piezoelectric element stops working above the Curie point is a consequence of this symmetry requirement. ∎

6.6.2 (b) Time-reversal symmetry — Kramers degeneracy and magnetic point groups

The Hamiltonian of an electron system commutes with $\hat{\mathcal{T}}$ if there is neither a magnetic field nor magnetic order. An electron has spin $1/2$ and $\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=-1$, so by Theorem 6.5 all levels are twofold degenerate. Let us write down the condition this imposes on the electronic structure in a crystal.

Connection to Materials Science: Kramers degeneracy in the band structure

Acting with $\hat{\mathcal{T}}$ on a Bloch state $\ket{n\bm{k}\sigma}$ gives $\bm{k}\to-\bm{k}$ and $\sigma\to-\sigma$ ($\bm{k}$ is a momentum and hence $\mathcal{T}$-odd; the spin is also $\mathcal{T}$-odd). Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \varepsilon_n(\bm{k},\uparrow) = \varepsilon_n(-\bm{k},\downarrow) \qquad(\text{time-reversal symmetry}) \label{eq:6-TRS-band} \end{equation} $$

On the other hand, if the crystal is also inversion symmetric, then $\varepsilon_n(\bm{k},\sigma)=\varepsilon_n(-\bm{k},\sigma)$. Combining the two gives

$$ \varepsilon_n(\bm{k},\uparrow)=\varepsilon_n(\bm{k},\downarrow) $$

that is, spin degeneracy at every $\bm{k}$. This is the reason why, when one plots the bands of a nonmagnetic, centrosymmetric crystal in a DFT calculation, the bands remain twofold degenerate even when the spin–orbit interaction is included.

Conversely, if inversion symmetry is broken (with $\mathcal{T}$ kept), then $\varepsilon_n(\bm{k},\uparrow)\neq\varepsilon_n(\bm{k},\downarrow)$ and spin splitting appears. This is realized by

  • The Rashba effect: the case in which structural inversion symmetry is broken at an interface or surface. $H_R=\alpha_R(\bm{\sigma}\times\bm{k})\cdot\hat{\bm{z}}$. Since $\bm{\sigma}$ is $\mathcal{P}$-even and $\mathcal{T}$-odd, and $\bm{k}$ is $\mathcal{P}$-odd and $\mathcal{T}$-odd, the cross product $\bm{\sigma}\times\bm{k}$ is $\mathcal{P}$-odd and $\mathcal{T}$-even. A term in the Hamiltonian must be $\mathcal{P}$-even and $\mathcal{T}$-even, so for this term to be allowed, a $\mathcal{P}$-odd structural vector, namely the interface normal $\hat{\bm z}$ (a polar vector), must be nonzero. It requires only the breaking of $\mathcal{P}$ (breaking of structural inversion symmetry), and $\mathcal{T}$ may remain intact—this is how to read Table 6.7. Values of $\alpha_R\sim3$–$4\ \mathrm{eV\,\mathring{A}}$ have been reported for the $\mathrm{Bi/Ag}(111)$ surface alloy and for $\mathrm{BiTeI}$.
  • The Dresselhaus effect: the case in which the bulk crystal structure itself is noncentrosymmetric (zincblende $\mathrm{GaAs}$, $\bar43m$). $H_D\propto\sigma_x k_x(k_y^2-k_z^2)+\text{cyclic permutations}$. That it is cubic in $\bm{k}$ also follows from the same symmetry counting.

However, at special points in $\bm{k}$ space—the time-reversal invariant momenta (TRIM) $\bm{\Gamma}_i$, that is, the points satisfying $-\bm{\Gamma}_i=\bm{\Gamma}_i+\bm{G}$ ($\Gamma$, $X$, $M$, $R$, etc.; eight in three dimensions)—Eq. \eqref{eq:6-TRS-band} connects states at the same $\bm{k}$, so $\mathcal{T}$ alone guarantees the degeneracy. The Rashba splitting necessarily closes at the TRIM. This fact is the foundation of the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ classification in (f).

When magnetic order enters, $\mathcal{T}$ is broken. The magnetization $\bm{M}$ is $\mathcal{T}$-odd (Table 6.7), so in a ferromagnet $\hat{\mathcal{T}}$ by itself is no longer a symmetry operation. To describe the symmetry of the crystal, the ordinary 230 space groups are therefore not enough, and an extension that includes $\mathcal{T}$ is required.

Definition: Magnetic point groups and magnetic space groups

A group that allows, in addition to the ordinary point-group operations $g$, the compound operations “perform $g$ and then time reversal $\mathcal{T}$,” $g'\equiv g\,\mathcal{T}$ (primed, antiunitary operations), is called a magnetic point group (Shubnikov group). There are 122 types in all (of which 32 are the ordinary point groups, 32 are the gray groups that contain $\mathcal{T}$ by itself, and 58 are the genuine magnetic point groups). Extended to space groups, there are 1651 types (Shubnikov groups).

Example of the notation: the antiferromagnetic phase of $\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}$ is $\bar3'm'$. A prime indicates that the operation is not a symmetry by itself but becomes one when combined with $\mathcal{T}$. $\bar3'$ means that “inversion $\times$ time reversal” is a symmetry operation—that is, both $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are broken individually, but the product $\mathcal{PT}$ is preserved.

Connection to Materials Science: What appears when $\mathcal{T}$ is broken

A $\mathcal{T}$-odd physical quantity must be zero as long as $\mathcal{T}$ is conserved (the $\mathcal{T}$ version of Theorem 6.1). Therefore all of the following properties are “indicators of $\mathcal{T}$ breaking.”

  • Spontaneous magnetization $\bm{M}$ (ferromagnetism, ferrimagnetism)
  • Anomalous Hall conductivity $\sigma_{xy}^{A}$: the antisymmetric component of $\sigma_{xy}$ is $\mathcal{T}$-odd. It appears even without an external magnetic field if $\mathcal{T}$ is broken. The fact that $\mathrm{Mn_3Sn}$ (a noncollinear antiferromagnet with nearly zero net magnetization) shows a large anomalous Hall effect at room temperature because its magnetic point group breaks $\mathcal{T}$; it is not directly related to the magnitude of the magnetization—this is a phenomenon that can be explained only by symmetry.
  • Faraday rotation and the magneto-optical Kerr effect: the antisymmetric off-diagonal component of the dielectric tensor, $\varepsilon_{xy}=-\varepsilon_{yx}$, is $\mathcal{T}$-odd (in both its real and imaginary parts). Its real part corresponds to the Faraday rotation angle, and its imaginary part to magnetic circular dichroism.
  • Lifting of Kramers degeneracy: in the presence of magnetic order, $\hat{\mathcal{T}}$ is no longer a symmetry, so the spin degeneracy of the bands is in general lifted.

Conversely, the question “does this material show an anomalous Hall effect?” can be answered just by examining the magnetic point group (Neumann’s principle). It can be decided by symmetry before any calculation. This is why magnetic point groups are used in practice.

6.6.3 (c) Onsager reciprocal relations and microscopic reversibility

The condition that time-reversal symmetry imposes on transport phenomena is the Onsager reciprocal relations. In the framework of statistical physics it is called “microscopic reversibility,” but its content is nothing other than $\mathcal{T}$ symmetry itself.

Derivation: The Onsager–Casimir reciprocal relations

Step 1: The framework of linear response. Suppose that thermodynamic flows $J_i$ and forces $X_j$ are linearly related (“Introduction to Linear Response Theory,” Chapters 1 and 7):

$$ J_i = L_{ij}\,X_j $$

Step 2: Write down the fluctuation correlation function. According to the Kubo formula, the coefficient $L_{ij}$ is given by the time correlation function of equilibrium fluctuations:

$$ L_{ij}\ \propto\ \int_0^\infty\dd t\ \braket{\dot{a}_i(t)\,\dot{a}_j(0)} $$

Here $a_i$ is a fluctuating extensive variable.

Step 3: Use $\mathcal{T}$ symmetry. Since the equilibrium state is stationary, $\braket{A(t)B(0)}=\braket{A(0)B(-t)}$. Furthermore, if the microscopic dynamics is time-reversal symmetric, then using the $\mathcal{T}$ parity $\epsilon_i=\pm1$ of each variable (positions are $+$; momentum, spin, and magnetization are $-$), we have

$$ \braket{a_i(t)a_j(0)} = \epsilon_i\epsilon_j\braket{a_i(-t)a_j(0)} $$

Step 4: State the conclusion. Inserting this into the integral gives the symmetry of the coefficient matrix:

$$ \begin{equation} L_{ij}(\bm{B},\bm{M}) = \epsilon_i\epsilon_j\,L_{ji}(-\bm{B},-\bm{M}) \label{eq:6-onsager} \end{equation} $$

Because the magnetic field $\bm{B}$ and the magnetization $\bm{M}$ are $\mathcal{T}$-odd, when writing the reciprocal relation their signs must also be reversed together (Casimir’s extension). ∎

Connection to Materials Science: Four experimental facts guaranteed by the Onsager relations

  1. Symmetry of thermoelectric effects: The relation $\Pi = T\,S$ between the Seebeck coefficient $S$ and the Peltier coefficient $\Pi$ (the second Kelvin relation) is a direct consequence of $L_{12}=L_{21}$. That independently measured values of $S$ and $\Pi$ always agree is an experimental verification of the Onsager relations. (Incidentally, $S$ enters the figure of merit $ZT=S^2\sigma T/\kappa$ squared because the open-circuit voltage $V=S\Delta T$ and the current are both proportional to $S$, so the output is $P\propto V^2/R\propto S^2\sigma$; this is not itself a consequence of Onsager symmetry. The Onsager relations come into play at the stage of the efficiency derivation where the Peltier term is rewritten as $\Pi=TS$. This is what allows the performance of power generation (Seebeck) and of cooling (Peltier) to be described by the same $ZT$.)
  2. Symmetry of the electrical conductivity: $\sigma_{ij}(\bm{B})=\sigma_{ji}(-\bm{B})$. Without a magnetic field, $\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ji}$ is a symmetric tensor and principal axes can be chosen. In a magnetic field an antisymmetric component (the Hall component) appears, and it is an odd function of $\bm{B}$. The experimental common knowledge that “the Hall resistance is an odd function of $\bm{B}$ and the magnetoresistance is an even function” is exactly what Eq. \eqref{eq:6-onsager} says.
  3. Reciprocity of multi-terminal measurements: In four-terminal measurements, $R_{12,34}(\bm{B})=R_{34,12}(-\bm{B})$ (Büttiker reciprocity). This is the basis of the standard technique in mesoscopic transport experiments of distinguishing artifacts by reversing the magnetic field and measuring again.
  4. Cross terms between heat conduction and diffusion: In multicomponent systems, the coefficients of the Soret effect (thermal diffusion) and the Dufour effect (diffusion heat flow) are equal.

What we should note here is the limit of applicability of the Onsager relations. Since the reciprocal relation, Eq. \eqref{eq:6-onsager}, is a theorem about linear response coefficients, nonlinear response lies outside this theorem to begin with (it is not that “the Onsager relations are violated”). When a structure that breaks inversion symmetry (chirality or polarity) and a magnetic order (or magnetic field) that breaks $\mathcal{T}$ are both present, and one enters the nonlinear regime, an electrical nonreciprocity with $R(+I)\neq R(-I)$ can appear even at $\bm{B}=0$. This leads to the topic of (e).

6.6.4 (d) $\mathcal{PT}$ symmetry — non-Hermitian physics

Up to now we have asked whether $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are individually conserved or violated. In 1998, however, Bender and Boettcher reported a surprising fact: even if the Hamiltonian is not Hermitian, all of its eigenvalues can be real as long as it is $\mathcal{PT}$ symmetric.

$\mathcal{PT}$-symmetric Hamiltonians

A one-dimensional non-Hermitian Hamiltonian

$$ \Ham = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+V(x),\qquad V(x)\in\mathbb{C} $$

is said to be $\mathcal{PT}$ symmetric if $[\Ham,\mathcal{PT}]=0$, that is,

$$ \begin{equation} V^{*}(-x) = V(x) \label{eq:6-PT-condition} \end{equation} $$

holds (since $\mathcal{P}$ gives $x\to-x$ and $\mathcal{T}$ gives complex conjugation). This can be rephrased as “the real part is an even function and the imaginary part is an odd function”: $\mathrm{Re}\,V(-x)=\mathrm{Re}\,V(x)$, $\mathrm{Im}\,V(-x)=-\mathrm{Im}\,V(x)$.

In the unbroken phase of $\mathcal{PT}$ symmetry (the phase in which the eigenfunctions are also eigenstates of $\mathcal{PT}$), all eigenvalues are real. When a parameter is varied past an exceptional point (EP), the eigenvalues split into complex-conjugate pairs (spontaneous breaking of $\mathcal{PT}$ symmetry).

Why are the eigenvalues real?

Suppose $[\Ham,\mathcal{PT}]=0$ and $\Ham\ket{\psi}=E\ket{\psi}$. Acting with $\mathcal{PT}$ on both sides, and using the fact that $\mathcal{PT}$ is antilinear, we obtain

$$ \Ham\,(\mathcal{PT}\ket{\psi}) = \mathcal{PT}\,E\ket{\psi} = E^{*}\,(\mathcal{PT}\ket{\psi}) $$

That is, $\mathcal{PT}\ket{\psi}$ is an eigenstate with eigenvalue $E^{*}$. If $\ket{\psi}$ itself is an eigenstate of $\mathcal{PT}$ ($\mathcal{PT}\ket{\psi}=\ee^{\ii\varphi}\ket{\psi}$), then $E=E^{*}$, that is, $E$ is real. Conversely, if it is not, then $E$ and $E^{*}$ appear as a pair. This is the origin of the “symmetric phase / broken phase.” The point is that Hermiticity, while a sufficient condition for real eigenvalues, was not a necessary one.

Connection to Materials Science: $\mathcal{PT}$-symmetric photonics and EP sensors

Let us translate the condition, Eq. \eqref{eq:6-PT-condition}, into an optical system. In the Helmholtz equation, the complex refractive index $n(x)=n_R(x)+\ii n_I(x)$ plays the role of the potential, and $n_I>0$ corresponds to loss and $n_I<0$ to gain (if, as in this book, the time dependence is taken to be $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$, the wave becomes $\ee^{\ii nk_0z}=\ee^{\ii n_Rk_0z}\,\ee^{-n_Ik_0z}$, so $n_I>0$ means attenuation, that is, absorption. This is the same sign convention as the absorption coefficient $\alpha=2\omega n_I/c>0$. Note that in the $\ee^{+\ii\omega t}$ convention often used in engineering the sign is reversed). The condition for $\mathcal{PT}$ symmetry is

$$ n^{*}(-x)=n(x) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n_R(-x)=n_R(x),\ \ n_I(-x)=-n_I(x) $$

that is, a structure in which “the real part of the refractive index is left–right symmetric, and gain and loss are balanced between left and right.” Concretely, it suffices to give one of two coupled waveguides gain ($\mathrm{Er}$ doping or excitation of a quantum well) and the other an equal amount of loss (metal absorption). The $2\times2$ effective Hamiltonian of coupled-mode theory is

$$ \mathcal{H}_{\text{eff}}=\begin{pmatrix}\ii\gamma & \kappa\\ \kappa & -\ii\gamma\end{pmatrix}, \qquad E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\kappa^2-\gamma^2} $$

(the diagonal $+\ii\gamma$ is the gain-side waveguide and $-\ii\gamma$ is the loss-side waveguide). For $\gamma<\kappa$ the eigenvalues are real (oscillation = beat); for $\gamma>\kappa$ they are purely imaginary (one of them is exponentially amplified). The boundary $\gamma=\kappa$ is the exceptional point, where the two eigenvalues and the eigenvectors simultaneously become degenerate. This is a phenomenon fundamentally different from degeneracy in a Hermitian system (where the eigenvectors remain orthogonal).

The practical significance is as follows.

  • EP sensors: Near an exceptional point, the shift of the eigenvalue in response to a perturbation $\epsilon$ is $\Delta E\propto\sqrt{\epsilon}$ (normally $\propto\epsilon$). The smaller $\epsilon$ is, the more $\sqrt{\epsilon}\gg\epsilon$, so the sensitivity jumps up. This has been applied to the detection of nanoparticles and viruses with microtoroid resonators (however, since noise is amplified at the same time, there is debate about the effective gain).
  • Unidirectional light propagation: A $\mathcal{PT}$-symmetric lattice shows an asymmetric response in which only light incident from a certain direction is amplified and transmitted.
  • The non-Hermitian skin effect and non-Hermitian topology: The band structure of an open system (a system with dissipation) has a winding number in the complex plane, which gives rise to a new topological classification absent in Hermitian systems.

And now the foreshadowing at the beginning pays off. The effective Hamiltonian $M-\ii\Gamma/2$ of the neutral $K$ meson that we saw in Section 6.4.5 is likewise a non-Hermitian $2\times2$ matrix. The structure is common: the difference of the real parts of the eigenvalues gives the oscillation (the oscillation due to the $K_S$–$K_L$ mass difference), and the difference of the imaginary parts gives the difference in decay. That the $K$ meson and the optical waveguide are described by the same mathematics is no coincidence: it is because they share the common structure of a “dissipative two-level system.” The two are not of the same type, however: the $K$ system is a purely dissipative system in which the imaginary parts of both eigenvalues are negative (both $K_S$ and $K_L$ decay), unlike a $\mathcal{PT}$-symmetric matrix in which gain and loss are balanced (trace $0$). Therefore a $\mathcal{PT}$-symmetric phase or an exceptional point does not appear in the $K$ system.

6.6.5 (e) Nonreciprocal response — appears only when both $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are broken

This section is the most vivid example in which the reasoning of particle physics can be used as it is in materials science. We state the conclusion first.

Theorem 6.7: Symmetry condition for nonreciprocal response

For the optical absorption coefficient $\alpha(\bm{k})$ in a medium, for $\alpha(\bm{k})\neq\alpha(-\bm{k})$ (nonreciprocal directional dichroism) to occur, the medium must break both $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$.

Proof. We show this by two routes.

(i) Suppose the medium is $\mathcal{P}$ symmetric. The wave vector $\bm{k}$ is a polar vector, so it becomes $-\bm{k}$ under $\mathcal{P}$. If the medium is invariant under $\mathcal{P}$, then $\alpha(\bm{k})=\alpha(-\bm{k})$.

(ii) Suppose the medium is $\mathcal{T}$ symmetric. $\bm{k}$ also becomes $-\bm{k}$ under $\mathcal{T}$ (since it is a momentum). If the medium is invariant under $\mathcal{T}$, then again $\alpha(\bm{k})=\alpha(-\bm{k})$.

Hence for nonreciprocity to appear, both $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ must be broken. ∎

Derivation: Reading magnetochiral dichroism and the directional dichroism of multiferroics from Table 6.7

“Both must be broken” is a necessary condition; let us see in equations which quantities actually contribute. The key is the principle that only a $\mathcal{P}$-even and $\mathcal{T}$-even scalar can be an observable (otherwise it vanishes by Theorem 6.1).

Case 1: Magnetochiral dichroism (MChD). Apply a magnetic field to a chiral crystal and pass light parallel or antiparallel to $\bm{B}$. The term that contributes to the difference in absorption coefficients is

$$ \Delta\alpha \ \propto\ \chi\ (\bm{k}\cdot\bm{B}) $$

Here $\chi$ is a pseudoscalar representing the chirality of the crystal ($+$ for a right-handed crystal, $-$ for a left-handed one). We pick up the transformation properties from Table 6.7.

And for it to be nonzero, we need $\chi\neq0$ (breaking of $\mathcal{P}$ = chirality) and $\bm{B}\neq0$ (breaking of $\mathcal{T}$). The content of Theorem 6.7 has appeared in the form of a term.

Case 2: Directional dichroism of multiferroics. Consider the case in which, without using a magnetic field, the material itself breaks $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$. In a multiferroic material in which a spontaneous polarization $\bm{P}$ ($\mathcal{P}$-odd, $\mathcal{T}$-even) and a spontaneous magnetization $\bm{M}$ ($\mathcal{P}$-even, $\mathcal{T}$-odd) coexist, the toroidal moment

$$ \begin{equation} \bm{T} \propto \bm{P}\times\bm{M} \qquad(\mathcal{P}\text{-odd},\ \mathcal{T}\text{-odd}) \label{eq:6-toroidal} \end{equation} $$

can be defined. We check the transformation properties: for $\mathcal{P}$, $(-)(+)=-$; for $\mathcal{T}$, $(+)(-)=-$. Therefore the candidate observable is

$$ \Delta\alpha\ \propto\ \bm{k}\cdot\bm{T}\ \propto\ \bm{k}\cdot(\bm{P}\times\bm{M}) $$

and for $\mathcal{P}$: $(-)(-)=+$, for $\mathcal{T}$: $(-)(-)=+$. Again it is even under both and so can be an observable, and it vanishes unless $\bm{P}\neq0$ and $\bm{M}\neq0$.

This combination $\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{P}\times\bm{M})$ has been confirmed experimentally. In $\mathrm{GaFeO_3}$, $\mathrm{CuB_2O_4}$, $\mathrm{Ba_2CoGe_2O_7}$, and others, a phenomenon (directional dichroism) in which the absorption spectrum changes merely by reversing the propagation direction of the light has been observed, and the sign reverses when either $\bm{P}$ or $\bm{M}$ is reversed. The change is a few $\%$ in the visible region, and in the terahertz region of $\mathrm{Ba_2CoGe_2O_7}$ a giant nonreciprocity approaching $100\%$ (“one-way transparency”: nearly transparent from one direction and opaque from the opposite) has been reported. ∎

Connection to Materials Science: The magnetoelectric effect comes out of the same bookkeeping

The linear magnetoelectric effect—the phenomenon in which an electric field induces a magnetization and a magnetic field induces a polarization—is described by

$$ P_i = \alpha_{ij}H_j,\qquad \mu_0 M_i = \alpha_{ji}E_j $$

Let us tally up the transformation properties of $\alpha_{ij}$. $\bm{P}$ is $\mathcal{P}$-odd and $\mathcal{T}$-even, and $\bm{H}$ is $\mathcal{P}$-even and $\mathcal{T}$-odd. Therefore, for the transformation properties of the two sides of the equation to agree, we must have

$$ \alpha_{ij}:\quad \mathcal{P}\text{-odd and }\mathcal{T}\text{-odd} $$

Hence the linear magnetoelectric effect appears only in crystals in which both $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are broken.

Here is the interesting point: it suffices that $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are individually broken, and the product $\mathcal{PT}$ may well be preserved. Indeed, the magnetic point group of $\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}$ is $\bar3'm'$, in which the composite operation $\bar3'=\bar3\cdot\mathcal{T}$ is a symmetry. That is, $\mathcal{PT}$ is preserved. Nevertheless (or rather, precisely for that reason) $\alpha_{ij}\neq0$, and $\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}$ was the first material in which the magnetoelectric effect was demonstrated, in 1960. The Néel temperature is $307\ \mathrm{K}$, $\alpha_{\parallel}$ has a maximum of $\approx4\ \mathrm{ps/m}$ near $260$–$270\ \mathrm{K}$ (going to $0$ at low temperature), and $\alpha_{\perp}\approx1\ \mathrm{ps/m}$ or so. Because the antiferromagnetic domains can be controlled by an electric field, it continues to be studied as a candidate material for magnetoelectric memory (MELRAM).

This situation, “$\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are individually broken while $\mathcal{PT}$ remains,” has exactly the same structure as the situation in which an EDM exists in particle physics ($\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are broken while $\mathcal{CPT}$ remains). $\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{E}$ and $\alpha_{ij}E_jH_i$ carry the same fingerprint of symmetry.

6.6.6 (f) $\mathbb{Z}_2$ topological insulators — order protected by time-reversal symmetry

Finally, we tell the story of how Eq. \eqref{eq:6-T-squared}, $\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=-1$, gave rise to a central concept of 21st-century condensed matter physics.

Connection to Materials Science: $\mathbb{Z}_2$ invariant and parity eigenvalues

1. Kramers pairs protect the bands. In a time-reversal-symmetric insulator, the bands are necessarily twofold degenerate at the TRIMs (time-reversal invariant momenta) $\bm{\Gamma}_i$ (eight in three dimensions, four in two dimensions) (Section 6.6.2). Away from the TRIMs the degeneracy is lifted, so the surface-state bands “stick together” at the TRIMs and split in between. The parity (even or odd) of the number of times the bands cross the Fermi level then cannot be changed by a continuous deformation. A $\mathbb{Z}_2$ (two-element) quantity, even or odd, is conserved.

$$ \begin{equation} \nu = 0\ (\text{ordinary insulator})\quad\text{or}\quad \nu = 1\ (\text{topological insulator}) \label{eq:6-Z2} \end{equation} $$

2. Why $\mathbb{Z}_2$ and not $\mathbb{Z}$? The Chern number of the integer quantum Hall effect takes values in $\mathbb{Z}$ (any integer), but that is a system in which $\mathcal{T}$ is broken. When $\mathcal{T}$ is preserved, the Chern number is forced to be zero (because $\mathcal{T}$ flips the sign of the Berry curvature under $\bm{k}\to-\bm{k}$), and instead a $\mathbb{Z}_2$ classification arising from $\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=-1$ appears. What is essential is that the spin is $1/2$ ($\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=-1$): in an integer-spin system, which has only the true $\mathcal{T}$ ($\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=+1$), no $\mathbb{Z}_2$ classification arises. However, in photonic crystals and phonon systems there are examples in which another antiunitary symmetry satisfying $\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=-1$ (a pseudo-time-reversal) was prepared artificially to realize a $\mathbb{Z}_2$ phase, so this does not mean that “bosonic systems never have one.” Eq. \eqref{eq:6-T-squared}, which distinguishes $\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=\pm1$ in particle physics, bears directly on the classification of materials.

3. Parity eigenvalues give $\nu$ (the Fu–Kane formula). When the crystal also has inversion symmetry, the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ invariant is found with surprising ease. At each TRIM $\bm{\Gamma}_i$, take one member of each Kramers pair of the occupied bands ($N/2$ of them) and multiply their parity eigenvalues $\xi_{2m}(\bm{\Gamma}_i)=\pm1$ together:

$$ \begin{equation} \delta_i = \prod_{m=1}^{N/2}\xi_{2m}(\bm{\Gamma}_i), \qquad (-1)^{\nu} = \prod_{i=1}^{8}\delta_i \label{eq:6-fu-kane} \end{equation} $$

That is all that is needed. Merely multiplying the parity eigenvalues $\pm1$ tells us whether a material is a topological insulator. Computationally, this is the same thing we did in particle physics when we multiplied the parities of the $\pi$ meson to obtain a selection rule. It is an example in which the discrete-symmetry feature of a “multiplicative quantum number” appears in its most practical form.

4. Specific materials. By building the Fu–Kane criterion into first-principles calculations, many topological insulators were predicted and then confirmed by experiment.

  • $\mathrm{Bi}_{1-x}\mathrm{Sb}_x$ ($x\approx0.1$): the first three-dimensional topological insulator to be confirmed. Its surface states are complicated; ARPES observed that the surface bands cross the Fermi level five times between $\bar\Gamma$ and $\bar M$, that is, an odd number of times. This odd number was the evidence for $\nu=1$.
  • $\mathrm{Bi_2Se_3}$: the “textbook” topological insulator with a single Dirac cone. Its bulk gap of $0.3\ \mathrm{eV}$ is well above the room-temperature value $k_BT=0.026\ \mathrm{eV}$. The origin of $\nu=1$ is that a band inversion between the $\mathrm{Se}\ p_z$ and $\mathrm{Bi}\ p_z$ bands occurs at the $\Gamma$ point, exchanging the parities.
  • $\mathrm{Bi_2Te_3}$, $\mathrm{Sb_2Te_3}$: members of the same family. They are also well known as thermoelectric materials, with $ZT\sim1$.
  • $\mathrm{HgTe}/\mathrm{CdTe}$ quantum wells: the two-dimensional version (the quantum spin Hall insulator). When the well width exceeds the critical value $6.3\ \mathrm{nm}$, a band inversion occurs and $\nu$ changes from $0$ to $1$.

5. Break $\mathcal{T}$ and the protection disappears. The surface states are protected by $\mathcal{T}$, so they are not backscattered by nonmagnetic impurities or lattice defects ($\bm{k}$ and $-\bm{k}$ form a Kramers pair and are not connected by time-reversal-symmetric scattering). If magnetic impurities are introduced, however, $\mathcal{T}$ is broken and a gap opens at the Dirac point. The observation of the quantum anomalous Hall effect in $\mathrm{Cr}$-doped $(\mathrm{Bi},\mathrm{Sb})_2\mathrm{Te_3}$ is precisely the result of controlling this breaking of $\mathcal{T}$. Because symmetry provides the protection, breaking the symmetry removes it—this simple principle serves as a guide for materials design.

Caution: Do not confuse $\mathcal{P}$ for elementary particles with $\bar{1}$ for crystals

In this section we deliberately treated the two with the same tool, but the differences should also be kept in mind.

  • The $\mathcal{P}$ of particle physics is a transformation of spacetime itself, and it asks whether the theory (the Lagrangian) is invariant. What is broken is on the side of the laws of nature.
  • The $\bar{1}$ of crystal physics is a symmetry possessed by a specific material, and what is broken is the structure of that material. The law of nature (the electromagnetic interaction) remains $\mathcal{P}$-symmetric.

In other words, the “breaking of $\mathcal{P}$” in a crystal is close to what particle physics calls spontaneous symmetry breaking (Chapter 13). The Lagrangian is symmetric, but the ground state (the crystal structure) is not. A ferroelectric phase transition is precisely a spontaneous breaking of $\mathcal{P}$. However, since what is broken is a discrete symmetry, no Goldstone mode appears—Goldstone’s theorem (Chapter 13, Section 13.7.3) presupposes a continuous symmetry. What appears at a ferroelectric transition is the soft phonon (Cochran), whose frequency goes to zero at the transition point, and the critical phenomenon of the order parameter (amplitude), which stiffens again on both sides of $T_{\rm c}$ as $\omega^2\propto\abs{T-T_{\rm c}}$. In that sense it is closer to the amplitude mode in the Higgs mechanism (Chapter 13, Section 13.9.4) than to the massless Goldstone mode produced by the breaking of a continuous symmetry.

6.7 Summary and Exercises

6.7.1 Summary

  • The quantum numbers of discrete symmetries are multiplicative. Whereas continuous symmetries give additive conserved quantities (momentum, angular momentum), $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ take the values $\pm1$, and for a composite system they give the product: $\eta_{\text{total}}=\left(\prod_a\eta_a\right)(-1)^\ell$.
  • The distinction between polar and axial vectors is the starting point for everything. Each time a cross product is taken, the $\mathcal{P}$ sign changes. $\bm{x},\bm{p},\bm{E},\bm{P},\bm{j}$ are polar, and $\bm{L},\bm{S},\bm{B},\bm{M}$ are axial. This classification is required by the $\mathcal{P}$ invariance of the Maxwell equations.
  • There is only one criterion (Theorem 6.1). If a symmetry is preserved, the expectation value of any quantity that is odd under that symmetry is zero. In particle physics this is $\braket{\bm{J}\cdot\bm{p}_e}$; in crystals it is $\chi^{(2)}_{ijk}$, $d_{ijk}$, or $\alpha_{ij}$. The theorem used is the same.
  • The $\pi$ meson is a pseudoscalar $0^{-}$. In $\pi^-d\to nn$, $J=1$ and the Pauli exclusion principle fix the final state uniquely as $^3P_1$ ($L=1$), and $\eta_\pi=(-1)^L=-1$ follows. This is an argument that uses symmetry alone, with no use at all of the details of the interaction.
  • The weak interaction violates $\mathcal{P}$ maximally. This was shown by the Wu experiment ($^{60}\mathrm{Co}$, asymmetry $\sim40\%$) and the Goldhaber experiment ($h(\nu_e)=-1$). The structure is $V-A$, with the projection operator $(1-\gamma^5)/2$. As a result, $\pi\to e\nu$ suffers helicity suppression, and the branching ratio is $1.23\times10^{-4}$.
  • $C$ parity determines the parity (even or odd) of the photon number. $C(n\gamma)=(-1)^n$. This alone explains the prohibition of $\pi^0\to3\gamma$ ($<3.1\times10^{-8}$) and the fact that the lifetimes of positronium split as widely as $125\ \mathrm{ps}$ and $142\ \mathrm{ns}$, a factor of $1100$. The origin of the lifetime ratio is “one extra factor of $\alpha$.”
  • $\mathcal{CP}$ is also slightly violated ($\abs{\varepsilon}=2.2\times10^{-3}$). Cronin and Fitch discovered $K_L\to\pi^+\pi^-$. Its origin is the Kobayashi–Maskawa phase, which requires the existence of three generations of quarks. The charge asymmetry $\delta_L=3.3\times10^{-3}$ in the semileptonic decay of $K_L$ provides a yardstick that distinguishes matter from antimatter in an absolute way.
  • $\hat{\mathcal{T}}$ must be antiunitary. Otherwise $[\hat x,\hat p]=\ii\hbar$ would be destroyed and the energy spectrum would no longer be bounded from below. For half-integer spin, $\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=-1$, and this gives rise to Kramers degeneracy.
  • Only $\mathcal{CPT}$ is a theorem. It follows from Lorentz invariance, locality, and spectral positivity. As a consequence, the masses and lifetimes of a particle and its antiparticle are exactly equal (upper limit on the $K^0$ mass difference: $6\times10^{-19}$). The EDM search ($d_n<1.8\times10^{-26}\ e\,\mathrm{cm}$) is an experiment that looks for a new violation of $\mathcal{T}$, that is, of $\mathcal{CP}$.
  • In materials science the same tools work as they are. Breaking of inversion symmetry $\to$ SHG, piezoelectricity, ferroelectricity. Breaking of $\mathcal{T}$ $\to$ magnetization, the anomalous Hall effect, lifting of Kramers degeneracy. Conservation of $\mathcal{T}$ $\to$ the Onsager reciprocity theorem and $\mathbb{Z}_2$ topological insulators (the Fu–Kane formula is a product of parity eigenvalues). Breaking of both $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ $\to$ the magnetoelectric effect and nonreciprocal directional dichroism $\Delta\alpha\propto\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{P}\times\bm{M})$. And $\mathcal{PT}$ symmetry forms the backbone of non-Hermitian physics.

(3) The Rashba term $(\bm{\sigma}\times\bm{k})\cdot\hat{\bm{z}}$ ($\hat{\bm z}$ is the interface normal, a polar vector)

(4) The spin–orbit interaction $\bm{L}\cdot\bm{S}$

(5) The Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction $\bm{D}\cdot(\bm{S}_1\times\bm{S}_2)$ ($\bm{D}$ is a constant vector determined by the crystal structure and, like $\bm{S}_1\times\bm{S}_2$, is an axial vector. Note that the inversion operation interchanges sites 1 and 2.)

(6) Explain why (2) and (4) above are allowed in any crystal. Also state what kind of symmetry breaking is needed for (3) and (5) to appear.

Hint: (1) $\mathcal{P}$: $(-)(+)=-$, $\mathcal{T}$: $(+)(-)=-$. Since this is a flow of energy, it is natural that it reverses under $\mathcal{T}$. (2) Both even. (3) $\bm{\sigma}$ is $(+,-)$, $\bm{k}$ is $(-,-)$, and $\hat{\bm z}$ is $(-,+)$. The product under $\mathcal{P}$ is $(+)(-)(-)=+$ … The cross product $\bm{\sigma}\times\bm{k}$ is $\mathcal{P}$-odd and $\mathcal{T}$-even, and its dot product with $\hat{\bm z}$ ($\mathcal{P}$-odd) is $\mathcal{P}$-even and $\mathcal{T}$-even. Therefore the existence of $\hat{\bm z}$, that is, the breaking of structural inversion symmetry, is needed. (4) Both even (both $\bm{L},\bm{S}$ are $\mathcal{P}$-even and $\mathcal{T}$-odd, and the product is $\mathcal{T}$-even). (5) $\bm{S}_1\times\bm{S}_2$ contains two factors of $\bm{S}_i$ and so is $\mathcal{P}$-even and $\mathcal{T}$-even, and if $\bm{D}$ is also an axial vector, the whole term is $\mathcal{P}$-even and $\mathcal{T}$-even. What matters here is not only the signs but the interchange of sites: if there is an inversion center at the midpoint of the bond, the inversion exchanges sites 1 and 2, so $\bm{S}_1\times\bm{S}_2\to\bm{S}_2\times\bm{S}_1=-\bm{S}_1\times\bm{S}_2$. On the other hand, $\bm{D}$ is a constant (axial) vector determined by the crystal structure and so does not change under inversion. Hence the whole term changes sign, and to preserve the symmetry we must have $\bm{D}=0$—this is Moriya’s rule that “the DM interaction arises only on bonds that have no inversion center.”

Exercise 6.4: Proof and applications of Kramers degeneracy

(1) For $\hat{\mathcal{T}}=-\ii\sigma_y K$ acting on spin $1/2$, verify component by component that $\hat{\mathcal{T}}\sigma_i\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}=-\sigma_i$ ($i=x,y,z$) (use the fact that $\sigma_x,\sigma_z$ are real and $\sigma_y$ is purely imaginary).

(2) Show that for a system of $N$ electrons, $\hat{\mathcal{T}}^2=(-1)^N$. Hence Kramers degeneracy is guaranteed only when $N$ is odd.

(3) Place $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ ($3d^9$, one unpaired electron) and $\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}$ ($3d^8$, two unpaired electrons) in a crystal field. For which of the two is the degeneracy of the ground state never completely lifted, however low the symmetry of the crystal field? Also state how this affects the measurability by ESR (electron spin resonance).

(4) Show that a magnetic field lifts the Kramers degeneracy, using the commutation relation between $\hat{\mathcal{T}}$ and the Zeeman term $-\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{B}$.

Hint: (2) Each electron carries $\hat{\mathcal{T}}_i^2=-1$, and the product gives $(-1)^N$. (3) $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ has $N$ odd, so it is a Kramers ion, and its lowest level is necessarily twofold degenerate. Since there is no zero-field splitting, ESR can be observed easily. $\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}$ is a non-Kramers ion, and a low-symmetry field can lift the degeneracy completely (the zero-field splitting $D$ becomes large, and it may become invisible in ordinary X-band ESR). (4) $\bm{\mu}\propto\bm{S}$ is $\mathcal{T}$-odd, and $\bm{B}$ is held fixed as an external parameter, so $\hat{\mathcal{T}}\Ham_{\text{Z}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}=-\Ham_{\text{Z}}\neq\Ham_{\text{Z}}$. $\mathcal{T}$ is no longer a symmetry, and the premise of Theorem 6.5 breaks down.

Exercise 6.5: Oscillation of neutral $K$ mesons

Suppose that a pure $\ket{K^0}$ is produced at $t=0$. Let $\ket{K^0}=(\ket{K_S}+\ket{K_L})/\sqrt2$ (ignore $\mathcal{CP}$ violation), and let the time evolution of $K_{S,L}$ be $\ee^{-\ii m_{S,L}t-\Gamma_{S,L}t/2}$.

(1) Show that the probability of finding $\bar K^0$ at time $t$ is

$$ P_{\bar K^0}(t)=\frac{1}{4}\left[\ee^{-\Gamma_S t}+\ee^{-\Gamma_L t}-2\ee^{-(\Gamma_S+\Gamma_L)t/2}\cos(\Delta m\,t)\right] $$

(with $\Delta m=m_L-m_S$).

(2) Compute the dimensionless parameter $x\equiv\Delta m/\Gamma_S=\Delta m\,\tau_S$ from $\Delta m=5.29\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\tau_S=8.954\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{s}$.

(3) Is it favorable or unfavorable for experiments that $x$ is around $1$? Discuss what happens in the cases $x\ll1$ and $x\gg1$.

Hint: (1) Use $\ket{\bar K^0}=(\ket{K_S}-\ket{K_L})/\sqrt2$ and take the squared absolute value of $\braket{\bar K^0|\psi(t)}$. (2) $x=5.29\times10^{9}\times8.954\times10^{-11}=0.474$. (3) If $x\ll1$, the phase difference does not have time to grow before the $K_S$ dies, so no oscillation is seen. If $x\gg1$, the oscillation is too fast to be resolved in time. $x\sim0.5$ is an ideal value: within the time window in which the interference term is alive ($t\lesssim7\tau_S$), the phase difference reaches exactly a half period $\pi$ (the phase accumulated in one $t=\tau_S$ is $x=0.474\ \mathrm{rad}\approx27^\circ$, only $7.5\%$ of one period; it reaches $\pi$ at $t=\pi/x\cdot\tau_S=6.6\,\tau_S$), and $\Delta m$ can be measured with high precision. It is a favorable condition that nature has provided.

Exercise 6.6: Estimating the sensitivity of a neutron EDM search

In a neutron EDM experiment, a magnetic field $\bm{B}$ and an electric field $\bm{E}$ are applied to ultracold neutrons, parallel or antiparallel, and the difference in the Larmor precession frequency is measured.

(1) Show from $H=-\bm{d}\cdot\bm{E}$ that, when the EDM is $d_n$, the energy difference on reversing the direction of the electric field is $\Delta E = 2 d_n E$.

(2) Find $\Delta E$ in eV for $d_n = 1.8\times10^{-26}\ e\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $E=10\ \mathrm{kV/cm}$.

(3) Find the corresponding frequency $\Delta\nu=\Delta E/h$ (with $h=4.136\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{eV\,s}$).

(4) Find the minimum observation time $T\sim1/\Delta\nu$ needed to measure this frequency, and discuss whether a single measurement suffices or whether statistics must be accumulated.

Hint: (2) $\Delta E=2\times1.8\times10^{-26}\ e\,\mathrm{cm}\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{V/cm}=3.6\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{eV}$. (3) $\Delta\nu=3.6\times10^{-22}/4.136\times10^{-15}=8.7\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{Hz}$, that is, about $87\ \mathrm{nHz}$. (4) $T\sim1.1\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{s}\approx4$ months. This is hopeless with a single neutron, and if $N$ neutrons are measured simultaneously, the statistical error shrinks as $1/\sqrt{N}$. In actual experiments, a storage time of $\sim200\ \mathrm{s}$ and $N\sim10^{4}$ neutrons per cycle are repeated for several years. One gets a feeling for why particle-physics experiments take so long.

Exercise 6.7: Determining material properties from the crystallographic point group

Answer using Table 6.8 and Theorem 6.1.

(1) Of $\mathrm{Si}$ ($m\bar3m$), $\mathrm{GaAs}$ ($\bar43m$), and $\mathrm{LiNbO_3}$ ($3m$), which show SHG in the bulk? Which show piezoelectricity?

(2) SHG is observed at the surface of $\mathrm{Si}$ and at the $\mathrm{Si/SiO_2}$ interface. Explain why an effect forbidden in the bulk appears at the interface.

(3) Monolayer $\mathrm{MoS_2}$ has point group $\bar6m2$ (no inversion center), and bilayer $2H$-stacked $\mathrm{MoS_2}$ has $\bar3m$ (with an inversion center). Predict whether the SHG signal “appears / does not appear” for the number of layers $n=1,2,3,4$.

(4) The magnetic point group of $\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}$ is $\bar3'm'$. Does this material (i) have a spontaneous polarization, (ii) have a spontaneous magnetization, (iii) show a linear magnetoelectric effect? Answer each with reasons.

Hint: (1) $\mathrm{Si}$ is centrosymmetric, so both are forbidden. $\mathrm{GaAs}$ and $\mathrm{LiNbO_3}$ are noncentrosymmetric, so both are allowed. Moreover, $\mathrm{LiNbO_3}$ has a polar point group and so also has a spontaneous polarization (it is a ferroelectric). (2) At the interface, translation symmetry and inversion symmetry are structurally broken, so $\chi^{(2)}\neq0$ only within a few atomic layers of the interface. This is the origin of the interface selectivity of SHG. (3) It appears for an odd number of layers and vanishes for an even number (SHG for $n=1,3$, vanishing for $n=2,4$). This even-odd oscillation has also been confirmed experimentally. (4) Note that $\bar3'm'$ does not contain a pure inversion $\bar1$; what it contains is $\bar1'=\bar1\cdot\mathcal{T}$ (inversion $\times$ time reversal). (i) $\bm{P}$ is a $\mathcal{T}$-even polar vector, so under $\bar1'$ we have $\bm{P}\to-\bm{P}$ and $\bm{P}_s=0$. (ii) $\bm{M}$ is a $\mathcal{T}$-odd axial vector, so under the same $\bar1'$ we have $\bm{M}\to-\bm{M}$ and $\bm{M}=0$ (in reality it is antiferromagnetic). (iii) Both $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are individually broken ($\bar3$ alone and $\mathcal{T}$ alone are both not symmetry operations), so $\alpha_{ij}\neq0$ is allowed. Indeed, $\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}$ is the material in which the magnetoelectric effect was first confirmed.

Exercise 6.8: A $\mathcal{PT}$-symmetric two-level system and exceptional points

The effective Hamiltonian of two coupled waveguides with balanced gain and loss is

$$ \mathcal{H}=\begin{pmatrix}\ii\gamma & \kappa\\ \kappa & -\ii\gamma\end{pmatrix} \qquad(\gamma,\kappa>0\ \text{ are real}) $$

.

(1) Verify that $\mathcal{H}$ is not Hermitian but is $\mathcal{PT}$-symmetric ($\mathcal{P}=\sigma_x$ interchanges the two waveguides, and $\mathcal{T}=K$ takes the complex conjugate). That is, show that $(\sigma_x K)\mathcal{H}(\sigma_x K)^{-1}=\mathcal{H}$.

(2) Show that the eigenvalues are $E_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\kappa^2-\gamma^2}$, and verify that they are real for $\gamma<\kappa$ and purely imaginary for $\gamma>\kappa$.

(3) Show that at $\gamma=\kappa$ (the exceptional point) the two eigenvectors coalesce. Describe how this differs from the degeneracy of a Hermitian matrix.

(4) Let $\kappa=\gamma(1+\delta)$ ($\delta\ll1$), expand $E_\pm$, and show that the splitting of the eigenvalues is $\propto\sqrt{\delta}$. Explain why this is said to be advantageous for a sensor.

Hint: (1) $K$ takes the complex conjugate of the components, and $\sigma_x$ interchanges the diagonal elements. We have $\ii\gamma\to-\ii\gamma\to$ (interchange) back to the original. (2) The trace is $0$ and the determinant is $\gamma^2-\kappa^2$. (3) When $\gamma=\kappa$, $\mathcal{H}=\gamma\begin{pmatrix}\ii&1\\ 1&-\ii\end{pmatrix}$, and both the determinant and the trace are zero. Both eigenvectors coalesce into the single vector $(1,\ -\ii)^{T}$ (the eigenspace is only one-dimensional; this is what is called a defective matrix). For a Hermitian matrix, even when eigenvalues are degenerate, two orthogonal eigenvectors remain, so this is a phenomenon peculiar to non-Hermitian matrices. (4) $E_\pm=\pm\gamma\sqrt{(1+\delta)^2-1}=\pm\gamma\sqrt{2\delta+\delta^2}\approx\pm\gamma\sqrt{2\delta}$. Since $\sqrt{\delta}\gg\delta$ as $\delta\to0$, the response to a tiny perturbation is larger than that of an ordinary linear response.
