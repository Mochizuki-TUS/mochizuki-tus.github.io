Appendix BSetting Up the Computing Environment and Introduction to PySCF

From Chapter 6 onward, this book checks on a computer the theory that we have followed by hand. For this we use a Python module called PySCF (Python-based Simulations of Chemistry Framework) and a module for materials science called pymatgen (Python Materials Genomics). Both are free of charge, and both run on a single laptop.

However, what trips up many students is not the theory but setting up the environment. “I don't know where python is,” “I ran pip install but the module cannot be found,” “I cannot specify the location of a file”—all of these come from not knowing the concepts of the terminal and the path. In this appendix we first settle these points, and then move on to how to use PySCF.

B.1 Five Words to Learn First

Definition: The minimum vocabulary for using a computer

  • Directory: A box that holds files. It is the same thing that is called a “folder” on Windows and macOS screens.
  • Path: The address of a file or directory. It is written like /Users/ymochizuki/Downloads/XYZ_H.xyz. A path that starts from / (the root) is called an absolute path, and a path that gives the position relative to where you currently are is called a relative path.
  • Command: An instruction that you type into the terminal. It is executed when you press the Enter key.
  • Shell: The “shell” that translates the commands written by a human into machine language. bash and zsh are the mainstream ones; the default on macOS is zsh, and the default on Ubuntu running on Windows is bash. You can check which one you are using with echo $SHELL.
  • Environment variable: A variable that determines the behavior of the shell. $SHELL and $PATH are examples. When you display one, you put a dollar sign at the front.

Why? Why do we use commands instead of the mouse?

There are two kinds of interaction with a computer. One is the GUI (Graphical User Interface), in which you manipulate icons on the screen with the mouse; the other is the CLI (Command Line Interface), in which you type commands as text.

The reason for using the CLI in research is that it lets us repeat the same task in large numbers, accurately, and while leaving a record. For example, in Chapter 8 we compute the total energy of $\mathrm{O_2}$ for 12 different bond lengths, from 0.9 Å to 2.0 Å. You could click 12 times in a GUI to run them, but if you changed the bond length in steps of 0.01 Å, it would become 110 runs. With the CLI, you only need to write three lines of a for loop, and afterward what you did remains as the history of commands. In research, where reproducibility is vital, this makes a decisive difference.

Not a Math Note but an “Operations Note”: The meaning of the dot

Let us sort out the meaning of the symbols that appear in paths.

  • . (one dot): the place where you are now
  • .. (two dots): one level up
  • ~ (tilde): the home directory
  • / (a leading slash): the root (the top level)
  • * (asterisk): a wildcard that matches any string. output_* means all files whose names begin with output_.

For example, cp ~/Downloads/XYZ_CH4.xyz . means “copy XYZ_CH4.xyz, located in Downloads in the home directory, to the place where you are now.”

Caution: rm has no trash can

When you delete a file in a GUI, it goes into the trash can, and you can restore it later. The terminal’s rm, however, deletes it immediately and permanently. In particular, rm -r deletes a whole directory, so before running it, make a habit of always checking with pwd and ls “where am I now, and what am I about to delete?” Accidents in which research data are lost in an instant usually happen with this command.

These are the three commands we use in Chapter 8 when systematically varying the bond length of $\mathrm{O_2}$.

Listing B.3: sed, grep, and awk. The vertical bar | is called a “pipe”; it passes the output of the command on the left to the input of the command on the right.

In awk, $1, $2, … are the numbers of the whitespace-separated columns, and $NF means the last column. Count the columns according to the format of the output, and extract only the numbers you need.

B.5 Installing PySCF and Your First Calculation

Once you are ready, move to the pyscf_tutorial1 directory distributed in the lecture and let us calculate the hydrogen atom.

$ cd ~/Downloads/pyscf_tutorial1
$ ls
XYZ_B.xyz  XYZ_Be.xyz  XYZ_CH4.xyz  XYZ_F.xyz  XYZ_H.xyz  XYZ_He.xyz
XYZ_Li.xyz XYZ_N.xyz   XYZ_Ne.xyz   XYZ_O.xyz  XYZ_O2.xyz  calc_pyscf.py

$ python3 calc_pyscf.py --xyz XYZ_H.xyz --spin 1

Listing B.9: Calculation of the hydrogen atom. --spin 1 means “the difference between the numbers of up-spin and down-spin electrons is 1.”

The output lists the computational method (Method : UHF), the total energy (Total Energy), the molecular orbital energies (MO energies), and the occupation numbers. The total energy of the hydrogen atom in the STO-3G basis is $-12.696$ eV, which has an error of 6.7% relative to the exact solution of $-13.606$ eV (Chapter 6, Section 6.7).

Caution: --spin is $2S$, not the number of electrons

The --spin of PySCF is neither the spin multiplicity $2S+1$ nor the number of electrons, but $2S$ (= the difference between the numbers of up-spin and down-spin electrons).

  • H atom (one electron): $2S=1$ → --spin 1
  • He atom (closed shell): $2S=0$ → --spin 0
  • $\mathrm{O_2}$ (triplet, ground state): $2S=2$ → --spin 2
  • C atom ($2p^2$, $^3P$): $2S=2$ → --spin 2

If you get this wrong, the calculation itself runs, but the answer has no physical meaning. When the energy deviates greatly from the experimental value, first suspect --spin.

B.8 Systematic Calculations with for Loops and Extracting the Results

Let us run through the task carried out in Chapter 8, “calculate the total energy while varying the bond length of $\mathrm{O_2}$,” from start to finish. Done by hand, it would take several dozen repetitions, but in the terminal it takes only a few lines.

B.8.1 Mass-producing structure files

# Source file (bond length 1.20000 Å)
$ cat XYZ_O2_1p20000.xyz
2
O2 molecule
  O   0.00000   0.00000   0.00000
  O   0.00000   0.00000   1.20000

# Create structure files from 0.90 Å to 1.90 Å in steps of 0.10 Å
$ for d in 0p90000 1p00000 1p10000 1p20000 1p30000 1p40000 1p50000 \
           1p60000 1p70000 1p80000 1p90000
  do
    r=$(echo $d | sed -e 's/p/./')
    sed -e "s/1.20000/$r/" XYZ_O2_1p20000.xyz > XYZ_O2_${d}.xyz
  done

$ ls XYZ_O2_*.xyz
XYZ_O2_0p90000.xyz  XYZ_O2_1p00000.xyz  ...  XYZ_O2_1p90000.xyz

Listing B.16: Mass-producing structure files with sed and a for loop. The dollar sign denotes a variable. Since a period in a file name would be confusing, p is substituted for it.

B.8.2 Running the calculations in batch

$ for f in XYZ_O2_*.xyz
  do
    tag=$(basename $f .xyz)
    python3 calc_pyscf.py --xyz $f --spin 2 --basis 6-31g > output_${tag}.txt
  done

Listing B.17: Calculating every file in turn and saving the results to text files. The symbol > means “write the output to a file” (redirection).

B.8.3 Extracting the results and plotting them

# Extract only the total-energy lines
$ grep "Total Energy" output_*.txt

# Pick out only the numbers
$ grep "Total Energy" output_*.txt | awk '{print $NF}'

# Make a file with the bond length in column 1 and the total energy in column 2
$ for f in output_XYZ_O2_*.txt
  do
    d=$(echo $f | sed -e 's/.*O2_//' -e 's/.txt//' -e 's/p/./')
    e=$(grep "Total Energy" $f | awk '{print $NF}')
    echo "$d $e"
  done > energy_vs_distance.dat

$ cat energy_vs_distance.dat
0.90000 -4060.401
1.00000 -4066.xxx
...
1.20000 -4069.348
...
2.00000 -4061.466

Listing B.18: Collecting the results into a two-column data file.

# plot_energy.py
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

d, e = np.loadtxt("energy_vs_distance.dat", unpack=True)

plt.figure(figsize=(5, 4))
plt.plot(d, e, "o-", color="#14684a")
plt.xlabel(r"Bond length $R$ (Å)", fontsize=13)
plt.ylabel("Total energy (eV)", fontsize=13)
plt.grid(alpha=0.4)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("O2_potential_curve.png", dpi=200)
plt.show()

Listing B.19: Plotting with matplotlib. This directly yields the interatomic potential curve that appears in textbooks.

Why? This sequence of steps is “computational science” itself

What we have just done consists of four stages: (1) systematically create the input, (2) run the calculations in batch, (3) extract only the necessary numbers from the output, and (4) turn them into a figure and read off the physics. Whether the object is a molecule or a solid, and whether the method is a quantum-chemical calculation or molecular dynamics, the structure of these four stages does not change. Being able to run through this sequence with your own hands is far more important than knowing how to use any individual piece of software.

B.9 Visualization with VESTA

VESTA (https://jp-minerals.org/vesta/) is free software for visualizing crystal structures, molecular structures, and wave functions. It was developed in Japan and is used by first-principles calculation users around the world.

Table B.5: Files that VESTA can open
ExtensionContentsUse in this book
.xyzMolecular structureChecking structures, measuring bond lengths and bond angles
POSCARCrystal structure (VASP format)The ionic crystals of Chapter 13
.cubeThree-dimensional scalar field (wave function, electron density)HOMO/LUMO visualization in Chapter 10

The key points of usage are as follows.

  • Drag to rotate, and use the mouse wheel to zoom in and out.
  • If you click two atoms in succession, the bond length is displayed at the bottom; if you click three in succession, the bond angle is displayed. Use these when comparing with experimental values.
  • When you open a cube file, an isosurface is displayed. Yellow is the positive region of the wave function, and blue is the negative region. The isosurface value can be changed under Properties → Isosurfaces.
  • By looking at how the positive and negative regions are arranged, you can judge whether an orbital is a bonding orbital or an antibonding orbital. If the sign is the same between the atoms (no node), it is bonding; if the signs are opposite (there is a node), it is antibonding (Chapter 11).

B.10 pymatgen and the Madelung Energy

In Chapter 13, we calculate the electrostatic energy of ionic crystals (the Madelung energy) with pymatgen. pymatgen is a Python module developed within the Materials Genome Initiative, which the United States launched in 2011, and it has become the de facto standard for Materials Informatics.

$ pip3 install pymatgen
$ cd ~/Downloads/pymatgen_tutorial
$ ls
POSCAR_NaCl_Fm-3m  POSCAR_MgO_Fm-3m  POSCAR_TiO2_P42mnm_rutile  ...
calc_madelung_constant.py

$ python3 calc_madelung_constant.py POSCAR_NaCl_Fm-3m

Listing B.20: Calculating the Madelung energy of NaCl.

The output lists the Madelung energy per cell (four Na and four Cl), the Madelung energy per formula unit (one Na and one Cl), and the Madelung constant. Be careful: if you confuse “per cell” with “per formula unit,” the result is off by a factor of four.

When the automatic estimation of the ion valences does not work well, they can be specified explicitly.

# Specify the valences explicitly
$ python3 calc_madelung_constant.py POSCAR_Mg2Si_Fm-3m --oxidation-state Mg=2 Si=-4

# Obtain the Madelung constant with respect to a specific cation-anion pair
$ python3 calc_madelung_constant.py POSCAR_NaCl_Fm-3m --reference-pair Na Cl

Listing B.21: Specifying the valences and specifying the reference ion pair.

The content of the script is essentially just a few lines.

from pymatgen.analysis.ewald import EwaldSummation
from pymatgen.io.vasp.inputs import Poscar

structure = Poscar.from_file("POSCAR_NaCl_Fm-3m").structure
structure.add_oxidation_state_by_guess()

ewald = EwaldSummation(structure)
print("Madelung energy (cell) :", ewald.total_energy, "eV")

Listing B.22: The core of pymatgen. EwaldSummation is responsible for calculating the electrostatic energy by the Ewald method.

Supplement: Why is the Ewald method needed?

As we see in Chapter 13, the series $\sum_j \pm N_j/c_j$ that determines the Madelung constant is conditionally convergent, so the answer changes depending on the order in which the terms are added. Even if we naively add the terms from the nearest ones outward, the sum hardly converges. The Ewald method is a technique that splits this sum into a sum in real space and a sum in reciprocal space (Fourier space), making both converge exponentially fast. It is used almost without exception in first-principles calculations of periodic systems.

The energy deviates greatly from the experimental valueThe basis set is too small / the spin state is wrongTry --basis 6-31g or larger. Change --spin and compare

Mindset: An error is not a failure

When setting up the environment and debugging eat up your time, it is easy to think, “I am not cut out for programming.” In research practice, however, a considerable part of the computing time is spent on exactly this. An error message is information that tells you what is wrong and how; it is not a scolding. Read it, search for it, and if you still cannot figure it out, it is a good idea to paste it into a generative AI and ask. What matters is the habit of staying calm when an error appears and checking again from pwd and ls.

Exercise B.2: Writing an xyz file yourself

Create by hand an xyz file for the $\mathrm{H_2}$ molecule with a bond length of 0.74 Å, and calculate the total energy with PySCF. Try both the sto-3g and 6-31g basis sets and compare the values.

Hint: The first line is the number of atoms (2), the second line is an arbitrary comment, and lines 3 and 4 are “H 0 0 0” and “H 0 0 0.74”. Since the molecule is closed-shell, use --spin 0. Confirm that enlarging the basis set always lowers the total energy (variational principle, Chapter 3).

Exercise B.3: Specifying the spin state

For the oxygen atom (XYZ_O.xyz), calculate the total energy for each of the three settings --spin 0, --spin 2 and --spin 4, and answer which one is the most stable. Is the result consistent with Hund's rules (Chapters 7 and 9)?

Hint: The electron configuration of the oxygen atom is $1s^2 2s^2 2p^4$. When four electrons are placed in the three $2p$ orbitals, Hund's rules make the number of up spins larger by two.

Exercise B.4: Systematic calculations with a for loop

Vary the bond length of the $\mathrm{H_2}$ molecule from 0.5 Å to 2.5 Å in steps of 0.1 Å, and plot the total energy. Read off the equilibrium bond length and compare it with the experimental value of 0.741 Å. Also find the dissociation energy (the difference between the sum of the energies of two H atoms at infinite separation and the energy at the equilibrium point), and compare it with the experimental value of 4.75 eV.

Hint: Listings B.16–B.19 can be reused as they are. Since $\mathrm{H_2}$ is closed-shell, use --spin 0. For the energy at infinite separation, it suffices to double the result of a calculation on a single H atom (--spin 1). It is known that restricted Hartree–Fock (RHF) cannot describe the dissociation limit correctly, so a large deviation should appear. Consider why this happens by relating it to the discussion in Chapter 11, Section 11.9.

Exercise B.5: Calculating a molecule of your choice

Download the structure of a molecule of your choice from PubChem, carry out the following in order, and discuss the results.

(1) SDF → xyz conversion → check the structure in VESTA

(2) Optimize the geometry with calc_pyscf_relax.py and compare the total energies before and after relaxation

(3) Obtain the density of states with calc_pyscf_dos.py --element-pdos, and state which elements and which orbitals make up the region near the HOMO

(4) Obtain the wave functions with calc_pyscf_wf.py --homo --lumo, visualize them in VESTA, and determine their bonding and antibonding character

Hint: A molecule with many atoms takes a long time, so it is a good idea to start by choosing a molecule with up to about 20 atoms. Benzene $\mathrm{C_6H_6}$ (12 atoms) and acetic acid $\mathrm{CH_3COOH}$ (8 atoms) are convenient choices.

Exercise B.6: Comparing Madelung constants

Among the POSCAR files in pymatgen_tutorial, calculate the Madelung constants for POSCAR_NaCl_Fm-3m (rock-salt type), POSCAR_CsCl_Pm-3m (cesium chloride type), POSCAR_NaCl_F-43m (zinc-blende type) and POSCAR_NaCl_P63mc (wurtzite type), and compare them with the values in the table of Chapter 13 (1.7476, 1.7627, 1.6381, 1.6413).

Hint: Confirm that even for the same composition NaCl, the Madelung constant differs if the structure differs, and that the Madelung constant tends to be larger the larger the coordination number is. However, which structure actually becomes stable is determined not only by the electrostatic energy but also by the ionic radius ratio and the repulsion term.
