Chapter 33Wave Functions of Many-Electron Systems — Atomic Units, the Born–Oppenheimer Approximation, Hartree Products, and Slater Determinants

In Parts III–VI so far, quantum mechanics has worked most beautifully for systems with just one electron, like the hydrogen atom. The Schrödinger equation for one electron can be solved exactly, and its solutions (orbitals) gave the framework of the periodic table (Chapter 15). But when we add one more electron to get the helium atom, the Coulomb repulsion $e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_{12})$ between the electrons means that an exact solution can no longer be written down, and we had to approximate with perturbation theory (Chapter 29) and the variational method (Chapter 30–Chapter 32). This is the same situation as the three-body problem of the Sun, the Earth, and the Moon, which cannot be solved in closed form.

Yet most of the atoms, molecules, and solids from which materials are made have two or more electrons. A cubic centimeter of copper contains about $8.5\times10^{22}$ atoms, and the total number of electrons comes to about $2.5\times10^{24}$. How are we to write the motion of so many particles in the language of quantum mechanics, and how are we to approximate it? That is the subject of Part VII.

As the starting point, this chapter prepares four tools for building the wave function of a many-electron system. The first is atomic units. In the Schrödinger equation of atoms and molecules, $\hbar$, $m_{\mathrm e}$, $e_0$, and $4\pi\varepsilon_0$ appear again and again and make the equations hard to read, so we nondimensionalize the equation and set all of them equal to 1. The second is the Born–Oppenheimer approximation. Molecules and solids contain atomic nuclei as well as electrons, but the nuclei are nearly three orders of magnitude heavier than the electrons (a proton is about 1836 times as heavy as an electron), so to the electrons the nuclei appear to be at rest. We therefore split the problem into two stages: “hold the nuclei fixed and solve the electronic problem first, then use the energy of the electrons as a potential for the nuclei and solve the motion of the nuclei.” The third is the Hartree product, the exact solution when there is no interaction between the electrons, and the fourth is the Slater determinant, which takes into account that electrons are indistinguishable particles. In classical mechanics (AD 03), particles can be told apart by their trajectories, however similar they are. In quantum mechanics that distinction is lost, and the wave function of electrons is subject to the constraint (the Pauli exclusion principle) that “it changes sign when the coordinates of two electrons are interchanged.” The mathematical tool called the determinant (AD 02, Chapter 8) satisfies this constraint exactly.

33.1 The Equation for the Helium Atom and Atomic Units

33.1.1 The Schrödinger equation for the helium atom

As a first example of a many-electron system, we consider the helium atom. We place the atomic nucleus (charge $+2e_0$) at the origin and denote the positions of the two electrons by $\bm{r}_1$ and $\bm{r}_2$ (Figure 33.1). In this book the elementary charge is denoted by $e_0=1.602\,176\,634\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$ (the same quantity as $e$ in AD 03; the charge of the electron is $-e_0$). Because the nucleus is far heavier than the electrons, we treat it here as fixed (it is the Born–Oppenheimer approximation of Section 33.2 that justifies this treatment). Each electron feels the Coulomb attraction of the nucleus, and the electrons feel Coulomb repulsion from each other. Combining the kinetic energy of the electrons, the potential energy of the attraction to the nucleus, and the potential energy of the repulsion between the electrons, the Schrödinger equation becomes:

\begin{align} &-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\left(\nabla_1^2+\nabla_2^2\right)\Psi(\bm{r}_1,\bm{r}_2) -\frac{2e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{\abs{\bm{r}_1}}+\frac{1}{\abs{\bm{r}_2}}\right)\Psi(\bm{r}_1,\bm{r}_2)\notag\\ &\qquad+\frac{(-e_0)^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{\abs{\bm{r}_1-\bm{r}_2}}\,\Psi(\bm{r}_1,\bm{r}_2)=E\,\Psi(\bm{r}_1,\bm{r}_2) \label{eq:33-he} \end{align}

Here $\nabla_i^2=\partial^2/\partial x_i^2+\partial^2/\partial y_i^2+\partial^2/\partial z_i^2$ is the Laplace operator with respect to the coordinates of electron $i$, and $m_{\mathrm e}$ is the mass of the electron. The first term on the left-hand side is the kinetic energy of the two electrons; the second term is the potential energy of the attraction $\dfrac{(2e_0)(-e_0)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_i}=-\dfrac{2e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_i}$ between the nucleus (charge $+2e_0$) and an electron (charge $-e_0$); the third term is the potential energy of the repulsion $\dfrac{(-e_0)(-e_0)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_{12}}$ between the electrons. Here we have neglected the small spin-dependent terms (the spin–orbit interaction, Chapter 26) and have written the wave function as a function of position only, $\Psi(\bm{r}_1,\bm{r}_2)$. Spin will be introduced from Section 33.4 onward.

Discussion: Why is the equation not exactly solvable?

If the third term of Eq. \eqref{eq:33-he} were absent, the Hamiltonian would be the sum of a “part for electron 1 only” and a “part for electron 2 only.” In that case $\Psi$ would split into a product of a function of $\bm{r}_1$ and a function of $\bm{r}_2$ (separation of variables; this is shown in Section 33.4). The third term contains the quantity $\abs{\bm{r}_1-\bm{r}_2}$, which involves the two coordinates at the same time, so the strategy of fixing one of the coordinates and solving for the other is not available. This is why “an exact solution cannot be found because of the electron–electron repulsion.” As the number of electrons increases to three, four, and more, every pair of electrons (for $N$ electrons, $N(N-1)/2$ pairs) becomes coupled, so the situation grows still worse.

33.1.2 Expectation value of the energy for a product wave function

Even so, if we assume that “the two-electron wave function can be written as a product of one-electron functions, $\Psi(\bm{r}_1,\bm{r}_2)=\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\,\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)$,” then the expectation value of the energy can be expressed in terms of one-electron quantities. From the standpoint of the variational principle of Chapter 30, this means that “we have chosen a trial function of product form.” We suppose that $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ are normalized ($\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_1}=\braket{\psi_2}{\psi_2}=1$, that is, $\int\abs{\psi_i}^2\dd\bm{r}=1$). Writing the Hamiltonian as $\hat{H}$, the left-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:33-he} is $\hat{H}\Psi$. The expectation value of the energy is defined as follows ($\dd\bm{r}_i=\dd x_i\dd y_i\dd z_i$):

$$ \mel{\Psi}{\hat{H}}{\Psi}=\iint\psi_1^*(\bm{r}_1)\,\psi_2^*(\bm{r}_2)\,\hat{H}\,\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\,\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2 . $$

Derivation: Energy expectation value for a product wave function

We substitute the three kinds of terms of Eq. \eqref{eq:33-he} and write out the integral term by term ($\psi_i$ means $\psi_i(\bm{r}_i)$):

\begin{align*} \mel{\Psi}{\hat{H}}{\Psi}&=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\iint\psi_1^*\psi_2^*\left(\nabla_1^2+\nabla_2^2\right)\psi_1\psi_2\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2\\ &\quad-\frac{2e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\iint\psi_1^*\psi_2^*\left(\frac{1}{\abs{\bm{r}_1}}+\frac{1}{\abs{\bm{r}_2}}\right)\psi_1\psi_2\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2\\ &\quad+\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\iint\psi_1^*\psi_2^*\frac{1}{\abs{\bm{r}_1-\bm{r}_2}}\psi_1\psi_2\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2 . \end{align*}

In the first term, $\nabla_1^2$ acts only on $\bm{r}_1$, so $\nabla_1^2\bigl[\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)\bigr]=\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)\,\nabla_1^2\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)$, and similarly $\nabla_2^2\bigl[\psi_1\psi_2\bigr]=\psi_1\nabla_2^2\psi_2$. Therefore the integrand is $\psi_1^*\psi_2^*(\nabla_1^2+\nabla_2^2)\psi_1\psi_2=\bigl(\psi_1^*\nabla_1^2\psi_1\bigr)\abs{\psi_2}^2+\abs{\psi_1}^2\bigl(\psi_2^*\nabla_2^2\psi_2\bigr)$, a sum of products of a function of $\bm{r}_1$ only and a function of $\bm{r}_2$ only, and the double integral splits into products of integrals. For example, the former is $\displaystyle\int\psi_1^*\nabla_1^2\psi_1\dd\bm{r}_1\times\int\abs{\psi_2}^2\dd\bm{r}_2$, and by the normalization $\int\abs{\psi_2}^2\dd\bm{r}_2=\braket{\psi_2}{\psi_2}=1$ only the first integral remains. The second term is the same ($1/\abs{\bm{r}_1}$ is a function of $\bm{r}_1$ only). In this way we obtain

\begin{align} \mel{\Psi}{\hat{H}}{\Psi}&=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\left(\int\psi_1^*\nabla_1^2\psi_1\dd\bm{r}_1+\int\psi_2^*\nabla_2^2\psi_2\dd\bm{r}_2\right)\notag\\ &\quad-\frac{2e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\int\psi_1^*\frac{1}{\abs{\bm{r}_1}}\psi_1\dd\bm{r}_1+\int\psi_2^*\frac{1}{\abs{\bm{r}_2}}\psi_2\dd\bm{r}_2\right)\notag\\ &\quad+\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\iint\psi_1^*(\bm{r}_1)\psi_2^*(\bm{r}_2)\frac{1}{\abs{\bm{r}_1-\bm{r}_2}}\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2 \label{eq:33-product-E} \end{align}

in which the first and second lines have separated into integrals containing only electron 1 and integrals containing only electron 2. We therefore define the one-electron expectation value of the energy, combining the kinetic energy of electron $i$ and its attraction to the nucleus, as

$$ E_i\equiv\int\psi_i^*(\bm{r})\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\nabla^2-\frac{2e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\abs{\bm{r}}}\right]\psi_i(\bm{r})\dd\bm{r}\qquad(i=1,2) $$

(the name of the integration variable is arbitrary). The third line contains the coordinates of both electrons and cannot be separated, so we let it be a single quantity $U$. In the end,

$$ \mel{\Psi}{\hat{H}}{\Psi}=E_1+E_2+U $$

is obtained.

∎

Definition 33.1: Electron–electron repulsion energy $U$

When two electrons are in the state given by the product of the one-electron orbitals $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$, we denote the expectation value of the Coulomb repulsion between the electrons,

\begin{equation} U=\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\iint\frac{\abs{\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)}^2\,\abs{\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)}^2}{\abs{\bm{r}_1-\bm{r}_2}}\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2 \label{eq:33-U} \end{equation}

by $U$ (in the third line of Eq. \eqref{eq:33-product-E} we wrote $\psi_1^*\psi_1=\abs{\psi_1}^2$ and $\psi_2^*\psi_2=\abs{\psi_2}^2$). $E_i$ is the expectation value of the one-electron energy (the one-electron integral), which combines the kinetic energy of electron $i$ and its attraction to the nucleus.

The meaning of $U$ is classical. $e_0\abs{\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)}^2\dd\bm{r}_1$ is the charge contained in the volume $\dd\bm{r}_1$ of a “charge cloud,” in which the magnitude of the charge of electron 1 is spread thinly through space. Eq. \eqref{eq:33-U} is nothing other than the electrostatic energy between two charge clouds (the sum of the Coulomb repulsion energies over all pairs of infinitesimal portions). The more portions there are for which the distance $\abs{\bm{r}_1-\bm{r}_2}$ between a portion of one cloud and a portion of the other is small, the larger $U$ is.

Intuition: Collecting the electron–electron repulsion into a single quantity

Eq. \eqref{eq:33-product-E} shows that the energy of two electrons can be written as the sum of “the energies of the individual electrons ($E_1$, $E_2$)” and “the electron–electron repulsion energy $U$.” The difficult two-electron interaction has been gathered into a single number, $U$. If $\psi_i$ is an eigenfunction (a hydrogen-like orbital) of the one-electron Hamiltonian $\hat{h}=-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\nabla^2-\dfrac{2e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$, then $E_i$ becomes its eigenvalue $\varepsilon_i$, $E_1+E_2$ is the “zeroth-order” energy that neglects the electron–electron repulsion, and $U$ is the first-order correction due to the electron–electron repulsion. This has exactly the same form as the first-order perturbation energy obtained when the electron–electron repulsion was treated as a perturbation in Chapter 29, Section 29.3.

Question: Why can the variables be “separated” into a product form?

Is this assumption valid? Strictly speaking, when there is electron–electron repulsion, $\Psi$ does not take the form of a product (the product form is an approximation). The product form is an exact solution only when the Hamiltonian can be written as a sum of terms, one for each electron—that is, only when there is no electron–electron interaction (Section 33.4). Even when there is electron–electron repulsion, if we use the product as a trial function, the expectation value of the energy can be computed as in Eq. \eqref{eq:33-product-E}, and by the variational principle it gives an upper bound on the true energy. The ways to improve the product form further are the Hartree method and the Hartree–Fock method (Chapter 36), and for that purpose we first need to build into the wave function the fact that electrons are indistinguishable (Sections 33.5 and 33.6).

The concrete value of this integral, that is, the calculation of $U$ (for the product of 1s orbitals of the helium atom, $U=5Z/8$ hartrees), was carried out line by line in Chapter 29, Section 29.2. After introducing atomic units in Section 33.1.3 below, we will substitute numerical values into this expression (Example 33.2). Note that the symbol $U$ used here is generalized in Chapter 35 to the Coulomb integral $U_{ab}$ between two spin orbitals $\chi_a$, $\chi_b$.

33.1.3 Nondimensionalizing the equation — atomic units

In Eq. \eqref{eq:33-he}, $\hbar$, $m_{\mathrm e}$, $e_0$, and $4\pi\varepsilon_0$ appear over and over, which makes the equation hard to read. When we produce numerical values, too, we have to handle small numbers such as $10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$ or $10^{-18}\ \mathrm{J}$. The standard device of physics is to choose the units of length and energy to suit the problem and to nondimensionalize the equation. Taking the equation of the helium atom as our subject, let us derive what the natural units turn out to be. In Chapters 30–32 we used atomic units in anticipation (first in Chapter 30, Section 30.5). Here we show where they come from.

Derivation: Nondimensionalization and atomic units

[i] We introduce a unit of length $\ell$ and a unit of energy $E_{\mathrm u}$, without yet fixing their values. We rewrite the length and the energy in terms of dimensionless quantities (denoted with a tilde) obtained by dividing each by its unit:

$$ \bm{r}_i=\ell\,\tilde{\bm{r}}_i,\qquad r_{12}=\ell\,\tilde{r}_{12},\qquad E=E_{\mathrm u}\tilde{E} $$

The derivatives become $\nabla_i^2=\ell^{-2}\tilde{\nabla}_i^2$ ($\tilde{\nabla}_i$ denotes differentiation with respect to $\tilde{\bm{r}}_i$). Substituting into Eq. \eqref{eq:33-he} gives

$$ \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}\ell^2}\left(\tilde{\nabla}_1^2+\tilde{\nabla}_2^2\right)-\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\ell}\left(\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_1}+\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_2}\right)+\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\ell}\frac{1}{\tilde{r}_{12}}\right]\Psi=E_{\mathrm u}\tilde{E}\,\Psi $$

[ii] Among the coefficients inside the brackets there are two combinations that have the dimension of energy: the kinetic-energy scale $\dfrac{\hbar^2}{m_{\mathrm e}\ell^2}$ and the Coulomb-energy scale $\dfrac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\ell}$. (Dimension check: the unit of $\hbar^2/(m_{\mathrm e}\ell^2)$ is $(\mathrm{J\,s})^2/(\mathrm{kg\,m^2})=\mathrm{J}$, and the unit of $e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\ell)$ is $\mathrm{C^2/[(F/m)\cdot m]}=\mathrm{C^2/F}=\mathrm{J}$.) Since the unit of length $\ell$ can be chosen freely, we choose it so that these two are equal:

$$ \frac{\hbar^2}{m_{\mathrm e}\ell^2}=\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\ell}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ell=\frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{m_{\mathrm e}e_0^2}\equiv a_0 . $$

This is none other than the Bohr radius (the extent of the 1s orbital of the hydrogen atom in Chapter 15). Then the two scales take the common value $\dfrac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a_0}=\dfrac{\hbar^2}{m_{\mathrm e}a_0^2}\equiv E_{\mathrm h}$. [iii] Taking the unit of energy to be $E_{\mathrm u}=E_{\mathrm h}$ and dividing both sides by $E_{\mathrm h}$, all the constants disappear, and the equation becomes

\begin{equation} \left[-\frac12\left(\tilde{\nabla}_1^2+\tilde{\nabla}_2^2\right)-\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_1}-\frac{2}{\tilde{r}_2}+\frac{1}{\tilde{r}_{12}}\right]\Psi=\tilde{E}\,\Psi \label{eq:33-he-au} \end{equation}

This is the helium equation in atomic units.

∎

Definition 33.2: Atomic units (Hartree units)

Take the unit of length to be the Bohr radius $a_0$ and the unit of energy to be the Hartree energy $E_{\mathrm h}$:

\begin{equation} a_0=\frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{m_{\mathrm e}e_0^2}=0.529\,177\ \text{Å},\qquad E_{\mathrm h}=\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a_0}=\frac{\hbar^2}{m_{\mathrm e}a_0^2}=\frac{m_{\mathrm e}e_0^4}{(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2\hbar^2}=27.211\,386\ \mathrm{eV} \label{eq:33-au} \end{equation}

The system of units so chosen is called atomic units (a.u.). The same thing can also be expressed by saying that we set $\hbar=m_{\mathrm e}=e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)=1$ (the electron mass is the unit of mass, $e_0$ is the unit of charge, and $\hbar$ is the unit of action). In atomic units, the length written in a formula indicates how many times $a_0$ it is, and the energy indicates how many times $E_{\mathrm h}$ it is. If “$1/r$” is written, it corresponds to $\dfrac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$ in SI.

The Hamiltonian of the helium atom written in atomic units (in general, of a He-like atom or ion with nuclear charge $Z$), with the tildes dropped, reads

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_{\mathrm{He}}=-\frac12\nabla_1^2-\frac12\nabla_2^2-\frac{Z}{r_1}-\frac{Z}{r_2}+\frac{1}{r_{12}}\qquad(Z=2) \label{eq:33-H-au} \end{equation}

Compared with Eq. \eqref{eq:33-he}, the structure is much easier to see. For example, the 1s orbital of the one-electron Hamiltonian $\hat{h}=-\tfrac12\nabla^2-Z/r$ is $\psi_{1s}(r)=\sqrt{Z^3/\pi}\,e^{-Zr}$, its energy is $\varepsilon_{1s}=-Z^2/2$, the expectation value of the kinetic energy is $\expval{T}=Z^2/2$, and the expectation value of the potential energy is $\expval{V}=-Z^2$ (used in Chapters 30 and 32). For the hydrogen atom ($Z=1$) the energy is $-\tfrac12E_{\mathrm h}=-13.606\ \mathrm{eV}$, and we see that $E_{\mathrm h}$ is exactly twice the ionization energy of the hydrogen atom. The name of $E_{\mathrm h}$ comes from Douglas Hartree.

Caution: Differences from other systems of units

(1) The Hartree energy of atomic units differs by a factor of 2 from the Rydberg unit ($1\ \mathrm{Ry}=E_{\mathrm h}/2$). The ground-state energy of the hydrogen atom is $-\tfrac12$ in atomic units and $-1$ in Rydberg units. In Rydberg units we take $\hbar=2m_{\mathrm e}=1$, so the kinetic-energy operator becomes $-\nabla^2$. To tell in which system of units a given reference is written, it helps to check whether the kinetic term of the Hamiltonian is $-\tfrac12\nabla^2$ or $-\nabla^2$. (2) In Gaussian units (used in some of the companion volumes and in some other books), the square of the charge, $e^2$, corresponds to $e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ of SI. If we set $\hbar=m_{\mathrm e}=e=1$ in Gaussian units, we obtain the same numerical values as in atomic units. (3) In the related simulators the Bohr radius is written $a_{\mathrm B}$, but it is the same quantity as $a_0$ in this book. (4) In atomic units the speed of light becomes $c=1/\alpha=137.036$ (used when we treat relativistic effects in Part XI).

A result obtained in atomic units is finally converted to SI by multiplying the units back in. For a length, multiply by $a_0$; for an energy, multiply by $E_{\mathrm h}$ (or use the conversions of Table 33.2). Let us practice with the next two examples.

Example 33.1: Computing atomic units from SI constants

(1) From $\hbar=1.054\,571\,8\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$, $m_{\mathrm e}=9.109\,383\,7\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $e_0=1.602\,176\,6\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$, and $\varepsilon_0=8.854\,187\,8\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m}$, find $a_0$, $E_{\mathrm h}$, and the unit of time $\hbar/E_{\mathrm h}$. (2) How many $a_0$ is the equilibrium internuclear distance $0.7414\ \text{Å}$ of the hydrogen molecule $\mathrm{H_2}$? (3) Express the ground-state energy of the hydrogen atom, $-\tfrac12E_{\mathrm h}$, in $\mathrm{eV}$ and in $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. (4) Express the bond energy $D_{\mathrm e}=0.0648\,E_{\mathrm h}$ of the hydrogen molecular ion $\mathrm{H_2^+}$ (Example 33.5) in $\mathrm{kJ/mol}$.

Solution. (1) We have $4\pi\varepsilon_0=4\pi\times8.854\,187\,8\times10^{-12}=1.112\,650\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{F/m}$, $\hbar^2=1.112\,122\times10^{-68}\ \mathrm{J^2s^2}$, and $e_0^2=2.566\,970\times10^{-38}\ \mathrm{C^2}$.

$$ a_0=\frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{m_{\mathrm e}e_0^2}=\frac{1.112\,650\times10^{-10}\times1.112\,122\times10^{-68}}{9.109\,384\times10^{-31}\times2.566\,970\times10^{-38}}=\frac{1.237\,402\times10^{-78}}{2.338\,351\times10^{-68}}=5.2918\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m} $$ $$ E_{\mathrm h}=\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a_0}=\frac{2.566\,970\times10^{-38}}{1.112\,650\times10^{-10}\times5.291\,77\times10^{-11}}=\frac{2.566\,970\times10^{-38}}{5.887\,89\times10^{-21}}=4.3597\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm{J} $$

Dividing by $1\ \mathrm{eV}=1.602\,177\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$ gives $E_{\mathrm h}=27.211\ \mathrm{eV}$. The unit of time is $\hbar/E_{\mathrm h}=\dfrac{1.054\,572\times10^{-34}}{4.359\,74\times10^{-18}}=2.4189\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm{s}=24.19\ \mathrm{as}$ (attoseconds, $1\ \mathrm{as}=10^{-18}\ \mathrm{s}$). As a check, $E_{\mathrm h}=\hbar^2/(m_{\mathrm e}a_0^2)=1.112\,122\times10^{-68}/(9.109\,384\times10^{-31}\times2.8003\times10^{-21})=4.3597\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm{J}$ agrees with this ($a_0^2=2.8003\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{m^2}$).

(2) $\dfrac{0.7414\ \text{Å}}{0.529\,177\ \text{Å}}=1.401$. The length of the bond in $\mathrm{H_2}$ is about $1.4\,a_0$.

(3) $-\tfrac12\times27.211\,386=-13.6057\ \mathrm{eV}$. In wavenumbers, $-\tfrac12\times219\,474.63=-109\,737.3\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (equal to the Rydberg constant $R_\infty=109\,737.3\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$).

(4) For one mole of $1\,E_{\mathrm h}$ (that is, a number of particles equal to the Avogadro constant $N_{\mathrm A}=6.022\,141\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$), the energy is $4.359\,745\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm{J}\times6.022\,141\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}=2.6255\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{J/mol}=2625.5\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$, so $0.0648\times2625.5\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}=170.1\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$. Going through eV gives the same result: $0.0648\times27.2114\ \mathrm{eV}=1.763\ \mathrm{eV}$, and since $1\ \mathrm{eV}=96.485\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$, $1.763\times96.485=170.1\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$, in agreement.

Example 33.2: Energy of the product wave function of the helium atom (atomic units)

Suppose that the two electrons of the helium atom ($Z=2$) are both in the 1s orbital $\psi(r)=\sqrt{Z^3/\pi}\,e^{-Zr}$ ($Z=2$) of He$^+$, and let $\Psi=\psi(\bm{r}_1)\psi(\bm{r}_2)$. (1) Find $E_1$ and $E_2$. (2) Using $U=\dfrac58Z$, find $\mel{\Psi}{\hat{H}}{\Psi}=E_1+E_2+U$ and convert it to $\mathrm{eV}$. (3) Compare it with the exact nonrelativistic energy $-2.9037\,E_{\mathrm h}$, and, using the energy $-2\,E_{\mathrm h}$ of the 1s electron of He$^+$, find the ionization energy in this approximation.

Solution. (1) The one-electron part of Eq. \eqref{eq:33-H-au} is $\hat{h}=-\tfrac12\nabla^2-2/r$. Since $\psi$ is the hydrogen-like 1s orbital with $Z=2$, we have $\hat{h}\psi=-\tfrac{Z^2}{2}\psi=-2\psi$, and so $E_1=E_2=\int\psi^*\hat{h}\psi\dd\bm{r}=-2$. To check: the kinetic energy is $\expval{T}=Z^2/2=2$, and the attraction to the nucleus is $\expval{-2/r}=-2\times\expval{1/r}=-2\times Z=-4$ ($\expval{1/r}=Z$, Chapter 15), so $E_i=2-4=-2\,E_{\mathrm h}$.

(2) Since $U=\tfrac58\times2=\tfrac54=1.25\,E_{\mathrm h}$,

$$ \mel{\Psi}{\hat{H}}{\Psi}=-2-2+1.25=-2.75\,E_{\mathrm h}=-2.75\times27.2114\ \mathrm{eV}=-74.83\ \mathrm{eV}. $$

(3) The exact value is $-2.9037\,E_{\mathrm h}=-79.01\ \mathrm{eV}$, and the error of the approximation is $0.154\,E_{\mathrm h}=4.18\ \mathrm{eV}$ (about $5\ \%$). The expectation value of the product form is higher than the true ground-state energy (variational principle). Since the energy of the 1s electron of He$^+$ is $-2\,E_{\mathrm h}$, the ionization energy is $-2-(-2.75)=0.75\,E_{\mathrm h}=20.4\ \mathrm{eV}$. The measured value is $24.59\ \mathrm{eV}$, so the product approximation falls short by about $4\ \mathrm{eV}$. This is because it neglects the effect of the electrons avoiding one another (electron correlation) and the shielding of the nuclear charge by the other electron (the extent of the orbital). The effect of this shielding can be incorporated by taking the exponent $Z$ as a variational parameter, which improves the energy to $-2.8477\,E_{\mathrm h}=-77.49\ \mathrm{eV}$ (Chapter 31).

33.2 The Hamiltonian of a Polyatomic System and the Born–Oppenheimer Approximation

In Section 33.1 we fixed the atomic nucleus at the origin. In solids and molecules, the nuclei move as well. How far can we simplify the equation for a system containing many nuclei and many electrons? In this section we write down the Hamiltonian of a general system and derive the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, which separates the nuclei from the electrons. Thanks to this approximation, a two-step procedure is allowed: first we compute the electron system self-consistently (SCF: a method in which the field produced by the other electrons and felt by a given electron is redetermined from that electron's own solution, and this operation is repeated until the solution no longer changes; Chapter 36 and Chapter 37), and then we move the atomic nuclei. After following the separation of the electronic and nuclear equations line by line, we make clear that this separation is an approximation and where the approximation enters.

33.2.1 The Hamiltonian for $M$ nuclei and $N$ electrons

Suppose that the system of interest contains $M$ atomic nuclei and $N$ electrons. We denote the position, mass, and charge of nucleus $I$ ($I=1,\dots,M$) by $\bm{R}_I$, $M_I$, and $Z_Ie_0$ ($Z_I$ is the atomic number), and the position and mass of electron $i$ ($i=1,\dots,N$) by $\bm{r}_i$ and $m_{\mathrm e}$. We abbreviate the set of all nuclear coordinates as $\bm{R}=\{\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\dots,\bm{R}_M\}$ and the set of all electron coordinates as $\bm{r}=\{\bm{r}_1,\bm{r}_2,\dots,\bm{r}_N\}$. The Hamiltonian of the system is the sum of five terms: the kinetic energy of the nuclei, the kinetic energy of the electrons, the nucleus–electron attraction, the nucleus–nucleus repulsion, and the electron–electron repulsion (Figure 33.2):

\begin{align} \hat{H}&=\sum_{I=1}^{M}\frac{-\hbar^2}{2M_I}\nabla_I^2+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{-\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\nabla_i^2-\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{I=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{Z_Ie_0^2}{\abs{\bm{R}_I-\bm{r}_i}}\notag\\ &\quad+\frac12\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{I=1}^{M}\sum_{J=1}^{M}\frac{Z_IZ_Je_0^2}{\abs{\bm{R}_I-\bm{R}_J}}+\frac12\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{e_0^2}{\abs{\bm{r}_i-\bm{r}_j}} \label{eq:33-H-general} \end{align}

Note, however, that the last two sums include only the terms with $I\neq J$ and $i\neq j$ (the self-interaction of a particle with itself is not included). $\nabla_I^2$ is the Laplace operator with respect to the nuclear coordinate $\bm{R}_I$. The repulsion of a pair of particles $\{I,J\}$ appears twice in the sum, as $(I,J)$ and $(J,I)$, so we attach the factor $\tfrac12$ to count it once. This is the same as counting only the pairs with $J>I$, once each, and the last two terms can be expressed as

\begin{equation} \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\sum_{I=1}^{M}\sum_{J\gt I}^{M}\frac{Z_IZ_Je_0^2}{\abs{\bm{R}_I-\bm{R}_J}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\gt i}^{N}\frac{e_0^2}{\abs{\bm{r}_i-\bm{r}_j}}\right) \label{eq:33-pairs} \end{equation}

(there are $M(M-1)/2$ pairs of nuclei and $N(N-1)/2$ pairs of electrons). Taking water as an example, $M=3$ (one O atom and two H atoms) and $N=10$, and the electron pairs alone number $45$. In a solid, $N$ is of the order of $10^{23}$. Also, the Hamiltonian contains no spin. If we write the spin variables of the electrons $\sigma_i=\pm\tfrac12$ (Chapter 23, Section 23.5) collectively as $\sigma=\{\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_N\}$, the wave function is a function of $\bm{r}$, $\sigma$, and $\bm{R}$, as in $\Psi(\bm{r},\sigma,\bm{R})$.

Written in atomic units (Section 33.1.3), Eq. \eqref{eq:33-H-general} simplifies as follows. The nuclear mass $M_I$ is expressed as a value in units of the electron mass $m_{\mathrm e}$ ($1836.15$ for a proton, about $2.2\times10^4$ for a carbon nucleus):

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=-\sum_{I=1}^{M}\frac{1}{2M_I}\nabla_I^2-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac12\nabla_i^2-\sum_{I=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{Z_I}{\abs{\bm{R}_I-\bm{r}_i}}+\sum_{I=1}^{M}\sum_{J\gt I}^{M}\frac{Z_IZ_J}{\abs{\bm{R}_I-\bm{R}_J}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\gt i}^{N}\frac{1}{\abs{\bm{r}_i-\bm{r}_j}} \label{eq:33-H-general-au} \end{equation}

33.2.2 Taking the total wave function to be a product — the electronic equation and the nuclear equation

Note that in what follows (Sections 33.2–33.3), we write the electronic wave functions of the many-electron system as $\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}$ and $\Psi_n$, and the nuclear wave function as $\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}$ (or $F_n$), with capital letters. These are distinct from the one-electron orbitals $\psi_i$ (lowercase) of Section 33.4 onward.

Let us classify the five terms by the variables on which they act. The nuclear kinetic energy operator $\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}$ (the first term) acts only on the nuclear coordinates $\bm{R}$, and the electronic kinetic energy operator $\hat{T}_{\mathrm{ele}}$ (the second term) acts only on the electron coordinates $\bm{r}$. The remaining three terms are potential energies (they merely multiply by the coordinates): the nucleus–nucleus term $V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})$ is a function of $\bm{R}$ only, the electron–electron term $V_{\mathrm{ee}}(\bm{r})$ is a function of $\bm{r}$ only, and the nucleus–electron term $V_{\mathrm{ne}}(\bm{r},\bm{R})$ is a function of both $\bm{r}$ and $\bm{R}$. That is, we can write

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}+\hat{T}_{\mathrm{ele}}+V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})+V_{\mathrm{ee}}(\bm{r})+V_{\mathrm{ne}}(\bm{r},\bm{R}) \label{eq:33-H-split} \end{equation}

Let us therefore try writing the total wave function of the system as the product of the electronic wave function $\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)$ and the nuclear wave function $\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R})$:

\begin{equation} \Psi(\bm{r},\sigma,\bm{R})=\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)\,\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R}) \label{eq:33-prod-total} \end{equation}

(Here we proceed on the assumption that $\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}$ on the right-hand side does not depend on $\bm{R}$; we will revisit this point in Section 33.2.3.) We apply $\hat{H}$ to the product. $\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}$ acts only on $\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}$, $\hat{T}_{\mathrm{ele}}$ acts only on $\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}$, and the potentials simply multiply, so the left-hand side of the Schrödinger equation $\hat{H}\Psi=E_{\mathrm{total}}\Psi$ separates into terms involving only the nuclei and terms involving the electrons:

\begin{align} \hat{H}\,\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)\,\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R})&=\Bigl[\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R})+V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R})\Bigr]\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)\notag\\ &\quad+\Bigl[\hat{T}_{\mathrm{ele}}\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)+V_{\mathrm{ee}}(\bm{r})\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)+V_{\mathrm{ne}}(\bm{r},\bm{R})\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)\Bigr]\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R})\notag\\ &=E_{\mathrm{total}}\,\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)\,\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R}) \label{eq:33-prod-schr} \end{align}

Dividing both sides by $\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)\,\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R})$ (the same functions in the numerator and denominator cancel), we obtain

\begin{equation} \underbrace{\frac{\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R})}{\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R})}+V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})}_{\bm{R}\text{-only quantity }\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nuc}}} +\underbrace{\frac{\hat{T}_{\mathrm{ele}}\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)}{\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)}+V_{\mathrm{ee}}(\bm{r})+V_{\mathrm{ne}}(\bm{r},\bm{R})}_{\bm{r}\text{ and }\bm{R}\text{ appear, but it is actually an }\bm{R}\text{-only quantity }\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})}=E_{\mathrm{total}} \label{eq:33-divided} \end{equation}

Here $\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}=-\sum_I\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2M_I}\nabla_I^2$ and $\hat{T}_{\mathrm{ele}}=-\sum_i\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\nabla_i^2$.

Why does this become two equations?

The first bracket of Eq. \eqref{eq:33-divided} is a function of the nuclear coordinates $\bm{R}$ only. We denote it by $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nuc}}$. The second bracket (denoted $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}$) looks, on the surface, like a function of the electron coordinates $\bm{r}$, but through the nucleus–electron attraction $V_{\mathrm{ne}}(\bm{r},\bm{R})$ it also depends on $\bm{R}$. Since the sum is the constant $E_{\mathrm{total}}$, the value of the second bracket must not change even if we hold the nuclear positions $\bm{R}$ fixed and move only the electron coordinates $\bm{r}$. That is, the second bracket must be a “value determined by $\bm{R}$ alone, independent of $\bm{r}$,” namely $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$. This means that the equation obtained by multiplying the second bracket by $\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}$ is the Schrödinger equation of the electrons with the nuclear positions $\bm{R}$ fixed as parameters (with eigenvalue $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$).

Thus we obtain the following simultaneous differential equations for the electronic wave function and the nuclear wave function:

\begin{equation} \left\{ \begin{aligned} &-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\nabla_i^2\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)+\Bigl[V_{\mathrm{ee}}(\bm{r})+V_{\mathrm{ne}}(\bm{r},\bm{R})\Bigr]\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})\,\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma)\\ &-\sum_{I=1}^{M}\frac{\hbar^2}{2M_I}\nabla_I^2\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R})+V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})\,\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R})=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nuc}}\,\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R}) \end{aligned} \right. \label{eq:33-BO-system} \end{equation}

The first equation is the electronic equation, and the second is the nuclear equation. Eq. \eqref{eq:33-divided} means $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nuc}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})=E_{\mathrm{total}}$. Therefore $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nuc}}=E_{\mathrm{total}}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$, and the right-hand side of the nuclear equation is not a constant but a quantity that depends on $\bm{R}$. If we move $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nuc}}\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}$ in the nuclear equation to the left-hand side and include $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$ again, we obtain Eq. \eqref{eq:33-nuc-eq}. This is the subject of Section 33.3.

Caution: Where does the approximation enter this derivation?

The approximations are the assumption, in the product of Eq. \eqref{eq:33-prod-total}, that $\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}$ is independent of $\bm{R}$, and the assumption that $\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}$ does not act on $\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}$. The actual electronic wave function is a solution of the first equation and depends on the nuclear positions $\bm{R}$ (if the nuclei move, the electron cloud moves too). In that case $\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}$ also acts on $\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{r},\sigma;\bm{R})$, and extra terms (the nonadiabatic coupling) appear. That is, since $V_{\mathrm{ne}}(\bm{r},\bm{R})$ depends on $\bm{R}$, the electronic wave function strictly speaking also depends on $\bm{R}$, and the assumption that “$\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}$ is independent of $\bm{R}$” holds only when we consider one particular $\bm{R}$ or when the change in $\bm{R}$ is small. These terms are smaller the smaller the mass ratio of the electron to the nucleus (the diagonal correction $\Lambda_{nn}$ is proportional to $m_{\mathrm e}/M_I$, and the off-diagonal coupling that connects different electronic states is of order $\kappa^3=(m_{\mathrm e}/M_I)^{3/4}$; $\kappa$ is introduced in Section 33.2.4), and the Born–Oppenheimer approximation drops them. Section 33.2.3, next, reexamines this assumption and derives the “exact expansion,” which makes explicit the terms to be dropped.

33.2.3 Advanced Topic: The exact expansion — making explicit the terms dropped by the approximation

This subsection is an advanced topic. On a first reading, you may read only the conclusions (the exact equation, Eq. \eqref{eq:33-exact-nuc}; the nuclear equation of the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, Eq. \eqref{eq:33-nuc-eq}; and the fact that the dropped terms are small in proportion to the mass ratio $m_{\mathrm e}/M$) and return to the derivation later.

Let us reconsider the problem correctly, without using the product assumption, Eq. \eqref{eq:33-prod-total}. For the time being, we fix the nuclear positions $\bm{R}$ as constants (parameters). Then the nucleus–nucleus repulsion $V_{\mathrm{nn}}$ becomes a constant, and the Hamiltonian describing the motion of the remaining electrons can be put together as follows.

Definition 33.3: Electronic Hamiltonian and electronic states

The Hamiltonian of the electron system when the nuclear positions $\bm{R}$ are regarded as parameters

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})=\hat{T}_{\mathrm{ele}}+V_{\mathrm{ee}}(\bm{r})+V_{\mathrm{ne}}(\bm{r},\bm{R}) \label{eq:33-Hele} \end{equation}

is called the electronic Hamiltonian, and the solutions of its eigenvalue problem

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})\,\Psi_n(\bm{r},\sigma;\bm{R})=\varepsilon_n(\bm{R})\,\Psi_n(\bm{r},\sigma;\bm{R})\qquad(n=0,1,2,\dots) \label{eq:33-ele-eig} \end{equation}

are called the electronic states. The case $n=0$ is the electronic ground state, and its energy $\varepsilon_0(\bm{R})$ is the $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$ of Section 33.2.2. For each fixed $\bm{R}$, the eigenfunctions $\Psi_n$ are orthonormal as functions of $\bm{r}$ and $\sigma$: $\displaystyle\sum_\sigma\int\Psi_m^*\Psi_n\dd\bm{r}=\delta_{mn}$. Since the eigenfunctions also depend on $\bm{R}$ as a parameter, we have written $\bm{R}$ after the semicolon.

At each $\bm{R}$ the set $\{\Psi_n\}$ forms a complete set of functions of the electron coordinates, so the total wave function can be written, without loss of generality, as an expansion whose coefficients are functions of $\bm{R}$

\begin{equation} \Psi(\bm{r},\sigma,\bm{R})=\sum_n F_n(\bm{R})\,\Psi_n(\bm{r},\sigma;\bm{R}) \label{eq:33-BH-expansion} \end{equation}

(the Born–Huang expansion). $F_n(\bm{R})$ is the nuclear wave function when the electrons are in the $n$th state (since this book uses the symbol $\chi_i$ for spin orbitals, we use $F_n$ for the nuclear wave function). Eq. \eqref{eq:33-prod-total} corresponds to the case in which only the single term $n=0$ is kept and the $\bm{R}$ dependence of $\Psi_0$ is ignored.

Derivation: Simultaneous equations for the nuclear wave function

We apply $\hat{H}=\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}+V_{\mathrm{nn}}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}$ to the expansion \eqref{eq:33-BH-expansion}. The electronic part is simply $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}\Psi_n=\varepsilon_n(\bm{R})\Psi_n$ by Eq. \eqref{eq:33-ele-eig}. The nuclear kinetic energy involves differentiation with respect to $\bm{R}_I$, so it also acts on $\Psi_n(\bm{r},\sigma;\bm{R})$. By the product rule, we have

$$ \nabla_I^2\bigl(F_n\Psi_n\bigr)=\bigl(\nabla_I^2F_n\bigr)\Psi_n+2\bigl(\nabla_IF_n\bigr)\cdot\bigl(\nabla_I\Psi_n\bigr)+F_n\nabla_I^2\Psi_n $$

Multiply both sides of $\hat{H}\Psi=E\Psi$ by $\Psi_m^*(\bm{r},\sigma;\bm{R})$, integrate over $\bm{r}$, and sum over $\sigma$ (keeping $\bm{R}$ fixed). By the orthonormality $\sum_\sigma\int\Psi_m^*\Psi_n\dd\bm{r}=\delta_{mn}$, among the terms in which no derivative acts on $\Psi_n$ ($\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}F_n$, $V_{\mathrm{nn}}F_n$, $\varepsilon_nF_n$) only the $n=m$ term survives, so we obtain

\begin{equation} \Bigl[\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}+V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})+\varepsilon_m(\bm{R})\Bigr]F_m+\sum_n\Lambda_{mn}F_n=E\,F_m \label{eq:33-exact-nuc} \end{equation}

Here we have collected the terms produced by the derivative also acting on $\Psi_n$ as follows:

$$ \Lambda_{mn}F_n\equiv-\sum_{I=1}^{M}\frac{\hbar^2}{2M_I}\Bigl[2\,\bm{d}^{I}_{mn}\cdot\nabla_IF_n+g^{I}_{mn}F_n\Bigr],\qquad \bm{d}^{I}_{mn}=\sum_\sigma\!\int\Psi_m^*\nabla_I\Psi_n\dd\bm{r},\quad g^{I}_{mn}=\sum_\sigma\!\int\Psi_m^*\nabla_I^2\Psi_n\dd\bm{r} $$

Eq. \eqref{eq:33-exact-nuc} holds for all $m=0,1,2,\dots$, and $\Lambda_{mn}$ connects different electronic states $m\neq n$. This is the nonadiabatic coupling. Eq. \eqref{eq:33-exact-nuc} is an exact equation that contains no approximation.

Properties of $\Lambda_{mn}$. If the electronic wave function is real, differentiating the normalization condition $\sum_\sigma\int\Psi_n^2\dd\bm{r}=1$ with respect to $\bm{R}_I$ gives $2\bm{d}^I_{nn}=0$, that is, $\bm{d}^I_{nn}=\bm{0}$. Differentiating once more gives $\sum_\sigma\int\bigl[\abs{\nabla_I\Psi_n}^2+\Psi_n\nabla_I^2\Psi_n\bigr]\dd\bm{r}=0$, so $g^I_{nn}=-\sum_\sigma\int\abs{\nabla_I\Psi_n}^2\dd\bm{r}\le0$. Hence the diagonal element is

$$ \Lambda_{nn}=\sum_{I}\frac{\hbar^2}{2M_I}\sum_\sigma\!\int\abs{\nabla_I\Psi_n}^2\dd\bm{r}\ \ge0 $$

which is a positive quantity resembling the nuclear kinetic energy. The quantity $\bm{d}^I_{mn}$ with $m\neq n$, which connects different states, follows when we differentiate Eq. \eqref{eq:33-ele-eig} with respect to $\bm{R}_I$ and take the integral with $\Psi_m^*$: from the $\Psi_m$ component of $\bigl(\nabla_I\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}\bigr)\Psi_n+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}\nabla_I\Psi_n=(\nabla_I\varepsilon_n)\Psi_n+\varepsilon_n\nabla_I\Psi_n$ (by the Hermiticity of $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}$, $\braket{\Psi_m}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}\nabla_I\Psi_n}=\varepsilon_m\bm{d}^I_{mn}$) we obtain

$$ \bm{d}^{I}_{mn}=\frac{\mel{\Psi_m}{\nabla_I\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}}{\Psi_n}}{\varepsilon_n-\varepsilon_m}\qquad(m\neq n) $$

($\nabla_I\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}=\nabla_IV_{\mathrm{ne}}$ is the operator for the force that the electrons feel when nucleus $I$ is moved.) The coupling is larger the smaller the spacing $\varepsilon_n-\varepsilon_m$ between the electronic levels.

∎

From this exact equation, we can distinguish two stages of approximation.

  1. Dropping the nonadiabatic coupling (adiabatic approximation): drop $\Lambda_{mn}$ ($m\neq n$), which connects different electronic states. Then Eq. \eqref{eq:33-exact-nuc} becomes an independent equation for each electronic state $n$, namely $\bigl[\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}+V_{\mathrm{nn}}+\varepsilon_n(\bm{R})+\Lambda_{nn}(\bm{R})\bigr]F_n=EF_n$. The nuclei move only on the potential energy surface of electronic state $n$ (Section 33.3).
  2. Also dropping the diagonal correction $\Lambda_{nn}$ (Born–Oppenheimer approximation): the nuclear equation becomes
\begin{equation} \Bigl[\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}+V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})+\varepsilon_n(\bm{R})\Bigr]F_n(\bm{R})=E\,F_n(\bm{R}) \label{eq:33-nuc-eq} \end{equation}

The case $n=0$ (the ground state) is the nuclear equation $\bigl[\hat{T}_{\mathrm{nuc}}+V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})\bigr]\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R})=E_{\mathrm{total}}\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R})$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:33-nuc-pes} in Section 33.3.1. The dropped $\Lambda_{nn}$ is called the “adiabatic correction” or the diagonal Born–Oppenheimer correction, and its magnitude is proportional to the mass ratio $m_{\mathrm e}/M_I$. It can be estimated as $\hbar^2/(2M_I)\times\bigl(\text{rate of change of the electron orbital}\bigr)^2\sim(m_{\mathrm e}/M_I)\times\hbar^2/(2m_{\mathrm e}a_0^2)$, so it is of the order of $(m_{\mathrm e}/M_I)E_{\mathrm h}$; for example, in the total energy of $\mathrm{H_2}$ it is about $1\times10^2\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. However, the two dissociated H atoms also have a correction of the same kind (about $60\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ per atom), so the two corrections nearly cancel each other, and the net effect that appears in the bond energy (about $3.8\times10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$) remains only about $5\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (about 0.01%).

33.2.4 Why is this allowed? — the mass ratio and the scales of energy and time

What justifies the approximation is that the nuclei are much heavier than the electrons. The mass of the proton is $m_{\mathrm p}/m_{\mathrm e}=1836.15$ times the mass of the electron, and the ratio is larger still for elements with heavier nuclei.

Example 33.3: Speeds of an electron and a proton with the same energy

Find the speeds of an electron and a proton that both have kinetic energy $1\ \mathrm{eV}$, and explain their ratio in terms of the mass ratio.

Solution. From the kinetic energy $K=\tfrac12mv^2$, we have $v=\sqrt{2K/m}$. With $K=1\ \mathrm{eV}=1.602\,177\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$, for the electron

$$ v_{\mathrm e}=\sqrt{\frac{2\times1.602\,177\times10^{-19}}{9.109\,384\times10^{-31}}}=5.93\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{m/s}, $$

and for the proton $v_{\mathrm p}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2\times1.602\,177\times10^{-19}}{1.672\,622\times10^{-27}}}=1.38\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{m/s}$. The ratio is

$$ \frac{v_{\mathrm e}}{v_{\mathrm p}}=\sqrt{\frac{m_{\mathrm p}}{m_{\mathrm e}}}=\sqrt{1836.15}=42.85\approx42.9 $$

(since $K$ is common, $v\propto m^{-1/2}$). At the same energy, the electron is about 43 times faster than the proton. Therefore, from the electron's point of view, the nuclei appear almost at rest. Conversely, from the nuclei's point of view, the electrons move around the nuclei instantaneously and behave as an average cloud determined by each position of the nuclei.

Let us compare the scales of energy and time a little more quantitatively. The energy of the electronic motion is of order $E_{\mathrm h}$. When the nuclei vibrate in the potential valley created by the electrons (spring constant $k\sim E_{\mathrm h}/a_0^2$), the quantum of this vibration is

$$ \hbar\omega_{\mathrm{vib}}=\hbar\sqrt{\frac{k}{M}}\sim\hbar\sqrt{\frac{E_{\mathrm h}}{Ma_0^2}}=\hbar\sqrt{\frac{\hbar^2/(m_{\mathrm e}a_0^2)}{Ma_0^2}}=\frac{\hbar^2}{a_0^2\sqrt{m_{\mathrm e}M}}=\sqrt{\frac{m_{\mathrm e}}{M}}\,E_{\mathrm h} $$

(we used $\hbar^2/(m_{\mathrm e}a_0^2)=E_{\mathrm h}$). The energy of the nuclear rotation (the whole molecule rotating) is of order $\hbar^2/(2Ma_0^2)\sim(m_{\mathrm e}/M)E_{\mathrm h}/2$. Born and Oppenheimer expanded in the small parameter $\kappa=(m_{\mathrm e}/M)^{1/4}$ built from the mass ratio and showed that the electronic energy, the vibration, and the rotation form a hierarchy of orders $\kappa^0$, $\kappa^2$, and $\kappa^4$, respectively:

\begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{ele}}:E_{\mathrm{vib}}:E_{\mathrm{rot}}\sim1:\sqrt{\frac{m_{\mathrm e}}{M}}:\frac{m_{\mathrm e}}{M} \label{eq:33-hierarchy} \end{equation}

This is the hierarchy of the three energies.

Example 33.4: Ratio of the time scales of the $\mathrm{H_2}$ vibration and the electronic motion

Find the period of the vibration of $\mathrm{H_2}$ (the wavenumber in the harmonic approximation is $\tilde{\nu}=\omega_{\mathrm e}=4401\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$; the fundamental vibration actually observed is lower because of anharmonicity, about $4161\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ for $\mathrm{H_2}$), and compare it with the period $2\pi\hbar/E_{\mathrm h}$ of the 1s electron of the hydrogen atom. Also express the vibrational energy $\hbar\omega$ and the rotational constant $B=60.85\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ as multiples of $E_{\mathrm h}$, and compare them with Eq. \eqref{eq:33-hierarchy}.

Solution. The wavenumber $\tilde{\nu}$ is the number of waves per 1 cm, so the frequency is $\nu=c\tilde{\nu}$ and the period is $T=1/\nu=1/(c\tilde{\nu})$ ($c=2.997\,925\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm/s}$):

$$ T_{\mathrm{vib}}=\frac{1}{2.997\,925\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm/s}\times4401\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}}=7.58\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{s}=7.58\ \mathrm{fs}. $$

The period of the 1s electron is $2\pi\hbar/E_{\mathrm h}=2\pi\times24.19\ \mathrm{as}=152\ \mathrm{as}=0.152\ \mathrm{fs}$ ($1\ \mathrm{as}=10^{-3}\ \mathrm{fs}$). The ratio is $7.58/0.152=49.9\approx50$, so the nuclei of $\mathrm{H_2}$ vibrate only once while an electron goes around about 50 times. In terms of energy, since $E_{\mathrm h}=219\,474.6\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, we have $\hbar\omega=hc\tilde{\nu}=4401/219\,474.6\,E_{\mathrm h}=0.0201\,E_{\mathrm h}$, and the rotational constant is $60.85/219\,474.6=2.77\times10^{-4}\,E_{\mathrm h}$. These agree well in order of magnitude with the estimates of Eq. \eqref{eq:33-hierarchy}, $\sqrt{m_{\mathrm e}/m_{\mathrm p}}=0.0233$ and $m_{\mathrm e}/m_{\mathrm p}=5.4\times10^{-4}$ (in $\mathrm{H_2}$ there are two nuclei and the reduced mass is $m_{\mathrm p}/2$, and the stiffness of the bond is somewhat smaller than $E_{\mathrm h}/a_0^2$, so the coefficient does not come out as 1). The vibrational energy is larger than the rotational energy by a factor of $4401/60.85=72$.

Let us check the size of the error of the Born–Oppenheimer approximation with the simplest example. The hydrogen atom is a system of one nucleus and one electron, and the ground state in the approximation in which the nucleus is held at rest (that is, has infinite mass) is $-13.6057\ \mathrm{eV}$. If the motion of the nucleus is correctly taken into account through the reduced mass, the mass of the electron changes from $m_{\mathrm e}$ to $\mu=m_{\mathrm e}m_{\mathrm p}/(m_{\mathrm e}+m_{\mathrm p})=m_{\mathrm e}/(1+m_{\mathrm e}/m_{\mathrm p})$, and the energy becomes $\mu/m_{\mathrm e}$ times as large, $-13.5983\ \mathrm{eV}$. The difference is $0.0074\ \mathrm{eV}$, and the relative difference is almost equal to $m_{\mathrm e}/m_{\mathrm p}=5.4\times10^{-4}$ (0.054%). This smallness is the reason the Born–Oppenheimer approximation can be used as the starting point of quantitative calculations.

33.3 The Adiabatic Approximation and the Potential Energy Surface

33.3.1 The electronic energy becomes a potential for the nuclei

Let us look again at Eq. \eqref{eq:33-nuc-eq}. If we solve the electronic equation (the first equation of Eq. \eqref{eq:33-BO-system}, or $n=0$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:33-ele-eig}) with the nuclear positions $\bm{R}$ held fixed, we obtain the (ground-state) energy $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$ of the electrons. In an actual calculation, we give a trial function for the electronic wave function and, each time $\bm{R}$ is fixed, minimize the expectation value of the energy with respect to the parameters of the trial function (the variational principle of Chapter 30):

\begin{equation} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})=\min_{\text{trial-function parameters}}\ \mel{\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})}{\Psi_{\mathrm{ele}}} \label{eq:33-variational-eps} \end{equation}

(If the trial function is good enough, the value approaches the exact one; otherwise it gives an upper bound.) In this way the value of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$ is fixed for each $\bm{R}$. Substituting it into the nuclear equation, Eq. \eqref{eq:33-nuc-eq}, we find that the equation that determines the motion of the nuclei is

\begin{equation} \left[-\sum_{I=1}^{M}\frac{\hbar^2}{2M_I}\nabla_I^2+\underbrace{V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})}_{\text{potential for the nuclei}}\right]\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R})=E_{\mathrm{total}}\,\Psi_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{R}) \label{eq:33-nuc-pes} \end{equation}

The nuclei are treated as particles moving in the “potential” created by the electrons. The Coulomb repulsion $V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})$ between the nuclei, plus the electronic energy $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$, is the potential energy for the nuclei.

Definition 33.4: Potential energy surface (PES)

The function of the nuclear coordinates $\bm{R}$

\begin{equation} V_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{R})=V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R}) \label{eq:33-Veff} \end{equation}

is called the potential energy surface (PES) of the electronic ground state. For a diatomic molecule, $\bm{R}$ is the single quantity $R$, the internuclear distance, and the PES is a curve. For a nonlinear molecule consisting of $N_{\mathrm{at}}$ atoms, the PES is a function of the $3N_{\mathrm{at}}-6$ coordinates that remain after removing translation and rotation ($3N_{\mathrm{at}}-5$ coordinates for a linear molecule). A function of more than two coordinates corresponds not to a curve but to a “surface” (a high-dimensional surface), and this is how the name arose.

As an example, let us look at the PES of the hydrogen molecular ion $\mathrm{H_2^+}$ (two protons and one electron). Because there is only one electron, there is no electron–electron repulsion $V_{\mathrm{ee}}$, and this is the molecule for which the calculation of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(R)$ is the simplest. We choose as the trial function the sum of 1s orbitals centered on the two protons A and B, $\psi=\bigl[1s_{\mathrm A}+1s_{\mathrm B}\bigr]/\sqrt{2(1+S)}$ (the LCAO approximation; it is derived in Chapter 39). Computing the expectation value for each $R$, we obtain, in atomic units,

\begin{equation} \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(R)=-\frac12-\frac{u(R)+w(R)}{1+S(R)},\qquad V_{\mathrm{nn}}(R)=\frac{1}{R} \label{eq:33-LCAO-eps} \end{equation}

Here, $S(R)=e^{-R}\bigl(1+R+\tfrac{R^2}{3}\bigr)$ is the overlap integral of the two 1s orbitals; $u(R)=\dfrac1R-e^{-2R}\Bigl(1+\dfrac1R\Bigr)$ is the magnitude of the attraction that the electron in $1s_{\mathrm A}$ feels from the other proton B ($\braket{1s_{\mathrm A}}{\tfrac{1}{r_{\mathrm B}}\,1s_{\mathrm A}}$); and $w(R)=e^{-R}(1+R)$ is the magnitude of the attraction that the overlap region of the two orbitals feels from proton A ($\braket{1s_{\mathrm A}}{\tfrac{1}{r_{\mathrm A}}\,1s_{\mathrm B}}$) ($R$ is in units of $a_0$). Figure 33.4 shows $V_{\mathrm{nn}}$, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}$, and their sum $V_{\mathrm{eff}}$.

The figure shows that the two large quantities $V_{\mathrm{nn}}$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}$ almost cancel each other. Because the electron gathers between the two protons and “shields” the repulsion between the protons, the sum $V_{\mathrm{eff}}$ has a valley. This is one picture of the origin of the chemical bond (treated in detail in Chapters 38–40). The shape of this valley is accurately represented by the Morse potential $D_{\mathrm e}\bigl[1-e^{-a(R-R_{\mathrm e})}\bigr]^2$ studied in Chapter 20, and through the parameter $a$ it can also express the deviation from the harmonic approximation (the anharmonicity). The bottom of the valley lies at the equilibrium internuclear distance $R_{\mathrm e}$ of the bond, and the depth of the valley (measured from the dissociation limit) is the bond energy $D_{\mathrm e}$.

33.3.2 The meaning of the Born–Oppenheimer approximation and the adiabatic approximation

Definition 33.5: Born–Oppenheimer approximation and adiabatic approximation

(1) Born–Oppenheimer approximation: Since the nuclei are much heavier than the electrons, we treat the nuclei as stationary when we set up the electronic equation. That is, in the electronic equation the nuclear positions $\bm{R}$ are held fixed as parameters, the nucleus–nucleus repulsion $V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})$ is also treated as a constant, and the electronic energy $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$ is found as a function of $\bm{R}$. The nuclear kinetic energy is set to $0$ only when we set up the electronic equation; the motion of the nuclei is treated quantum-mechanically by the nuclear equation, Eq. \eqref{eq:33-nuc-eq}, which has $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$ as its potential. In this approximation, in addition to the nonadiabatic coupling $\Lambda_{mn}$ ($m\neq n$), the diagonal correction $\Lambda_{nn}$ is also dropped.

(2) Adiabatic approximation: As the derivation above shows, we assume that the atomic nuclei (ions) move only on the surface of the potential energy $V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$ of the electronic ground state (the surface of its minimum value, that is, the surface when the electrons have settled to the lowest energy). As the nuclei move slowly, the electrons change their form accordingly while always remaining in the ground state (they are not excited to other electronic states). This amounts to dropping the nonadiabatic coupling $\Lambda_{mn}$ ($m\neq n$).

(1) is the procedure that leads to Eq. \eqref{eq:33-ele-eig}, and (2) is the assumption that fixes the stage on which the nuclei move in Eq. \eqref{eq:33-nuc-pes}. The word “adiabatic” in (2) has the same etymology as “adiabatic change” in thermodynamics, and means that “the parameters (here, the nuclear positions) change slowly while the quantum numbers of the system (here, the electronic state) do not change” (the adiabatic theorem; Chapter 51). Note also that under the Born–Oppenheimer approximation the total energy is expressed as $E_{\mathrm{total}}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nuc}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:33-divided}), the sum of the energy of the nuclear motion ($\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nuc}}$) and the electronic energy ($\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}$).

Discussion: Is the Born–Oppenheimer approximation a “mean-field approximation”?

The quantity $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$ that appears in Eq. \eqref{eq:33-nuc-pes} is the expectation value of the energy over the electronic wave function (the “average” value for the electrons). Since “the electrons act on the nuclei as an average potential,” the Born–Oppenheimer approximation may seem to be a kind of mean-field approximation. However, these are two different approximations, and they must not be confused.

  • What is approximated. The Born–Oppenheimer approximation is the approximation that separates the motions of the nuclei and electrons, on the basis of the difference in mass ($m_{\mathrm e}/M\ll1$). The electron–electron interaction $V_{\mathrm{ee}}$ remains intact in Eq. \eqref{eq:33-Hele}, and if the electronic equation, Eq. \eqref{eq:33-ele-eig}, is solved exactly, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$ contains all of the electron–electron interaction (including electron correlation). The mean-field approximation (the Hartree approximation and the Hartree–Fock approximation; Chapters 35 and 36), on the other hand, is the approximation that replaces the electron–electron interaction by the form in which each electron moves independently in the average potential created by the other electrons.
  • The meaning of “average.” The reason $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$ is an “expectation value” is that in quantum mechanics the energy of a single eigenstate can be written as $\mel{\Psi}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}}{\Psi}$; it is not that the electron–electron interaction has been replaced by an average potential. Only when the Hartree–Fock wave function (a single Slater determinant) is chosen as the electronic trial function does $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$ become the energy in the mean field of the electrons. But this is the result of choosing, in addition, an approximation between the electrons (the mean field).
  • The two can be used together. In actual calculations for molecules and solids, the nuclei are held fixed by the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$ is found at each $\bm{R}$ by a mean-field approximation (the Hartree–Fock method or density functional theory). The error of the former is determined by the mass ratio, and the error of the latter by electron correlation.

33.3.3 What the potential energy surface tells us

Once the PES $V_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{R})$ has been found, information about the motion of the nuclei in a molecule or solid can be extracted from the nuclear equation, Eq. \eqref{eq:33-nuc-pes}.

  • Equilibrium structure: The force on the nuclei is $\bm{F}_I=-\nabla_IV_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{R})$, so the point where the force is $0$ (a minimum of the PES) is the equilibrium structure. For a diatomic molecule it is the equilibrium internuclear distance $R_{\mathrm e}$; for a crystal, it is the lattice constant.
  • Vibration: If we expand about the minimum as $V_{\mathrm{eff}}\simeq V_{\mathrm{eff}}(R_{\mathrm e})+\tfrac12k(R-R_{\mathrm e})^2$ ($k=V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(R_{\mathrm e})$), the nuclear equation becomes the equation of a harmonic oscillator, the frequency is $\omega=\sqrt{k/\mu}$, and the levels are $\hbar\omega(n+\tfrac12)$ (Chapter 19, Section 19.1). Here $\mu=M_{\mathrm A}M_{\mathrm B}/(M_{\mathrm A}+M_{\mathrm B})$ is the reduced mass of the nuclei. For polyatomic molecules and crystals, diagonalizing the matrix of second derivatives of the PES (the Hessian) gives the normal modes (phonons, in a crystal).
  • Dissociation and reactions: The depth of a valley of the PES is the bond energy, and the pass from one valley to another is the activation barrier of a chemical reaction.
  • Forces: The forces on the nuclei are determined by the gradient of the PES. By the Hellmann–Feynman theorem below, the forces can be computed directly from the charge density of the electrons.

Theorem 33.1: Hellmann–Feynman theorem (force on the nuclei)

For the electronic ground state $\Psi_0$ (a normalized exact eigenfunction, or the state that minimizes the energy including the parameters that depend on $\bm{R}$) and its energy $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})=\mel{\Psi_0}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})}{\Psi_0}$, the following holds:

\begin{equation} \nabla_I\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})=\mel{\Psi_0}{\nabla_I\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}}{\Psi_0}=\mel{\Psi_0}{\nabla_IV_{\mathrm{ne}}}{\Psi_0} \label{eq:33-HF-theorem} \end{equation}

Therefore the force on nucleus $I$ is $\bm{F}_I=-\nabla_IV_{\mathrm{nn}}-\mel{\Psi_0}{\nabla_IV_{\mathrm{ne}}}{\Psi_0}$, which is nothing but the electrostatic force (Coulomb force) exerted by the other nuclei and the charge distribution of the electrons.

Proof

Differentiate $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}=\mel{\Psi_0}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}}{\Psi_0}$ with respect to $\bm{R}_I$. By the product rule and $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}\Psi_0=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}\Psi_0$, we have

$$ \nabla_I\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}=\mel{\nabla_I\Psi_0}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}}{\Psi_0}+\mel{\Psi_0}{\nabla_I\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}}{\Psi_0}+\mel{\Psi_0}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}}{\nabla_I\Psi_0} =\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}\Bigl[\braket{\nabla_I\Psi_0}{\Psi_0}+\braket{\Psi_0}{\nabla_I\Psi_0}\Bigr]+\mel{\Psi_0}{\nabla_I\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}}{\Psi_0} $$

(Because $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}$ is Hermitian, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}$ comes out whichever side $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}$ is made to act on.) The bracket vanishes because $\nabla_I\braket{\Psi_0}{\Psi_0}=\nabla_I1=0$, and only the second term remains. Of the terms in $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}$, only $V_{\mathrm{ne}}$ depends on $\bm{R}_I$, so $\nabla_I\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}=\nabla_IV_{\mathrm{ne}}$.

∎

This theorem means that the forces can be found without computing how the electronic wave function depends on $\bm{R}$, and it is the foundation of structure optimization and first-principles molecular dynamics. However, since we used $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ele}}\Psi_0=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}\Psi_0$ in the proof, the theorem holds when $\Psi_0$ is an exact eigenfunction (or when the basis is independent of $\bm{R}$ and the energy is stationary with respect to the expansion coefficients). In calculations that use basis functions attached to the atomic nuclei (Chapter 37), the basis functions themselves depend on $\bm{R}$, so in addition to the force above, a correction arising from that dependence (the Pulay force) is needed.

Example 33.6: The isotope effect—$\mathrm{H_2}$, HD, and $\mathrm{D_2}$ have the same PES

Let the harmonic vibrational wavenumber of $\mathrm{H_2}$ be $\omega_{\mathrm e}=4401\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (the observed fundamental vibration is lower because of anharmonicity, about $4161\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ for $\mathrm{H_2}$). Using the fact that the PES is independent of the isotope, predict the wavenumbers of HD and $\mathrm{D_2}$. (Atomic masses: H $=1.00783\ \mathrm{u}$, D $=2.01410\ \mathrm{u}$.)

Solution. $V_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{R})=V_{\mathrm{nn}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}$ is determined only by the charges of the nuclei ($Z=1$) and the number of electrons, and it does not contain the nuclear mass $M_I$ (no $M_I$ appears in Eq. \eqref{eq:33-Hele} or in $V_{\mathrm{nn}}$). Therefore the spring constant $k$ is independent of the isotope, and the only difference is the reduced mass $\mu$ in the nuclear kinetic energy $-\hbar^2\nabla^2/2\mu$. Since $\omega=\sqrt{k/\mu}\propto\mu^{-1/2}$,

$$ \tilde{\nu}'=\tilde{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{\mu(\mathrm{H_2})}{\mu'}}. $$

With $\mu(\mathrm{H_2})=\tfrac{1.00783}{2}=0.50392\ \mathrm{u}$, $\mu(\mathrm{HD})=\dfrac{1.00783\times2.01410}{1.00783+2.01410}=0.67171\ \mathrm{u}$, and $\mu(\mathrm{D_2})=\tfrac{2.01410}{2}=1.00705\ \mathrm{u}$, we obtain

$$ \tilde{\nu}(\mathrm{HD})=4401\times\sqrt{\frac{0.50392}{0.67171}}=4401\times0.8661=3812\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}},\qquad \tilde{\nu}(\mathrm{D_2})=4401\times\sqrt{\frac{0.50392}{1.00705}}=4401\times0.7074=3113\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}} $$

The measured harmonic frequencies are about $3813$ and $3116\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, and the difference is of order $0.1\ \%$ (the observed fundamental vibrations are lower than these because of anharmonicity, about $3632\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ for HD and about $2994\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ for $\mathrm{D_2}$; what we are comparing here are the harmonic frequencies with each other). This small difference comes from corrections to the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, such as $\Lambda_{nn}$, which depend on the mass ratio. That the PES does not change with the isotope is one of the most important experimental consequences of the Born–Oppenheimer approximation (Chapter 19, Section 19.3; Chapter 20, Section 20.4).

Application: The Born–Oppenheimer approximation works very well in solids

In solids (crystals), too, the basic procedure of first-principles calculations (density functional theory and the like) is to hold the nuclear positions $\bm{R}$ (the lattice constant and the displacements of the atoms) fixed and find the total electronic energy $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})+V_{\mathrm{nn}}(\bm{R})$. If we reinterpret the horizontal axis of Figure 33.4 as the lattice constant, the minimum of this curve gives the equilibrium lattice constant and its curvature gives the bulk modulus. Moreover, the phonon frequencies can be calculated from the second derivative of the energy when an atom is displaced slightly (the force constant). The reason the Born–Oppenheimer approximation holds especially well in solids is that, in semiconductors and insulators, the electronic excitation energy (the band gap, about $1\ \mathrm{eV}$ or more) is much larger than the quantum $\hbar\omega$ of the lattice vibrations. For example, the optical phonon of silicon is about $15.6\ \mathrm{THz}$ (energy $65\ \mathrm{meV}$) and the band gap is $1.12\ \mathrm{eV}$, so the ratio is about $0.06$. That the electrons can follow while the nuclei move was also seen in Eq. \eqref{eq:33-hierarchy} and Figure 33.3. The chapters on phonons in “Solid-State Physics” (AD 06) and the first-principles calculations in “Density Functional Theory” (AD 07) rest on this approximation.

Caution: When the Born–Oppenheimer approximation breaks down

This approximation becomes poor when the energy difference $\varepsilon_n-\varepsilon_m$ between electronic states $n$ and $m$ becomes small and the nonadiabatic coupling $\bm{d}^I_{mn}\propto1/(\varepsilon_n-\varepsilon_m)$ (Section 33.2.3) becomes large. (1) Metals: Because the electronic excitation energy can be arbitrarily small, the nonadiabatic coupling can in principle become large. In ordinary metals, however, the phonon energy $\hbar\omega$ is far smaller than the Fermi energy $E_{\mathrm F}$ ($\hbar\omega/E_{\mathrm F}\sim10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$), so the standard treatment is to add the electron–lattice interaction perturbatively on top of the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, as the change of the PES under displacement (the deformation potential). The electron–lattice interaction that gives rise to electrical resistance and superconductivity (the formation of Cooper pairs) is thus explained inside the Born–Oppenheimer approximation (“Solid-State Physics” (AD 06) (in Japanese)). The cracks in the approximation become conspicuous in graphene, doped semiconductors, and the like, where the phonon energy approaches the electronic energy scale. (2) Jahn–Teller effect (Chapter 44, Section 44.6): When the electronic states become degenerate in a structure of high symmetry, several PESs touch in its neighborhood, and the nuclei cannot stay on a single surface. A point where PESs are connected at the apex of a cone is called a conical intersection. (3) Photochemical reactions: A molecule excited by light passes through a conical intersection into another electronic state in tens of femtoseconds. (4) When the nuclei are light (muonic atoms and molecules, etc.; Exercise 33.8). In such cases, several electronic states and the motion of the nuclei must be treated simultaneously.

33.4 The Hartree Product — When There Is No Electron–Electron Interaction

From here on, we consider the problem of the electrons alone, with the nuclei held fixed (the electronic equation). How do we construct the wave function of $N$ electrons? We first consider the case in which the electrons do not interact with one another. In this case the exact solution is easy to write down. Real electrons repel one another, so this is an approximation, but it is the starting point of the Hartree–Fock approximation (Chapters 35 and 36).

33.4.1 The electron coordinate $\bm{x}=(\bm{r},\sigma)$ and spin orbitals

An electron has spin (Chapter 23). The state of a single electron is therefore described by a function that contains not only the position $\bm{r}$ but also the spin variable $\sigma=\pm\tfrac12$. We combine the position and the spin variable and write

$$ \bm{x}=(\bm{r},\sigma) $$

and let the coordinate of electron $i$ be $\bm{x}_i=(\bm{r}_i,\sigma_i)$. Integration over $\bm{x}$ means integrating over $\bm{r}$ and summing over the spin variable $\sigma$: $\displaystyle\int f(\bm{x})\dd\bm{x}\equiv\sum_{\sigma=\pm1/2}\int f(\bm{r},\sigma)\dd\bm{r}$. (Since $\sigma$ takes only two values, the “integral” is in fact a sum of two terms. This $\sigma$ is not the same as the Pauli matrices $\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z$; see Chapter 23, Section 23.5.) The spin functions $\alpha(\sigma)$ and $\beta(\sigma)$ ($\alpha=\ket{\uparrow}$, $\beta=\ket{\downarrow}$) are given by $\alpha(\tfrac12)=1$, $\alpha(-\tfrac12)=0$, $\beta(\tfrac12)=0$, $\beta(-\tfrac12)=1$, and are orthonormal:

\begin{equation} \sum_\sigma\alpha^*(\sigma)\alpha(\sigma)=\sum_\sigma\beta^*(\sigma)\beta(\sigma)=1,\qquad\sum_\sigma\alpha^*(\sigma)\beta(\sigma)=0 \label{eq:33-spin-orth} \end{equation}

(Some authors formally write the sum over $\sigma$ as $\int\dd\sigma$, in which case the orthogonality is written $\int\dd\sigma\,\alpha^*\beta=0$).

Definition 33.6: Spin orbital

The function of the coordinate $\bm{x}=(\bm{r},\sigma)$ obtained by multiplying a function of position $\psi(\bm{r})$ (a spatial orbital) by the spin function $\alpha(\sigma)$ or $\beta(\sigma)$,

\begin{equation} \chi(\bm{x})=\psi(\bm{r})\,\alpha(\sigma)\qquad\text{or}\qquad\chi(\bm{x})=\psi(\bm{r})\,\beta(\sigma) \label{eq:33-spinorbital} \end{equation}

is called a spin orbital. The function $\chi$ contains both the position coordinate $\bm{r}=(x,y,z)$ and the spin coordinate $\sigma$. If $\psi$ is normalized, then by Eq. \eqref{eq:33-spin-orth} we have $\displaystyle\int\abs{\chi}^2\dd\bm{x}=\int\abs{\psi}^2\dd\bm{r}\sum_\sigma\abs{\alpha}^2=1$, so $\chi$ is also normalized. The functions $\psi\alpha$ and $\psi\beta$, which share the same spatial orbital $\psi$, are orthogonal because of the orthogonality of their spin parts.

In this book, spatial orbitals are denoted $\psi_i$ and spin orbitals $\chi_i$ (from Chapter 35 onward, a bar is added to the orbitals of $\beta$ spin, as in $\bar{\chi}_i$). The Hamiltonian of the electron system with the nuclei held fixed contains no spin, so an eigenfunction of one electron may be either $\psi(\bm{r})\alpha(\sigma)$ or $\psi(\bm{r})\beta(\sigma)$, and both have the same energy (doubly degenerate).

33.4.2 When there is no electron–electron interaction — the Hartree product

Consider a system containing $N$ electrons, and suppose that there is no Coulomb interaction between the electrons. Each electron feels only the potential produced by the atomic nucleus (charge $+Ze_0$). The Hamiltonian is then the sum of the Hamiltonians of the individual electrons:

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\nabla_i^2\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Ze_0^2}{r_i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{h}_i,\qquad \hat{h}_i=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\nabla_i^2-\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Ze_0^2}{r_i} \label{eq:33-noninteracting} \end{equation}

Here $r_i=\abs{\bm{r}_i}$ is the distance between electron $i$ and the nucleus, and $\hat{h}_i$ is a one-electron Hamiltonian that acts only on the coordinate of electron $i$. For $Z=1$ it is exactly the Hamiltonian of the hydrogen atom.

Caution: The sign of the kinetic energy term is negative

The kinetic energy operator is $-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}}\nabla_i^2$, which carries a minus sign. If the sign is dropped, even the hydrogen atom comes out wrong. Let us examine the 1s orbital $\psi=e^{-r}/\sqrt{\pi}$ ($r>0$) in atomic units. Since $\nabla^2\psi=\psi''+\tfrac2r\psi'=\bigl(1-\tfrac{2}{r}\bigr)\psi$, with the correct $\hat{h}=-\tfrac12\nabla^2-\tfrac1r$ we obtain $\hat{h}\psi=\bigl(-\tfrac12+\tfrac1r-\tfrac1r\bigr)\psi=-\tfrac12\psi$, so the orbital is an eigenfunction (eigenvalue $-\tfrac12E_{\mathrm h}$). If, on the other hand, the sign is wrongly taken as $+\tfrac12\nabla^2-\tfrac1r$, we get $\bigl(\tfrac12-\tfrac1r-\tfrac1r\bigr)\psi=\bigl(\tfrac12-\tfrac2r\bigr)\psi$, which is not a constant multiple of the orbital.

Suppose we have solved the one-electron problem $\hat{h}\chi_k=\varepsilon_k\chi_k$. The solutions $\chi_k(\bm{x})$ ($k=1,2,\dots$; spin orbitals) are the orbitals $\psi_{nlm}(\bm{r})$ of the hydrogen-like atom multiplied by a spin function (Chapter 15). Then a solution of the $N$-electron Schrödinger equation $\hat{H}\Psi=E\Psi$ can be built as a product of one-electron solutions.

Formula 33.1: Hartree product (the solution when there is no electron–electron interaction)

Let $\hat{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{h}_i$ and $\hat{h}_i\chi_k(\bm{x}_i)=\varepsilon_k\chi_k(\bm{x}_i)$. The product obtained by assigning the one-electron spin orbitals $\chi_{k_1},\chi_{k_2},\dots,\chi_{k_N}$ to electrons 1 through $N$, respectively,

\begin{equation} \Psi(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2,\dots,\bm{x}_N)=\chi_{k_1}(\bm{x}_1)\,\chi_{k_2}(\bm{x}_2)\cdots\chi_{k_N}(\bm{x}_N) \label{eq:33-hartree-product} \end{equation}

is called a Hartree product and satisfies $\hat{H}\Psi=E\Psi$. The energy is

\begin{equation} E=\varepsilon_{k_1}+\varepsilon_{k_2}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{k_N} \label{eq:33-hartree-E} \end{equation}

that is, the sum of the one-electron energies.

Proof

Since $\hat{h}_i$ acts only on the coordinate $\bm{x}_i$, in the product in Eq. \eqref{eq:33-hartree-product} it acts only on $\chi_{k_i}(\bm{x}_i)$, and the other factors come out as constants:

$$ \hat{h}_i\Psi=\Bigl[\hat{h}_i\chi_{k_i}(\bm{x}_i)\Bigr]\prod_{j\neq i}\chi_{k_j}(\bm{x}_j)=\varepsilon_{k_i}\chi_{k_i}(\bm{x}_i)\prod_{j\neq i}\chi_{k_j}(\bm{x}_j)=\varepsilon_{k_i}\Psi . $$

Summing this over $i=1,\dots,N$ gives $\hat{H}\Psi=\sum_i\hat{h}_i\Psi=\bigl(\sum_i\varepsilon_{k_i}\bigr)\Psi$.

∎

Thus the eigenfunction of a Hamiltonian that is a sum of terms for the individual electrons becomes a product (the variables can be separated). This is the answer to the question left open in Section 33.1.2. The reason Eq. \eqref{eq:33-he} for the helium atom cannot be separated into variables is that $\hat{H}$ is not a sum of terms for the individual electrons but contains the two-electron term $1/\abs{\bm{r}_1-\bm{r}_2}$.

33.4.3 The probability of finding the electrons becomes a product — what “no electron–electron interaction” means

What kind of state is a “state with no electron–electron interaction”? Let us examine it through the probability of finding the electrons in a Hartree product. By the Born interpretation, the probability that electron 1 is found in $\dd\bm{x}_1$ near $\bm{x}_1$, electron 2 in $\dd\bm{x}_2$ near $\bm{x}_2$, …, and electron $N$ in $\dd\bm{x}_N$ near $\bm{x}_N$, all at the same time, is

\begin{equation} \abs{\Psi}^2\dd\bm{x}_1\cdots\dd\bm{x}_N=\abs{\chi_{k_1}(\bm{x}_1)}^2\dd\bm{x}_1\cdot\abs{\chi_{k_2}(\bm{x}_2)}^2\dd\bm{x}_2\cdots\abs{\chi_{k_N}(\bm{x}_N)}^2\dd\bm{x}_N \label{eq:33-independent} \end{equation}

This is the product of the probabilities of finding the individual electrons. The probability of finding electron 1 does not depend on the positions of the other electrons. In other words, each electron moves independently of the others (statistically independently). This is the meaning, in terms of probability, of “no electron–electron interaction.”

In reality, however, when electrons come close together they avoid one another because of the Coulomb repulsion. When electron 1 is at some place, electron 2 is hard to find near it. This “mutual avoidance” (electron correlation) is not contained in the Hartree product.

Illustration 33.1: The playing-card example: the probability of independent events is a product

Let us compare the absence of electron–electron interaction to playing cards. When we draw one card from a deck of 52 playing cards, the probability of drawing the ace of hearts is $\dfrac{1}{52}$. This equals the product of the probability of drawing a heart, $\dfrac14$ (hearts are one of four suits), and the probability of drawing an ace, $\dfrac1{13}$ (the ace is one of 13 ranks):

$$ \frac{1}{52}=\frac{1}{4}\times\frac{1}{13}. $$

This is because the suit and the rank are determined independently of each other. The same holds for the Hartree product in Eq. \eqref{eq:33-independent}: the probability that electron 1 is found at place A and the probability that electron 2 is found at place B are independent, so the probability of finding them simultaneously is the product. On the other hand, for a deck in which cards of a particular suit tend to carry particular ranks (there is a correlation), such a product relation does not hold. When there is repulsion between the electrons, the probability of finding them simultaneously at the same place becomes smaller than the product.

Example 33.7: The probability that two electrons in the hydrogen 1s orbital are simultaneously within $a_0$

Consider the Hartree product $\Psi=\psi(\bm{r}_1)\psi(\bm{r}_2)$ in which two electrons occupy the hydrogen-like 1s orbital ($Z=1$) $\psi(r)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-r}$ (atomic units). (1) Find the probability $P$ that one electron is within $a_0$ of the nucleus. (2) Find the probability that both electrons are within $a_0$ at the same time.

Solution. (1) By spherical symmetry, $\displaystyle P=\int_0^{1}\abs{\psi}^24\pi r^2\dd r=4\int_0^1r^2e^{-2r}\dd r$. Repeated integration by parts gives $\displaystyle\int r^2e^{-2r}\dd r=-e^{-2r}\Bigl(\dfrac{r^2}{2}+\dfrac{r}{2}+\dfrac14\Bigr)$, so

$$ P=4\left[-e^{-2r}\Bigl(\frac{r^2}{2}+\frac{r}{2}+\frac14\Bigr)\right]_0^1=4\left[\frac14-e^{-2}\Bigl(\frac12+\frac12+\frac14\Bigr)\right]=1-5e^{-2}=0.3233 . $$

(2) In the Hartree product the two electrons are independent, so by Eq. \eqref{eq:33-independent} the simultaneous probability is the product, $P^2=0.3233^2=0.1045$. If there is electron–electron repulsion, the two electrons avoid being in the same small region at the same time, so the true probability is expected to be smaller than this (this is the mutual avoidance of the electrons, that is, the effect of electron correlation; a quantitative treatment was seen for the helium atom in Chapter 31, Section 31.4). Note that this setup, with two electrons placed in the $Z=1$ 1s orbital, is a formal example (in the real $\mathrm{H^-}$, the spread of the orbital differs from this because of the repulsion).

33.4.4 The drawbacks of the Hartree product

The Hartree product assigns orbitals to electrons by numbering them: “electron 1 is in orbital $\chi_{k_1}$, electron 2 is in orbital $\chi_{k_2}$, …”. But electrons are all completely identical particles and cannot be distinguished. Moreover, the Hartree product also allows the product $\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\,\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\cdots\chi_1(\bm{x}_N)$, in which all $N$ electrons are placed in the same lowest spin orbital $\chi_1$. This contradicts the fact that the electron configurations of atoms form shells, as the periodic table shows.

For example, consider the Li atom ($Z=3$) with the electron–electron repulsion neglected, using Hartree products. The hydrogen-like $1\mathrm{s}$ energy is $-Z^2/2=-\tfrac92\,E_{\mathrm h}$, and if all three electrons are placed in $1\mathrm{s}$, the total energy is $3\times(-\tfrac92)=-13.5\,E_{\mathrm h}$. If the third electron is placed in $2\mathrm{s}$ (energy $-Z^2/8=-\tfrac98$), the total is $2\times(-\tfrac92)-\tfrac98=-10.125\,E_{\mathrm h}$, and the former is lower by as much as $3.375\,E_{\mathrm h}$ (about $92\ \mathrm{eV}$). That is, from the standpoint of the Hartree product one predicts that “the state of lowest energy has all three in $1\mathrm{s}$,” and there is no mechanism to forbid this. Yet the real lithium atom has a first ionization energy of only $5.39\ \mathrm{eV}$ (removing one more electron from $\mathrm{Li^+}$ requires $75.6\ \mathrm{eV}$), and the third electron is not in $1\mathrm{s}$ but in the $2\mathrm{s}$ orbital, much farther out. The chemical property that lithium, like sodium and potassium, is an alkali metal that readily becomes a monovalent cation also reflects the shell structure in which there is one electron outside the $1\mathrm{s}^2$ closed shell. Removing this drawback is the subject of the next two sections.

33.5 Indistinguishability of Identical Particles and the Pauli Exclusion Principle

33.5.1 Electrons are indistinguishable

In classical mechanics (AD 03), however similar particles may be, they can be told apart by following their trajectories. Even if two billiard balls of the same material and the same size collide, ball 1 remains ball 1, and we can keep track of which is which. In quantum mechanics this is impossible. The position of an electron is represented by the spread of its wave function, and in the region where the wave functions of two electrons overlap, we cannot follow “which electron is where,” even in principle. All electrons have exactly the same mass, charge, and spin; for example, there is no mark that distinguishes the electron of a hydrogen atom from the electron of a helium atom. This is the indistinguishability of identical particles.

That electrons are indistinguishable means that interchanging the labels of the coordinates of two electrons (electron 1, electron 2) changes no quantity that can be observed in a measurement. For a wave function $\Psi(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)$, what is observable is the probability density $\abs{\Psi}^2$, so

\begin{equation} \abs{\Psi(\bm{x}_2,\bm{x}_1)}^2=\abs{\Psi(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)}^2 \label{eq:33-indist} \end{equation}

must hold. Let us express this with an operator.

Definition 33.7: The exchange operator $\hat{P}_{12}$

The operator that interchanges the coordinates (position and spin variable) of electron 1 and electron 2 is called the exchange operator (permutation operator) $\hat{P}_{12}$:

\begin{equation} \bigl(\hat{P}_{12}\Psi\bigr)(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2,\bm{x}_3,\dots)=\Psi(\bm{x}_2,\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_3,\dots) \label{eq:33-P12} \end{equation}

Interchanging twice in succession restores the original, so $\hat{P}_{12}^2=1$.

To find the eigenvalues of $\hat{P}_{12}$, apply $\hat{P}_{12}$ once more to both sides of $\hat{P}_{12}\Psi=\lambda\Psi$: from $\Psi=\hat{P}_{12}^2\Psi=\lambda\hat{P}_{12}\Psi=\lambda^2\Psi$ we get $\lambda^2=1$, that is, $\lambda=\pm1$. Moreover, the postulate that electrons are indistinguishable means that the interchanged wave function $\hat{P}_{12}\Psi$ represents physically the same state as the original $\Psi$. (Eq. \eqref{eq:33-indist} is merely an equality at each point, and by itself it leaves open the possibility of a coordinate-dependent phase. If we further require that the expectation values and matrix elements of all operators that are symmetric under interchange of the electron labels be unchanged by the interchange, we can conclude that $\hat{P}_{12}\Psi$ and $\Psi$ represent the same state. For a rigorous discussion, see the chapters on identical particles in Sakurai or Griffiths.) In quantum mechanics, “the same state” means that the two differ only by a constant phase factor $e^{i\theta}$, so $\hat{P}_{12}\Psi=e^{i\theta}\Psi$. Applying it twice gives $e^{2i\theta}=1$, so again $e^{i\theta}=\pm1$. Furthermore, since electrons are identical particles, the Hamiltonian is invariant under interchange of the electron labels, and $\hat{H}\hat{P}_{12}=\hat{P}_{12}\hat{H}$ ($\comm{\hat{H}}{\hat{P}_{12}}=0$) holds. Therefore, the eigenvalue $\pm1$ of $\hat{P}_{12}$ is a conserved quantity that does not change with time.

33.5.2 The Pauli exclusion principle (antisymmetry principle)

A wave function with eigenvalue $+1$ is called symmetric, and one with eigenvalue $-1$ is called antisymmetric. Which type each particle in nature belongs to is an experimental fact.

Axiom 33.1: The Pauli exclusion principle (antisymmetry principle)

For electrons, the wave function changes sign when the coordinates of any two electrons (each $\bm{x}_i$ being the position and the spin variable taken together) are interchanged.

\begin{equation} \Psi(\dots,\bm{x}_i,\dots,\bm{x}_j,\dots)=-\Psi(\dots,\bm{x}_j,\dots,\bm{x}_i,\dots)\qquad(\text{for arbitrary }i\neq j) \label{eq:33-antisym} \end{equation}

Particles with half-integer spin (electrons, protons, neutrons, $^3\mathrm{He}$ atoms, etc.) are fermions, and their wave functions are antisymmetric. Particles with integer spin (photons, $^4\mathrm{He}$ atoms, mesons, etc.) are bosons, and their wave functions are symmetric (the sign does not change).

This relation between spin and statistics (symmetric or antisymmetric) is proved from relativistic quantum field theory as the spin–statistics theorem (beyond the scope of this book). In this book we use it as an empirical fact (Chapter 23, Section 23.4; AD 03, Chapter 52, Section 52.4 (in Japanese)). The name “exclusion principle” comes from Pauli's statement in 1925 that an atom must not contain two or more electrons with all four quantum numbers $(n,l,m_l,m_s)$ equal. The antisymmetry principle is a more general restatement of this. Why antisymmetry implies “exclusion” (two electrons cannot occupy the same state) is shown in Section 33.6.

Caution: The Hartree product is not antisymmetric

Applying $\hat{P}_{12}$ to the Hartree product $\Psi=\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)$ gives $\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)$, which is not $\pm\Psi$ (when $\chi_1\neq\chi_2$). The reason we are able to say “electron 1 is in $\chi_1$ and electron 2 is in $\chi_2$” is that the Hartree product distinguishes the electrons. If electrons cannot be distinguished, the state “electron 1 in $\chi_1$, electron 2 in $\chi_2$” and the state “electron 1 in $\chi_2$, electron 2 in $\chi_1$” must be mixed with equal weight.

For two electrons, an antisymmetric linear combination of the two Hartree products satisfies Axiom 33.1.

\begin{equation} \Phi(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigl[\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)-\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\Bigr] \label{eq:33-antisym-2} \end{equation}

Indeed, $\hat{P}_{12}\Phi=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)-\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\bigr]=-\Phi$. The factor $1/\sqrt2$ is there to normalize $\Phi$ when $\chi_1,\chi_2$ are orthonormal (Section 33.6). For $N$ electrons, we sum over the $N!$ interchanges (permutations) $P$, attaching the sign $(-1)^P$: $+$ for an even permutation and $-$ for an odd permutation. This operation can be expressed by the antisymmetrization operator $\hat{A}=\dfrac{1}{N!}\sum_P(-1)^P\hat{P}$. The result is the Slater determinant of Section 33.6.

33.5.3 The spatial and spin parts of two electrons

When the Hamiltonian contains no spin (the spin–orbit interaction is neglected), the wave function of two electrons can be taken in the form of a product $\Psi(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)=\Psi_{\mathrm{orb}}(\bm{r}_1,\bm{r}_2)\,\Theta(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)$ of a spatial part $\Psi_{\mathrm{orb}}$ and a spin part $\Theta$ (we avoided $\Phi$ because it denotes a Slater determinant, and $S$ because it is used for the overlap integral and the total spin quantum number). The overall antisymmetry \eqref{eq:33-antisym} is determined by the combination of the symmetries of the spatial part and the spin part. There are four functions of the spins of two electrons:

\begin{align} &\text{symmetric (triplet)}\quad \alpha(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2),\quad \beta(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2),\quad \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\alpha(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2)+\beta(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2)\bigr]\notag\\ &\text{antisymmetric (singlet)}\quad \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\alpha(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2)-\beta(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2)\bigr] \label{eq:33-spin-states} \end{align}

One can check directly that under the interchange $\sigma_1\leftrightarrow\sigma_2$ the first three above do not change and the last one changes sign. The three have total spin $S=1$ (triplet), and the one has $S=0$ (singlet) (Chapter 25, Section 25.8). Therefore, there are only the following two kinds of combinations that are antisymmetric overall.

  • The spatial part is symmetric and the spin part is antisymmetric (singlet, $S=0$).
  • The spatial part is antisymmetric and the spin part is symmetric (triplet, $S=1$).

The spatial part of the ground state $1\mathrm{s}^2$ of the helium atom is $\psi_{1s}(\bm{r}_1)\psi_{1s}(\bm{r}_2)$, which is symmetric, so the spin part must be a singlet. When two electrons occupy the same spatial orbital, only the singlet with opposite spins ($\uparrow\downarrow$) is possible. On the other hand, in an excited state such as $1\mathrm{s}2\mathrm{s}$, in which the two electrons occupy different orbitals, the spatial part can be either symmetric or antisymmetric, and both singlet and triplet exist. The triplet is lower, and the splitting is about $0.80\ \mathrm{eV}$ (Chapter 29, Section 29.5). The exchange integral that produces this difference is derived in Chapter 35.

Let us see how the Pauli exclusion principle changes the total energy by looking at three electrons in a box. The ways of placing the electrons in the levels are shown in Figure 33.5, and the energy is found in Example 33.8.

Example 33.8: Three electrons in a box—how the exclusion principle changes the energy

Three electrons, with the electron–electron interaction neglected, are placed in a one-dimensional infinite well of width $L=1\ \mathrm{nm}$. Find the total energy (1) when the exclusion principle is ignored and all three electrons are placed in the lowest level, and (2) when the exclusion principle is obeyed ($E_1=h^2/(8m_{\mathrm e}L^2)$). (3) For a GaAs quantum well (effective mass $m^*=0.067\,m_{\mathrm e}$, $L=10\ \mathrm{nm}$), find $E_1$. Here the effective mass is the mass obtained when an electron in a crystal, under the influence of the periodic potential, is regarded as moving as if it were a free particle of mass $m^*$ (Chapter 41).

Solution. $E_1=\dfrac{h^2}{8m_{\mathrm e}L^2}=\dfrac{(6.626\,07\times10^{-34})^2}{8\times9.109\,38\times10^{-31}\times(10^{-9})^2}=6.024\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{J}=0.376\ \mathrm{eV}$ (Chapter 10). The levels are $E_n=n^2E_1$, so $E_2=4E_1=1.504\ \mathrm{eV}$.

(1) $3E_1=1.128\ \mathrm{eV}$. (2) Since the electron spin has only two possibilities, $\uparrow$ and $\downarrow$, each spatial level can hold at most two electrons (two electrons in the same spatial level must have opposite spins). The third electron goes into $n=2$, giving $2E_1+E_2=2\times0.376+1.504=2.256\ \mathrm{eV}=6E_1$. The difference from (1) is $E_2-E_1=3E_1=1.128\ \mathrm{eV}$; in this case, the exclusion principle doubles the total energy.

(3) If an electron in a crystal is regarded as a free particle of mass $m^*$, it suffices to replace $m_{\mathrm e}$ in the particle-in-a-box formula by $m^*$. A lighter effective mass raises the levels, and a wider well lowers them: since $E_1\propto1/(m^*L^2)$, $E_1=\dfrac{0.376\ \mathrm{eV}}{0.067\times10^2}=0.0561\ \mathrm{eV}=56\ \mathrm{meV}$ (for $L=10\ \mathrm{nm}$, $L^2=100$ times larger). For the same three electrons, $2E_1+E_2=6E_1=337\ \mathrm{meV}$.

Application: The world of matter supported by the exclusion principle

The Pauli exclusion principle underlies the properties of matter. (1) The periodic table: since at most two electrons (with spins $\uparrow\downarrow$) can enter a single spatial orbital, as the atomic number increases the electrons occupy orbitals of higher and higher energy in turn, and a shell structure arises (Chapter 34). (2) Metals: the electrons of a metal fill the levels two at a time, from the lowest level upward, and the highest occupied level (the Fermi energy, Chapter 10, Section 10.7) reaches $E_{\mathrm F}\sim$ a few eV. Since this is much larger than the thermal energy at room temperature (about $0.026\ \mathrm{eV}$), most of the electrons are unaffected by heat, and only the electrons near the Fermi energy contribute to the heat capacity and to conduction (AD 03, Chapter 52, Section 52.8 (in Japanese)). (3) The stability of matter and its resistance to compression: if electrons are squeezed into a narrow region, there are no vacant low levels, and they are forced into levels of high kinetic energy, so matter resists compression. This pressure is called the degeneracy pressure (it arises because, owing to the exclusion principle, the low levels are already filled, and under compression the electrons are forced into levels of high kinetic energy); in white dwarfs the degeneracy pressure of electrons, and in neutron stars that of neutrons, supports the star against gravity.

33.6 The Slater Determinant

33.6.1 Definition of the Slater determinant

The Slater determinant is a way of writing the antisymmetrization operation all at once, using the mathematical tool called the determinant. Let the spin-orbital wave function representing electron $i$ be $\chi_j(\bm{x}_i)$ ($j$ is the orbital index and $i$ is the electron index). The determinant of the $N\times N$ matrix arranged with electrons in rows and spin orbitals in columns gives an approximation to the wave function of the $N$-electron system.

Definition 33.8: Slater determinant

The function built from $N$ (mutually distinct) spin orbitals $\chi_1,\chi_2,\dots,\chi_N$,

\begin{equation} \Phi(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2,\dots,\bm{x}_N)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}} \begin{vmatrix} \chi_1(\bm{x}_1)&\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)&\cdots&\chi_N(\bm{x}_1)\\ \chi_1(\bm{x}_2)&\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)&\cdots&\chi_N(\bm{x}_2)\\ \vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\ \chi_1(\bm{x}_N)&\chi_2(\bm{x}_N)&\cdots&\chi_N(\bm{x}_N) \end{vmatrix} \label{eq:33-slater} \end{equation}

is called the Slater determinant. The rows correspond to the electrons ($\bm{x}_1,\dots,\bm{x}_N$) and the columns to the spin orbitals ($\chi_1,\dots,\chi_N$). The coefficient $1/\sqrt{N!}$ is the factor that normalizes $\Phi$ when the $\chi_j$ are orthonormal (Section 33.6.4).

The determinant is defined, using the signs of permutations, as $\det[M_{ij}]=\sum_P(-1)^P\prod_iM_{i,P(i)}$ ($P$ is a permutation of the $N$ indices, and $(-1)^P$ is $+1$ for an even permutation and $-1$ for an odd permutation; University Mathematics, Chapter 8, Section 8.2). Since it is a sum of $N!$ terms, Eq. \eqref{eq:33-slater} is the sum, with signs attached, of $N!$ products, each of which is a Hartree product.

Math Tool: Properties of determinants

The properties of the Slater determinant follow immediately from the properties of determinants (University Mathematics, Chapter 8, Section 8.5). (1) Interchanging two rows or two columns changes the sign of the determinant. (2) When two rows are equal, or two columns are equal, the determinant is $0$. (Interchanging the two equal rows in (1) gives $\det=-\det$, so $\det=0$.) (3) The determinant of a product of matrices is the product of the determinants, $\det(AB)=\det A\det B$. In Sections 33.6.2–33.6.5 below, (1) corresponds to antisymmetry, (2) to the exclusion principle, and (3) to invariance under orbital transformations.

Caution: Notation for the Slater determinant

Some references abbreviate the Slater determinant as $\ket{\chi_1\chi_2\cdots\chi_N}$, writing only the list of spin orbitals. However, if one simply writes $\chi_1\chi_2\cdots\chi_N$, it can also be read as a Hartree product (Section 33.4) and cause misunderstanding, so this book writes Eq. \eqref{eq:33-slater} as $\Phi$ and does not use this abbreviation (from Chapter 35 on, when writing matrix elements, we use two-electron kets such as $\ket{\chi_a\chi_b}$ in a different sense, so to distinguish them we call the determinant by the name $\Phi$. Note also that in Chapter 35 the abbreviation enclosed in vertical bars, $\lvert\chi_a\chi_b\rvert$, is defined in Chapter 35, Section 35.1 and is then used only for attaching subscripts to $\Phi$).

33.6.2 Antisymmetry — Axiom 33.1 is satisfied

The Slater determinant satisfies the Pauli exclusion principle (antisymmetry principle), which the Hartree product could not. When the coordinates $\bm{x}_i,\bm{x}_j$ of electron $i$ and electron $j$ are interchanged, the $i$th and $j$th rows of the matrix are interchanged, so by property (1) of determinants the sign changes:

$$ \Phi(\dots,\bm{x}_j,\dots,\bm{x}_i,\dots)=-\Phi(\dots,\bm{x}_i,\dots,\bm{x}_j,\dots). $$

Also, if the order of two spin orbitals $\chi_j$, $\chi_k$ is interchanged, the $j$th and $k$th columns are interchanged and only the sign of $\Phi$ changes. The overall sign (phase) of a wave function has no physical significance (Chapter 8), so the state does not change with the order in which the orbitals are arranged. For two electrons, Eq. \eqref{eq:33-slater} coincides with Eq. \eqref{eq:33-antisym-2} of the previous section:

$$ \Phi(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{vmatrix}\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)&\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\\\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)&\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)\end{vmatrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)-\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\Bigr]. $$

33.6.3 Why is it the “exclusion” principle?

The Slater determinant satisfies the antisymmetry $\Phi(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)=-\Phi(\bm{x}_2,\bm{x}_1)$. But why is this called the “exclusion” principle? Where does the statement that “two electrons cannot occupy the same state” come from? Let us check this for a two-electron system.

Illustration 33.2: Slater determinant of two electrons with the same spin in different spatial orbitals

Let the spatial orbitals of electrons 1 and 2 be $\psi_1(\bm{r})$, $\psi_2(\bm{r})$ (normalized), and suppose that the spin function of both is $\alpha(\sigma)$. The spin orbitals are $\chi_1=\psi_1\alpha$, $\chi_2=\psi_2\alpha$. Find the total wave function $\Phi(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)$ and its normalization integral $\braket{\Phi}{\Phi}$, and examine what happens when $\psi_1=\psi_2$.

Solution. Expand the determinant.

$$ \Phi(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \begin{vmatrix} \psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\alpha(\sigma_1)&\psi_2(\bm{r}_1)\alpha(\sigma_1)\\ \psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\alpha(\sigma_2)&\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)\alpha(\sigma_2) \end{vmatrix} =\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)-\psi_2(\bm{r}_1)\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\Bigr]\alpha(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2). $$

(The spin part $\alpha(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2)$ is common to both terms, so we factored it out.) The total probability of finding the electrons, $\braket{\Phi}{\Phi}$, is obtained by integrating $\abs{\Phi}^2$ over $\bm{r}_1,\bm{r}_2$ and summing over $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$. Expanding the square of the bracketed expression in $\abs{\Phi}^2$ gives four terms:

\begin{align} \braket{\Phi}{\Phi}&=\frac12\iint\Bigl[\abs{\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)}^2\abs{\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)}^2+\abs{\psi_2(\bm{r}_1)}^2\abs{\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)}^2\notag\\ &\qquad-\psi_1^*(\bm{r}_1)\psi_2(\bm{r}_1)\cdot\psi_2^*(\bm{r}_2)\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)-\psi_2^*(\bm{r}_1)\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\cdot\psi_1^*(\bm{r}_2)\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)\Bigr]\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2\times\sum_{\sigma_1}\abs{\alpha(\sigma_1)}^2\sum_{\sigma_2}\abs{\alpha(\sigma_2)}^2 \label{eq:33-norm-2e} \end{align}

Here the leading factor $\tfrac12=(1/\sqrt2)^2$ comes from squaring the coefficient $1/\sqrt{2}$ of the determinant. Each of the spin sums is $1$. The integrals over $\bm{r}_1$ and $\bm{r}_2$ split, term by term, into products of one-electron integrals. Let $S=\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_2}=\int\psi_1^*\psi_2\dd\bm{r}$ (the overlap integral). Then the first and second terms are both $\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_1}\braket{\psi_2}{\psi_2}=1$, the third term is $\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_2}\braket{\psi_2}{\psi_1}=S\,S^*=\abs{S}^2$, and the fourth term is the same. Therefore

$$ \braket{\Phi}{\Phi}=\frac12\bigl(1+1-\abs{S}^2-\abs{S}^2\bigr)=1-\abs{S}^2 . $$

If $\psi_1,\psi_2$ are orthogonal ($S=0$), then $\braket{\Phi}{\Phi}=1$ and $\Phi$ is normalized. Next, let $\psi_1=\psi_2$. Then $S=\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_1}=1$, so $\braket{\Phi}{\Phi}=0$; that is, $\Phi\equiv0$, and such a state does not exist. Indeed, two columns of the determinant are equal ($\chi_1=\chi_2$), and by property (2) of determinants $\Phi=0$. A state in which two electrons with the same spin $\alpha$ occupy the same spatial orbital is impossible. The Slater determinant shows that two electrons cannot occupy exactly the same spin orbital.

For electrons to occupy the same spatial orbital, it is enough that their spins differ. If $\chi_1=\psi\alpha$, $\chi_2=\psi\beta$, then $\Phi=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\psi(\bm{r}_1)\psi(\bm{r}_2)\bigl[\alpha(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2)-\beta(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2)\bigr]$, which is a state with a symmetric spatial part and a singlet spin part, like the ground state of the helium atom (Section 33.5.3). A single spatial orbital can hold at most two electrons, with spin $\uparrow$ and $\downarrow$. This is the content of the “exclusion” principle.

Caution: What is excluded is the “same spin orbital,” not the “same energy”

When two electrons are in the same spatial orbital $\psi_1=\psi_2$ and have the same spin, $\Phi=0$. Merely having “the same orbital energy” does not lead to exclusion. For example, $2p_x$ and $2p_y$ have the same energy but are different spatial orbitals, and two electrons can occupy them even with the same spin (this is used in Hund's rules in Chapter 34).

The Slater determinant has one more important property. When two electrons come to the same position with the same spin ($\bm{x}_1=\bm{x}_2$), the first and second rows of the matrix become equal, so $\Phi=0$. That is, electrons with the same spin cannot approach each other, owing only to the antisymmetry of the wave function and regardless of the Coulomb repulsion (the Fermi hole of Section 33.6.7).

33.6.4 Orthonormality

Theorem 33.2: Orthonormality of Slater determinants

When the spin orbitals $\chi_1,\dots,\chi_N$ are orthonormal ($\braket{\chi_j}{\chi_k}=\delta_{jk}$), the Slater determinant of Eq. \eqref{eq:33-slater} is normalized:

\begin{equation} \braket{\Phi}{\Phi}=\int\Phi^*\Phi\dd\bm{x}_1\cdots\dd\bm{x}_N=1 \label{eq:33-norm-N} \end{equation}

Furthermore, two Slater determinants $\Phi_a$, $\Phi_b$ built from a set of orthonormal spin orbitals are orthogonal to each other if they differ in at least one spin orbital: $\braket{\Phi_a}{\Phi_b}=0$. (That is, Slater determinants built from orthonormal spin orbitals form an orthonormal system.)

Proof

By the definition of the determinant, $\det=\sum_P(-1)^P\prod_i\chi_{P(i)}(\bm{x}_i)$, we can write $\Phi=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\sum_P(-1)^P\,\Pi_P$, where $\Pi_P=\prod_{i=1}^N\chi_{P(i)}(\bm{x}_i)$. Here $\Pi_P$ is the Hartree product in which the spin orbital $\chi_{P(i)}$ is assigned to electron $i$.

$$ \braket{\Phi}{\Phi}=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_P\sum_Q(-1)^{P}(-1)^{Q}\braket{\Pi_P}{\Pi_Q},\qquad \braket{\Pi_P}{\Pi_Q}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\int\chi_{P(i)}^*(\bm{x}_i)\chi_{Q(i)}(\bm{x}_i)\dd\bm{x}_i=\prod_{i=1}^N\delta_{P(i),Q(i)}. $$

(The inner product of Hartree products becomes a product of integrals, one for each electron.) By the orthonormality $\braket{\chi_j}{\chi_k}=\delta_{jk}$, this product equals 1 only when $P(i)=Q(i)$ for all $i$ (that is, $P=Q$), and equals 0 otherwise. Therefore only the $N!$ terms with $P=Q$ survive in the sum, and each of them has $(-1)^{2P}=+1$, so

$$ \braket{\Phi}{\Phi}=\frac{1}{N!}\times N!=1 . $$

Orthogonality: suppose there is a spin orbital $\chi_k$ that appears in $\Phi_a$ but not in $\Phi_b$. In each term of the expansion of $\braket{\Phi_a}{\Phi_b}$, $\chi_k$ is assigned to some electron $i$ in the product from $\Phi_a$, while the same electron in the product from $\Phi_b$ is assigned a spin orbital other than $\chi_k$ (an orthogonal one). The integral over electron $i$ therefore becomes $0$, and every term becomes $0$.

∎

If the one-electron orbitals are orthonormal, the many-electron states built as Slater determinants are also orthonormal, and they can be used as a basis for the space spanned by the states of quantum mechanics. Note that the calculation for $N=2$ ($\braket{\Phi}{\Phi}=1-\abs{S}^2$, Illustration 33.2) shows that when the orbitals are nonorthogonal, the normalization factor deviates from $1/\sqrt{N!}$.

33.6.5 Invariance under orbital transformations

A Slater determinant has one more important property: mixing the $N$ occupied spin orbitals with one another does not change the state. Let the new spin orbitals be $\chi_j'=\sum_{k=1}^N\chi_kU_{kj}$ ($U$ is an $N\times N$ unitary matrix, $U^\dagger U=1$). Using the matrix $M_{ij}=\chi_j(\bm{x}_i)$, we have $M'_{ij}=\chi'_j(\bm{x}_i)=\sum_kM_{ik}U_{kj}$, that is, $M'=MU$. By property (3) of determinants,

\begin{equation} \Phi'=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det(MU)=\det U\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det M=\det U\cdot\Phi \label{eq:33-unitary} \end{equation}

holds. If $U$ is unitary, then $\abs{\det U}=1$, and $\Phi'$ is just $\Phi$ multiplied by a phase factor, so it represents the same state. In other words, what a Slater determinant determines is not the individual orbitals but the subspace spanned by the $N$ orbitals. This freedom is the reason why, in the Hartree–Fock method, we may use either “canonical orbitals” or “localized orbitals” (Chapter 36).

33.6.6 The three-electron Slater determinant — six terms

Let us expand the Slater determinant for $N=3$. By the definition of the determinant, we add the $3!=6$ products obtained by choosing one column from each row, never choosing the same column twice, each with the sign of its permutation (Figure 33.6). Writing the coordinate $\bm{x}_i$ of electron $i$ simply as $(i)$, we have

\begin{equation} \Phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt6}\Bigl[\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)\chi_3(3)+\chi_2(1)\chi_3(2)\chi_1(3)+\chi_3(1)\chi_1(2)\chi_2(3)-\chi_3(1)\chi_2(2)\chi_1(3)-\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)\chi_3(3)-\chi_1(1)\chi_3(2)\chi_2(3)\Bigr] \label{eq:33-slater3} \end{equation}

The sign is $+$ if the arrangement of the chosen columns is obtained from $(1,2,3)$ by an even number of interchanges, and $-$ if it is obtained by an odd number (the identity and the two cyclic permutations are even permutations; the three transpositions are odd permutations). If any two electrons are interchanged, for example electrons 1 and 2, the six terms change sign and go into one another, so that overall $\Phi\to-\Phi$.

Example 33.9: Expanding the Slater determinant of the lithium atom $1\mathrm{s}^22\mathrm{s}$

We express the ground-state electron configuration $1\mathrm{s}^22\mathrm{s}$ of the lithium atom as a Slater determinant of the spin orbitals $\chi_1=\psi_{1s}\alpha$, $\chi_2=\psi_{1s}\beta$, $\chi_3=\psi_{2s}\alpha$. Let $\psi_{1s}$ and $\psi_{2s}$ be orthonormal spatial orbitals. (1) Expand $\Phi$ into a spatial part and a spin part. (2) Verify that $\braket{\Phi}{\Phi}=1$.

Solution. (1) We abbreviate $s_i=\psi_{1s}(\bm{r}_i)$, $t_i=\psi_{2s}(\bm{r}_i)$, $\alpha_i=\alpha(\sigma_i)$, $\beta_i=\beta(\sigma_i)$. Substituting $\chi_1(i)=s_i\alpha_i$, $\chi_2(i)=s_i\beta_i$, $\chi_3(i)=t_i\alpha_i$ into the six terms of Eq. \eqref{eq:33-slater3} gives:

$$ \begin{aligned} &\chi_1(1)\chi_2(2)\chi_3(3)=s_1s_2t_3\,\alpha_1\beta_2\alpha_3, &&-\chi_2(1)\chi_1(2)\chi_3(3)=-s_1s_2t_3\,\beta_1\alpha_2\alpha_3,\\ &\chi_2(1)\chi_3(2)\chi_1(3)=s_1t_2s_3\,\beta_1\alpha_2\alpha_3, &&-\chi_1(1)\chi_3(2)\chi_2(3)=-s_1t_2s_3\,\alpha_1\alpha_2\beta_3,\\ &\chi_3(1)\chi_1(2)\chi_2(3)=t_1s_2s_3\,\alpha_1\alpha_2\beta_3, &&-\chi_3(1)\chi_2(2)\chi_1(3)=-t_1s_2s_3\,\alpha_1\beta_2\alpha_3. \end{aligned} $$

Grouping together the terms that have the same spatial part ($s_1s_2t_3$, $s_1t_2s_3$, $t_1s_2s_3$), we get

$$ \begin{aligned} s_1s_2t_3\bigl(\alpha_1\beta_2-\beta_1\alpha_2\bigr)\alpha_3,\qquad s_1t_2s_3\bigl(\beta_1\alpha_3-\alpha_1\beta_3\bigr)\alpha_2,\qquad t_1s_2s_3\bigl(\alpha_2\beta_3-\beta_2\alpha_3\bigr)\alpha_1 \end{aligned} $$

as the three groups (the second group is $s_1t_2s_3\bigl[\beta_1\alpha_2\alpha_3-\alpha_1\alpha_2\beta_3\bigr]=s_1t_2s_3\alpha_2\bigl(\beta_1\alpha_3-\alpha_1\beta_3\bigr)$, and the third group is $t_1s_2s_3\bigl[\alpha_1\alpha_2\beta_3-\alpha_1\beta_2\alpha_3\bigr]=t_1s_2s_3\alpha_1\bigl(\alpha_2\beta_3-\beta_2\alpha_3\bigr)$). Using the spin singlet $\varsigma_{ij}=\alpha_i\beta_j-\beta_i\alpha_j$ of two electrons $i,j$, we have

$$ \Phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt6}\Bigl[s_1s_2t_3\,\varsigma_{12}\,\alpha_3-s_1t_2s_3\,\varsigma_{13}\,\alpha_2+t_1s_2s_3\,\varsigma_{23}\,\alpha_1\Bigr] $$

This is the state in which “the two electrons in $1\mathrm{s}$ form a singlet and the remaining one electron is in $2\mathrm{s}$ (spin $\alpha$),” written as a sum over the three ways of choosing which of the three electrons is in $2\mathrm{s}$ (since the electrons cannot be distinguished, we cannot decide that “electron 3 is in $2\mathrm{s}$”).

(2) The spatial parts $s_1s_2t_3$, $s_1t_2s_3$, $t_1s_2s_3$ of the three terms are mutually orthogonal because $\psi_{1s}$ and $\psi_{2s}$ are orthogonal (for example, $\braket{s_1s_2t_3}{s_1t_2s_3}=\braket{\psi_{1s}}{\psi_{1s}}\braket{\psi_{1s}}{\psi_{2s}}\braket{\psi_{2s}}{\psi_{1s}}=0$). Therefore $\braket{\Phi}{\Phi}$ is the sum of the normalization integrals of the three terms, $\tfrac16(N_1+N_2+N_3)$. The normalization integral of the spatial part of each term is $1$. The spin part is, for example for the first term, $\sum_{\sigma_1\sigma_2}\abs{\alpha_1\beta_2-\beta_1\alpha_2}^2\sum_{\sigma_3}\abs{\alpha_3}^2=\bigl(1+1\bigr)\times1=2$ ($\abs{\alpha_1\beta_2}^2$ and $\abs{\beta_1\alpha_2}^2$ are each 1, and the cross term vanishes because the sum of $\alpha^*\beta$ is 0). Since $N_1=N_2=N_3=2$, we have $\braket{\Phi}{\Phi}=\tfrac16\times6=1$.

33.6.7 The Fermi hole — electrons with the same spin cannot get close

The antisymmetry of the Slater determinant has a concrete consequence for the distribution of the electrons' positions. For two electrons with the same spin, the Slater determinant becomes $0$ at $\bm{r}_1=\bm{r}_2$ (Section 33.6.3). That is, around the position where one of the electrons is found, there is a region where an electron with the same spin is hard to find. This is called the Fermi hole (also called the exchange hole). If we fix the position $\bm{x}_1$ of one electron and look at the probability as we move the position $\bm{x}_2$ of the other electron, an antisymmetric wave function has a valley near $\bm{x}_2=\bm{x}_1$ where the probability is 0. This valley is the Fermi hole (Figure 33.7(d)). It is an effect distinct from the mutual avoidance of electrons caused by the Coulomb repulsion, and it arises solely from the antisymmetry of the wave function (the Pauli exclusion principle). No such hole forms between electrons with opposite spins.

To see the Fermi hole, let us examine the case of two electrons in a one-dimensional box of width $L$ (Chapter 10). The spatial orbitals are $\psi_1$ ($n=1$) and $\psi_2$ ($n=2$), and we compare the joint probability density $P(x_1,x_2)$ of the positions $x_1$, $x_2$ of the two electrons for the following three spatial wave functions. (a) The Hartree product $\psi_1(x_1)\psi_2(x_2)$. (b) The antisymmetric combination $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\psi_1(x_1)\psi_2(x_2)-\psi_2(x_1)\psi_1(x_2)\bigr]$ (the triplet, whose spin part is symmetric; a Slater determinant with the same spin $\alpha\alpha$). (c) The symmetric combination $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\psi_1(x_1)\psi_2(x_2)+\psi_2(x_1)\psi_1(x_2)\bigr]$ (the spatial part of the singlet, whose spin part is antisymmetric). In addition, in (d) we plot, for all three, the distribution of $x_2$ (the conditional probability density) when $x_1=0.3\,L$ is fixed.

Example 33.10: The mean squared distance $\expval{(x_1-x_2)^2}$ of two electrons in a box

For a box of width $L$, find $\expval{(x_1-x_2)^2}$ for the three spatial wave functions (a) the Hartree product, (b) the antisymmetric combination, and (c) the symmetric combination. You may use $\expval{x}_n=\tfrac{L}{2}$, $\expval{x^2}_n=L^2\Bigl(\dfrac13-\dfrac{1}{2n^2\pi^2}\Bigr)$ (Chapter 10), and $\mel{\psi_1}{\hat{x}}{\psi_2}=-\dfrac{16L}{9\pi^2}$ (Example 10.4 of Chapter 10).

Solution. $(x_1-x_2)^2=x_1^2+x_2^2-2x_1x_2$.

(a) For $\Psi=\psi_1(x_1)\psi_2(x_2)$, electrons 1 and 2 are independent, and $\expval{x_1^2}=\expval{x^2}_1$, $\expval{x_2^2}=\expval{x^2}_2$, $\expval{x_1x_2}=\expval{x}_1\expval{x}_2$. Since $\expval{x^2}_1=L^2\bigl(\tfrac13-\tfrac{1}{2\pi^2}\bigr)=0.28267\,L^2$, $\expval{x^2}_2=L^2\bigl(\tfrac13-\tfrac{1}{8\pi^2}\bigr)=0.32067\,L^2$, and $\expval{x}_1\expval{x}_2=\tfrac{L^2}{4}$, we obtain

$$ \expval{(x_1-x_2)^2}=\bigl(0.28267+0.32067-2\times0.25\bigr)L^2=0.1033\,L^2 . $$

(b), (c) Let $\Psi_\pm=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\psi_1(x_1)\psi_2(x_2)\pm\psi_2(x_1)\psi_1(x_2)\bigr]$ ($+$ is symmetric and $-$ is antisymmetric). Then $\abs{\Psi_\pm}^2=\tfrac12\bigl[\psi_1(x_1)^2\psi_2(x_2)^2+\psi_2(x_1)^2\psi_1(x_2)^2\pm2\psi_1(x_1)\psi_2(x_1)\psi_1(x_2)\psi_2(x_2)\bigr]$, where the $\psi_i$ are taken to be real. For the expectation value of $x_1^2$, the third term gives $\int\psi_1\psi_2x_1^2\dd x_1\cdot\int\psi_1\psi_2\dd x_2$, whose second factor is $0$ by orthogonality, so that term drops out and $\expval{x_1^2}=\tfrac12\bigl(\expval{x^2}_1+\expval{x^2}_2\bigr)$ (and likewise for $\expval{x_2^2}$). For the expectation value of $x_1x_2$, the first and second terms give $\expval{x}_1\expval{x}_2$ and the third term gives $\pm\mel{\psi_1}{\hat{x}}{\psi_2}^2$, so $\expval{x_1x_2}=\expval{x}_1\expval{x}_2\pm\mel{\psi_1}{\hat{x}}{\psi_2}^2$. Therefore

$$ \expval{(x_1-x_2)^2}_\pm=\underbrace{\expval{x^2}_1+\expval{x^2}_2-2\expval{x}_1\expval{x}_2}_{\text{value of (a) }0.1033\,L^2}\mp2\,\mel{\psi_1}{\hat{x}}{\psi_2}^2 . $$

Since $2\mel{\psi_1}{\hat{x}}{\psi_2}^2=2\times\dfrac{256}{81\pi^4}L^2=0.0649\,L^2$, (b) the antisymmetric case gives $0.1033+0.0649=0.1682\,L^2$, and (c) the symmetric case gives $0.1033-0.0649=0.0384\,L^2$.

In the antisymmetric case (the Slater determinant with the same spin), the electrons are farther apart than in the Hartree product ($0.1682>0.1033$), and in the symmetric case they are closer together ($0.0384$). When both electrons occupy the same spatial orbital ($\psi_1(x_1)\psi_1(x_2)$), the value is $2\bigl(\expval{x^2}_1-\expval{x}_1^2\bigr)=0.0653\,L^2$.

This result bears directly on the electron–electron repulsion energy. Since the Coulomb repulsion is larger the closer the electrons are, a state with the same spin and an antisymmetric spatial part (in which the electrons keep apart) has a smaller repulsion energy and therefore a lower energy. This is why, within the same electron configuration, the state with parallel spins (Hund's rules; Chapter 34) is stabilized, and it is the exchange integral $J_{ab}$ (Chapter 35) that gives the energy difference.

Caution: Notation for the exchange integral $J_{ab}$

This book denotes the Coulomb integral by $U_{ab}$ and the exchange integral by $J_{ab}$ (defined in Chapter 35). In the textbook by Szabo and Ostlund and in the companion volume “Density Functional Theory” (AD 07), the Coulomb integral is written $J_{ab}$ and the exchange integral $K_{ab}$, so please be careful about the correspondence. Both are distinct from the $J$ of the total angular momentum of an atom.

33.6.8 The role of the Slater determinant

  • Single configuration: A single Slater determinant is a wave function that specifies one “electron configuration” (which spin orbitals the electrons occupy). If there were no electron–electron interaction, the Slater determinant, which is an antisymmetrized Hartree product, would be an exact eigenfunction, and its energy would be the sum of the one-electron energies, $\sum\varepsilon_i$. When there is an electron–electron interaction, the Slater determinant is only an approximation. The best one (the set of orbitals that minimizes the expectation value of the energy) is the solution of the Hartree–Fock approximation (Chapter 36). The exact wave function is a superposition of many Slater determinants (configuration interaction).
  • Computational cost: The Slater determinant of $N$ electrons is a sum of $N!$ terms; for $N=10$ it has $10!=3\,628\,800$ terms, and for $N=100$ about $10^{158}$ terms. However, the value of a determinant can be found without expanding it, by row reduction of the matrix (an operation count of the order of $N^3$). Moreover, the expectation value of the energy is also expressed as a sum over pairs of orbitals (Chapter 35). The determinant form is what makes many-electron calculations practical.
  • Relation to second quantization: In the occupation-number representation (second quantization), the Slater determinant takes the form $\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_2^\dagger\cdots\hat{c}_N^\dagger\ket{0}$, that is, the vacuum $\ket{0}$ acted on by creation operators, or the form $\ket{n_1n_2\cdots}$ ($n_j=0,1$) in terms of the occupation number of each orbital. The property that the determinant changes sign corresponds to the anticommutation relation of the creation operators, $\acomm{\hat{c}_i^\dagger}{\hat{c}_j^\dagger}=0$ (Chapter 57).

33.7 Summary and Exercises

33.7.1 Summary

  • Atomic units: Nondimensionalizing the equation for the helium atom gives the unit of length $a_0=4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2/(m_{\mathrm e}e_0^2)=0.529\,177\ \text{Å}$ and the unit of energy $E_{\mathrm h}=e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0a_0)=27.2114\ \mathrm{eV}=2\ \mathrm{Ry}$, and, by setting $\hbar=m_{\mathrm e}=e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)=1$, we obtain the expression $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{He}}=-\tfrac12\nabla_1^2-\tfrac12\nabla_2^2-\tfrac{2}{r_1}-\tfrac{2}{r_2}+\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}$. The unit of time is $\hbar/E_{\mathrm h}=24.19\ \mathrm{as}$.
  • The expectation value of the energy for the product wave function $\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)$ is $E_1+E_2+U$. Here $U$ is the Coulomb repulsion energy between the two charge clouds, and for 1s$^2$ of He it is $-2.75\,E_{\mathrm h}=-74.83\ \mathrm{eV}$ (exact value $-79.01\ \mathrm{eV}$).
  • The Hamiltonian of a polyatomic system is the sum of five terms: the kinetic energy of the nuclei, the kinetic energy of the electrons, the nucleus–electron attraction, the nucleus–nucleus repulsion, and the electron–electron repulsion. The mass of a nucleus is more than $10^3$ times that of the electron, and for the same energy an electron is 42.9 times faster than a proton.
  • Born–Oppenheimer approximation: Hold the nuclei fixed and solve the electronic equation to obtain the electronic energy $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{R})$. Then $V_{\mathrm{nn}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ele}}$ is the potential for the nuclei (the potential energy surface), and the nuclei move on it (the adiabatic approximation). In the exact expansion, this is an approximation that drops the nonadiabatic coupling $\Lambda_{mn}$ connecting different electronic states. It is distinct from the mean-field approximation.
  • If there is no electron–electron interaction, the eigenfunction of $\hat{H}=\sum\hat{h}_i$ is the Hartree product $\chi_{k_1}(\bm{x}_1)\cdots\chi_{k_N}(\bm{x}_N)$, and the energy is $\sum\varepsilon_{k_i}$. The probability of finding the electrons is the product of the probabilities for the individual electrons, and the electrons move independently of one another. The coordinate of an electron is $\bm{x}=(\bm{r},\sigma)$, and a spin orbital is $\chi=\psi(\bm{r})\alpha(\sigma)$ or $\psi(\bm{r})\beta(\sigma)$.
  • Electrons are indistinguishable, and the eigenvalues of the exchange operator $\hat{P}_{12}$ are $\pm1$. The wave function of electrons is antisymmetric (the Pauli exclusion principle). A Hartree product is not antisymmetric.
  • The Slater determinant $\Phi=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det[\chi_j(\bm{x}_i)]$ (row = electron, column = spin orbital) is antisymmetric, and if it is built from orthonormal spin orbitals it is orthonormal. Using the same spin orbital twice gives $0$ (exclusion). It is invariant under a unitary transformation of the orbitals, apart from a phase. A Fermi hole forms for electrons with the same spin.
