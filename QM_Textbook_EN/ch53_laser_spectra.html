Chapter 53Stimulated Emission and Lasers, and the Optical Absorption Spectra of Materials

If you point a red laser pointer at a wall in a dark room, a thin, vivid point of light appears. An incandescent light bulb emits light in every direction, at many different wavelengths, and with random phases, whereas a laser emits light at almost a single wavelength, with the phases aligned, and almost entirely in a single direction. The mechanism that produces this difference is the stimulated emission that we learned about in the previous chapter. In stimulated emission, one more photon, exactly identical to the incoming photon (same frequency, direction, polarization, and phase), is born. If the photons multiply from one to two, from two to four, and so on, the light grows stronger like a rolling snowball. Ordinary matter, however, absorbs light and makes it dimmer. As we saw with the Einstein coefficients (Chapter 52), absorption and stimulated emission occur with the same probability. What decides whether the light grows stronger or weaker is just one point: whether there are more “atoms in the upper level” or more “atoms in the lower level.” In the first half of this chapter (Sections 53.1–53.2), starting from this point, we learn why the special state in which the upper level is more populated (population inversion) is necessary, how it can be created (three-level and four-level systems), and how ruby, Nd:YAG, and semiconductor lasers differ.

In the second half (Sections 53.3–53.5), we put the same quantum mechanics of absorption and emission to use in reading the optical absorption spectra of materials. As light travels through a material, its intensity decreases as $I=I_0e^{-\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}d}$. This form is the same as the absorption of light in classical physics (an extension of the refractive index in AD 03 “Introductory University Physics,” Chapter 13, Section 13.3), but the absorption coefficient $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$ varies by many orders of magnitude with the wavelength because the quantum-mechanical level spacings and the transition matrix elements determine it. The second half of this chapter answers the following four questions.

  • Why does glass transmit visible light? (Section 53.4)—Because the photon energy falls short of every level spacing across which a valence electron could be lifted.
  • Why does silicon absorb visible light and appear black? (Section 53.4)—Because photons with energy larger than the band gap lift valence electrons into the conduction band.
  • Why is ruby red? (Section 53.5)—Because the $d$ electrons of $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ absorb green.
  • Why does carbon monoxide absorb infrared radiation at 4.67 μm? (Section 53.5)—Because the molecule climbs one rung of the harmonic oscillator's ladder.

In this chapter we derive the strength and shape of such absorption from Fermi's golden rule (Chapter 51) and the electric dipole approximation (Chapter 52). What differs from the classical-physics “absorption of light” is that absorption occurs as a transition between discrete levels, that its strength is determined by the squared matrix element, and that the strengths of allowed and forbidden transitions differ by four or more orders of magnitude.

53.1 Light Amplification and Population Inversion — The Balance Between Absorption and Stimulated Emission

53.1.1 When light passes through matter, does it increase or decrease?

A beam of monochromatic light (light with a single, definite frequency) is traveling through a material in the $+z$ direction. Suppose that the atoms of the material (molecules or ions will do as well) have a transition between a lower level of energy $E_1$ and an upper level of energy $E_2$ that is resonant with the light ($\hbar\omega\simeq E_2-E_1$). As in the previous chapter, this light causes three processes: absorption, in which an atom in the lower level absorbs one photon and goes up to the upper level; stimulated emission, in which an atom in the upper level is induced by the light to add one photon; and spontaneous emission, in which an atom in the upper level emits light spontaneously even when there is no light. The light of spontaneous emission goes out in almost all directions, with random phases, so it contributes almost nothing to the intensity of the beam. What increases or decreases the beam is just the two processes, absorption and stimulated emission.

Let $N_1$ be the number of atoms in the lower level per unit volume and $N_2$ the number in the upper level (in units of $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$), and let the intensity of the light (the energy carried per unit area and per unit time) be $I\ [\mathrm{W/m^2}]$. Since the energy of one photon is $\hbar\omega$, the number of photons passing through per unit area and per unit time (the photon flux) is $\Phi=I/\hbar\omega$. The probability (per unit time) that a single atom absorbs a photon from light of flux $\Phi$ and moves from the lower level to the upper level is proportional to $\Phi$, so it can be written $\sigma\Phi$. The constant of proportionality $\sigma$ has the dimension of an area ($\mathrm{m^2}$) and is called the absorption cross section (it helps to think of it as the “target area” that catches photons). The magnitude of the cross section is determined by the strength of the transition (the matrix element) and by the detuning from resonance. Stimulated emission has a cross section of the same magnitude. Indeed, when the degeneracies are equal ($g_1=g_2$), the Einstein coefficients satisfied $B_{12}=B_{21}$ (Chapter 52). Therefore the probability per unit time that a single atom in the upper level adds one photon by stimulated emission is also $\sigma\Phi$.

Derivation: Equation for light amplification

Consider a thin layer (thickness $\dd z$) of area $S$ perpendicular to the direction of propagation of the beam. The layer contains $N_1S\,\dd z$ atoms in the lower level and $N_2S\,\dd z$ atoms in the upper level. Per unit time, the number of photons lost by absorption is $\sigma\Phi\cdot N_1S\,\dd z$, and the number of photons gained by stimulated emission is $\sigma\Phi\cdot N_2S\,\dd z$. On the other hand, the change in the flux of photons passing through the layer, multiplied by the area $S$, is $S\,\dd\Phi$ as a change in the number of photons per unit time. Therefore we have

$$ S\,\dd\Phi=\sigma\Phi\,(N_2-N_1)\,S\,\dd z $$

Dividing both sides by $S\,\dd z$ and using $I=\hbar\omega\,\Phi$ ($\hbar\omega$ is a constant), we obtain the following equation.

\begin{equation} \diff{I}{z}=\sigma\,(N_2-N_1)\,I \label{eq:53-gain} \end{equation}

Each side has the dimension $\mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}$, and the equation is dimensionally consistent because $\sigma\,[\mathrm{m^2}]\times N\,[\mathrm{m^{-3}}]$ is $[\mathrm{m^{-1}}]$. ∎

The absorption cross section $\sigma$ is tied to Einstein's $B$ coefficient of the previous chapter. Monochromatic light of intensity $I$ is radiation of energy density $I/c$ (in vacuum; inside a material, replace $c$ by $c/n_{\mathrm r}$), so if we express the shape of the resonance line by a normalized function $g(\omega)$ ($\int g(\omega)\dd\omega=1$; the linewidth was discussed in the previous chapter), the transition probability per atom is $B\,(I/c)\,g(\omega)$. Dividing this by the photon flux $\Phi=I/\hbar\omega$ gives the cross section:

\begin{equation} \sigma(\omega)=\frac{\hbar\omega}{c}\,B\,g(\omega)=\frac{\pi\omega\,\abs{d_{12}}^2}{3\varepsilon_0\hbar c}\,g(\omega) \label{eq:53-sigmaB} \end{equation}

In the second equality we used $B=\pi\abs{d_{12}}^2/(3\varepsilon_0\hbar^2)$ from the previous chapter ($d_{12}$ is the matrix element of the electric dipole). That is, the cross section is proportional to the square of the magnitude of the transition dipole and, near resonance, traces the line shape $g(\omega)$. The sharper the line (the higher the peak of $g$), the larger the cross section at the peak.

The coefficient $\sigma(N_2-N_1)$ on the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:53-gain} is a constant independent of $z$ (when $I$ is small), so the solution is an exponential function:

\begin{equation} I(z)=I_0\exp\bigl[\sigma(N_2-N_1)\,z\bigr] \label{eq:53-gainsol} \end{equation}

Here $I_0$ is the intensity at $z=0$. Depending on the sign of the coefficient in the exponent, the material either weakens or strengthens the light.

Definition 53.1: Absorption, gain, and population inversion

(1) When $N_1\gt N_2$, letting $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\equiv\sigma(N_1-N_2)\gt0$ we have $I=I_0e^{-\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}z}$, and the light is weakened exponentially. $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$ is called the absorption coefficient (unit $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$). This is the Beer–Lambert law (Section 53.3).

(2) When $N_2\gt N_1$, letting $\gamma\equiv\sigma(N_2-N_1)\gt0$ we have $I=I_0e^{\gamma z}$, and the light is strengthened exponentially. $\gamma$ is called the gain coefficient (unit $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$). The state $N_2\gt N_1$, in which there are more atoms in the upper level than in the lower level, is called population inversion.

When the degeneracies differ ($g_1\ne g_2$), by Einstein's relation $g_1B_{12}=g_2B_{21}$, we replace $N_2-N_1$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:53-gain} by $N_2-(g_2/g_1)N_1$. The condition for population inversion is $N_2/g_2\gt N_1/g_1$.

In other words, Beer–Lambert absorption (the light is absorbed by the material and weakens) and laser amplification (the light is wrung out of the material and strengthens) are nothing but two cases of the same Eq. \eqref{eq:53-gain}. Light amplification can also be regarded as “negative absorption.” The name laser (LASER) is an acronym formed from the initial letters of Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.

Intuition: Stimulated emission is an “exact copy”

A photon born by stimulated emission is identical to the incident photon in frequency, direction of travel, polarization, and even phase. In the language of classical physics, this means that an electron (dipole) set vibrating by the incident wave emits a wave in phase with the incident wave, which is added on top of it. In absorption, the atom produces a wave out of phase with the incident wave and thereby weakens it; in stimulated emission it produces a wave in phase and thereby strengthens it. Therefore the amplified light becomes a clean wave with aligned phases (a coherent wave). This is the decisive difference between the light of a light bulb (a collection of spontaneous emissions, with random phases) and laser light.

53.1.2 In thermal equilibrium, it is always absorption

An ordinary material is in thermal equilibrium (temperature $T$). Then the occupation numbers of the levels $E_1$ and $E_2$ obey the Boltzmann distribution,

\begin{equation} \frac{N_2/g_2}{N_1/g_1}=\exp\left(-\frac{E_2-E_1}{k_{\mathrm B}T}\right)=e^{-\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}\ \lt\ 1 \label{eq:53-boltz} \end{equation}

As long as the temperature is positive, the upper level always has fewer atoms (per unit degeneracy). Therefore in thermal equilibrium the coefficient in Eq. \eqref{eq:53-gain} is always negative, and the material absorbs light. For example, for the ruby transition $\hbar\omega=1.786\ \mathrm{eV}$ at room temperature ($k_{\mathrm B}T=0.02585\ \mathrm{eV}$), $N_2/N_1=e^{-69.1}\approx10^{-30}$. Even at the surface temperature of the Sun, $6000\ \mathrm{K}$ ($k_{\mathrm B}T=0.517\ \mathrm{eV}$), it is only $e^{-3.45}=0.032$. In the limit $T\to\infty$ we have $N_2/g_2=N_1/g_1$, but this is merely a transparent state with “neither absorption nor gain.” Population inversion does not correspond to thermal equilibrium at any positive temperature. Unless a state far from thermal equilibrium (a nonequilibrium state) is created from outside, light is not amplified. (If $T$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:53-boltz} is formally made negative, we obtain a population inversion. For this reason a state with population inversion is sometimes called a “negative temperature.”)

Related simulator: Planck's formula (how the intensity and color of thermal radiation change as the temperature is varied. This corresponds to the fact that, even at high temperature, the occupation of the upper level does not exceed that of the lower level).

53.1.3 A two-level system cannot produce a steady population inversion

Then, if we keep pushing the atoms in the lower level up to the upper level with intense light, can we achieve inversion? Consider the stationary state reached when light of radiation energy density $u$ (as in the previous chapter, the energy of the radiation per unit volume and per unit angular frequency) continues to shine on atoms that have only two levels. Let $g_1=g_2$, $B_{12}=B_{21}\equiv B$, and $A_{21}\equiv A$. The change in the occupation number of the upper level is the sum of the increase due to absorption, the decrease due to stimulated emission, and the decrease due to spontaneous emission:

\begin{equation} \diff{N_2}{t}=B\,u\,N_1-B\,u\,N_2-A\,N_2 \label{eq:53-rate2} \end{equation}

In the stationary state $\dd N_2/\dd t=0$, so $Bu\,N_1=(Bu+A)N_2$; that is, we obtain

\begin{equation} \frac{N_2}{N_1}=\frac{B\,u}{A+B\,u}\ \lt\ 1 \label{eq:53-2level} \end{equation}

The right-hand side is smaller than 1 no matter how large $u$ is. In the limit $u\to\infty$ of strong illumination, we merely approach $N_2/N_1\to1$, and $N_2\gt N_1$ never occurs. Writing the occupation numbers in terms of the total number of atoms $N=N_1+N_2$, we get

\begin{equation} N_2=\frac{Bu}{A+2Bu}\,N,\qquad N_1=\frac{A+Bu}{A+2Bu}\,N,\qquad N_2-N_1=-\frac{A}{A+2Bu}\,N\lt0 \label{eq:53-2levelN} \end{equation}

As $u\to\infty$, $N_2\to N/2$ (exactly half), and the absorption coefficient $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=\sigma(N_1-N_2)=\sigma N/(1+2Bu/A)$ tends to 0. This phenomenon is called saturation of absorption (bleaching): when the light is too intense, the absorption weakens and the material becomes transparent. Why does this happen? Because the probabilities of absorption and stimulated emission are equal, each time a photon is absorbed, stimulated emission occurs at almost the same rate, and on balance the intensity of the light cannot be changed.

Caution: The range in which “inversion is impossible” holds

What we have shown here is the result for the stationary state reached under continued excitation with unaligned phases, in rate equations that discard the phase information. If coherent resonant light is applied for half a period of a Rabi oscillation (a π pulse; Chapter 51, Section 51.3), $N_2\gt N_1$ can be achieved temporarily even in a two-level system. However, this is a transient state that disappears on the time scale of dephasing (in solids often of the order of picoseconds) or of spontaneous emission. To keep amplifying light continuously, a three-level or four-level system is needed.

Example 53.1: The two-level system: agreement with thermal equilibrium and saturation

(1) For a two-level system in thermal radiation (temperature $T$, cavity radiation), show that Eq. \eqref{eq:53-2level} agrees with the Boltzmann distribution, Eq. \eqref{eq:53-boltz}. You may use the relation $A/(Bu)=e^{\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}-1$ from the previous chapter. (2) Show that the absorption coefficient of the two-level system is $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}(u)=\alpha_0/(1+2Bu/A)$ ($\alpha_0=\sigma N$), and find the fraction of $\alpha_0$ to which $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$ drops when $Bu/A=10$.

Solution. (1) Letting $x\equiv\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T$, the relation from the previous chapter gives $Bu/A=1/(e^x-1)$. Substituting this into Eq. \eqref{eq:53-2level}: $$ \frac{N_2}{N_1}=\frac{Bu/A}{1+Bu/A}=\frac{1/(e^x-1)}{1+1/(e^x-1)}=\frac{1}{(e^x-1)+1}=e^{-x} $$ which agrees with the Boltzmann distribution, Eq. \eqref{eq:53-boltz}, for $g_1=g_2$. That is, in thermal radiation (light in which spontaneous emission, absorption, and stimulated emission are balanced), the occupation numbers of the atoms settle into the Boltzmann distribution, so thermal radiation never produces a population inversion.

(2) By Eq. \eqref{eq:53-2levelN}, $N_1-N_2=NA/(A+2Bu)=N/(1+2Bu/A)$, so $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=\sigma(N_1-N_2)=\sigma N/(1+2Bu/A)=\alpha_0/(1+2Bu/A)$. For $Bu/A=10$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=\alpha_0/21$, that is, it is weakened to $4.8\,\%$ (the ratio of the occupation numbers is $N_2/N_1=10/11=0.909$). When the light is made intense, the absorption approaches 0, but it never becomes gain. At $Bu/A=1/2$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$ becomes exactly half.

53.1.4 Three-level and four-level systems — using a third level

To create a population inversion, we must get away from the fact that absorption and stimulated emission are “opposite processes between the same two levels.” We make the level that is pumped with light (the pump level) and the level of the laser transition different levels, and arrange for the atoms to fall quickly from the pump level to the upper level of the laser without emitting light (as heat). Then the pump light does not cause stimulated emission (almost no atoms accumulate in the pump level), so atoms can be accumulated in the upper level 2. Figure 53.1 compares the two-level, three-level, and four-level systems.

Three-level system (Figure 53.1(b)): Level 1 is the ground state, level 2 is the upper level of the laser (the metastable level, which has a long lifetime), and level 3 is an excitation band that is pumped by light. The pump light excites atoms in the ground state to level 3 with probability $W$ per unit time, and atoms fall from level 3 to level 2 by fast nonradiative relaxation (rate $S_{32}$). In the absence of laser light, level 2 decays to the ground state at the rate $A_{21}$ ($=1/\tau$, the reciprocal of the lifetime $\tau$; the sum of the spontaneous emission rate and the nonradiative relaxation rate). In the stationary state, the occupation number of level 3 follows from $\dd N_3/\dd t=WN_1-S_{32}N_3=0$ as $N_3=(W/S_{32})N_1$, which is nearly 0 if $S_{32}\gg W$ (atoms do not pile up in level 3). The equation for level 2 becomes $\dd N_2/\dd t=S_{32}N_3-A_{21}N_2=WN_1-A_{21}N_2=0$, so we obtain

\begin{equation} N_2=\frac{W}{A_{21}}\,N_1,\qquad \frac{N_2-N_1}{N}=\frac{x-1}{x+1}\quad\left(x\equiv\frac{W}{A_{21}},\ N=N_1+N_2\right) \label{eq:53-3level} \end{equation}

The condition for population inversion $N_2\gt N_1$ is $x\gt1$, that is, the pumping rate must be greater than the decay rate of level 2 ($W\gt A_{21}=1/\tau$). In this case, more than half of the atoms in the ground state have been raised to level 2. This is the reason why a three-level system requires a large amount of pump energy. Conversely, the longer the lifetime $\tau$ of level 2 (the smaller $A_{21}$), the smaller the required pumping rate. As we saw in the previous chapter, the spontaneous emission rate is $A\propto\omega^3\abs{d_{12}}^2$, so electric-dipole-forbidden transitions (the Laporte-forbidden $d$–$d$ transitions, $f$–$f$ transitions, and spin-forbidden transitions) have long lifetimes and can serve as metastable levels. The “drawback” of forbidden transitions (weak absorption) becomes an “advantage” here (energy can be stored).

Four-level system (Figure 53.1(c)): The lower level 1 of the laser transition is placed above the ground state 0. The lower level 1 empties into the ground state by fast nonradiative relaxation (rate $S_{10}$). In the stationary state, if level 3 is (nearly) empty, the equation for level 2, $\dd N_2/\dd t=WN_0-A_{21}N_2=0$, gives $N_2=(W/A_{21})N_0$, and the equation for level 1, $\dd N_1/\dd t=A_{21}N_2-S_{10}N_1=0$, gives $N_1=(A_{21}/S_{10})N_2\equiv rN_2$ ($r=A_{21}/S_{10}$). From the total number of atoms $N=N_0+N_1+N_2$ we obtain

\begin{equation} \frac{N_2-N_1}{N}=\frac{x\,(1-r)}{1+x\,(1+r)}\ \xrightarrow{\ r\to0\ }\ \frac{x}{1+x} \label{eq:53-4level} \end{equation}

Here $x=W/A_{21}$ (for the derivation, see Exercise 53.1). If $r\lt1$ (the lower level relaxes faster than the upper level decays), $N_2\gt N_1$ holds no matter how small the pumping is. Moreover, if the energy of the lower level 1 is sufficiently higher than $k_{\mathrm B}T$, there is no concern that thermal excitation will make atoms pile up in the lower level. The threshold of the pumping rate for a four-level system is much smaller than that for a three-level system.

Example 53.2: Excitation of a ruby rod: number of photons, fraction excited, and stored energy

Consider a rod of ruby ($\mathrm{Cr^{3+}{:}Al_2O_3}$). The concentration of $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ is $1.6\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ and the volume is $5\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ (the concentration is the value for laser ruby in Chapter 48, Section 48.4). The laser transition is at $694.3\ \mathrm{nm}$, and the lifetime of the upper level is $\tau=3\ \mathrm{ms}$. (1) Find the energy of one photon in eV and in J. (2) Find the number of photons contained in a pulse of energy $1\ \mathrm{J}$, and the fraction of $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ ions needed to emit them. (3) For a population inversion, at least half of the $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ ions (ignoring the degeneracy correction) must be excited to the upper level. How much energy is stored in the excited state of that half? (4) What is the threshold pumping rate $W$ for a three-level system? Also, what is the energy lost per unit time (the power) through spontaneous emission and the like from the excited state of that half of the ions?

Solution. (1) Since $hc=1239.84\ \mathrm{eV\,nm}$, $\hbar\omega=1239.84/694.3=1.786\ \mathrm{eV}$. Converting to J gives $1.786\times1.602\times10^{-19}=2.861\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$.

(2) The number of photons per $1\ \mathrm{J}$ is $1/(2.861\times10^{-19})=3.50\times10^{18}$. The total number of $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ ions in the rod is $1.6\times10^{19}\times5=8.0\times10^{19}$, so, assuming that the transition of one $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ ion is used to emit each photon, the fraction is $3.50\times10^{18}/(8.0\times10^{19})=4.4\,\%$.

(3) Half is $4.0\times10^{19}$ ions, and the stored energy is $4.0\times10^{19}\times2.861\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}=11.4\ \mathrm{J}$. To extract a pulse of $1\ \mathrm{J}$, at least 11 times that energy must be stored beforehand in the excited state.

(4) From the threshold $x=1$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:53-3level}, $W=A_{21}=1/\tau=1/(3\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s})=3.3\times10^2\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The power lost in keeping half of the ions in the excited state is $(4.0\times10^{19}/\tau)\hbar\omega=4.0\times10^{19}\times333\times2.861\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{W}=3.8\times10^3\ \mathrm{W}$ ($763\ \mathrm{W/cm^3}$). Since it is difficult to keep exciting while making up for this large loss, the ruby laser is used in pulsed operation, in which a flashlamp excites the rod all at once in a time shorter than the lifetime $\tau$. (In Chapter 48, taking into account the degeneracy and the occupation fraction of the $\mathrm{R_1}$ level, we took the required fraction of excited ions to be about $48\,\%$.)

53.1.5 Resonator and threshold — gain = loss

A medium with a population inversion multiplies the light by a factor of $e^{\gamma\ell}$ each time the light passes through it ($\ell$ is the length of the medium). Amplification in a single pass, however, is usually not enough. So we place two mirrors (reflection coefficients $R_1$, $R_2$) facing each other at the two ends of the medium and make the light travel back and forth. This pair of mirrors is called a resonator (a Fabry–Pérot resonator). In each round trip the intensity is amplified by a factor of $e^{2\gamma\ell}$ and reduced by a factor of $R_1R_2$ at the mirrors (diffraction loss, scattering loss, and so on are ignored here). The condition that the intensity returns exactly to its original value in one round trip is the lasing threshold:

\begin{equation} R_1R_2\,e^{2\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}\ell}=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \gamma_{\mathrm{th}}=\frac{1}{2\ell}\ln\frac{1}{R_1R_2},\qquad \Delta N_{\mathrm{th}}=\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}}{\sigma}\equiv(N_2-N_1)_{\mathrm{th}} \label{eq:53-threshold} \end{equation}

When the gain coefficient $\gamma=\sigma(N_2-N_1)$ exceeds this threshold value $\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}$, the part of the spontaneously emitted light that travels along the axis grows stronger with each round trip and becomes laser light. In other words, the threshold is the point at which the gain becomes equal to the loss. The resonator has one more important role. Only standing waves that fit within an optical path length of $n_{\mathrm r}\ell$ between the mirrors ($n_{\mathrm r}$ is the refractive index of the medium) reinforce one another, so only the wavelengths (longitudinal modes) satisfying $2n_{\mathrm r}\ell=p\lambda$ ($p$ is an integer) can lase. The frequency spacing between adjacent modes is $\Delta\nu=c/(2n_{\mathrm r}\ell)$.

Derivation: The stationary state above threshold (a simple model of a four-level system)

Let $n_{\mathrm{ph}}$ be the photon number density in the resonator. Since light travels through the medium at speed $c/n_{\mathrm r}$, with gain coefficient $\gamma$ the density $n_{\mathrm{ph}}$ increases per unit time at the rate $(c/n_{\mathrm r})\gamma n_{\mathrm{ph}}$, and because of the resonator loss (corresponding to the loss per unit length $\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}$) it decreases at the rate $(c/n_{\mathrm r})\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}n_{\mathrm{ph}}$:

$$ \diff{n_{\mathrm{ph}}}{t}=\frac{c}{n_{\mathrm r}}\,(\gamma-\gamma_{\mathrm{th}})\,n_{\mathrm{ph}},\qquad \gamma=\sigma N_2\quad(N_1\simeq0) $$

The equation for the upper level, with the pump ($R_{\mathrm p}$ per unit volume and per unit time), the spontaneous emission $N_2/\tau$, and the stimulated emission $(c/n_{\mathrm r})\sigma N_2n_{\mathrm{ph}}$, is $\dd N_2/\dd t=R_{\mathrm p}-N_2/\tau-(c/n_{\mathrm r})\sigma N_2n_{\mathrm{ph}}$. In the stationary state with $n_{\mathrm{ph}}\gt0$, the equation for $n_{\mathrm{ph}}$ gives $\gamma=\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}$; that is, the population of the upper level is pinned at $N_2=\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}/\sigma=N_{\mathrm{th}}$ (gain saturation). Substituting this into the equation for the upper level gives $(c/n_{\mathrm r})\sigma N_{\mathrm{th}}n_{\mathrm{ph}}=R_{\mathrm p}-N_{\mathrm{th}}/\tau$, that is,

$$ n_{\mathrm{ph}}=\frac{n_{\mathrm r}}{c\,\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}}\,\bigl(R_{\mathrm p}-R_{\mathrm{th}}\bigr),\qquad R_{\mathrm{th}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{th}}}{\tau} $$

Even if the pump is made stronger than the threshold $R_{\mathrm{th}}$, the magnitude of the population inversion $N_2$ does not increase beyond $N_{\mathrm{th}}$, and all of the excess pump energy goes into the photon number $n_{\mathrm{ph}}$ of the laser light. This is the reason why, once the laser output exceeds the threshold, it increases in proportion to the pump intensity. ∎

Example 53.3: Threshold of a ruby rod, compared with a four-level system

At the two ends of a ruby rod of length $\ell=10\ \mathrm{cm}$ we place mirrors with reflection coefficients $R_1=1$ (a fully reflecting mirror) and $R_2=0.9$. Let the stimulated-emission cross section of the $\mathrm{R_1}$ line (694.3 nm) be $\sigma=2.5\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$ (a literature value; we use this value as an effective one that includes the degeneracy and so on), and let the concentration of $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ be $N=1.6\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$. (1) Find the threshold gain coefficient $\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}$ and the population inversion $\Delta N_{\mathrm{th}}$. (2) Treating the ruby as a three-level system, find the fraction $N_2/N$ of atoms that must be in the upper level at threshold. (3) For a (hypothetical) crystal with four levels and the same $\sigma$, what fraction must be excited to produce the same $\Delta N_{\mathrm{th}}$? By what factor do the stored energies differ? (4) Take the refractive index of the rod to be $n_{\mathrm r}=1.76$ and find the longitudinal-mode spacing $\Delta\nu$.

Solution. (1) From Eq. \eqref{eq:53-threshold}, $\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}=\dfrac{1}{2\times10\ \mathrm{cm}}\ln\dfrac{1}{1\times0.9}=\dfrac{0.10536}{20}\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}=5.27\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. $\Delta N_{\mathrm{th}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}/\sigma=5.27\times10^{-3}/(2.5\times10^{-20})=2.1\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$.

(2) In a three-level system, from $N_1+N_2=N$ and $N_2-N_1=\Delta N_{\mathrm{th}}$ we obtain $N_2=(N+\Delta N_{\mathrm{th}})/2$, so $N_2/N=\dfrac12\left(1+\dfrac{2.1\times10^{17}}{1.6\times10^{19}}\right)=\dfrac12(1+0.0132)=0.507$. That is, it suffices to excite only 0.7% more than half. What determines the threshold is almost entirely the “half” condition, and it depends little on the reflection coefficients of the mirrors or on $\sigma$. (From Eq. \eqref{eq:53-3level}, the threshold is $x=(1+0.0132)/(1-0.0132)=1.027$; this is the red point in Figure 53.2.)

(3) In a four-level system $N_1\simeq0$, so $N_2=\Delta N_{\mathrm{th}}$, and the fraction is $2.1\times10^{17}/1.6\times10^{19}=1.3\,\%$ (from $x/(1+x)=0.0132$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:53-4level} we obtain $x=0.0134$; this is the blue point in Figure 53.2). The ratio of the stored energies is $0.507/0.0132=38$ times. The threshold energy of the four-level system is smaller by more than an order of magnitude.

(4) $\Delta\nu=c/(2n_{\mathrm r}\ell)=3.00\times10^8/(2\times1.76\times0.10)=8.5\times10^8\ \mathrm{Hz}=0.85\ \mathrm{GHz}$. Within the width of the $\mathrm{R_1}$ line of ruby (of the order of several hundred GHz at room temperature), several hundred longitudinal modes line up.

53.2 Lasers — Ruby, Nd:YAG, and Semiconductor Lasers

The principle of amplification by stimulated emission was first realized with microwaves, which have long wavelengths. In 1954, Gordon, Zeiger, and Townes used the population inversion of ammonia molecules to build a microwave amplifier (the maser; MASER stands for Microwave Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation). If only the molecules in the upper level, whose energy rises in an electric field, are selected from a molecular beam by an inhomogeneous electric field and sent into a resonator, a population inversion is obtained (an application of Chapter 28, and Chapter 50). In 1958, Schawlow and Townes proposed a way to extend the same principle to visible and infrared light (a resonator formed by placing the medium between two mirrors), and in 1960 Maiman succeeded in obtaining the first laser oscillation, with ruby. In this section we apply the ideas of the three-level and four-level systems from the previous section to three real types of laser.

53.2.1 The ruby laser — the first three-level laser

Ruby is a crystal in which some of the $\mathrm{Al^{3+}}$ in corundum $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$ is replaced by $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ ($3d^3$) (about $0.05\,\%$ by weight, converted to $\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}$; Chapter 48, Section 48.4). The levels of $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ are shown in Figure 53.3. From the ground state ${}^4A_2$, the ions are excited with light from a xenon flashlamp into two spin-allowed, broad absorption bands, ${}^4T_2$ ($555.6\ \mathrm{nm}$, about $18\,000\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$) and ${}^4T_1$ ($404.9\ \mathrm{nm}$, about $24\,700\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$). The excited $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ falls to the sharp level ${}^2E$ (about $14\,400\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, $1.786\ \mathrm{eV}$) by nonradiative relaxation in picoseconds to nanoseconds, handing the excess energy to the lattice as heat. The transition from ${}^2E$ to the ground state ${}^4A_2$ is spin-forbidden (doublet → quartet), so the lifetime is long, about $3\ \mathrm{ms}$. Excited $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ ions accumulate in this metastable level ${}^2E$, and when they reach half or more of the ground-state population (Section 53.1.4), stimulated emission exceeds absorption, and red light of wavelength $694.3\ \mathrm{nm}$ ($\hbar\omega=1.786\ \mathrm{eV}$) is amplified. This is exactly the three-level system of the previous section. Because the excitation band is broad, light from a lamp close to white can be used efficiently. This is the advantage, for the laser, of the nature of the $d$–$d$ absorption bands of ruby (the coupling between the electrons and the lattice vibrations is strong, and the bands are broad; Section 53.5). The laser transition ${}^2E\to{}^4A_2$, on the other hand, leaves the electron configuration unchanged (it remains $(t_{2g})^3$) and the bond length unchanged, so the line is sharp (Chapter 48).

Because the ruby laser is a three-level system, its threshold is high. As we saw in Example 53.2, at least $11\ \mathrm{J}$ must be stored as excited states in a rod of $5\ \mathrm{cm^3}$. For this reason it operates not continuously but in pulsed operation with a flashlamp. Today the more efficient four-level systems are the mainstream, but ruby has gone down in history as the first crystal to demonstrate that “a laser can be made from stimulated emission” (T. H. Maiman, Nature 187, 493 (1960)).

53.2.2 The Nd:YAG laser — a four-level solid-state laser

Nd:YAG is a crystal in which some of the $\mathrm{Y^{3+}}$ (about 1 atomic percent) in yttrium aluminum garnet ($\mathrm{Y_3Al_5O_{12}}$, YAG) is replaced by neodymium $\mathrm{Nd^{3+}}$ ($4f^3$). The lasing wavelength is $1064\ \mathrm{nm}$ (photon energy $1.165\ \mathrm{eV}$), which corresponds to the ${}^4F_{3/2}\to{}^4I_{11/2}$ transition of $\mathrm{Nd^{3+}}$ (Geusic and coworkers achieved lasing in 1964). The $4f$ electrons are surrounded by the outer $5s$ and $5p$ electrons and are therefore hardly affected by the crystal field, so the lines of the $f$–$f$ transitions are sharp (as with the R line of ruby, the lines are sharp because the coupling between the electrons and the lattice vibrations is weak. The reasons, however, are different: in ruby it is because ${}^2E$ and ${}^4A_2$ have the same electron configuration $(t_{2g})^3$ and the bond length does not change, whereas in $\mathrm{Nd^{3+}}$ it is because the $4f$ electrons are shielded by the $5s$ and $5p$ electrons), and the stimulated-emission cross section is large (about $3\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, about 10 times that of ruby). The lifetime of the upper level ${}^4F_{3/2}$ is about $230\ \mu\mathrm{s}$, which is long because the $f$–$f$ transition is Laporte-forbidden.

The reason this system becomes a four-level system is as follows. (i) The pumping is done by absorption from the ground state ${}^4I_{9/2}$ into the absorption bands near $808\ \mathrm{nm}$ ($1.534\ \mathrm{eV}$) (${}^4F_{5/2}$ and ${}^2H_{9/2}$, which correspond to level 3), and from there the ions fall quickly and nonradiatively to the upper level ${}^4F_{3/2}$ (level 2). (ii) The lower level of the laser transition, ${}^4I_{11/2}$ (level 1), lies only about $2000\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ ($0.25\ \mathrm{eV}$) above the ground state, and from here the ions relax nonradiatively to the ground state in a nanosecond or less and the level becomes empty. Thermal excitation at room temperature gives a Boltzmann factor of only $e^{-2000/208.5}=7\times10^{-5}$ ($k_{\mathrm B}T=208.5\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$), so there are almost no atoms in the lower level. Therefore $N_1\simeq0$, and the $x/(1+x)$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:53-4level} can be used. The upper bound on the efficiency of conversion from the pump photon $1.534\ \mathrm{eV}$ to the laser photon $1.165\ \mathrm{eV}$ (the quantum efficiency) is $1.165/1.534=0.76$, and the remaining $24\,\%$ becomes heat. If the infrared $1064\ \mathrm{nm}$ light is converted in a nonlinear crystal into the second harmonic (half the wavelength, $532\ \mathrm{nm}$, $2.33\ \mathrm{eV}$), green laser light (the green of a laser pointer) is obtained.

53.2.3 Semiconductor lasers

A semiconductor laser (laser diode, LD) operates simply by passing a current through it, is small, and is efficient. Most of the lasers around us, such as those used in optical communication, optical discs, and laser pointers, are semiconductor lasers. The biggest difference from an atomic laser is that the “levels” are bands and that the pump is not light but current injection.

Population-inversion condition in a semiconductor. Consider transitions between a valence-band state $\ket{v,\bm{k}}$ and a conduction-band state $\ket{c,\bm{k}}$ (the photon wavenumber is negligible, so $\bm{k}$ does not change; Section 53.4). Let $f_{\mathrm c}$ be the probability that the conduction-band state is occupied by an electron, and $f_{\mathrm v}$ the probability that the valence-band state is occupied by an electron (these are values of the Fermi distribution function, that is, occupation probabilities, and are a different quantity from the oscillator strength $f$ that appears later). For stimulated emission to occur, there must be an electron in $\ket{c,\bm{k}}$ and, moreover, $\ket{v,\bm{k}}$ must be empty, so its probability is proportional to $f_{\mathrm c}(1-f_{\mathrm v})$. For absorption to occur, there must be an electron in $\ket{v,\bm{k}}$ and $\ket{c,\bm{k}}$ must be empty, so it is proportional to $f_{\mathrm v}(1-f_{\mathrm c})$. Since the matrix element of the transition is the same, the net gain is proportional to

$$ f_{\mathrm c}(1-f_{\mathrm v})-f_{\mathrm v}(1-f_{\mathrm c})=f_{\mathrm c}-f_{\mathrm v} $$

In an ordinary semiconductor the valence band is almost full ($f_{\mathrm v}\simeq1$) and the conduction band is almost empty ($f_{\mathrm c}\simeq0$), so $f_{\mathrm c}-f_{\mathrm v}\lt0$, and the result is absorption. Population inversion means $f_{\mathrm c}\gt f_{\mathrm v}$, which says that “at the same $\bm{k}$, the conduction-band state holds more electrons than the valence-band state.” In a semiconductor into which a current is injected, the electrons and holes each reach thermal equilibrium quickly within their own bands (picoseconds), whereas electron–hole recombination is much slower (nanoseconds). The electrons in the conduction band can therefore be regarded as obeying a Fermi distribution with quasi-Fermi level $F_{\mathrm c}$, and those in the valence band a Fermi distribution with quasi-Fermi level $F_{\mathrm v}$ (“quasi-” means that the electrons and holes are regarded as being in separate quasi-equilibria, to each of which a separate Fermi level is assigned), namely $f_{\mathrm c}=1/[e^{(E_{\mathrm c}-F_{\mathrm c})/k_{\mathrm B}T}+1]$ and $f_{\mathrm v}=1/[e^{(E_{\mathrm v}-F_{\mathrm v})/k_{\mathrm B}T}+1]$. Then $f_{\mathrm c}\gt f_{\mathrm v}$ is equivalent to $E_{\mathrm c}-F_{\mathrm c}\lt E_{\mathrm v}-F_{\mathrm v}$, that is, to

\begin{equation} F_{\mathrm c}-F_{\mathrm v}\ \gt\ E_{\mathrm c}-E_{\mathrm v}=\hbar\omega\ \ge\ E_{\mathrm g} \label{eq:53-BD} \end{equation}

(the Bernard–Duraffourg condition, 1961). In other words, when electrons and holes are injected so densely that the difference between the quasi-Fermi levels of the electrons and holes exceeds the band gap, light of energy $E_{\mathrm g}\le\hbar\omega\lt F_{\mathrm c}-F_{\mathrm v}$ is amplified. This condition corresponds to applying a forward voltage $V_{\mathrm f}$ large enough that $e_0V_{\mathrm f}\simeq F_{\mathrm c}-F_{\mathrm v}\gt E_{\mathrm g}$ ($e_0V_{\mathrm f}$ is the energy gained by a charge $e_0$ across a voltage $V_{\mathrm f}$) (this is why a GaAs semiconductor laser operates at a voltage of about 1.5 V or more).

The pn junction and the double heterostructure. When a $p$-type and an $n$-type semiconductor are joined (a pn junction) and a current is passed in the forward direction, electrons are injected from the $n$ side to the $p$ side and holes from the $p$ side to the $n$ side, and they recombine near the junction and emit light. This is the light-emitting diode (LED). In 1962 Hall and coworkers succeeded in making the first semiconductor laser oscillate, using a GaAs pn junction (a homojunction), but the injected electrons and holes diffuse as far as several $\mu\mathrm{m}$ from the junction plane and thin out, so the current required for population inversion was large, and the device could not operate continuously except at low temperature. The solution to this problem was the double heterostructure (proposed by Alferov and Kroemer, who received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2000; continuous operation at room temperature was achieved around 1970). As in Figure 53.4, a thin active layer with a small band gap (GaAs; thickness about $0.1\ \mu\mathrm{m}$) is sandwiched between two cladding layers with a large gap (the outer layers enclosing the active layer; $\mathrm{Al}_x\mathrm{Ga}_{1-x}\mathrm{As}$). The band offsets between the active layer and the cladding layers ($\Delta E_{\mathrm c}$ in the conduction band, $\Delta E_{\mathrm v}$ in the valence band) confine the electrons and holes to the active layer, so a high density can be maintained in the thin layer. Moreover, GaAs, whose gap is small, has a high refractive index (larger than that of AlGaAs), so the light, too, is confined to the active layer, which acts as a waveguide. In this way the threshold is exceeded with a small current.

Materials and wavelength. The lasing wavelength of a semiconductor laser is determined almost entirely by the band gap $E_{\mathrm g}$ of the active layer: $\lambda\,[\mathrm{nm}]\simeq1239.84/E_{\mathrm g}\,[\mathrm{eV}]$. GaAs ($E_{\mathrm g}=1.42\ \mathrm{eV}$, room temperature) lases in the infrared at $873\ \mathrm{nm}$, and adding Al to widen the gap shortens the wavelength. Being able to design the gap freely by changing the composition of the material is the greatest strength of semiconductor lasers (Table 53.2). Note that an indirect-gap semiconductor such as Si cannot emit light efficiently, because the recombination of an electron and a hole requires a phonon, and so it cannot be used for lasers (the reason is given in Section 53.4).

Example 53.4: Designing the lasing wavelength: band gap and photon energy

(1) Find the wavelengths corresponding to the gaps of GaAs ($E_{\mathrm g}=1.42\ \mathrm{eV}$) and GaN ($3.4\ \mathrm{eV}$). (2) For InGaN, obtained by adding In to GaN to make $E_{\mathrm g}=3.0\ \mathrm{eV}$, and for blue light of wavelength $450\ \mathrm{nm}$, find the photon energies. (3) Find the photon energies of the $1064\ \mathrm{nm}$ line of the Nd:YAG laser, of the $808\ \mathrm{nm}$ pump light, and of the $532\ \mathrm{nm}$ second harmonic, and find the quantum efficiency for the conversion from $808\ \mathrm{nm}$ to $1064\ \mathrm{nm}$.

Solution. We use $\lambda\,[\mathrm{nm}]=1239.84/E\,[\mathrm{eV}]$. (1) For GaAs, $1239.84/1.42=873\ \mathrm{nm}$ (near-infrared); for GaN, $1239.84/3.4=365\ \mathrm{nm}$ (ultraviolet). (2) For $E_{\mathrm g}=3.0\ \mathrm{eV}$, $1239.84/3.0=413\ \mathrm{nm}$ (blue-violet). For $450\ \mathrm{nm}$, $1239.84/450=2.76\ \mathrm{eV}$. The gap of GaN ($3.4\ \mathrm{eV}$) by itself corresponds to the ultraviolet ($365\ \mathrm{nm}$), but adding In lowers $E_{\mathrm g}$ and gives light from blue-violet to blue ($405$–$450\ \mathrm{nm}$). (3) $1239.84/1064=1.165\ \mathrm{eV}$, $1239.84/808=1.534\ \mathrm{eV}$, and $1239.84/532=2.331\ \mathrm{eV}$. The quantum efficiency is $1.165/1.534=808/1064=0.759$, and $24\,\%$ of the energy of a pump photon becomes heat.

Example 53.5: Threshold gain of a semiconductor laser

Cleave a GaAs crystal of length $\ell=300\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ (making use of the fact that a crystal splits cleanly along particular crystal planes), and use the cleaved faces directly as mirrors. Take the refractive index of the crystal to be $n_{\mathrm r}=3.6$, and find the reflection coefficient $R$ of a cleaved face (the boundary between the crystal and air) and the threshold gain coefficient $\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}$. Assume that the only losses are the mirrors at the two ends. Also check that the gain may be small compared with the magnitude of the absorption coefficient, $10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (Section 53.3).

Solution. The reflection coefficient at normal incidence is $R=\left(\dfrac{n_{\mathrm r}-1}{n_{\mathrm r}+1}\right)^2=\left(\dfrac{2.6}{4.6}\right)^2=0.319$ (about $32\,\%$) (for the refractive index, see AD 03, “Introductory University Physics,” Chapter 13, Section 13.3). Setting $R_1=R_2=R$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:53-threshold}, we obtain $\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}=\dfrac{1}{2\ell}\ln\dfrac{1}{R^2}=\dfrac{1}{\ell}\ln\dfrac1R=\dfrac{1.141}{0.030\ \mathrm{cm}}=38\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. Let the absorption coefficient when the valence band is full and the conduction band is empty be $\alpha_0\sim10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (Section 53.3). For a general occupation the absorption coefficient can then be written $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=\alpha_0(f_{\mathrm v}-f_{\mathrm c})$, so the gain is $\gamma=\alpha_0(f_{\mathrm c}-f_{\mathrm v})$. The value of $f_{\mathrm c}-f_{\mathrm v}$ needed for $\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}=38\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ is only $38/10^4=0.0038$. In a material whose absorption has the large value of $10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, a slight excess of electrons and holes (the difference in occupation becoming positive by $0.4\,\%$) is enough to reach the threshold gain. Thanks to the large transition intensity of semiconductors, a laser can be made from a tiny crystal of $0.3\ \mathrm{mm}$, instead of a ruby rod (threshold $5\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, length $10\ \mathrm{cm}$).

However, this estimate is optimistic in two respects. (i) It assumes that all of the light passes through the active layer. In reality, the fraction of the light that lies within the active layer (the optical confinement factor $\Gamma$) is only about $0.3$ for a double heterostructure with a thickness of about $0.1\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ ($0.01$–$0.05$ for a quantum well), so the material gain of the active layer must grow as large as $\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}/\Gamma$. (ii) Internal losses due to free-carrier absorption and scattering also add to the threshold gain. When these are included, the threshold material gain is of the order of $10^2$ to several hundred $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, and the required $f_{\mathrm c}-f_{\mathrm v}$ is of the order of a few $\%$. Moreover, $\alpha_0=10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ is the value at about $0.1\ \mathrm{eV}$ above the absorption edge; the value near $\hbar\omega\simeq E_{\mathrm g}$, where lasing occurs, is smaller still. Please read the estimate above only as an order-of-magnitude argument: “since the absorption in a semiconductor is large, a slight excess is enough to reach the threshold.” The double heterostructure is a structure that raises the electron and hole densities and $\Gamma$ at the same time.

53.2.4 Quantum cascade lasers — wavelength independent of the gap

The wavelength of a semiconductor laser is determined by $E_{\mathrm g}$, so light with a wavelength longer than that corresponding to the gap of the material (mid-infrared to far-infrared) cannot be produced by an ordinary semiconductor laser. What was devised for this purpose is the quantum cascade laser (QCL), which uses transitions between the levels of a quantum well (intersubband) within a single band (the conduction band) (Faist and coworkers achieved lasing in 1994. Science 264, 553 (1994)). The spacing of the “particle in a box” levels of Chapter 10, $E_n=\dfrac{n^2h^2}{8m^*L^2}=\dfrac{\pi^2\hbar^2n^2}{2m^*L^2}$ (determined by the width $L$ of the well), becomes the photon energy. Only electrons are involved (unipolar), so an electron emits one photon in one quantum-well stage, then advances by tunneling to the next stage (period) and emits a photon again, and it repeats this for several tens of stages (cascade). A single electron can emit many photons.

Example 53.6: Wavelength of an intersubband transition in a quantum well

Approximate the conduction-band electron (effective mass $m^*=0.067m_{\mathrm e}$; for the effective mass, see Chapter 41, Section 41.5) of a GaAs quantum well (width $L=10\ \mathrm{nm}$) by an infinite well (Chapter 10). Find the transition energy and the wavelength for $n=1\to2$. What happens if we use the free-electron mass $m_{\mathrm e}$?

Solution. From $E_n=n^2h^2/(8m^*L^2)=\pi^2\hbar^2n^2/(2m^*L^2)$ we obtain $E_2-E_1=3h^2/(8m^*L^2)=3\pi^2\hbar^2/(2m^*L^2)$. For $m^*=m_{\mathrm e}$ and $L=1\ \mathrm{nm}$, $h^2/(8m_{\mathrm e}L^2)=0.3760\ \mathrm{eV}$, so for $L=10\ \mathrm{nm}$ ($L^2$ is 100 times larger) it is $3.760\ \mathrm{meV}$, and for $m^*=0.067m_{\mathrm e}$ (the mass becomes $1/0.067$ times) we get $E_1=3.760/0.067=56.1\ \mathrm{meV}$. Therefore $E_2-E_1=3E_1=168.4\ \mathrm{meV}$, and the wavelength is $1239.84/0.1684=7.36\times10^3\ \mathrm{nm}=7.4\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ (mid-infrared). With the free-electron mass, $E_2-E_1=3\times3.760=11.3\ \mathrm{meV}$ and the wavelength is $110\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ (far-infrared). By changing the width of the well ($\lambda\propto L^2$), the wavelength can be designed from the mid-infrared to the far-infrared without changing the band gap. (A real quantum well has a finite height, so its levels lie lower than these infinite-well values and the spacing is narrower. This is only a rough guide to the wavelength.)

Application: Uses of lasers, and how they differ from LEDs

  • Optical discs: The CD uses a semiconductor laser of $780\ \mathrm{nm}$, the DVD one of $650\ \mathrm{nm}$, and Blu-ray one of $405\ \mathrm{nm}$. The diameter of the focused spot is proportional to the wavelength because of diffraction ($\propto\lambda/\mathrm{NA}$), so the shorter the wavelength, the finer the recording that is possible (the capacity of one layer is about $0.7\ \mathrm{GB}$, $4.7\ \mathrm{GB}$, and $25\ \mathrm{GB}$, respectively). The blue-violet semiconductor laser grew out of the crystal-growth technology for nitride semiconductors (such as GaN) (Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano, and Shuji Nakamura; the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics, for their work on the blue LED).
  • Optical communication: A silica ($\mathrm{SiO_2}$) optical fiber has its smallest optical loss near a wavelength of $1.55\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ (about $0.2\ \mathrm{dB/km}$). InGaAsP semiconductor lasers emit light at $1.3\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ and $1.55\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ and support the trunk lines of the Internet.
  • Materials processing and measurement: Cutting, welding, and marking with Nd:YAG ($1064\ \mathrm{nm}$) and fiber lasers; processing with the carbon dioxide laser (which uses vibrational levels of the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ molecule; $10.6\ \mu\mathrm{m}$); and trace detection of gases with QCLs (infrared absorption of molecules; Section 53.5).
  • Difference between an LED and a laser diode: An LED emits light by the spontaneous emission from electrons and holes; its spectral width is broad, several times $\sim k_{\mathrm B}T$ (tens of nm in wavelength), and the light is not in phase and goes out in all directions. In a laser diode, once the threshold current is exceeded, stimulated emission becomes dominant; the spectral width becomes narrow (at most $0.1\ \mathrm{nm}$ for a single mode), the light is directional, and it can be modulated rapidly (GHz). Even with the pn junction of the same semiconductor, whether spontaneous emission or stimulated emission is dominant makes it a different light source.

53.3 Absorption Coefficient, Molar Absorption Coefficient, and Oscillator Strength

In Section 53.1 we saw that when light passes through an ordinary material in which many atoms are in the lower level ($N_1\gt N_2$), it is weakened according to Eq. \eqref{eq:53-gain}. In this section we organize the quantities that measure this “weakening”—the absorption coefficient $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$, the absorption cross section $\sigma$, the absorbance $A$, and the molar absorption coefficient $\varepsilon$—and then derive from Fermi's golden rule the oscillator strength $f$, which expresses the strength of an absorption by a single dimensionless number. To say that the $d$–$d$ absorption is “weak because it is forbidden” means that, in terms of $f$, it is about $10^{-4}$. Note that we attached the subscript abs to the symbol $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$ for the absorption coefficient, in order to distinguish it from the fine-structure constant $\alpha$ and from the polarizability $\alpha_{\mathrm p}$. In this chapter we denote the cross section by $\sigma$ (in Chapter 48 we wrote $\sigma_{\mathrm{abs}}$ to distinguish it from the $\sigma$ orbital; it is the same quantity).

53.3.1 The Beer–Lambert law

In a thin layer of thickness $\dd z$, suppose that there are $N$ absorbers (atoms or molecules) in the lower level per unit volume, each with cross section $\sigma$. The atoms in the upper level are negligible ($N_2\simeq0$, $N_1\simeq N$; this is almost always the case for electronic levels at room temperature). Eq. \eqref{eq:53-gain} becomes $\dd I/\dd z=-\sigma NI$, and the solution, giving the intensity after passing through a sample of thickness $d$, is

\begin{equation} I=I_0\,e^{-\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}d},\qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=N\sigma \label{eq:53-beer} \end{equation}

(the Beer–Lambert law). Here $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$ is the absorption coefficient, and its reciprocal $1/\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$ is the absorption length (the thickness over which the intensity of light decreases to $1/e=0.368$ of its initial value). At a thickness of three times the absorption length, the intensity decreases to $e^{-3}=5\,\%$.

Caution: Conditions under which the Beer–Lambert law holds

Eq. \eqref{eq:53-beer} holds under the following conditions. (i) The light is weak, and the occupation of the upper level is negligible (for strong light the absorption saturates, as in Section 53.1.3, and $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$ depends on $I$). (ii) No light is lost to scattering. (iii) $I_0$ is the intensity of the light entering the sample, after subtracting the part reflected at the surface of the sample. A semiconductor has a large refractive index, and the reflection coefficient at normal incidence is as large as $R\approx0.3$ (Example 53.5), so solar cells are fitted with an antireflection coating to reduce this loss.

In chemical spectroscopy, the absorbance $A=\log_{10}(I_0/I)$ is defined with the base-10 logarithm, and, assuming that it is proportional to the molar concentration $c_{\mathrm M}\ [\mathrm{mol/L}]$ of the absorber and to the optical path length $\ell\ [\mathrm{cm}]$, the molar absorption coefficient $\varepsilon\ [\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,cm^{-1}}]$ is defined by $A=\varepsilon c_{\mathrm M}\ell$ (Chapter 48). (In this equation $c_{\mathrm M}$ is the concentration, not the speed of light $c$. In spectroscopy the unit $\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,cm^{-1}}$ is also written $\mathrm{M^{-1}cm^{-1}}$. Here M means $\mathrm{mol/L}$, and the values of $\varepsilon$ in the literature are often given in this notation.) Paying attention to the conversion between the natural and common logarithms, $\ln10=2.3026$, we connect the two ways of expressing the strength. Taking the common logarithm of both sides of Eq. \eqref{eq:53-beer} gives $A=\log_{10}e^{\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}d}=\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}d/\ln10=0.4343\,\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}d$, and since the number density of a solution is $N=c_{\mathrm M}N_{\mathrm A}/1000\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ ($1\ \mathrm{L}=10^3\ \mathrm{cm^3}$), we have $A=\dfrac{\sigma N_{\mathrm A}}{1000\ln10}\,c_{\mathrm M}\ell$, and therefore we obtain

\begin{equation} \varepsilon=\frac{N_{\mathrm A}}{1000\ln10}\,\sigma,\qquad \sigma=\frac{1000\ln10}{N_{\mathrm A}}\,\varepsilon=3.82\times10^{-21}\,\varepsilon\ \mathrm{cm^2},\qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=\ln10\cdot\varepsilon c_{\mathrm M}=2.303\,\varepsilon c_{\mathrm M}\ [\mathrm{cm^{-1}}] \label{eq:53-eps-sigma} \end{equation}

(with $c_{\mathrm M}$ in $\mathrm{mol/L}$ and $\varepsilon$ in $\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,cm^{-1}}$). Summarizing the relations among these quantities gives Table 53.3 and Table 53.4.

Example 53.7: Finding the absorbance, transmittance, absorption coefficient, and cross section from the molar absorption coefficient

An aqueous solution of $[\mathrm{Ti(H_2O)_6}]^{3+}$ has an absorption band at the wavenumber $20\,300\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, with $\varepsilon\simeq5\ \mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,cm^{-1}}$ (a rough value to one significant figure) (source: Lever, Inorganic Electronic Spectroscopy, 2nd ed., the table of spectra of complexes). For a cell with concentration $c_{\mathrm M}=0.10\ \mathrm{mol/L}$ and optical path length $\ell=1\ \mathrm{cm}$, find the absorbance $A$, the transmittance $T$, the absorption coefficient $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$, the absorption length, the absorption cross section $\sigma$, and the number density $N$ of the absorbers. Also, what concentration should be used, with the same optical path length, to make $T=10\,\%$?

Solution. $A=\varepsilon c_{\mathrm M}\ell=5\times0.10\times1=0.50$. $T=10^{-0.50}=0.316$ ($31.6\,\%$). From Eq. \eqref{eq:53-eps-sigma}, the absorption coefficient is $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=2.303\times5\times0.10=1.15\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, and the absorption length is $1/1.15=0.87\ \mathrm{cm}$. The cross section is $\sigma=3.82\times10^{-21}\times5=1.9\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$. The number density is $N=c_{\mathrm M}N_{\mathrm A}/1000=0.10\times6.022\times10^{23}/1000=6.0\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, and $N\sigma=6.0\times10^{19}\times1.9\times10^{-20}=1.15\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ agrees with the value above. To obtain $T=10\,\%$ we need $A=1$, and $c_{\mathrm M}=A/(\varepsilon\ell)=1/5=0.20\ \mathrm{mol/L}$ (twice the concentration).

This $\sigma=1.9\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$ is about $10^{-5}$ of the size of a molecule ($(5\ \text{Å})^2=2.5\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$), so in a $d$–$d$ transition only a tiny fraction ($10^{-5}$) of the photons that strike a molecule are absorbed. In contrast, for an allowed transition ($\varepsilon\sim10^4$), $\sigma\sim4\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, which reaches $10^{-2}$ of the cross section of the molecule.

Math Tool: The absorption coefficient and the complex refractive index

In a medium with complex refractive index $\tilde n=n+i\kappa$ ($n$ is the refractive index and $\kappa$ is the extinction coefficient; it is the same letter as the decay constant $\kappa$ in tunneling, but a different quantity), the electric field of light can be written $E\propto e^{i(\tilde n\omega z/c-\omega t)}$ (the $e^{-i\omega t}$ convention). The imaginary part of $\tilde n$ produces the amplitude decay $e^{-\kappa\omega z/c}$, and since the intensity is proportional to the square of the electric field, $I\propto e^{-2\kappa\omega z/c}$. Therefore

\begin{equation} \alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=\frac{2\omega\kappa}{c}=\frac{4\pi\kappa}{\lambda} \label{eq:53-alpha-kappa} \end{equation}

($\lambda$ is the wavelength in vacuum). For Si at $800\ \mathrm{nm}$ ($\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=850\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$), $\kappa=850\times8.0\times10^{-5}/(4\pi)=5.4\times10^{-3}$, which is much smaller than the refractive index $n\simeq3.7$. The real and imaginary parts of the refractive index correspond to the real and imaginary parts of the complex permittivity $\varepsilon_1+i\varepsilon_2=\tilde n^2$ ($\varepsilon_2=2n\kappa$), and one can also write $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=\omega\varepsilon_2/(nc)$. The real part $n$ is the refractive index of AD 03 (Chapter 13, Section 13.3), and it determines the phase velocity $c/n$ of light. We will learn in Chapter 54, Section 54.5 that the imaginary part $\varepsilon_2$ (absorption) and the real part (refractive index) are linked by the Kramers–Kronig relations (there too we write $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=2\omega\kappa/c=\omega\varepsilon_2/(nc)$).

53.3.2 Oscillator strength — a “yardstick” for the strength of absorption

The strength of an absorption band can be roughly gauged from the magnitude of $\varepsilon$ or $\sigma$, but if the band is broad, the height of the peak alone cannot express the overall strength. We therefore use the area of the whole absorption band (the frequency integral of the cross section), $\int\sigma\dd\nu$. Making this quantity dimensionless, so as to connect it directly to calculations in quantum mechanics, gives the oscillator strength. It is defined through the matrix element of the transition.

Definition 53.2: Oscillator strength

We define the oscillator strength of the optical transition from the initial state $\ket{i}$ to the final state $\ket{f}$ (taking the electric field of the light along the $x$ direction) by

\begin{equation} f_{fi}=\frac{2m\omega_{fi}}{\hbar}\,\abs{x_{fi}}^2,\qquad x_{fi}=\mel{f}{\hat{x}}{i},\quad \omega_{fi}=\frac{E_f-E_i}{\hbar} \label{eq:53-f} \end{equation}

Here $m$ is the mass of the particle, and $m=m_{\mathrm e}$ for the transition of an electron. $f_{fi}$ is dimensionless. For an isotropic material (three-dimensional), we average over directions, replacing $\abs{x_{fi}}^2\to\abs{\bm{r}_{fi}}^2/3$ (if the final state is degenerate, we sum over those states), and obtain $f=(2m\omega_{fi}/3\hbar)\abs{\bm{r}_{fi}}^2$ (Chapter 48).

Let us check the dimension. $m\omega\abs{x}^2/\hbar$ is $\mathrm{kg\cdot s^{-1}\cdot m^2/(J\,s)}=\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}/J}=1$, which is dimensionless. The reason why $f$ serves as a yardstick for the strength of absorption shows up in the following formula for the integrated absorption cross section.

Derivation: Integrated absorption cross section $\int\sigma\dd\nu=e_0^2f/(4\varepsilon_0m_{\mathrm e}c)$

Suppose that monochromatic light $E(t)=E_0\cos\omega t$ polarized in the $x$ direction strikes an electron (charge $-e_0$). The interaction is $\hat{H}_1=-\hat{d}E=e_0\hat{x}E_0\cos\omega t=\hat{V}_0e^{-i\omega t}+\hat{V}_0^\dagger e^{i\omega t}$, with $\hat{V}_0=\tfrac12e_0E_0\hat{x}$ (the electric dipole approximation; Chapter 52). By Fermi's golden rule for a harmonic perturbation (Chapter 51), the probability of absorption $\ket{i}\to\ket{f}$ ($E_f\gt E_i$) per unit time is

$$ w_{i\to f}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\abs{\mel{f}{\hat{V}_0}{i}}^2\delta(E_f-E_i-\hbar\omega)=\frac{\pi}{2\hbar}\,e_0^2E_0^2\abs{x_{fi}}^2\,\delta(E_f-E_i-\hbar\omega) $$

The energy absorbed by one atom per unit time is $\hbar\omega\,w_{i\to f}$. Since the intensity of the light is $I=\tfrac12c\varepsilon_0E_0^2$, the cross section (the absorbed power divided by the intensity) is

$$ \sigma(\omega)=\frac{\hbar\omega\,w_{i\to f}}{I}=\frac{\pi\omega\,e_0^2\abs{x_{fi}}^2}{c\,\varepsilon_0}\,\delta(E_f-E_i-\hbar\omega)=\frac{\pi\omega\,e_0^2\abs{x_{fi}}^2}{\hbar\,c\,\varepsilon_0}\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{fi}) $$

(in the last step we used $\delta(\hbar y)=\delta(y)/\hbar$). Integrating this over the angular frequency gives $\int\sigma\dd\omega=\pi\omega_{fi}e_0^2\abs{x_{fi}}^2/(\hbar c\varepsilon_0)$, and using $\dd\nu=\dd\omega/2\pi$ we obtain

$$ \int\sigma\dd\nu=\frac{\omega_{fi}\,e_0^2\abs{x_{fi}}^2}{2\hbar\,c\,\varepsilon_0} $$

Here, from Eq. \eqref{eq:53-f}, $\omega_{fi}\abs{x_{fi}}^2/\hbar=f/(2m_{\mathrm e})$, so we obtain the following result. ∎

Formula 53.1: Integrated absorption cross section and oscillator strength

\begin{equation} \int\sigma\,\dd\nu=\frac{e_0^2}{4\varepsilon_0m_{\mathrm e}c}\,f=\pi r_{\mathrm e}c\,f\ \left[=f\times2.654\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2\,Hz}\right],\qquad \int\sigma\,\dd\tilde\nu=\pi r_{\mathrm e}\,f \label{eq:53-intsigma} \end{equation}

Here $r_{\mathrm e}=e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0m_{\mathrm e}c^2)=2.818\ \mathrm{fm}$ is the classical electron radius. In the form integrated over the wavenumber $\tilde\nu=\nu/c$ (the second equation), the right-hand side is determined by $f$ and constants alone.

The quantity $e_0^2/(4\varepsilon_0m_{\mathrm e}c)=\pi\,[e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0m_{\mathrm e}c^2)]\,c=\pi r_{\mathrm e}c$ has the numerical value $\pi\times2.818\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{m}\times2.998\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}=2.654\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2\,Hz}$. This value does not depend on the type of transition or on the material; it is determined only by the mass and charge of a single electron. The practical formula $f=4.32\times10^{-9}\int\varepsilon\,\dd\tilde\nu$ used in Chapter 48 is obtained by substituting $\sigma=3.82\times10^{-21}\varepsilon\ \mathrm{cm^2}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:53-eps-sigma}) and $\pi r_{\mathrm e}=8.853\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{cm}$ into the second equation of Eq. \eqref{eq:53-intsigma}.

This derivation is for light in vacuum ($I=\tfrac12c\varepsilon_0E_0^2$). In solutions and crystals, because of the corrections for the refractive index and the local field (a factor of roughly $(1/n_{\mathrm r})\left[(n_{\mathrm r}^2+2)/3\right]^2$), the value of $f$ obtained from the same absorption band shifts by about 10%, but this does not affect order-of-magnitude arguments (in Section 53.4.2 we keep $n_{\mathrm r}$ explicitly). Let us also check consistency with the cross-section formula of Section 53.1.1. For an isotropic sample, setting $\abs{d_{12}}^2=3e_0^2\abs{x_{fi}}^2$, Eq. \eqref{eq:53-sigmaB} gives $\int\sigma\dd\omega=\pi\omega\abs{d_{12}}^2/(3\varepsilon_0\hbar c)=\pi\omega e_0^2\abs{x_{fi}}^2/(\hbar c\varepsilon_0)$, which agrees with the result of the derivation above.

Intuition: What multiple of the “classical absorption of a single electron” is $f$?

In classical physics, an electron in an atom is regarded as a charged particle tied to the atomic nucleus by a spring (the Lorentz model). The value of $\int\sigma\dd\nu$ when this single classical oscillator absorbs light is exactly $e_0^2/(4\varepsilon_0m_{\mathrm e}c)$. The $\int\sigma\dd\nu$ of a quantum-mechanical transition is some multiple of this, and that multiple is $f$. Indeed, for the $n=0\to1$ transition of a harmonic oscillator (mass $m$, natural frequency $\omega$), $\abs{x_{10}}^2=\hbar/(2m\omega)$ (Chapter 13), so Eq. \eqref{eq:53-f} gives $f=2m\omega\cdot\hbar/(2m\omega)/\hbar=1$, in exact agreement with the classical oscillator. In other words, “$f=1$” means the ideal case in which a single electron carries all of the absorption in one transition.

If the same electron has several transitions, the sum of their $f$ values cannot exceed the number of electrons (the Thomas–Reiche–Kuhn sum rule $\sum_ff_{fi}=1$, per electron). We derive this sum rule from the response function in Chapter 54, Section 54.3 (the $f$ sum rule also appears in Chapter 8, Section 8.7 of “Linear Response Theory” (AD 08)). Since the sum is 1, if the $f$ of one transition is close to 1, the $f$ values of the other transitions are small.

53.3.4 Absorption length and thickness — GaAs and Si

The magnitude of the absorption coefficient varies by many orders of magnitude depending on the material and the wavelength. Let us look at values near the absorption edge of semiconductors. For GaAs, at a photon energy of $1.5\ \mathrm{eV}$ (wavelength $827\ \mathrm{nm}$), $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\simeq10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (M. D. Sturge, Phys. This difference reflects the fact that GaAs is a direct-gap semiconductor and Si is an indirect-gap semiconductor (Section 53.4).

For $12\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}d=1.02$ and the absorption is $1-e^{-1.02}=64\,\%$. For $100\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}d=8.5$ and the absorption is $1-e^{-8.5}=1-2.0\times10^{-4}=99.98\,\%$. (3) From $1-e^{-\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}d}=0.99$ we get $d=\ln100/\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=4.605/\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$. For GaAs this is $4.605/10^4\ \mathrm{cm}=4.6\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{cm}=4.6\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, and for Si it is $4.605/850\ \mathrm{cm}=5.4\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm}=54\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, so Si requires a thickness about 12 times greater. (In actual solar cells, because of structures that confine light and because of the absorption of near-infrared light, for which the absorption is much weaker, the thickness of Si ends up at roughly 100–200 $\mu\mathrm{m}$.)

53.4 Interband Transitions in Semiconductors — Direct and Indirect Transitions

Glass transmits visible light, but a silicon crystal absorbs visible light and appears black. The band gap of glass ($\mathrm{SiO_2}$) is about 9 eV (the photon wavelength is $140\ \mathrm{nm}$, in the ultraviolet), and the energy of a visible-light photon ($1.8$–$3.1\ \mathrm{eV}$) is not enough to lift a valence electron into the conduction band, so it is not absorbed. The band gap of Si is $1.12\ \mathrm{eV}$ (wavelength $1107\ \mathrm{nm}$), and every visible-light photon exceeds the gap, so it is absorbed. In this section we derive from Fermi's golden rule how, and how much, photons with energies larger than the band gap are absorbed. The conclusion is that, beyond the absorption edge $\hbar\omega=E_{\mathrm g}$, the absorption coefficient rises like $\sqrt{\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}}$ in direct-gap materials and rises gently, like $(\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}\pm\hbar\Omega)^2$, in indirect-gap materials.

53.4.1 Photons do not change $\bm{k}$ — vertical transitions

An electron in a crystal is described by a Bloch state $\psi_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}u_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r})$ ($n$ is the band index and $\bm{k}$ is the wavenumber) (Chapter 41). The momentum that a photon gives to an electron is $\hbar\bm{k}_{\mathrm{ph}}$ ($k_{\mathrm{ph}}=2\pi/\lambda$). The wavelength of light ($\lambda\sim500\ \mathrm{nm}$ for visible light) is three orders of magnitude larger than the lattice constant $a$ ($\sim0.5\ \mathrm{nm}$), so the photon wavenumber $k_{\mathrm{ph}}$ is very small compared with the size of the Brillouin zone, $\pi/a$:

\begin{equation} \frac{k_{\mathrm{ph}}}{\pi/a}=\frac{2\pi/\lambda}{\pi/a}=\frac{2a}{\lambda}=\frac{2\times0.5\ \mathrm{nm}}{500\ \mathrm{nm}}=0.002 \label{eq:53-qratio} \end{equation}

That is, $k_{\mathrm{ph}}=1.26\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$ is only $0.2\,\%$ of $\pi/a=6.28\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$. This means that the electric dipole approximation $e^{i\bm{k}_{\mathrm{ph}}\cdot\bm{r}}\simeq1$ (Chapter 52) holds on the scale of the crystal's wavenumbers as well. Then the matrix element between Bloch states is nonzero only between states with equal $\bm{k}$ ($\bm{k}'=\bm{k}+\bm{k}_{\mathrm{ph}}\simeq\bm{k}$). Concretely, if we write the interaction of light and electrons in the velocity gauge as $\hat{H}_1=(e_0/m_{\mathrm e})\bm{A}\cdot\hat{\bm p}$ (Chapter 52, Section 52.2), the vector potential $\bm{A}$ is almost uniform within a unit cell of the crystal, and the momentum operator $\hat{\bm p}$ is an operator that is periodic under translations of the crystal, so the matrix element $\mel{c,\bm{k}'}{\hat{\bm p}}{v,\bm{k}}$ between Bloch states is meaningful, and it is nonzero only for $\bm{k}'=\bm{k}$ (Section 53.4.2 below). Therefore, on a band diagram (a plot of $E$ as a function of $\bm{k}$; Figure 53.6), the absorption of light is drawn as a vertical transition that does not change $\bm{k}$. In a material in which the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band are at the same $\bm{k}$ (direct-gap materials, such as GaAs), a single photon with the energy of the band gap $E_{\mathrm g}$ can lift an electron. In a material in which the two are at different $\bm{k}$ (indirect-gap materials, such as Si and Ge), a photon alone is not enough, and a quantum of lattice vibration (a phonon) supplies the crystal momentum. (The positions of the band edges were examined in detail in Chapter 42, Section 42.7.)

Example 53.10: The photon wavenumber and the Brillouin zone, and the amount needed to supply the momentum

(1) Find the ratio of the wavenumber $k_{\mathrm{ph}}$ of light of wavelength $\lambda=500\ \mathrm{nm}$ to $\pi/a$ for a crystal with lattice constant $a=0.5\ \mathrm{nm}$. (2) Find the ratio between the wavenumber $k_{\mathrm{ph}}$ of a photon with the energy of the band gap $1.12\ \mathrm{eV}$ of Si ($a=0.543\ \mathrm{nm}$) and the position $k_0\simeq0.85\times(2\pi/a)$ of the bottom of the conduction band. (3) Find the wavelength and the wavenumber of a photon with the same energy as the phonon energy $\hbar\Omega=58\ \mathrm{meV}$, and compare the phonon and the photon as carriers of momentum.

Solution. (1) $k_{\mathrm{ph}}=2\pi/(500\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m})=1.257\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and $\pi/a=\pi/(0.5\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m})=6.283\times10^9\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, so the ratio is $0.0020$ (the same as $2a/\lambda$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:53-qratio}). (2) The wavelength for $1.12\ \mathrm{eV}$ is $1239.84/1.12=1107\ \mathrm{nm}$, so $k_{\mathrm{ph}}=5.68\times10^6\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, and $k_0=0.85\times2\pi/(0.543\times10^{-9})=9.84\times10^9\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$. We have $k_0/k_{\mathrm{ph}}=1.7\times10^3$, so a wavenumber about 1700 times the photon wavenumber is needed. (3) The wavelength of a $58\ \mathrm{meV}$ photon is $1239.84/0.058=2.14\times10^4\ \mathrm{nm}=21\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ (far infrared), and its wavenumber is only $k_{\mathrm{ph}}=2.9\times10^5\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$. A phonon of the same energy (a lattice vibration whose wavelength is a few times the lattice constant), on the other hand, has a wavenumber of the order of $10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and can supply $k_0$. Light can carry energy, but it can hardly carry any wavenumber (crystal momentum). A phonon has small energy ($58\ \mathrm{meV}\ll E_{\mathrm g}$) but a large wavenumber. Therefore, an indirect transition requires a “team of two,” a photon and a phonon.

53.4.2 Joint density of states and the absorption coefficient

We consider only vertical transitions. When we add up, at the photon energy $\hbar\omega$, the probabilities of transitions from a valence-band state $\ket{v,\bm{k}}$ to a conduction-band state $\ket{c,\bm{k}}$ in a crystal of volume $V$, we obtain the absorption coefficient. The central quantity here is how many values of $\bm{k}$ satisfy the energy conservation $E_{\mathrm c}(\bm{k})-E_{\mathrm v}(\bm{k})=\hbar\omega$ (the number of states for which the separation between the upper and lower bands equals $\hbar\omega$). We define it as follows.

Definition 53.3: Joint density of states

The joint density of states between the valence band $E_{\mathrm v}(\bm{k})$ and the conduction band $E_{\mathrm c}(\bm{k})$ is defined, per unit volume and per spin, as

\begin{equation} J_{\mathrm{cv}}(\hbar\omega)=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}}\delta\bigl(E_{\mathrm c}(\bm{k})-E_{\mathrm v}(\bm{k})-\hbar\omega\bigr)=\int\frac{\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\delta\bigl(E_{\mathrm c}(\bm{k})-E_{\mathrm v}(\bm{k})-\hbar\omega\bigr) \label{eq:53-Jcv} \end{equation}

Its unit is $\mathrm{J^{-1}\,m^{-3}}$. (It is a different quantity from the exchange integral $J_{ab}$ and from the total angular momentum $J$, and we distinguish it by attaching the subscript cv. When we convert a sum over wavenumbers into an integral, we put $\sum_{\bm{k}}\to V\int\dd^3k/(2\pi)^3$. The periodic boundary conditions are the same as in Chapter 10, Section 10.7.)

Derivation: Absorption coefficient for interband transitions

We extend the same golden-rule calculation as in Section 53.3 to a crystal. However, the position operator $\hat{\bm r}$ is not periodic under translations of the crystal, so the matrix element $\mel{c,\bm{k}}{\hat{\bm r}}{v,\bm{k}}$ between Bloch states cannot be defined as it stands. We therefore start from the interaction $\hat{H}_1=(e_0/m_{\mathrm e})\bm{A}\cdot\hat{\bm p}$ of the velocity gauge (Chapter 52, Sections 52.1–52.2). Since $\hat{\bm p}=-i\hbar\nabla$ is periodic under translations, the matrix elements between Bloch states are meaningful. Write the Bloch state as $\psi_{n\bm{k}}=V^{-1/2}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}u_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r})$ ($u_{n\bm{k}}$ is a function with the periodicity of the lattice, normalized in the unit cell (volume $\Omega$) by $\Omega^{-1}\int_{\mathrm{cell}}\abs{u}^2\dd^3r=1$). Then, for states with the same $\bm{k}$, the $\hbar\bm{k}$ term vanishes by the orthogonality of the $u$, $\int_{\mathrm{cell}}u_{c\bm{k}}^*u_{v\bm{k}}\dd^3r=0$, and the matrix element between them becomes

$$ \bm{p}_{\mathrm{cv}}(\bm{k})\equiv\mel{c,\bm{k}}{\hat{\bm p}}{v,\bm{k}}=\frac{1}{\Omega}\int_{\mathrm{cell}}u_{c\bm{k}}^*(\bm{r})\,(-i\hbar\nabla)\,u_{v\bm{k}}(\bm{r})\,\dd^3r $$

(This is a quantity determined only by an integral within the unit cell.) The vector potential of light polarized in the $\hat{\bm e}$ direction, $E(t)=E_0\cos\omega t$, is $\bm{A}(t)=-(E_0/\omega)\sin\omega t\,\hat{\bm e}$ ($\bm{E}=-\partial\bm{A}/\partial t$), so that the interaction is

$$ \hat{H}_1=-\frac{e_0E_0}{m_{\mathrm e}\omega}\sin\omega t\;\hat{\bm e}\cdot\hat{\bm p}=\hat{V}_0e^{-i\omega t}+\hat{V}_0^\dagger e^{i\omega t},\qquad \hat{V}_0=-\frac{ie_0E_0}{2m_{\mathrm e}\omega}\,\hat{\bm e}\cdot\hat{\bm p} $$

By the golden rule (the formula is the same as in the derivation of Section 53.3), the probability of a transition from $\ket{v,\bm{k}}$ to $\ket{c,\bm{k}}$ per unit time is

$$ w_{\bm{k}}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\abs{\mel{c,\bm{k}}{\hat{V}_0}{v,\bm{k}}}^2\delta\bigl(E_{\mathrm c}(\bm{k})-E_{\mathrm v}(\bm{k})-\hbar\omega\bigr)=\frac{\pi}{2\hbar}\left(\frac{e_0E_0}{m_{\mathrm e}\omega}\right)^2\abs{\hat{\bm e}\cdot\bm{p}_{\mathrm{cv}}(\bm{k})}^2\,\delta\bigl(E_{\mathrm c}(\bm{k})-E_{\mathrm v}(\bm{k})-\hbar\omega\bigr) $$

(If we define the matrix element of the position $\bm{r}_{\mathrm{cv}}$ through the relation $\bm{p}_{\mathrm{cv}}=im_{\mathrm e}\omega\bm{r}_{\mathrm{cv}}$ (Chapter 52), this expression can be written in the length-gauge form $(\pi/2\hbar)e_0^2E_0^2\abs{\hat{\bm e}\cdot\bm{r}_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2\delta(\cdots)$. In a crystal, $\bm{r}_{\mathrm{cv}}$ is a quantity determined by this relation.) The number of transitions per unit volume and per unit time, obtained by summing over the two spin states and over all $\bm{k}$, is $W=\frac{2}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}}w_{\bm{k}}$. Dividing the absorbed energy $\hbar\omega W$ by the intensity inside the material, $I=\tfrac12n_{\mathrm r}c\varepsilon_0E_0^2$ ($n_{\mathrm r}$ is the refractive index; local-field corrections are neglected), gives the rate of attenuation of the intensity, that is, the absorption coefficient $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=\hbar\omega W/I$. If $\abs{\hat{\bm e}\cdot\bm{p}_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2$ is independent of $\bm{k}$ (which holds very close to the band edge), then $\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}}\delta(\cdots)$ is exactly $J_{\mathrm{cv}}$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:53-Jcv}, and $W=2J_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{2\hbar}\left(\dfrac{e_0E_0}{m_{\mathrm e}\omega}\right)^2\abs{\hat{\bm e}\cdot\bm{p}_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2$. Therefore we obtain

$$ \alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}(\omega)=\frac{\hbar\omega\cdot2J_{\mathrm{cv}}\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{2\hbar}\dfrac{e_0^2E_0^2}{m_{\mathrm e}^2\omega^2}\abs{\hat{\bm e}\cdot\bm{p}_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2}{\frac12n_{\mathrm r}c\varepsilon_0E_0^2}=\frac{2\pi e_0^2\,\abs{\hat{\bm e}\cdot\bm{p}_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2}{n_{\mathrm r}c\,\varepsilon_0\,m_{\mathrm e}^2\,\omega}\,J_{\mathrm{cv}}(\hbar\omega) $$

The factor $E_0^2$ cancels between the numerator and the denominator and disappears. ∎

Formula 53.2: Absorption coefficient for interband transitions

\begin{equation} \alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}(\omega)=\frac{2\pi e_0^2\,\abs{\hat{\bm{e}}\cdot\bm{p}_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2}{n_{\mathrm r}\,c\,\varepsilon_0\,m_{\mathrm e}^2\,\omega}\;J_{\mathrm{cv}}(\hbar\omega)\ \ \propto\ \ \frac{\abs{p_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2}{\omega}\,J_{\mathrm{cv}}(\hbar\omega) \label{eq:53-alpha-bb} \end{equation}

The intensity of the absorption is the product of (i) the momentum matrix element $\abs{p_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2$ (the “strength” of the transition, determined by the selection rules and the properties of the Bloch functions) and (ii) the joint density of states $J_{\mathrm{cv}}$ (the “number” of transitions that satisfy energy conservation). In the rest of this section, we assume that $\abs{p_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2$ is nearly constant near the band edge (an “allowed transition”) and determine the shape of the absorption edge from the form of $J_{\mathrm{cv}}$.

53.4.3 Direct transitions — $\sqrt{\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}}$ and the Tauc plot

Suppose that the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band are both at the same point $\bm{k}=0$ and that both can be approximated by parabolas (the effective-mass approximation; Chapter 41, Section 41.5):

$$ E_{\mathrm c}(\bm{k})=E_{\mathrm g}+\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m_{\mathrm e}^*},\qquad E_{\mathrm v}(\bm{k})=-\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m_{\mathrm h}^*} $$

($m_{\mathrm e}^*$ and $m_{\mathrm h}^*$ are the effective masses of the electron and the hole; the top of the valence band is taken as the origin of energy). The separation between the two bands is

$$ E_{\mathrm c}(\bm{k})-E_{\mathrm v}(\bm{k})=E_{\mathrm g}+\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2\mu},\qquad \frac{1}{\mu}=\frac{1}{m_{\mathrm e}^*}+\frac{1}{m_{\mathrm h}^*} $$

where $\mu$ is the reduced mass of the electron and the hole (the same form as the reduced mass of a two-body problem; Chapter 19, Section 19.1. It is distinct from the $\mu$ of the magnetic moment). The separation is the kinetic energy $\hbar^2k^2/2\mu$ of a free particle of mass $\mu$ plus $E_{\mathrm g}$. Therefore $J_{\mathrm{cv}}$ is the “density of states of a free particle” with $m$ replaced by $\mu$ and the energy $E$ replaced by $\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}$. Let us actually compute it.

Derivation: Joint density of states in three dimensions

Using $\dd^3k=4\pi k^2\dd k$ (a spherical shell), we apply to Eq. \eqref{eq:53-Jcv} the property of the $\delta$ function, $\delta(g(k))=\delta(k-k_0)/\abs{g'(k_0)}$ ($g(k_0)=0$). Here $g(k)=E_{\mathrm g}+\hbar^2k^2/2\mu-\hbar\omega$. From $g(k_0)=0$ we obtain $k_0=\sqrt{2\mu(\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g})}/\hbar$, and $g'(k_0)=\hbar^2k_0/\mu$.

$$ J_{\mathrm{cv}}=\frac{4\pi}{(2\pi)^3}\int_0^\infty k^2\,\delta(g(k))\,\dd k=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\,\frac{k_0^2}{\hbar^2k_0/\mu}=\frac{\mu k_0}{2\pi^2\hbar^2}=\frac{1}{4\pi^2}\left(\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}} $$

(The last equality was obtained by substituting for $k_0$ and using $\mu\sqrt{2\mu}/\hbar^3=\tfrac12(2\mu/\hbar^2)^{3/2}$.) For $\hbar\omega\lt E_{\mathrm g}$ there is no $k$ with $g(k)=0$, so $J_{\mathrm{cv}}=0$. ∎

This form is the same as the one obtained from the density of states (per spin) of a free electron of mass $m$, $g(E)=\dfrac{1}{4\pi^2}\left(\dfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{E}$ (AD 03, Chapter 52, Section 52.8 (in Japanese); this book, Chapter 10, Section 10.7), by setting $m\to\mu$ and $E\to\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}$. Substituting this result into Formula \eqref{eq:53-alpha-bb} gives the following result.

Formula 53.3: Absorption edge of a direct-gap semiconductor (allowed transitions)

\begin{equation} J_{\mathrm{cv}}(\hbar\omega)=\frac{1}{4\pi^2}\left(\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}},\qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}(\omega)=K\,\frac{\sqrt{\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}}}{\hbar\omega}\ \ \propto\ \ \sqrt{\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}}\quad(\hbar\omega\gt E_{\mathrm g}) \label{eq:53-direct} \end{equation}

Here $K$ is a constant determined by $\abs{p_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2$ and $\mu^{3/2}$ (with the dimension $\mathrm{cm^{-1}\,eV^{1/2}}$). Near the absorption edge ($\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}\ll E_{\mathrm g}$), $1/\hbar\omega$ is nearly constant, so one writes $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\propto\sqrt{\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}}$.

Example 53.11: Estimating the joint density of states and the magnitude of the absorption coefficient of GaAs

For GaAs, take the effective mass of the electron to be $m_{\mathrm e}^*=0.067\,m_{\mathrm e}$, the effective mass of the heavy hole to be $m_{\mathrm h}^*=0.45\,m_{\mathrm e}$, and the refractive index to be $n_{\mathrm r}=3.6$. (1) Find the reduced mass $\mu$. (2) Find $J_{\mathrm{cv}}$ at $\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}=0.1\ \mathrm{eV}$. (3) Setting the momentum matrix element to $\abs{\hat{\bm{e}}\cdot\bm{p}_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2=m_{\mathrm e}E_{\mathrm P}/6$ with $E_{\mathrm P}=25\ \mathrm{eV}$ ($E_{\mathrm P}=2\abs{\mel{S}{\hat p_x}{X}}^2/m_{\mathrm e}$ is a quantity called the Kane energy; it is determined by the momentum matrix element between the state $S$ at the bottom of the $s$-like conduction band and the state $X$ at the top of the $p$-like valence band, and is $25$–$29\ \mathrm{eV}$ for GaAs. The factor $1/6$ is the value for the transition from the heavy-hole states $(X\pm iY)/\sqrt2$ to $S$, averaged over the direction of polarization), estimate $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$ at $\hbar\omega=1.52\ \mathrm{eV}$ from Formula \eqref{eq:53-alpha-bb} and compare it with the measured value of $\sim10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. In addition, (4) how does the result change when the contribution of the valence band of the light hole ($m_{\mathrm{lh}}^*=0.08\,m_{\mathrm e}$) is also included?

Solution. (1) $\mu=\dfrac{0.067\times0.45}{0.067+0.45}m_{\mathrm e}=0.0583\,m_{\mathrm e}=5.31\times10^{-32}\ \mathrm{kg}$. (2) $J_{\mathrm{cv}}=\dfrac{1}{4\pi^2}\left(\dfrac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{0.1\ \mathrm{eV}}$. We have $2\mu/\hbar^2=2\times5.31\times10^{-32}/(1.0546\times10^{-34})^2=9.55\times10^{36}\ \mathrm{J^{-1}m^{-2}}$, and its $3/2$ power is $2.95\times10^{55}$. Also $\sqrt{0.1\ \mathrm{eV}}=\sqrt{1.602\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{J}}=1.266\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{J^{1/2}}$. Therefore $J_{\mathrm{cv}}=(1/39.48)\times2.95\times10^{55}\times1.266\times10^{-10}=9.4\times10^{43}\ \mathrm{J^{-1}m^{-3}}=1.5\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{eV^{-1}cm^{-3}}$. (3) We have $\omega=1.52\ \mathrm{eV}/\hbar=2.31\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\abs{p_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2=m_{\mathrm e}E_{\mathrm P}/6=9.109\times10^{-31}\times25\times1.602\times10^{-19}/6=6.08\times10^{-49}\ \mathrm{kg\,J}$. Substituting into Formula \eqref{eq:53-alpha-bb}, we get $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=\dfrac{2\pi\times(1.602\times10^{-19})^2\times6.08\times10^{-49}}{3.6\times2.998\times10^8\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times(9.109\times10^{-31})^2\times2.31\times10^{15}}\times9.4\times10^{43}=5.0\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}=5.0\times10^3\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. This is of the same order of magnitude as the measured value of $\sim10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$.

(4) The value in (3) is the contribution of the heavy-hole valence band alone. The light-hole valence band also has transitions of the same strength (the value of $\abs{p_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2$ per valence state, averaged over the polarization, is equal to that of the heavy hole). Since the joint density of states is proportional to $\mu^{3/2}$, the reduced mass of the light hole is $\mu_{\mathrm{lh}}=0.067\times0.08/(0.067+0.08)\,m_{\mathrm e}=0.0365\,m_{\mathrm e}$, so its contribution is $(0.0365/0.0583)^{3/2}=0.50$ times that of the heavy hole, and the total is $5.0\times10^3\times1.5=7.6\times10^3\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. If we further take $E_{\mathrm P}=29\ \mathrm{eV}$, it becomes $8.8\times10^3\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. The remaining difference from the measured value of $\sim10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ is due to the spread in the value of $E_{\mathrm P}$, the crudeness of the averaging factor $1/6$, and the enhancement of the absorption by the Coulomb attraction between the electron and the hole (the exciton effect; discussed later). The golden rule and the joint density of states alone were enough to explain why the absorption coefficient of a semiconductor is of the order of $10^3$–$10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$.

Let us turn Formula \eqref{eq:53-direct} into a method for reading off the band gap from an experimental absorption spectrum. Squaring both sides of $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\hbar\omega=K\sqrt{\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}}$ gives

\begin{equation} (\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\hbar\omega)^2=K^2\,(\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}) \label{eq:53-tauc} \end{equation}

so $(\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\hbar\omega)^2$ is a linear function of $\hbar\omega$. We plot the measured points with $\hbar\omega$ on the horizontal axis and $(\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\hbar\omega)^2$ on the vertical axis (a Tauc plot), and extend (extrapolate) the straight portion to the point where the vertical axis is 0; that point is $E_{\mathrm g}$. The square root of the slope of the line is $K$.

In general, depending on the type of transition, one plots $(\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\hbar\omega)^{1/r}$ against $\hbar\omega$ and extrapolates the straight portion: $r=\tfrac12$ (direct allowed; the case above), $r=\tfrac32$ (direct forbidden; the case in which $p_{\mathrm{cv}}=0$ at the edge and $\abs{p_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2\propto k^2$, so that $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\propto(\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g})^{3/2}$), $r=2$ (indirect allowed; as shown in the next subsection, $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\propto(\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}\pm\hbar\Omega)^2$), and $r=3$ (indirect forbidden). The method was originally used by J. Tauc and coworkers to analyze the absorption edge of amorphous Ge (transitions in which the crystal momentum is not conserved, with $(\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\hbar\omega)^{1/2}\propto\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}$) (Phys. Status Solidi 15, 627 (1966)), and today any analysis of this kind is called a Tauc plot.

53.4.4 Indirect transitions — phonon assistance and $(\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}\pm\hbar\Omega)^2$

In an indirect-gap material, the bottom of the conduction band lies at $\bm{k}_0\ne0$. Since a photon cannot change $\bm{k}$, an electron at the top of the valence band ($\bm{k}=0$) cannot reach the bottom of the conduction band ($\bm{k}_0$) merely by absorbing a photon. The absorption or emission of a phonon (energy $\hbar\Omega$, wavenumber $\bm{q}\simeq\bm{k}_0$) takes place at the same time, shifting $\bm{k}$ by $\bm{k}_0$. Because both a photon and a phonon are involved, this process occurs as a second-order effect of the perturbation: the transition amplitude has the form $\sum_m\dfrac{\mel{c}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ep}}}{m}\mel{m}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{opt}}}{v}}{E_v+\hbar\omega-E_m}$ ($\hat{H}_{\mathrm{opt}}$ is the interaction with light, $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ep}}$ is the electron–phonon interaction, and $m$ is an intermediate state; this is the same form as the second-order perturbation theory of Chapter 27), and the denominator is of magnitude $\sim$ eV, being the distance to an intermediate state (for example, a state of a conduction band of higher energy at the same $\bm{k}$). Because of this denominator and the smallness of the electron–phonon interaction, the absorption coefficient of an indirect transition is one to two orders of magnitude smaller than that of a direct transition (Figure 53.7, $10^3\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ and $10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$).

The shape of the absorption edge is determined as follows. The phonon can supply any wavenumber around $\bm{q}\simeq\bm{k}_0$, and the matrix element of the electron–phonon interaction depends only weakly on $\bm{q}$. Therefore the kinetic energies of the valence-band hole in the initial state (wavenumber $\bm{k}_{\mathrm v}$) and of the conduction-band electron in the final state (wavenumber $\bm{k}_{\mathrm c}$) can be chosen independently of each other (the conservation of momentum $\bm{k}_{\mathrm c}=\bm{k}_{\mathrm v}+\bm{q}$ is satisfied by the phonon's $\bm{q}$). The sum of the kinetic energy of the electron measured from the bottom of the band, $E_{\mathrm e}=\hbar^2\abs{\bm{k}_{\mathrm c}-\bm{k}_0}^2/2m_{\mathrm e}^*$, and the kinetic energy $E_{\mathrm h}$ of the hole measured from the top satisfies $E_{\mathrm e}+E_{\mathrm h}=\hbar\omega\pm\hbar\Omega-E_{\mathrm g}\equiv E$ ($+\hbar\Omega$ if a phonon is absorbed, $-\hbar\Omega$ if one is emitted; this is the conservation of energy). The number of such final states is proportional to the convolution of the electron density of states $g_{\mathrm e}(E_{\mathrm e})\propto\sqrt{E_{\mathrm e}}$ and the hole density of states $g_{\mathrm h}(E_{\mathrm h})\propto\sqrt{E_{\mathrm h}}$, namely

\begin{equation} \int_0^{E}\sqrt{E_{\mathrm e}}\,\sqrt{E-E_{\mathrm e}}\,\dd E_{\mathrm e}=\frac{\pi}{8}\,E^2 \label{eq:53-conv} \end{equation}

(In a direct transition, $\bm{k}$ was fixed to a single value, so the result was $\sqrt{E}$, the equivalent of a single density of states. In an indirect transition, the freedom in how the kinetic energy is divided between the electron and the hole is added, and $\sqrt{\ }\times\sqrt{\ }\to E^{2}$. The calculation of the integral is in Exercise 53.4.) Thus the absorption edge of an indirect-gap material, including the phonon occupation-number factors ($\bar n$ for absorption and $\bar n+1$ for emission, where $\bar n=1/(e^{\hbar\Omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}-1)$ is the Bose distribution), is

\begin{equation} \alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}(\omega)\propto\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\left[\bar n\,(\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}+\hbar\Omega)^2+(\bar n+1)\,(\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}-\hbar\Omega)^2\right] \label{eq:53-indirect} \end{equation}

where each term is present only when the quantity in parentheses is positive. The process that absorbs a phonon rises from a photon energy of $E_{\mathrm g}-\hbar\Omega$ (and is weak at low temperatures, where there are no phonons), and the process that emits a phonon rises from $E_{\mathrm g}+\hbar\Omega$. In Si, precise measurements of the absorption edge by Macfarlane et al. (Phys. Rev. 111, 1245 (1958)) showed that two kinds of phonons, of about $18\ \mathrm{meV}$ and about $58\ \mathrm{meV}$ (transverse acoustic and transverse optical), are involved. At $300\ \mathrm{K}$, $\bar n=0.12$ for the $58\ \mathrm{meV}$ phonon and $\bar n=0.99$ for the $18\ \mathrm{meV}$ phonon.

Intuition: Why is $(\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g})^2$ “gentle,” and how does it differ from $\sqrt{\ }$?

Compare the two just above the edge ($E=\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}\to0$). The $\sqrt{E}$ of the direct transition rises steeply as soon as the energy is exceeded even slightly (the slope is infinite), whereas the $E^2$ of the indirect transition is flatter the closer $E$ is to 0, and its slope starts from 0. The difference lies in how the final states are counted. In a direct transition, the photon does not change $\bm{k}$, so the electron and the hole have the same $\bm{k}$, and the number of states for which the sum of the kinetic energies $\hbar^2k^2/2\mu$ is at most $E$ is the volume of a sphere in three-dimensional wavenumber space, $\propto k^3\propto E^{3/2}$; differentiating it (taking the number of states per energy width) gives $\sqrt{E}$. In an indirect transition, the phonon makes up the difference in wavenumber, so the wavenumber of the electron and that of the hole can be chosen independently. The wavenumber space doubles from three to six dimensions, and the number of states for which the sum of the kinetic energies is at most $E$ becomes the volume of a six-dimensional sphere, $\propto k^6\propto E^{3}$, which becomes $E^2$ on differentiation (Eq. \eqref{eq:53-conv}). The more dimensions there are, the larger the power-law exponent of the rise at the edge and the gentler the rise.

The magnitude of the absorption coefficient of the indirect-gap Si is $850\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ at $800\ \mathrm{nm}$ ($1.55\ \mathrm{eV}$), about 1/10 of that of the direct-gap GaAs ($\sim10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ at $1.5\ \mathrm{eV}$). For this reason, an indirect-gap semiconductor requires a thick crystal to absorb light (Si solar cells), and it is inefficient at emitting light (a phonon is also needed for electron–hole recombination). GaAs, GaN, and InP are direct-gap materials and are used for LEDs and semiconductor lasers (Section 53.2). Indirect-gap Si is not suited to light-emitting devices.

53.4.5 Excitons and the Urbach tail (names only)

A real absorption edge has two features that the simple model above does not provide. The first is the exciton. An electron in the conduction band and a hole in the valence band are bound by the Coulomb attraction like a hydrogen atom (the Wannier–Mott exciton), and the energies form the series $E_n=E_{\mathrm g}-R^*/n^2$, $R^*=13.6\ \mathrm{eV}\times(\mu/m_{\mathrm e})/\varepsilon_{\mathrm r}^2$ ($\varepsilon_{\mathrm r}$ is the relative permittivity). The $R^*$ of GaAs is small, a few meV, and at room temperature ($k_{\mathrm B}T=26\ \mathrm{meV}$) most excitons are broken by heat; in GaN (about $25\ \mathrm{meV}$), ZnO (about $60\ \mathrm{meV}$), organic semiconductors, and layered materials ($0.1$–$1\ \mathrm{eV}$), however, they are stable even at room temperature and produce a sharp absorption peak slightly below the gap. Above the gap as well, the Coulomb attraction between the electron and the hole enhances the absorption (Elliott, Phys. Rev. 108, 1384 (1957)). The second is the Urbach tail, a phenomenon in which an absorption tail that decreases exponentially, as in $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\propto\exp[(\hbar\omega-E_0)/E_{\mathrm U}]$, extends below the gap too (Urbach, Phys. Rev. 92, 1324 (1953)). It is caused by the blurring of the edge of the density of states by lattice vibrations and disorder. Because of this tail, reading $E_{\mathrm g}$ from the region where the absorption coefficient is small ($\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\lt10^3\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$) gives a smaller value. This is why only the straight-line portion away from the absorption edge is used for the extrapolation in a Tauc plot. These topics go beyond the scope of this book, so we hope you will study them in Solid-State Physics (AD 06), Chapter 17, Section 17.1 (in Japanese).

53.4.6 A common question: if there is no inversion symmetry, does the transition become indirect?

Question: Are crystals without inversion symmetry prone to being indirect-gap?

We often hear the question: “In a crystal whose unit cell has no inversion symmetry, don't the band extrema shift away from $\bm{k}=0$, so that the transition becomes indirect?” The answer is no: whether a transition is direct or indirect is not decided by the presence or absence of inversion symmetry. First, the surroundings of a “lattice site” (a point of the Bravais lattice) are inversion-symmetric in every crystal (if $\bm{R}$ is a lattice site, so is $-\bm{R}$). What matters is the symmetry of the crystal including the arrangement of atoms attached to each lattice site (the basis). There are three reasons. (1) $E_n(\bm{k})=E_n(-\bm{k})$ holds even without inversion symmetry, because of time-reversal symmetry (even when the spin–orbit interaction is included, the sets of band energies at $\bm{k}$ and $-\bm{k}$ are equal; the direction of the spin is reversed). This property makes $\bm{k}=0$ a point where the energy is stationary, but even without inversion symmetry, nothing prevents a band extremum from lying at $\bm{k}=0$. (2) There are many counterexamples. Si, Ge, and diamond have inversion symmetry yet are indirect-gap, while GaAs, InP, and GaN lack inversion symmetry yet are direct-gap. (3) The decisive factors are the kinds of atomic orbitals that form the band edges (whether the conduction band is $s$-like or $p$-like) and the signs of the overlaps of those orbitals. For example, GaAs is direct-gap because the bottom of the conduction band is an $s$-like state at $\Gamma$ and the top of the valence band is a $p$-like state at $\Gamma$. In Si, the bottom of the conduction band is not at $\Gamma$ but shifted onto the $\Gamma$–X line. The details are collected in Chapter 42, Section 42.7 (Direct and Indirect Transitions — Is Inversion Symmetry the Deciding Factor?).

Application: Choosing materials for solar cells, and the emission color of LEDs

  • Thickness of the absorber layer: As in Example 53.9, the thickness that absorbs $99\,\%$ of the light is about $5\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ for GaAs and about $54\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ for Si. Thin-film solar cells of direct-gap GaAs need little material, are light, and can be bent. Indirect-gap Si requires a thick crystal (about $100$–$200\ \mu\mathrm{m}$), but because the material is abundant and cheap and the processing technology is mature, it makes up most of today's solar cells.
  • Choice of the gap: If the gap is too large, most of the photons in sunlight have energies below the gap and are not absorbed; if it is too small, most of the energy of the absorbed photons becomes heat. Because of this trade-off, the theoretical upper bound on the efficiency of a single-junction solar cell (the Shockley–Queisser limit, about $33\,\%$) is largest when the gap is near $1.3$–$1.4\ \mathrm{eV}$. GaAs ($1.42\ \mathrm{eV}$) and Si ($1.12\ \mathrm{eV}$) both lie close to this.
  • Emission color of LEDs: The photon energy of LED emission is approximately $E_{\mathrm g}$, and $\lambda\,[\mathrm{nm}]\simeq1239.84/E_{\mathrm g}$ (Table 53.2). Adding In to the $3.4\ \mathrm{eV}$ of GaN ($365\ \mathrm{nm}$; ultraviolet) gives blue ($450\ \mathrm{nm}$, $2.76\ \mathrm{eV}$). Shining the light of a blue LED on a yellow phosphor (the $\mathrm{YAG{:}Ce^{3+}}$ of Section 53.5) and mixing blue and yellow gives white.
  • Band gap and color: A material (gap $2$–$3\ \mathrm{eV}$) that absorbs only photons of visible light ($1.77$–$3.10\ \mathrm{eV}$) has a color (example: the yellow of CdS, $E_{\mathrm g}=2.4\ \mathrm{eV}$; Chapter 48), and if the gap is larger than $3.1\ \mathrm{eV}$, the material is colorless (transparent).

53.5 Colors of Transition-Metal Complexes and Infrared Absorption of Molecules

So far we have examined the quantities that determine the intensity of absorption (matrix elements, density of states). In this section we check, with two familiar examples, how the position of the absorption (at which wavelength) and its shape (the width of the band) are determined by the spacing of the levels and the selection rules: the colors of transition-metal complexes in the visible region, and the absorption by molecular vibrations in the infrared region. Both are concrete manifestations of Bohr's frequency condition (Chapter 5), “a photon is absorbed when its energy equals the spacing between two levels,” and of the selection rules of the previous chapter.

53.5.1 The color of a complex — reading $[\mathrm{Ti(H_2O)_6}]^{3+}$

The color of a substance is not the color of the light absorbed but the color of the light that remains unabsorbed (the complementary color of the absorbed color). The hydrated ion of titanium(III), $[\mathrm{Ti(H_2O)_6}]^{3+}$, is $3d^1$ (one electron); in the octahedral ligand field (Chapter 44) the $d$ orbitals split into $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$, and the single electron occupies $t_{2g}$. The visible absorption is just one transition, in which that electron is promoted from $t_{2g}$ to $e_g$, and the position of the band gives the magnitude of the splitting $\Delta_o$ itself: $\Delta_o=20\,300\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (Chapter 44, Section 44.4). Using the conversion in Table 53.4, we obtain

\begin{equation} E=20\,300\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}\times1.23984\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV/cm^{-1}}=2.517\ \mathrm{eV},\qquad \lambda=\frac{10^7}{20\,300}\ \mathrm{nm}=492.6\ \mathrm{nm}\simeq493\ \mathrm{nm} \label{eq:53-Ti} \end{equation}

The wavelength $493\ \mathrm{nm}$ is blue-green, and its complementary color is red. However, the absorption band is broad (the full width at half maximum is $4000$–$5000\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$), so in fact light over a wide range from blue to yellow-green (the range within the full width at half maximum is $443$–$554\ \mathrm{nm}$) is absorbed, and the remaining red and violet light mix, so that the solution looks reddish purple to red-purple (Figure 53.9; the band shape was approximated by a Gaussian). The molar absorption coefficient is only $\varepsilon\simeq5\ \mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,cm^{-1}}$ at the peak. This is because the $d$–$d$ transition is Laporte-forbidden owing to the inversion center of the octahedron (Chapter 48, Section 48.2), and is weakly allowed because the inversion symmetry is slightly broken by vibrations of the ligands. In a little more detail: when the ligands move in an odd-parity vibration (of $t_{1u}$ or $t_{2u}$ type), a little of an odd-parity state (the metal $p$ orbitals and the ligand orbitals) mixes into the $d$ orbitals, and the $d$–$d$ transition acquires an electric dipole component. The more strongly this vibration is thermally excited, the stronger this effect becomes, so $\varepsilon$ increases with temperature as $\propto\coth(\hbar\Omega_{\mathrm u}/2k_{\mathrm B}T)$ ($\Omega_{\mathrm u}$ is the angular frequency of that odd-parity vibration). Tetrahedral complexes have no inversion center, so $d$ and $p$ mix without waiting for a vibration, and $\varepsilon$ is large, $10^2$–$10^3$ (Table 53.5, Chapter 48, Section 48.2). Expressed as an oscillator strength, $f\sim10^{-4}$ (Example 53.8).

53.5.2 Why is the absorption band broad? — The Franck–Condon principle

The absorption band in Figure 53.9 has a width as large as $4500\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ ($0.56\ \mathrm{eV}$). The electronic levels $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$ should give a single line fixed by $\Delta_o$, so why is the band so wide? In the previous chapter (Chapter 52) we saw that the width of a line is determined by the lifetime of the state (the natural linewidth) and is narrow, $\Delta E=\hbar A\sim10^{-9}$–$10^{-7}\ \mathrm{eV}$ (from forbidden to allowed transitions). This $0.56\ \mathrm{eV}$ comes from a quite different cause: the vibration of the metal–ligand bonds in the complex.

The excited electron enters the antibonding $e_g$ orbital that extends toward the ligands. When an electron enters an antibonding orbital, the metal–ligand bond weakens and the equilibrium distance lengthens (Figure 53.10). Because the motion of the nuclei is much slower than the motion of the electrons, the absorption of light occurs before the nuclei move (the Franck–Condon principle). The transition then occurs “vertically” from the equilibrium nuclear configuration of the ground state ($Q=0$) toward the potential curve of the excited state. Since it arrives at a place displaced from the equilibrium configuration $Q_0$ of the excited state, many lines to the high vibrational levels ($m=0,1,2,\ldots$) of the excited state appear, according to the magnitude of the overlap of the nuclear configurations (the Franck–Condon factor). These lines overlap one another and form a single broad band. The excited complex gives out light (light emission) after the vibration in the excited state has relaxed by passing heat to the lattice, so the emission is shifted to the lower-energy side relative to the absorption. This shift is called the Stokes shift (the red dashed arrow in Figure 53.10).

Derivation: Band width — the Franck–Condon factor of a displaced harmonic oscillator

Let the potential of the ground state be $\tfrac12\mu\Omega^2Q^2$ and that of the excited state be $E_0+\tfrac12\mu\Omega^2(Q-Q_0)^2$ (the simplest model: harmonic oscillators of the same frequency $\Omega$ whose equilibrium positions differ only by a shift $Q_0$; $\mu$ is the mass of the mode). At $T=0$ we start from the vibrational ground state $\ket{0}_{\mathrm g}$ of the electronic ground state. The vibrational level $\ket{m'}_{\mathrm e}$ of the excited state is the vibrational level $\ket{m}_{\mathrm g}$ of the ground state translated by $Q_0$; that is, $\ket{m'}_{\mathrm e}=\hat{D}\ket{m}_{\mathrm g}$. The translation operator is $\hat{D}=e^{-iQ_0\hat{P}/\hbar}$ ($\hat{P}$ is the momentum conjugate to $Q$), and if we use the ladder operator $\hat{a}=\sqrt{\mu\Omega/2\hbar}\,\hat{Q}+i\hat{P}/\sqrt{2\mu\Omega\hbar}$ (Chapter 13), it can be written $\hat{D}=\exp[\sqrt{S}\,(\hat{a}^\dagger-\hat{a})]$ with $S=\mu\Omega Q_0^2/2\hbar$.

What we want is the coefficient $\braket{m'_{\mathrm e}}{0_{\mathrm g}}={}_{\mathrm g}\!\bra{m}\hat{D}^{-1}\ket{0}_{\mathrm g}$ obtained when $\ket{0}_{\mathrm g}$ is expanded in the levels $\ket{m'}_{\mathrm e}$ of the excited state. Let us apply $\hat{D}^{-1}=e^{-\sqrt{S}(\hat{a}^\dagger-\hat{a})}$ to $\ket{0}_{\mathrm g}$. Since $[\hat{a},\hat{a}^\dagger]=1$ is a constant, we can use the formula for the case where $[X,Y]$ is a constant (the BCH formula), $e^{X+Y}=e^Xe^Ye^{-[X,Y]/2}$. Letting $X=-\sqrt{S}\,\hat{a}^\dagger$ and $Y=\sqrt{S}\,\hat{a}$, we have $[X,Y]=-S[\hat{a}^\dagger,\hat{a}]=S$, so, using $\hat{a}\ket{0}=0$ and $(\hat{a}^\dagger)^m\ket{0}=\sqrt{m!}\ket{m}$, we obtain

$$ e^{-\sqrt{S}(\hat{a}^\dagger-\hat{a})}\ket{0}_{\mathrm g}=e^{-S/2}e^{-\sqrt{S}\hat{a}^\dagger}e^{\sqrt{S}\hat{a}}\ket{0}_{\mathrm g}=e^{-S/2}e^{-\sqrt{S}\hat{a}^\dagger}\ket{0}_{\mathrm g}=e^{-S/2}\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(-\sqrt{S})^m}{\sqrt{m!}}\ket{m}_{\mathrm g} $$

Therefore, if we use $\hat{D}\ket{m}_{\mathrm g}=\ket{m}_{\mathrm e}$ in $\ket{0}_{\mathrm g}=\hat{D}\,\hat{D}^{-1}\ket{0}_{\mathrm g}$, the ground state is expanded in the levels of the excited state as follows (The sign in $-\sqrt{S}$ comes from $\hat{D}^{-1}$. A state of this form is called a coherent state; we will study it in Chapter 56):

$$ \ket{0}_{\mathrm g}=e^{-S/2}\sum_{m'=0}^\infty\frac{(-\sqrt{S})^{m'}}{\sqrt{m'!}}\ket{m'}_{\mathrm e},\qquad S=\frac{\mu\Omega Q_0^2}{2\hbar}\ \ (\text{Huang–Rhys factor}) $$

The intensity of the absorption of light is proportional to the square of the overlap (the Franck–Condon factor) between the initial state $\ket{0}_{\mathrm g}$ and the final state $\ket{m'}_{\mathrm e}$, so the intensity of the $m'$th line (energy $E_0+m'\hbar\Omega$) is given by the Poisson distribution

$$ w_{m'}=\abs{\braket{m'_{\mathrm e}}{0_{\mathrm g}}}^2=e^{-S}\,\frac{S^{m'}}{m'!} $$

The mean of the Poisson distribution is $S$ and its variance is $S$, so the center of gravity of the absorption band is $E_0+S\hbar\Omega$ (which coincides with the energy of the vertical transition, $E_0+\tfrac12\mu\Omega^2Q_0^2$), and its variance is $S(\hbar\Omega)^2$. At a temperature $T$, the vibration of the initial state is thermally excited, and the variance increases to $S(\hbar\Omega)^2\coth(\hbar\Omega/2k_{\mathrm B}T)$ ($2\bar n+1=\coth(\hbar\Omega/2k_{\mathrm B}T)$). For $S\gg1$ the band is nearly Gaussian, and its full width at half maximum is $\sqrt{8\ln2}$ times the standard deviation:

\begin{equation} \Delta E_{1/2}=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\;\hbar\Omega\,\sqrt{S\coth\frac{\hbar\Omega}{2k_{\mathrm B}T}} \label{eq:53-FCwidth} \end{equation}

The larger the displacement $Q_0$ of the bond length (the larger $S$), the broader the band. ∎

Example 53.15: Estimating the width of the absorption band of a complex

Consider a totally symmetric vibration (the breathing mode) in which the metal is held fixed at the center and six ligands (a water molecule is regarded as a single particle of mass $18\ \mathrm{u}$) all stretch and contract their bond lengths by the same amount $\Delta r$. Let the vibrational energy be $\hbar\Omega=400\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, the mass of the mode be $\mu=18\ \mathrm{u}$, and the coordinate of the mode be $Q=\sqrt6\,\Delta r$ (normalization of the magnitudes of the displacements of the $6$ ligands). Assuming that the bond length in the excited state is stretched by $\Delta r=0.10\ \text{Å}$ and by $0.15\ \text{Å}$ (assumed values; they take into account that the $e_g$ electron weakens the bonds), find the Huang–Rhys factor $S$ and the full width at half maximum of the band at $300\ \mathrm{K}$.

In other words, if we suppose that the bond length is stretched in the excited state by $0.1$–$0.15\ \text{Å}$ (the bond length is about $2\ \text{Å}$, so this is about $5$–$7\,\%$), the breadth of the $d$–$d$ absorption band ($0.3$–$0.6\ \mathrm{eV}$) can be explained. On the other hand, the R line of ruby (${}^2E\to{}^4A_2$) is sharp, because the electron configuration $(t_{2g})^3$ does not change and the bond length hardly changes ($S\approx0$) (Chapter 48, Section 48.4). (The values used here are assumptions made to explain the order of magnitude of the band width; they are not measured values for individual complexes.)

53.5.3 Ruby, emerald, and sapphire, and phosphors

Even for the same $\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}$ ($3d^3$), the color changes if the host crystal is different. Ruby (in corundum $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$) looks red because the Y band ($\Delta\simeq18\,000\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, $555.6\ \mathrm{nm}$) and the U band at $404.9\ \mathrm{nm}$ absorb yellow-green and violet. In emerald (in beryl), the bonds are slightly longer, the splitting drops to $\Delta\simeq16\,000\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (about $625\ \mathrm{nm}$), the absorption band shifts to longer wavelengths, red is absorbed, and green is transmitted. Ruby glows red (fluorescence) because of the light emission of the ${}^2E\to{}^4A_2$ transition at $694.3\ \mathrm{nm}$ ($1.786\ \mathrm{eV}$), which is the same light as the laser light of Section 53.2. On the other hand, the blue of blue sapphire comes from the transfer of an electron between neighboring $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ and $\mathrm{Ti^{4+}}$ in $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$ (an intervalence charge-transfer transition); this is the strong absorption of a charge-transfer transition, not of a $d$–$d$ transition. We studied this in detail in Chapter 48, Section 48.4.

Application: Phosphors and white LEDs, coloring of glass

  • $\mathrm{YAG{:}Ce^{3+}}$ (yellow phosphor): The $4f\leftrightarrow5d$ transition of $\mathrm{Ce^{3+}}$ ($4f^1$) changes the parity ($f\to d$), so it is Laporte-allowed and strong, and the lifetime is short, about $60$–$70\ \mathrm{ns}$. The phosphor absorbs the light of a blue InGaN LED (about $450\ \mathrm{nm}$) and emits yellow light (a broad band centered at about $550\ \mathrm{nm}$), which mixes with the remaining blue to make a white LED.
  • $\mathrm{Y_2O_3{:}Eu^{3+}}$ (red phosphor): The $f$–$f$ transitions of $\mathrm{Eu^{3+}}$ ($4f^6$) are Laporte-forbidden, the emission lines are sharp (because the $f$ electrons are shielded), and the lifetime is long, on the order of milliseconds. At a site without an inversion center, ${}^5D_0\to{}^7F_2$ (an electric dipole transition) gives a vivid red at about $611\ \mathrm{nm}$. At a site with an inversion center, this transition is weak, and the magnetic dipole transition ${}^5D_0\to{}^7F_1$ (about $590\ \mathrm{nm}$, Chapter 52) dominates. From the emission spectrum we can read off the symmetry of the $\mathrm{Eu^{3+}}$ site.

From the ligand-field splitting $\Delta$ and the selection rules that determine the position and intensity of the absorption bands (depending on the presence or absence of an inversion center), the color can be designed (Chapter 48, Section 48.3).

53.5.4 Infrared absorption of molecules — climbing one rung of the harmonic oscillator ladder

Light of wavelengths longer than visible light (infrared radiation, with wavelength $1$–$100\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ and wavenumber $100$–$10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$; see the spectrum of electromagnetic waves in AD 03, “Introductory University Physics,” Chapter 42, Section 42.6 (in Japanese)) excites the vibration of molecules. The vibration of the internuclear distance $x$ (the deviation from equilibrium) of a diatomic molecule is that of a harmonic oscillator of reduced mass $\mu$, $\tfrac12\mu\omega^2x^2$ (Chapter 19), and the energy levels form an equally spaced ladder $E_n=(n+\tfrac12)\hbar\omega$ ($n=0,1,2,\ldots$) (Chapter 13, University Mathematics, Chapter 46, Section 46.2). Infrared light acts on the molecule through the electric dipole interaction $-\hat{d}E$. The electric dipole moment $d(x)$ of the molecule is a function of the internuclear distance; expanded about equilibrium, it is

\begin{equation} d(x)=d_0+\left(\frac{\partial d}{\partial x}\right)_0x+\frac12\left(\frac{\partial^2d}{\partial x^2}\right)_0x^2+\cdots \label{eq:53-dipole} \end{equation}

We compute the transition matrix element $\mel{n'}{\hat{d}}{n}$ using the position operator $\hat{x}=\sqrt{\hbar/2\mu\omega}\,(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)$ (the ladder operators; Chapter 13). By orthogonality, the constant term $d_0$ contributes only to $n'=n$ and is irrelevant to transitions ($n'\ne n$). For the first-order term, the action of the ladder operators, $\hat{a}\ket{n}=\sqrt{n}\ket{n-1}$ and $\hat{a}^\dagger\ket{n}=\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1}$, gives

\begin{equation} \mel{n+1}{\hat{x}}{n}=\sqrt{\frac{(n+1)\hbar}{2\mu\omega}},\qquad \mel{n-1}{\hat{x}}{n}=\sqrt{\frac{n\hbar}{2\mu\omega}},\qquad \mel{n'}{\hat{x}}{n}=0\ \ (n'\ne n\pm1) \label{eq:53-xmel} \end{equation}

(Here $\hat{a}$ and $\hat{a}^\dagger$ change $n$ by exactly one. For the oscillator of a single particle of mass $m$, we replace $\mu\to m$ and obtain $\sqrt{(n+1)\hbar/2m\omega}$.) Therefore, the intensity of infrared absorption is proportional to $(\partial d/\partial x)_0^2\abs{\mel{n'}{\hat{x}}{n}}^2$, and for the harmonic oscillator we have

Law 53.1: Selection rule for the infrared absorption of molecules

(1) Only transitions in which the vibrational quantum number changes by exactly one ($\Delta n=\pm1$) are allowed (in the harmonic oscillator approximation; transitions with $\Delta n=\pm2$ or more are forbidden, and appear weakly through the anharmonicity and the $x^2$ term). (2) The dipole moment must change with the internuclear distance ($(\partial d/\partial x)_0\ne0$). Homonuclear diatomic molecules ($\mathrm{N_2}$, $\mathrm{O_2}$, $\mathrm{H_2}$) have $d\equiv0$ throughout the stretching, so they are infrared-inactive.

The energy of the transition $n\to n+1$ to the adjacent rung of the equally spaced ladder is $\hbar\omega$ regardless of $n$, so the infrared absorption is a single line at $\hbar\omega$. The fraction of molecules excited at room temperature is given by the Boltzmann factor $e^{-\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}$, which for CO is $e^{-2143/208.5}=3.4\times10^{-5}$, so almost all molecules are in $n=0$, and what is observed is the single line $n=0\to1$ (the fundamental). (Homonuclear molecules cannot be seen in the infrared, but they are observed in light scattering (Raman scattering), because the polarizability $\alpha_{\mathrm p}$ changes with the internuclear distance.)

Example 53.16: Infrared absorption of CO: energy, wavelength, spring constant, and amplitude

The infrared absorption (the fundamental) of carbon monoxide ${}^{12}\mathrm{C}{}^{16}\mathrm{O}$ lies at the wavenumber $2143\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. (1) Find the photon energy (eV), the wavelength ($\mu\mathrm{m}$), and the frequency (THz). (2) From the reduced mass $\mu=6.856\ \mathrm{u}$, find the spring constant $k_0=\mu\omega^2$ and compare it with the value in Table 19.2 of Chapter 19. (3) Find the amplitude of the zero-point vibration $x_0=\sqrt{\hbar/2\mu\omega}$ and compare it with the bond length $112.8\ \mathrm{pm}$. (4) For the transition matrix elements $\mel{n\pm1}{\hat{x}}{n}$, find the ratio $\mel{1}{\hat{x}}{0}:\mel{2}{\hat{x}}{1}:\mel{3}{\hat{x}}{2}$. (5) When the anharmonicity is included, the wavenumber of the first overtone ($n=0\to2$) is $2\omega_{\mathrm e}-6\omega_{\mathrm e}x_{\mathrm e}$. From $\omega_{\mathrm e}=2169.81\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ and $\omega_{\mathrm e}x_{\mathrm e}=13.29\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, find the wavenumbers of the fundamental and the first overtone.

Solution. (1) $E=2143\times1.23984\times10^{-4}=0.2657\ \mathrm{eV}$. The wavelength is $10^4/2143=4.666\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ ($4.67\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, mid-infrared), and the frequency is $c\tilde\nu=2.998\times10^{10}\times2143=6.42\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{Hz}=64.2\ \mathrm{THz}$. (2) $\omega=2\pi c\tilde\nu=2\pi\times2.998\times10^{10}\times2143=4.037\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and from $\mu=6.856\times1.6605\times10^{-27}=1.1385\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$ we get $k_0=\mu\omega^2=1.1385\times10^{-26}\times(4.037\times10^{14})^2=1855\ \mathrm{N/m}$. This is $2.5\,\%$ smaller than the value $1902\ \mathrm{N/m}$ in Table 19.2 (which is obtained from the wavenumber $2170\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$; this is the wavenumber $\omega_{\mathrm e}$ of the harmonic vibration with the anharmonicity removed). Because of the anharmonicity, the observed wavenumber of the fundamental, $2143\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, is about $1.2\,\%$ lower than the wavenumber $\omega_{\mathrm e}=2170\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ of the harmonic vibration, which is determined by the curvature at the bottom of the well (Chapter 20). (3) $x_0=\sqrt{\hbar/(2\mu\omega)}=\sqrt{1.0546\times10^{-34}/(2\times1.1385\times10^{-26}\times4.037\times10^{14})}=3.39\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m}=3.4\ \mathrm{pm}$. This is $3.0\,\%$ of the bond length $112.8\ \mathrm{pm}$; even in the zero-point vibration the nuclei hardly move. That is why the harmonic approximation is a good approximation. (4) By Eq. \eqref{eq:53-xmel}, $x_0\,(\sqrt1:\sqrt2:\sqrt3)=1:1.414:1.732$. The absorption intensity is the square of the matrix element, so the intensities of the transitions from $n=0,1,2$ are in the ratio $1:2:3$ and are proportional to $(n+1)$. (5) The fundamental is $\omega_{\mathrm e}-2\omega_{\mathrm e}x_{\mathrm e}=2169.81-26.58=2143.23\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (in agreement with the observed value $2143\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$). The first overtone is $2\times2169.81-6\times13.29=4259.88\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (wavelength $2.35\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, near-infrared), which is about $26\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ lower than twice the fundamental ($4286\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$). (Its intensity is about two orders of magnitude weaker than that of the fundamental.)

Application: Identifying molecules by infrared absorption — greenhouse gases and NDIR gas sensors

The position (wavenumber) of an infrared absorption is a “fingerprint” of the kind and strength (spring constant) of a bond, and it is widely used in the analysis of materials (FT-IR spectroscopy) (Chapter 19, Section 19.2). Whether a vibration is infrared-active or inactive is determined by $(\partial d/\partial x)_0\ne0$. Among the components of the atmosphere, $\mathrm{N_2}$ ($78\,\%$) and $\mathrm{O_2}$ ($21\,\%$) are infrared-inactive, so they do not absorb the infrared radiation emitted from the Earth's surface, and most of the atmosphere is transparent to infrared radiation. On the other hand, $\mathrm{CO_2}$ (the antisymmetric stretching vibration is at $2349\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, wavelength $4.26\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, and there is also a bending vibration at $667\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$), $\mathrm{H_2O}$, and $\mathrm{CH_4}$ change their dipole moments on stretching or bending, so they absorb infrared radiation (the greenhouse effect). Using this absorption at $4.26\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, we can let a gas flow between an infrared light source and a detector and measure the $\mathrm{CO_2}$ concentration from the magnitude of the absorption (an NDIR (non-dispersive infrared) gas sensor. This is a method that does not separate the light by wavelength with a prism or a diffraction grating, but takes out only one wavelength with a filter. The absorption obeys the Beer–Lambert law, Eq. \eqref{eq:53-beer}).

53.6 Summary and Exercises

53.6.1 Summary

  • Amplification and absorption of light: The intensity of monochromatic light obeys $\dd I/\dd z=\sigma(N_2-N_1)I$ ($g_1=g_2$; in general, $N_2-(g_2/g_1)N_1$). If $N_1\gt N_2$ (thermal equilibrium always falls in this case), we have Beer–Lambert absorption $I=I_0e^{-\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}d}$; if $N_2\gt N_1$ (population inversion), we have exponential amplification (a laser). The cross section is $\sigma(\omega)=(\hbar\omega/c)Bg(\omega)$.
  • A two-level system cannot be inverted in the stationary state: In the stationary state under incoherent excitation, $N_2/N_1=Bu/(A+Bu)\lt1$ (even as $u\to\infty$ the ratio only approaches 1, and the absorption saturates and the medium becomes transparent). In thermal radiation, the ratio follows the Boltzmann distribution $e^{-\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}$.
  • Three-level systems (ruby type) and four-level systems (Nd:YAG type): Fast nonradiative relaxation accumulates atoms in a metastable level, and the degree of inversion, as a function of $x=W/A_{21}$, is $(x-1)/(x+1)$ for three levels ($x\gt1$; more than half of the ground-state atoms are excited) and $x(1-r)/[1+x(1+r)]\to x/(1+x)$ for four levels (inversion with only a little pumping). The long lifetime of a forbidden transition helps the accumulation.
  • Resonator and threshold: $R_1R_2e^{2\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}\ell}=1$ (gain = loss). Above the threshold, the population inversion is clamped at $\Delta N_{\mathrm{th}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}/\sigma$, and all the extra pumping goes into laser light. Ruby: $694.3\ \mathrm{nm}=1.786\ \mathrm{eV}$; Nd:YAG: $1064\ \mathrm{nm}=1.165\ \mathrm{eV}$; a semiconductor laser has $\hbar\omega\gtrsim E_{\mathrm g}$ (difference of the quasi-Fermi levels $F_{\mathrm c}-F_{\mathrm v}\gt E_{\mathrm g}$, double heterostructure); a quantum cascade laser uses transitions between subbands, so its wavelength is determined by the width of the well.
  • Conversion between absorption quantities: $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=N\sigma$, $A=\varepsilon c_{\mathrm M}\ell=\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}d/\ln10$, $\sigma=3.82\times10^{-21}\varepsilon\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=2.303\,\varepsilon c_{\mathrm M}$. $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}=4\pi\kappa/\lambda$ (the imaginary part of the complex refractive index).
  • Oscillator strength: $f=2m\omega\abs{x_{fi}}^2/\hbar$. From Fermi's golden rule, $\int\sigma\dd\nu=e_0^2f/(4\varepsilon_0m_{\mathrm e}c)=\pi r_{\mathrm e}cf$ ($=2.654\times10^{-6}f\ \mathrm{m^2\,Hz}$). We have $f=1$ for a harmonic oscillator, $0.961$ for the $1\to2$ transition in a box, $0.416$ for the $1s\to2p$ transition in hydrogen, and $\sim10^{-4}$ for the $d$–$d$ transitions of octahedral complexes. $A_{21}=e_0^2\omega^2f/(2\pi\varepsilon_0m_{\mathrm e}c^3)$. The sum rule $\sum f=1$ appears in Chapter 54.
  • Interband transitions in semiconductors: The transitions are vertical (the photon wavenumber is $0.002$ times $\pi/a$). $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\propto(\abs{p_{\mathrm{cv}}}^2/\omega)J_{\mathrm{cv}}$. For parabolic bands, from $J_{\mathrm{cv}}=\frac{1}{4\pi^2}(2\mu/\hbar^2)^{3/2}\sqrt{\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}}$, a direct transition gives $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\propto\sqrt{\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}}$ (the Tauc plot $(\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}\hbar\omega)^2$ is a straight line), and an indirect transition gives $(\hbar\omega-E_{\mathrm g}\pm\hbar\Omega)^2$, from the convolution of the densities of states of the electron and the hole ($\int\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sqrt{E-\varepsilon}\,\dd\varepsilon=\pi E^2/8$). An indirect transition has an absorption about one order of magnitude weaker and is unsuited to light emission. Whether a transition is direct or indirect is determined not by inversion symmetry but by the positions of the band edges in $\bm{k}$ (the kind of orbital).
  • Colors of complexes: The color is that of the light that is not absorbed (the complementary color). $[\mathrm{Ti(H_2O)_6}]^{3+}$ has $\Delta_o=20\,300\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}=2.517\ \mathrm{eV}\to493\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $\varepsilon\sim5$ (the Laporte-forbidden transition is weakly allowed by vibronic coupling). Charge-transfer transitions ($\mathrm{MnO_4^-}$, $\varepsilon\sim2.4\times10^3$) are about $400$–$500$ times stronger. The band is broad because the bond length increases in the excited state (Franck–Condon), and $\Delta E_{1/2}=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\hbar\Omega\sqrt{S\coth(\hbar\Omega/2k_{\mathrm B}T)}$.
  • Infrared absorption of molecules: From $\mel{n\pm1}{\hat{x}}{n}=\sqrt{(n+1)\hbar/2\mu\omega}$ and so on (using $\hat{a}$ and $\hat{a}^\dagger$), both $\Delta n=\pm1$ and $(\partial d/\partial x)_0\ne0$ are required. CO has $2143\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}=0.2657\ \mathrm{eV}=4.67\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ and $k_0=1855\ \mathrm{N/m}$. $\mathrm{N_2}$ and $\mathrm{O_2}$ are infrared-inactive.
