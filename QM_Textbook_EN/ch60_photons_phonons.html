Chapter 60Photons and Phonons — Field Quantization and Processes That Change the Particle Number

When a hot iron rod glows red, what is going on inside the rod? The atoms in a crystal vibrate constantly about their equilibrium positions (lattice vibrations), and this vibration emits light through the motion of charges. To emit light means that the energy of the electromagnetic field increases. However, the wave function of the Schrödinger equation, which we studied in the preceding chapters, was a tool for investigating the motion of a system with a fixed number of particles. To describe events such as “one photon is created” and “one quantum of lattice vibration is annihilated,” we need the language of creation and annihilation operators that we learned in Part X. As the last chapter of Part X, this chapter applies that language to the two fields that matter most for the physical properties of materials—lattice vibrations and the electromagnetic field. Their quanta are the phonon and the photon.

The phrase “quantize a field” sounds weighty, but what we do is a repetition of Chapter 56. The key is that $N$ coupled harmonic oscillators become $N$ mutually independent harmonic oscillators when we switch to suitable coordinates (normal coordinates). Each independent mode is exactly the single harmonic oscillator we solved in Chapter 56, and the quantum number $n$ becomes “the number of quanta (phonons or photons) in that mode.” In this chapter, we first see this concretely for a chain of atoms lined up in a row (Section 60.1), and then show that the plane-wave modes of the electromagnetic field have the same structure (Section 60.2). Next, starting from the average number per mode, $\bar{n}=1/(e^{\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}-1)$, we rebuild Planck's formula and compare the three kinds of photon-number distributions: thermal, coherent, and Fock (Section 60.3).

The difference from classical physics (AD 03, Chapter 53, Section 53.2 (in Japanese), Section 53.7 (in Japanese)) also becomes clearly visible for the first time in this chapter. In classical electromagnetism, the electromagnetic field in vacuum is zero, and nothing makes an excited atom emit light. The quantized electromagnetic field, however, has a zero-point vibration even when there is not a single photon, and this is what makes an excited atom emit light. Spontaneous emission can be regarded as stimulated emission by vacuum fluctuations (Section 60.4). The process in which a semiconductor absorbs light and creates an electron–hole pair, the process by which an LED emits light, the process in which an electron emits a phonon and loses energy, and the reason why temperature can be measured by Raman scattering (Section 60.5) can all be written with terms of the same form, “the number of photons or phonons increases or decreases by one.” Finally, we show that an oscillator driven by an external force ends up in a coherent state, discuss the Franck–Condon principle and the Huang–Rhys factor, and derive from the Heisenberg equation the mechanical response function $\chi(\omega)=1/[m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)]$, which serves as an introduction to the linear response that we studied in Part IX (Chapter 54) and to the companion volume “Linear Response Theory” (AD 08) (Section 60.6).

Position within Part X. In Chapter 56 we studied a single oscillator, in Chapter 57 many modes and bosons and fermions, in Chapter 58 one-body and two-body operators and field operators, and in Chapter 59 the electron gas and the Hubbard model. This chapter puts those tools to use on “processes in which the number of particles changes.” In Chapter 52 of Part IX, we treated light as a classical electromagnetic wave and determined the Einstein coefficients from an argument based on thermal equilibrium. In this chapter, we quantize the electromagnetic field, derive the same spontaneous emission rate $A=\omega^3\abs{d}^2/(3\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c^3)$ directly, and check that it agrees with the result of Chapter 52.

60.1 From Coupled Oscillators to Normal Modes — Quantization of Lattice Vibrations and Phonons

The harmonic oscillator we solved in Chapter 56 was a system consisting of a single point mass attached to a spring. An atom in a crystal is bound to its equilibrium position by the forces acting between it and the surrounding atoms, so within the range of small displacements each atom resembles a harmonic oscillator. However, atoms are not isolated. If one atom moves, its distance from the neighboring atom changes, and a force acts on that atom as well. In other words, $N$ harmonic oscillators are connected to one another by springs. Let us examine this situation in its simplest form—a chain of atoms lined up in a straight line.

60.1.1 Equations of motion of a chain of atoms

Definition 60.1: Model of a one-dimensional atomic chain

$N$ atoms of mass $M$ are arranged at the equilibrium positions $x_n=na$ ($n=0,1,\dots,N-1$, where $a$ is the spacing between atoms), and neighboring atoms are connected by springs of spring constant $K$ (upper panel of Figure 60.1). Let $u_n$ denote the displacement of atom $n$ from its equilibrium position (positive in the direction along the chain), and let $p_n=M\dot{u}_n$ be its momentum. To eliminate the effects of the two ends, we close the chain into a ring and set $u_{n+N}=u_n$ (periodic boundary conditions; Born and von Kármán, 1912).

The energy is the sum of the kinetic energy and the energy stored in the springs between neighbors:

\begin{equation} H=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{p_n^2}{2M}+\frac{K}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(u_{n+1}-u_n\right)^2 \label{eq:60-chain-H} \end{equation}

Since the stretching of a spring is $u_{n+1}-u_n$, the second term is the sum of $\tfrac12K(\text{stretching})^2$ over the springs, one term per spring. Two forces act on atom $n$: one from the spring on its right (if its stretching is $u_{n+1}-u_n$, a force $K(u_{n+1}-u_n)$ directed to the right) and one from the spring on its left (if its stretching is $u_n-u_{n-1}$, a force $K(u_n-u_{n-1})$ directed to the left), so the equation of motion is

\begin{equation} M\ddot{u}_n=K\left(u_{n+1}-u_n\right)-K\left(u_n-u_{n-1}\right)=K\left(u_{n+1}-2u_n+u_{n-1}\right) \label{eq:60-chain-eom} \end{equation}

The $N$ equations for $n=0,1,\dots,N-1$ are intertwined with one another through $u_{n\pm1}$. To solve them, recall that in the limit $a\to0$ they become the wave equation of a string (University Mathematics, Chapter 43, Section 43.2), and let us try a wavelike solution $u_n=A\,e^{i(kna-\omega t)}$. This is the plane wave $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ at position $x=na$, evaluated only at the atomic positions.

Derivation: Wavelike solution and the dispersion relation

Substitute $u_n=A\,e^{i(kna-\omega t)}$ into Eq. \eqref{eq:60-chain-eom}. The second derivative with respect to time is $\ddot{u}_n=-\omega^2u_n$, and $u_{n\pm1}=A\,e^{i(kna-\omega t)}e^{\pm ika}=u_n\,e^{\pm ika}$, so canceling the common factor $u_n$ gives

$$ -M\omega^2=K\left(e^{ika}-2+e^{-ika}\right)=K\,(2\cos ka-2)=-4K\sin^2\frac{ka}{2} $$

(the last equality uses $1-\cos ka=2\sin^2(ka/2)$). Therefore $\omega^2=\dfrac{4K}{M}\sin^2\dfrac{ka}{2}$, and taking $\omega\ge0$ we obtain the following dispersion relation.

∎

\begin{equation} \omega_k=2\sqrt{\frac{K}{M}}\;\left|\sin\frac{ka}{2}\right| \label{eq:60-disp} \end{equation}

The periodic boundary conditions $u_{n+N}=u_n$ require $e^{ikNa}=1$. Hence the wavenumber $k$ is restricted to the discrete values

\begin{equation} k=\frac{2\pi}{Na}\,m\qquad(m\text{ is an integer}) \label{eq:60-k} \end{equation}

Furthermore, $k$ and $k+2\pi/a$ cannot be distinguished at the atomic positions $x=na$, since $e^{i(2\pi/a)na}=e^{2\pi in}=1$ (a wave whose wavelength is shorter than the atomic spacing gives, on the lattice, the same displacements as a longer wave). Thus the independent values of $k$ are only the $N$ values in $-\pi/a\lt k\le\pi/a$ (the first Brillouin zone), which agrees with the number $N$ of degrees of freedom of the motion of the $N$ atoms.

Let us look at the form of the dispersion relation \eqref{eq:60-disp} (Figure 60.2(a)). For long wavelengths ($ka\ll1$), $\sin(ka/2)\approx ka/2$, so

$$ \omega_k\approx a\sqrt{\frac{K}{M}}\;\abs{k}\equiv v\abs{k}\qquad\left(v=a\sqrt{K/M}\right) $$

and $\omega$ is proportional to $k$, with $v$ the speed of sound (the same as for waves on a continuous string; this is the speed of sound of the lattice vibrations of a solid, AD 03, Section 53.7 (in Japanese)). As $k$ grows, however, the curve departs from the straight line and flattens, and at the zone edge $k=\pi/a$ it takes its maximum value $\omega_{\max}=2\sqrt{K/M}$. The group velocity $\dd\omega/\dd k=a\sqrt{K/M}\cos(ka/2)$ becomes $0$ at the edge, and that wave is a standing wave that does not propagate (Figure 60.1(c)). Since no wave exists with a wavelength shorter than twice the atomic spacing ($2a$), the frequency has an upper limit. This is an effect of the discrete arrangement of the atoms, one that the continuous string did not have. Note also that the band $E(k)=\alpha+2\beta\cos ka$ for electron waves (Chapter 41, Section 41.2) also describes a wave on the same lattice, so the periodicity in $k$ and the flat shape at the zone edge are common to both.

Example 60.1: Spring constant and maximum frequency of a Cu-type one-dimensional chain

Replace a Cu crystal by a one-dimensional chain. Let the atomic mass be $M=63.55\ \mathrm{u}$ ($1\ \mathrm{u}=1.660\,539\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$), the spacing $a=0.255\ \mathrm{nm}$ (the nearest-neighbor interatomic distance), and the speed of sound $v=4.76\ \mathrm{km/s}$. (1) Find the spring constant $K$. (2) Find the maximum angular frequency $\omega_{\max}$, the frequency $\omega_{\max}/2\pi$, and the phonon energy $\hbar\omega_{\max}$. (3) Find the frequency of the mode with $ka=\pi/2$.

Solution. (1) $M=63.55\times1.660\,539\times10^{-27}=1.0553\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$. From $v=a\sqrt{K/M}$ we get $K=Mv^2/a^2$. Since $v^2=(4.76\times10^{3})^2=2.2658\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}$ and $a^2=(0.255\times10^{-9})^2=6.5025\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, we have

$$ K=\frac{1.0553\times10^{-25}\times2.2658\times10^{7}}{6.5025\times10^{-20}}=\frac{2.3911\times10^{-18}}{6.5025\times10^{-20}}=36.8\ \mathrm{N/m} $$

(the unit is $\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}}/\mathrm{m^2}=\mathrm{N/m}$).

(2) $\omega_{\max}=2\sqrt{K/M}=2v/a=\dfrac{2\times4760}{0.255\times10^{-9}}=3.73\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{rad/s}$. The frequency is $\omega_{\max}/2\pi=5.94\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{Hz}=5.94\ \mathrm{THz}$ (converted to a wavenumber, $5.94\times10^{12}/(2.998\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{cm/s})=198\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$). The phonon energy is $\hbar\omega_{\max}=1.0546\times10^{-34}\times3.733\times10^{13}=3.94\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{J}=24.6\ \mathrm{meV}$ ($1\ \mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$).

(3) For $ka=\pi/2$, $\sin(ka/2)=\sin(\pi/4)=1/\sqrt2$, so $\omega=\omega_{\max}/\sqrt2$ and the frequency is $5.94/\sqrt2=4.20\ \mathrm{THz}$ ($\hbar\omega=17.4\ \mathrm{meV}$). The actual highest lattice-vibration frequency of Cu is about $7\ \mathrm{THz}$. Because of three-dimensional effects (transverse and longitudinal waves, and coupling to more distant neighbors), it differs from the value for the one-dimensional chain by about 20%, but the order of magnitude agrees well.

60.1.2 Normal coordinates — coupled oscillators become independent oscillators

The solution $e^{i(kna-\omega t)}$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-chain-eom} is a motion in which the $N$ atoms all oscillate together with a definite phase relation, and it is called a normal mode (Figure 60.1). The general motion is a superposition of these modes. Let us check this fact carefully by showing that the energy expression \eqref{eq:60-chain-H} can be rewritten as a “sum of independent harmonic oscillators.” We define the Fourier transforms of the coordinates and momenta (the normal coordinates and the momenta conjugate to them) by

\begin{equation} Q_k=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}u_n\,e^{-ikna},\qquad P_k=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}p_n\,e^{-ikna} \label{eq:60-QP} \end{equation}

($k$ runs over the $N$ values in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-k}). The inverse transform is

\begin{equation} u_n=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{k}Q_k\,e^{ikna},\qquad p_n=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_kP_k\,e^{ikna} \label{eq:60-QPinv} \end{equation}

That these are indeed inverse transforms follows from the discrete orthogonality relations

\begin{equation} \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{i(k-k')na}=\delta_{kk'},\qquad \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}e^{ik(n-n')a}=\delta_{nn'} \label{eq:60-orth} \end{equation}

(the formula for the sum of a geometric series; University Mathematics, Chapter 28, Section 28.3). Since $u_n$ is real, Eq. \eqref{eq:60-QP} gives $Q_{-k}=Q_k^*$, and similarly $P_{-k}=P_k^*$.

Derivation: The energy becomes diagonal in the normal coordinates

For the kinetic energy, substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:60-QPinv} and summing over $n$ with the help of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-orth} (Parseval's identity) gives

$$ \sum_np_n^2=\frac{1}{N}\sum_n\sum_{k,k'}P_kP_{k'}^*\,e^{i(k-k')na}=\sum_{k,k'}P_kP_{k'}^*\,\delta_{kk'}=\sum_k\abs{P_k}^2 $$

(we wrote $p_n^2=p_np_n^*$ and used $p_n^*=N^{-1/2}\sum_{k'}P_{k'}^*e^{-ik'na}$). For the spring energy, from $u_{n+1}-u_n=N^{-1/2}\sum_kQ_k(e^{ika}-1)e^{ikna}$ we similarly obtain

$$ \sum_n\left(u_{n+1}-u_n\right)^2=\sum_k\abs{e^{ika}-1}^2\abs{Q_k}^2=\sum_k4\sin^2\frac{ka}{2}\,\abs{Q_k}^2 $$

(here $\abs{e^{ika}-1}^2=2-2\cos ka=4\sin^2(ka/2)$). Therefore Eq. \eqref{eq:60-chain-H} becomes

\begin{equation} H=\sum_k\left[\frac{\abs{P_k}^2}{2M}+\frac12M\omega_k^2\abs{Q_k}^2\right],\qquad \omega_k^2=\frac{4K}{M}\sin^2\frac{ka}{2} \label{eq:60-Hk} \end{equation}

(we used $\tfrac K2\cdot4\sin^2(ka/2)=\tfrac12M\omega_k^2$ for the spring term).

∎

Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Hk} is the energy $\dfrac{p^2}{2M}+\dfrac12M\omega^2q^2$ of a harmonic oscillator of mass $M$ and angular frequency $\omega_k$, summed over $k$. Since $Q_{-k}=Q_k^*$, the two modes $k$ and $-k$ correspond to two real coordinates (the real and imaginary parts of $Q_k$; the traveling wave $e^{ikna}$ split into the standing waves $\cos kna$ and $\sin kna$). The motion of the $N$ coupled atoms has thus been decomposed into $N$ mutually independent harmonic oscillators. This is the first step of “field quantization.”

Math Tool: Translational symmetry and the Fourier transform

The equation of motion \eqref{eq:60-chain-eom} can be written as $\ddot{u}_n=-\sum_{m}D_{nm}u_m$ with an $N\times N$ matrix $D$ (diagonal elements $2K/M$, adjacent elements $-K/M$, and, because the chain closes into a ring, $D_{0,N-1}=D_{N-1,0}=-K/M$). This matrix is a circulant matrix whose form does not change when $n$ is shifted by one (it has translational symmetry), and its eigenvectors are necessarily plane waves $(e^{ikna})_n$, with eigenvalues $\omega_k^2$. The Fourier transform \eqref{eq:60-QP} is itself the coordinate transformation that diagonalizes the matrix $D$ (diagonalization of matrices; University Mathematics, Chapter 12, Section 12.1). The translational symmetry of the crystal is the reason why “$k$ is a good quantum number.”

60.1.3 Quantization — creation and annihilation operators for phonons

Classically the system has become a set of independent harmonic oscillators, so quantization is a repetition of Chapter 56. We promote the displacement $u_n$ and the momentum $p_n$ of the atoms to operators $\hat{u}_n$, $\hat{p}_n$ and require the canonical commutation relations

\begin{equation} \comm{\hat{u}_n}{\hat{p}_m}=i\hbar\,\delta_{nm},\qquad\comm{\hat{u}_n}{\hat{u}_m}=\comm{\hat{p}_n}{\hat{p}_m}=0 \label{eq:60-ccr} \end{equation}

If we define the normal-coordinate operators in the same form as Eq. \eqref{eq:60-QP}, $\hat{Q}_k=N^{-1/2}\sum_n\hat{u}_ne^{-ikna}$ and $\hat{P}_k=N^{-1/2}\sum_n\hat{p}_ne^{-ikna}$, then Eq. \eqref{eq:60-orth} determines the commutation relation between $\hat{Q}_k$ and $\hat{P}_{k'}$ to be

\begin{equation} \comm{\hat{Q}_k}{\hat{P}_{k'}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n,m}e^{-ikna}e^{-ik'ma}\,i\hbar\,\delta_{nm}=i\hbar\,\frac1N\sum_ne^{-i(k+k')na}=i\hbar\,\delta_{k,-k'} \label{eq:60-QPcomm} \end{equation}

(Since $k$ is determined only up to integer multiples of $2\pi/a$, $\delta_{k,-k'}$ means $k\equiv-k'\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi/a)$. At the zone edge $k=\pi/a$ we have $-k\equiv k$, so $\hat{Q}_{\pi/a}$ is a real (Hermitian) operator.) Since $\hat{Q}_k^\dagger=\hat{Q}_{-k}$ and $\hat{P}_k^\dagger=\hat{P}_{-k}$, the same relation can also be written as $\comm{\hat{Q}_k}{\hat{P}_{k'}^\dagger}=i\hbar\,\delta_{kk'}$. That is, for each $k$, $\hat{Q}_k$ and $\hat{P}_k^\dagger$ form a canonically conjugate pair. The Hamiltonian is obtained by replacing $\abs{P_k}^2=P_k^\dagger P_k$ and the like in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Hk} by operators: $\hat{H}=\sum_k\left[\hat{P}_k^\dagger\hat{P}_k/2M+\tfrac12M\omega_k^2\hat{Q}_k^\dagger\hat{Q}_k\right]$.

Here only the mode $k=0$, for which $\omega_k=0$, is treated separately. $\hat{P}_0=N^{-1/2}\sum_n\hat{p}_n$ is ($1/\sqrt N$ times) the momentum of the whole chain, and the $k=0$ term is only the kinetic energy $\hat{P}_0^\dagger\hat{P}_0/2M$ of the center of mass, a motion with no restoring force (Figure 60.1(a)). This is not a vibration, so below we consider only the $N-1$ modes with $k\ne0$ (if $N$ is even, these consist of $(N-2)/2$ pairs $\pm k$ and the zone-edge mode $k=\pi/a$, which has a single real coordinate. The number of real degrees of freedom is $2\times(N-2)/2+1=N-1$). For $k\ne0$ we define an operator of the same form as $\hat{a}=\sqrt{m\omega/2\hbar}\,(\hat{x}+i\hat{p}/m\omega)$ of Chapter 56:

\begin{equation} \hat{a}_k=\sqrt{\frac{M\omega_k}{2\hbar}}\left(\hat{Q}_k+\frac{i}{M\omega_k}\hat{P}_k\right)\qquad(k\ne0) \label{eq:60-a} \end{equation}

Theorem 60.1: Quantization of lattice vibrations

The operators $\hat{a}_k$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-a} satisfy the commutation relations

\begin{equation} \comm{\hat{a}_k}{\hat{a}_{k'}^\dagger}=\delta_{kk'},\qquad\comm{\hat{a}_k}{\hat{a}_{k'}}=0 \label{eq:60-aa} \end{equation}

and the Hamiltonian of the chain (apart from the kinetic energy of the center of mass) becomes

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=\sum_{k\ne0}\hbar\omega_k\left(\hat{a}_k^\dagger\hat{a}_k+\tfrac12\right) \label{eq:60-Hph} \end{equation}

The displacement and the momentum are expanded as follows.

\begin{equation} \hat{u}_n=\sum_{k\ne0}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2NM\omega_k}}\left(\hat{a}_k\,e^{ikna}+\hat{a}_k^\dagger\,e^{-ikna}\right),\qquad \hat{p}_n=-i\sum_{k\ne0}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar M\omega_k}{2N}}\left(\hat{a}_k\,e^{ikna}-\hat{a}_k^\dagger\,e^{-ikna}\right) \label{eq:60-u} \end{equation}

Proof

Commutation relations. Since $\hat{a}_{k'}^\dagger=\sqrt{M\omega_{k'}/2\hbar}\,\bigl(\hat{Q}_{k'}^\dagger-i\hat{P}_{k'}^\dagger/M\omega_{k'}\bigr)$, using the fact that the $\hat{Q}$'s commute among themselves, as do the $\hat{P}$'s, we have

$$ \comm{\hat{a}_k}{\hat{a}_{k'}^\dagger}=\frac{M\sqrt{\omega_k\omega_{k'}}}{2\hbar}\left(\frac{-i}{M\omega_{k'}}\comm{\hat{Q}_k}{\hat{P}_{k'}^\dagger}+\frac{i}{M\omega_k}\comm{\hat{P}_k}{\hat{Q}_{k'}^\dagger}\right) $$

Inserting $\comm{\hat{Q}_k}{\hat{P}_{k'}^\dagger}=i\hbar\,\delta_{kk'}$ and $\comm{\hat{P}_k}{\hat{Q}_{k'}^\dagger}=-\comm{\hat{Q}_{k'}^\dagger}{\hat{P}_k}=-i\hbar\,\delta_{kk'}$ ($\comm{\hat{Q}_{-k'}}{\hat{P}_k}=i\hbar\,\delta_{-k',-k}$), and noting that $\omega_k=\omega_{k'}$ for $k=k'$, we obtain

$$ \comm{\hat{a}_k}{\hat{a}_{k'}^\dagger}=\frac{M\omega_k}{2\hbar}\left(\frac{\hbar}{M\omega_k}+\frac{\hbar}{M\omega_k}\right)\delta_{kk'}=\delta_{kk'} $$

For $\comm{\hat{a}_k}{\hat{a}_{k'}}$, from $\comm{\hat{Q}_k}{\hat{P}_{k'}}=i\hbar\,\delta_{k,-k'}$ we get $\dfrac{M\sqrt{\omega_k\omega_{k'}}}{2\hbar}\left(\dfrac{i}{M\omega_{k'}}-\dfrac{i}{M\omega_k}\right)i\hbar\,\delta_{k,-k'}$, which is $0$ because $\omega_{-k}=\omega_k$.

The Hamiltonian. Expanding $\hat{a}_k^\dagger\hat{a}_k$ (the same calculation as the factorization in Chapter 56) gives

$$ \hat{a}_k^\dagger\hat{a}_k=\frac{M\omega_k}{2\hbar}\left[\hat{Q}_k^\dagger\hat{Q}_k+\frac{\hat{P}_k^\dagger\hat{P}_k}{(M\omega_k)^2}+\frac{i}{M\omega_k}\left(\hat{Q}_k^\dagger\hat{P}_k-\hat{P}_k^\dagger\hat{Q}_k\right)\right] $$

Multiply both sides by $\hbar\omega_k$ and sum over $k$. For the last term, replacing $k\to-k$ in the second part, we get $\sum_k\dfrac{i}{M\omega_k}\left(\hat{Q}_{-k}\hat{P}_k-\hat{P}_k\hat{Q}_{-k}\right)=\sum_k\dfrac{i}{M\omega_k}\comm{\hat{Q}_{-k}}{\hat{P}_k}=\sum_k\dfrac{i\cdot i\hbar}{M\omega_k}$. Therefore

$$ \sum_k\hbar\omega_k\hat{a}_k^\dagger\hat{a}_k=\sum_k\left[\frac{\hat{P}_k^\dagger\hat{P}_k}{2M}+\frac12M\omega_k^2\hat{Q}_k^\dagger\hat{Q}_k\right]-\sum_k\frac{\hbar\omega_k}{2} $$

and the first term on the right-hand side is $\hat{H}$. Moving the last term to the other side gives Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Hph}.

Expansion. Take the sum and the difference of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-a} and its Hermitian conjugate $\hat{a}_{-k}^\dagger=\sqrt{M\omega_k/2\hbar}\,\bigl(\hat{Q}_k-i\hat{P}_k/M\omega_k\bigr)$ (we used $\hat{Q}_{-k}^\dagger=\hat{Q}_k$ and $\hat{P}_{-k}^\dagger=\hat{P}_k$). This gives $\hat{Q}_k=\sqrt{\hbar/2M\omega_k}\,(\hat{a}_k+\hat{a}_{-k}^\dagger)$ and $\hat{P}_k=-i\sqrt{\hbar M\omega_k/2}\,(\hat{a}_k-\hat{a}_{-k}^\dagger)$. Substituting these into Eq. \eqref{eq:60-QPinv} and replacing $k\to-k$ in the term containing $\hat{a}_{-k}^\dagger$ ($\omega_{-k}=\omega_k$), we obtain Eq. \eqref{eq:60-u}.

∎

Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Hph} is, for each $k$, exactly the $\hat{H}=\hbar\omega(\hat{N}+\tfrac12)$ of Chapter 56. The eigenvalues $n_k=0,1,2,\dots$ of the number operator $\hat{N}_k=\hat{a}_k^\dagger\hat{a}_k$ give the number of quanta in mode $k$. These are the phonons. The states are specified by sets of occupation numbers, $\ket{\{n_k\}}=\prod_k\dfrac{(\hat{a}_k^\dagger)^{n_k}}{\sqrt{n_k!}}\ket{0}$ (Chapter 57; here $\ket{0}$ is “the vacuum in which no mode contains a phonon”), and the energy is $E=\sum_k\hbar\omega_k(n_k+\tfrac12)$.

Definition 60.2: Phonon

The quantum of a normal mode of lattice vibration (wavenumber $k$) is called a phonon. $\hat{a}_k^\dagger$ creates one phonon of energy $\hbar\omega_k$, and $\hat{a}_k$ annihilates one. A phonon is an apparent particle in a crystal (a quasiparticle), and it has energy $\hbar\omega_k$ and crystal momentum $\hbar k$. It is a boson (any number of phonons can occupy the same mode), and its number is not conserved.

Discussion: Notation for phonon operators — $\hat{a}_k$ and $\hat{b}_{\bm{q}\nu}$

Up to the end of Section 60.1, we write the operator for the quantum of lattice vibration as $\hat{a}_k$, in order to show its continuity with the $\hat{a}$ of Chapter 56. From Section 60.2 on, where photons appear, we use $\hat{b}_{\bm{q}\nu}$ to distinguish it from the photon operator $\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}$ ($\bm{q}$ is the wavevector and $\nu$ is the branch index; these are the same symbols as in AD 09). A one-dimensional monatomic chain has only one branch, so $\hat{b}_q=\hat{a}_k$ ($q=k$). In a three-dimensional crystal with $r$ atoms in the unit cell, there are $3r$ branches in all: three acoustic modes and $3(r-1)$ optical modes (treated in Chapter 13, Phonons I (in Japanese) of the companion volume “Solid-State Physics,” AD 06). In the hole operator $\hat{h}$, the $h$ is the initial letter of “hole” and has nothing to do with the Planck constant.

Caution: The crystal momentum $\hbar k$ is not real momentum

A phonon is not a motion of the crystal as a whole but the quantum of the motion in which atoms shake around their equilibrium positions, so the mode of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-u} does not carry the total momentum of the chain (the total momentum $\sum_n\hat{p}_n=\sqrt{N}\hat{P}_0$ consists only of the $k=0$ component and does not include modes with $k\ne0$). $\hbar k$ is the crystal momentum, which is determined by the translational symmetry of the crystal (a shift by $a$ gives a phase factor $e^{ika}$), and it does not change under the replacement $k\to k+2\pi/a$. In a collision involving phonons, crystal momentum is conserved except for a difference of $2\pi\hbar m/a$ ($m$ is an integer, and $2\pi m/a$ is a reciprocal lattice vector). The case $m\ne0$ is called an Umklapp process.

The term on the right in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-u}, $\hat{a}_k^\dagger e^{-ikna}$, is the Hermitian conjugate (abbreviated h.c.) of the term on the left, $\hat{a}_ke^{ikna}$. Since $\hat{u}_n$ is a Hermitian operator, this pair always appears in the sum. Looking at the expansion of the displacement in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-u}, we see that the coefficient of $\hat{a}_k$ is proportional to $1/\sqrt{\omega_k}$. A mode with a softer spring (smaller $\omega_k$) moves more, even for the same quantum number. Also, because of the factor $1/\sqrt N$, the displacement per atom caused by a single phonon is smaller the larger the crystal is.

Example 60.2: The number of phonons in a lattice-vibration mode

For the chain of Example 60.1, find the average phonon number $\bar{n}=1/(e^{\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}-1)$ of the mode at the zone edge ($ka=\pi$; $\hbar\omega_{\max}=24.6\ \mathrm{meV}$) at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ and at $10\ \mathrm{K}$. Also compare the value at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ for the mode with $ka=\pi/2$ ($\hbar\omega=17.4\ \mathrm{meV}$) and the average energy $\hbar\omega(\bar{n}+\tfrac12)$ of the zone-edge mode with $k_{\mathrm B}T$.

Solution. $k_{\mathrm B}T$ is $25.85\ \mathrm{meV}$ at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ and $0.8617\ \mathrm{meV}$ at $10\ \mathrm{K}$ ($k_{\mathrm B}=8.617\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/K}$). For the zone-edge mode at $300\ \mathrm{K}$, $\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T=24.57/25.85=0.9505$ and $e^{0.9505}=2.587$, so the average phonon number is

$$ \bar{n}=\frac{1}{2.587-1}=0.63 $$

At $10\ \mathrm{K}$, $\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T=24.57/0.8617=28.5$, and $\bar{n}\approx e^{-28.5}=4\times10^{-13}$. At $10\ \mathrm{K}$, virtually not even $1$ phonon is excited in this mode. For the mode with $ka=\pi/2$, at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ we have $\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T=17.37/25.85=0.672$, and $e^{0.672}=1.958$ gives $\bar{n}=1/0.958=1.04$. Its frequency is lower than that of the zone edge, so there are more phonons.

The average energy of the zone-edge mode at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ is $\hbar\omega(\bar{n}+\tfrac12)=24.57\times1.13=27.8\ \mathrm{meV}$, which is slightly larger than the classical equipartition value $k_{\mathrm B}T=25.85\ \mathrm{meV}$ (when $\hbar\omega\approx k_{\mathrm B}T$, the zero-point energy $\tfrac12\hbar\omega=12.3\ \mathrm{meV}$ creates the discrepancy between classical theory and quantum theory). At $10\ \mathrm{K}$ the average energy is almost equal to $\tfrac12\hbar\omega=12.3\ \mathrm{meV}$, which is far larger than $k_{\mathrm B}T=0.86\ \mathrm{meV}$. At low temperatures, an energy much larger than the one given by classical theory ($k_{\mathrm B}T$) remains as zero-point vibration.

60.1.4 Zero-point vibration and thermal vibration — how much do atoms shake?

Using Eq. \eqref{eq:60-u}, we can calculate the mean square $\expval{u_n^2}$ of the displacement of a single atom. Expanding $\hat{u}_n^2$ gives terms such as $\hat{a}_k\hat{a}_{k'}$ and $\hat{a}_k^\dagger\hat{a}_{k'}$ ($k\ne k'$), but in a thermal-equilibrium state or in $\ket{\{n_k\}}$ the expectation value of a product of operators of different modes is $0$ (each mode is occupied independently), so only the pairs belonging to the same mode remain, and we obtain

\begin{equation} \expval{u_n^2}=\sum_k\frac{\hbar}{2NM\omega_k}\expval{\hat{a}_k\hat{a}_k^\dagger+\hat{a}_k^\dagger\hat{a}_k}=\sum_k\frac{\hbar}{2NM\omega_k}\left(2\bar{n}_k+1\right)=\sum_k\frac{\hbar}{2NM\omega_k}\coth\frac{\hbar\omega_k}{2k_{\mathrm B}T} \label{eq:60-u2} \end{equation}

(We used $\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger=\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+1$ and $2\bar{n}+1=\dfrac{e^{x}+1}{e^{x}-1}=\coth\dfrac{x}{2}$, with $x=\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T$.) The part with $\bar{n}_k=0$, which corresponds to the $\tfrac12$ on the right-hand side, is the zero-point vibration, and it expresses the fact that atoms keep shaking even at absolute zero. This is a consequence of the zero-point energy of the harmonic oscillator that we saw in Chapter 13, Section 13.5, together with the uncertainty principle (if an atom is confined to a lattice site, its momentum cannot be $0$). In the Einstein model, in which the frequencies of all modes are replaced by a single value $\omega_{\mathrm E}$ (AD 03, Chapter 51, Section 51.2 (in Japanese)), the sum over $k$ becomes $N$ equal terms, and we obtain

\begin{equation} \expval{u^2}=\frac{\hbar}{2M\omega_{\mathrm E}}\coth\frac{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm E}}{2k_{\mathrm B}T} \label{eq:60-u2E} \end{equation}

As $T\to0$, $\coth\to1$ and we obtain the zero-point value $\hbar/2M\omega_{\mathrm E}$; at high temperatures, $\coth(x/2)\to2/x$ and $\expval{u^2}\to k_{\mathrm B}T/M\omega_{\mathrm E}^2$ (this is classical equipartition: the spring energy is $\tfrac12M\omega_{\mathrm E}^2\expval{u^2}=\tfrac12k_{\mathrm B}T$).

Example 60.3: Magnitudes of the zero-point and thermal vibration of an atom, and the Debye–Waller factor

For an Einstein oscillator with $M=63.55\ \mathrm{u}$ and $\omega_{\mathrm E}/2\pi=4.5\ \mathrm{THz}$ (about the values for Cu), find the root-mean-square displacement in one direction, $u_{\mathrm{rms}}=\sqrt{\expval{u^2}}$, at $0\ \mathrm{K}$ and at $300\ \mathrm{K}$, and compare it with the nearest-neighbor interatomic distance of $0.255\ \mathrm{nm}$. Furthermore, for the diffraction line at the scattering angle $2\theta=90^\circ$ with Cu K$\alpha$ X-rays (wavelength $0.15406\ \mathrm{nm}$), estimate by how much its intensity is reduced by thermal vibration, using $\exp(-\Delta k^2\expval{u^2})$ ($\Delta k=\abs{\bm{k}'-\bm{k}}$ is the difference between the wavevectors of the incident and scattered X-rays, that is, the magnitude of the scattering vector).

Solution. $\omega_{\mathrm E}=2\pi\times4.5\times10^{12}=2.827\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $M=1.0553\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$. The zero-point value is

$$ \frac{\hbar}{2M\omega_{\mathrm E}}=\frac{1.0546\times10^{-34}}{2\times1.0553\times10^{-25}\times2.827\times10^{13}}=1.767\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{m^2} $$

Hence $u_{\mathrm{rms}}(0\ \mathrm{K})=4.20\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m}=4.2\ \mathrm{pm}$. At $300\ \mathrm{K}$, $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm E}/2k_{\mathrm B}T=\dfrac{18.61}{2\times25.85}=0.3600$ and $\coth0.3600=2.897$, so $u_{\mathrm{rms}}=4.204\times\sqrt{2.897}=7.2\ \mathrm{pm}$ (the ratio is $\sqrt{2.897}=1.70$). These correspond to $1.6\ \%$ (zero-point) and $2.8\ \%$ ($300\ \mathrm{K}$) of the interatomic distance of $255\ \mathrm{pm}$. A crystal is said to melt when the amplitude of the thermal vibration reaches about $10\ \%$ of the interatomic distance (Lindemann's empirical law).

The scattering vector of the X-rays is $\Delta k=\dfrac{4\pi\sin\theta}{\lambda}=\dfrac{4\pi\sin45^\circ}{0.15406\ \mathrm{nm}}=57.7\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}=5.77\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$ (5.77 Å−1). $\Delta k^2\expval{u^2}$ is $(5.77\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}})^2\times1.767\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{m^2}=0.0588$ at $0\ \mathrm{K}$; at $300\ \mathrm{K}$, since $\expval{u^2}=(7.155\ \mathrm{pm})^2=5.12\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, we get $(5.77\times10^{10})^2\times5.12\times10^{-23}=0.170$. Therefore the intensity factors are $e^{-0.0588}=0.943$ ($0\ \mathrm{K}$) and $e^{-0.170}=0.843$ ($300\ \mathrm{K}$). The zero-point vibration alone lowers the intensity by about $6\ \%$, and at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ the intensity is lowered by about $16\ \%$.

Application: The Debye–Waller factor in X-ray diffraction and the atomic displacement parameter

In X-ray diffraction by a crystal, if atoms are displaced from their lattice sites from one instant to the next, the phases of the scattered waves become spread out, and the intensity of the Bragg reflection decreases. If the distribution of the displacement $u_\parallel$ along the direction of the scattering vector $\Delta\bm{k}$ is the Gaussian distribution of a harmonic oscillator, the factor of the scattering amplitude is $\expval{e^{i\Delta k\,u_\parallel}}=e^{-\Delta k^2\expval{u_\parallel^2}/2}$, and the intensity, which is its square, is $e^{-\Delta k^2\expval{u_\parallel^2}}$ (the Debye–Waller factor). Since $\expval{u_\parallel^2}$ increases with temperature in the form of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-u2}, reflections at higher scattering angles and at higher temperatures become weaker. In structure analysis, $\expval{u^2}$ (the atomic displacement parameter, ADP) is refined for each atom to determine the magnitude and direction of the shaking of atoms in the crystal. Related simulator: Phonons and Specific Heat (for the derivation from the Einstein model to the Debye model, see Specific Heat Arises from the Uncertainty of Energy).

60.1.5 The number of phonons is not conserved

In the harmonic approximation (in which the spring energy is taken to be a quadratic function of $u$), the modes are independent of one another, and $\hat{H}$ commutes with every $\hat{N}_k$: $\comm{\hat{H}}{\hat{N}_k}=0$. Phonons are neither born nor annihilated, and they pass through one another. However, real springs do not obey Hooke's law exactly. Atoms repel each other strongly when they approach, and the repulsion becomes weaker when they move apart, so the potential has terms of third and higher order in $u$ (anharmonic terms). For example, $\hat{H}_3=\kappa_3\sum_n(\hat{u}_{n+1}-\hat{u}_n)^3$ contains, when Eq. \eqref{eq:60-u} is substituted, terms such as $\hat{a}_{k_1}^\dagger\hat{a}_{k_2}^\dagger\hat{a}_{k_3}$ that annihilate one phonon and create two (or annihilate two and create one). These occur only within the range in which energy and crystal momentum are conserved ($\omega_{k_3}=\omega_{k_1}+\omega_{k_2}$, and $k_3=k_1+k_2$ or $k_1+k_2+2\pi m/a$). That is, in a system with anharmonic terms, the phonon number itself is not conserved. This is the origin of thermal expansion (when the temperature rises, phonons increase, and because of the anharmonic terms the average interatomic distance becomes longer) and of heat conduction (harmonic-approximation phonons do not scatter one another, so the flow of heat does not decay. Even with anharmonic terms, ordinary collisions that conserve crystal momentum alone do not reduce the flow of heat. Thermal resistance is produced by Umklapp processes, in which the crystal momentum changes by a reciprocal lattice vector, and by scattering from impurities, defects, and surfaces).

Precisely because there are terms that do not conserve the number, that is, terms that do not commute with $\hat{N}_k$, the phenomena in which “phonons appear and disappear” occur. In Section 60.5, we study the process in which an electron emits a phonon and the process in which a phonon is created by the scattering of light. In the next section, let us study the case of another representative boson, the photon.

60.2 Quantization of the Electromagnetic Field — Photons and Vacuum Fluctuations

The electromagnetic field, too, has the same structure as the one-dimensional atomic chain. The values of the electric and magnetic fields at each point of space are infinitely many variables. However, if we decompose them into plane-wave modes, each mode becomes a harmonic oscillator independent of the others. A photon is a quantum of such a mode. In this section we start from the results of classical electromagnetism (AD 03, Chapter 42, Section 42.3 (in Japanese), Section 42.5 (in Japanese)).

60.2.1 Mode expansion of the electromagnetic field in a box

Consider a vacuum with neither charges nor currents. If we choose the Coulomb gauge (scalar potential $0$, $\nabla\cdot\bm{A}=0$), which we also used in Chapter 52, the electric field and the magnetic flux density are determined from the vector potential $\bm{A}(\bm{r},t)$ by

\begin{equation} \bm{E}=-\frac{\partial\bm{A}}{\partial t},\qquad\bm{B}=\nabla\times\bm{A} \label{eq:60-EBA} \end{equation}

and $\bm{A}$ satisfies the wave equation $\nabla^2\bm{A}-c^{-2}\partial_t^2\bm{A}=0$. Divide space into a box of side $L$ and volume $V=L^3$ and impose periodic boundary conditions (as for lattice vibrations). The wavevector then takes the discrete values $\bm{k}=\dfrac{2\pi}{L}(n_x,n_y,n_z)$ ($n_x,n_y,n_z$ are integers). For each $\bm{k}$, the angular frequency is $\omega_{\bm{k}}=c\abs{\bm{k}}$. For a plane wave $e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}$, $\nabla\cdot\bm{A}=0$ means $\bm{k}\cdot\bm{e}=0$. In other words, an electromagnetic wave is a transverse wave, and the direction of $\bm{A}$ (the polarization vector $\bm{e}$) is one of the two directions perpendicular to $\bm{k}$. Choose two real unit vectors $\bm{e}_{\bm{k}1},\bm{e}_{\bm{k}2}$ perpendicular to $\bm{k}$ (and orthogonal to each other), and let $\lambda=1,2$ number the polarizations. The general solution can be written as a superposition of the modes $(\bm{k},\lambda)$:

\begin{equation} \bm{A}(\bm{r},t)=\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}\left[A_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,e^{i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\omega_{\bm{k}}t)}+\mathrm{c.c.}\right] \label{eq:60-Aexp} \end{equation}

(c.c. stands for the complex conjugate; it is added so that $\bm{A}$ is real). Here $A_{\bm{k}\lambda}$ is a complex amplitude, and the sum runs over all $\bm{k}$ (counting $\bm{k}$ and $-\bm{k}$ separately) and all $\lambda$. From Eq. \eqref{eq:60-EBA}, the contribution of each term is

\begin{equation} \bm{E}=\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}\left[i\omega_{\bm{k}}A_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,e^{i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\omega_{\bm{k}}t)}+\mathrm{c.c.}\right],\qquad \bm{B}=\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}\left[i\,A_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,(\bm{k}\times\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda})\,e^{i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\omega_{\bm{k}}t)}+\mathrm{c.c.}\right] \label{eq:60-EBexp} \end{equation}

(we used $\nabla\to i\bm{k}$, $-\partial_t\to i\omega$). Using this expansion, let us calculate the energy of the electromagnetic field, $H=\dfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\displaystyle\int\left(\bm{E}^2+c^2\bm{B}^2\right)\dd^3r$ (AD 03, Section 42.5 (in Japanese)).

Derivation: The energy of the electromagnetic field becomes a sum over modes

Substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:60-EBexp} into $\bm{E}^2$ and $\bm{B}^2$ produces products of two modes. The spatial integral is $\displaystyle\int_V e^{i(\bm{k}\pm\bm{k}')\cdot\bm{r}}\dd^3r=V\delta_{\bm{k},\mp\bm{k}'}$, and only two kinds of pairs give a result other than $0$.

(i) Products with the complex conjugate ($\bm{k}'=\bm{k}$). From $\bm{E}^2$ we obtain $2\times\omega^2\abs{A}^2\,V$ (the product of $A\,e^{i\cdots}$ and its c.c. can be formed in two ways; the polarization vectors satisfy $\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}\cdot\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda'}=\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}$), and from $c^2\bm{B}^2$ we obtain $2\times c^2\abs{\bm{k}\times\bm{e}}^2\abs{A}^2\,V=2\omega^2\abs{A}^2V$ (since $\bm{k}\perp\bm{e}$, $\abs{\bm{k}\times\bm{e}}=k$, and $ck=\omega$).

(ii) Products between the modes at $\bm{k}$ and at $-\bm{k}$ (the terms proportional to $e^{-i(\omega+\omega)t}$). From $\bm{E}^2$ we obtain $-\omega^2A_{\bm{k}\lambda}A_{-\bm{k}\lambda'}\,\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}\cdot\bm{e}_{-\bm{k}\lambda'}$, and from $c^2\bm{B}^2$ we obtain $c^2\,\bigl[i(\bm{k}\times\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda})\bigr]\cdot\bigl[i(-\bm{k})\times\bm{e}_{-\bm{k}\lambda'}\bigr]A_{\bm{k}\lambda}A_{-\bm{k}\lambda'}=+\omega^2A_{\bm{k}\lambda}A_{-\bm{k}\lambda'}\,\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}\cdot\bm{e}_{-\bm{k}\lambda'}$ (we used $(\bm{k}\times\bm{e})\cdot(\bm{k}\times\bm{e}')=k^2\,\bm{e}\cdot\bm{e}'$), so the sum is $0$.

Therefore only (i) remains, and $H=\dfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}(2+2)\,\omega_{\bm{k}}^2\abs{A_{\bm{k}\lambda}}^2V$, that is,

\begin{equation} H=\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}2\varepsilon_0V\omega_{\bm{k}}^2\,\abs{A_{\bm{k}\lambda}}^2 \label{eq:60-Hcl} \end{equation}

is obtained. In each mode, the electric-field energy and the magnetic-field energy each account for exactly half.

∎

Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Hcl} is a sum over the modes $(\bm{k},\lambda)$, and the magnitude $\abs{A_{\bm{k}\lambda}}$ of $A_{\bm{k}\lambda}(t)=A_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,e^{-i\omega t}$ is independent of time. To read this as a harmonic oscillator, it suffices to regard the real and imaginary parts of the complex amplitude $A_{\bm{k}\lambda}$ as the coordinate and the momentum. We want to bring the energy $2\varepsilon_0V\omega^2\abs{A}^2$ into the harmonic-oscillator form $\tfrac12(p^2+\omega^2q^2)$, so we take the coefficient to be $2\sqrt{\varepsilon_0V}$:

\begin{equation} q_{\bm{k}\lambda}=2\sqrt{\varepsilon_0V}\,\Real A_{\bm{k}\lambda},\qquad p_{\bm{k}\lambda}=2\sqrt{\varepsilon_0V}\,\omega_{\bm{k}}\,\Imag A_{\bm{k}\lambda} \label{eq:60-qp} \end{equation}

With this choice, the time dependence of $A_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,e^{-i\omega t}$ gives $\dot{q}=p$ (the time derivative of the real part $\Real(Ae^{-i\omega t})$ is $\omega\Imag(Ae^{-i\omega t})$), and the energy becomes $H_{\bm{k}\lambda}=2\varepsilon_0V\omega^2\abs{A}^2=\dfrac12\left(p^2+\omega^2q^2\right)$. This is the energy of a harmonic oscillator of mass $1$ and angular frequency $\omega_{\bm{k}}$ (the dimension of $q$ is $\sqrt{\mathrm{kg}}\,\mathrm{m}$). Thus the electromagnetic field has become a collection of independent harmonic oscillators labeled by $(\bm{k},\lambda)$. The infinitely many variables $\bm{E},\bm{B}$ at every point of space have been replaced by two variables $(q,p)$ for the oscillator of each mode.

60.2.2 Quantization — creation and annihilation operators for photons

All that remains is to apply the quantization of Chapter 56 to each mode. Each mode can be thought of as a bucket that holds quanta (photons) of energy $\hbar\omega$ (Figure 60.3). Take $q_{\bm{k}\lambda}$ and $p_{\bm{k}\lambda}$ to be operators, set $\comm{\hat{q}}{\hat{p}}=i\hbar$, and define $\hat{a}=\dfrac{\omega\hat{q}+i\hat{p}}{\sqrt{2\hbar\omega}}$ (for an oscillator of mass $1$, this is $\hat{a}=\sqrt{m\omega/2\hbar}\,(\hat{x}+i\hat{p}/m\omega)$). Conversely, since $\omega q+ip=2\sqrt{\varepsilon_0V}\,\omega A$, we have $A_{\bm{k}\lambda}=\dfrac{\omega q+ip}{2\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon_0V}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{\hbar}{2\varepsilon_0V\omega}}\,\hat{a}$. That is, we replace the amplitude $A_{\bm{k}\lambda}$ by $\sqrt{\hbar/2\varepsilon_0V\omega_{\bm{k}}}\;\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}$ and the c.c. by $\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}^\dagger$. Let us state in advance the magnitude of the vacuum electric field, which we will find later: for a mode of wavelength $500\ \mathrm{nm}$ in a box of volume $1\ \mathrm{\mu m^3}$, it is about $1.5\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{V/m}$, surprisingly large (Example 60.4).

Theorem 60.2: Quantization of the electromagnetic field

The vector potential, the electric field, and the magnetic flux density of the electromagnetic field in a box can be written in terms of the photon creation and annihilation operators $\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}^\dagger$, $\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}$ ($\comm{\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}}{\hat{a}_{\bm{k}'\lambda'}^\dagger}=\delta_{\bm{k}\bm{k}'}\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}$) as follows.

\begin{align} \hat{\bm{A}}(\bm{r},t)&=\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2\varepsilon_0V\omega_{\bm{k}}}}\left[\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,e^{i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\omega_{\bm{k}}t)}+\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}^\dagger\,e^{-i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\omega_{\bm{k}}t)}\right] \label{eq:60-Aop}\\ \hat{\bm{E}}(\bm{r},t)&=\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_{\bm{k}}}{2\varepsilon_0V}}\left[\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,e^{i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\omega_{\bm{k}}t)}-\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}^\dagger\,e^{-i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\omega_{\bm{k}}t)}\right] \label{eq:60-Eop} \end{align}

(For $\hat{\bm{B}}$, replace $\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}$ by $\bm{k}\times\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}$ and $\sqrt{\hbar\omega/2\varepsilon_0V}$ by $\sqrt{\hbar/2\varepsilon_0V\omega}$.) For the Hamiltonian,

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}\hbar\omega_{\bm{k}}\left(\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}^\dagger\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}+\tfrac12\right),\qquad\omega_{\bm{k}}=c\abs{\bm{k}} \label{eq:60-Hphoton} \end{equation}

holds.

The electric field of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Eop} is obtained by differentiating Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Aop} with $-\partial_t$ (the $i\omega$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-EBexp} becomes $\sqrt{\hbar/2\varepsilon_0V\omega}\times i\omega=i\sqrt{\hbar\omega/2\varepsilon_0V}$). The time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$ is $\hat{a}(t)=\hat{a}\,e^{-i\omega t}$ in the Heisenberg picture (Chapter 50, Section 50.3), the same as $\hat{x}(t)$ in Chapter 56. Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Hphoton} is obtained by carrying out the same steps as in the classical calculation (the Derivation) while keeping the order of the operators: this gives $\hat{H}=\sum\dfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}(\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger)$, and then $\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger=\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+1$ yields the result. The cross terms between $\bm{k}$ and $-\bm{k}$ vanish for the same reason as in the classical case.

Definition 60.3: Photon

The quantum of the mode $(\bm{k},\lambda)$ of the electromagnetic field is called a photon. The operator $\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}^\dagger$ creates one photon with energy $\hbar\omega_{\bm{k}}=\hbar c\abs{\bm{k}}$, momentum $\hbar\bm{k}$, and polarization $\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}$. The total momentum of the electromagnetic field is $\varepsilon_0\displaystyle\int\hat{\bm{E}}\times\hat{\bm{B}}\,\dd^3r=\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}\hbar\bm{k}\,\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}^\dagger\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}$ (the zero-point contributions cancel between $\bm{k}$ and $-\bm{k}$), and the photon has appeared, in the language of second quantization, as a particle with the energy–momentum relations $E=\hbar\omega$, $\bm{p}=\hbar\bm{k}$ ($E=cp$) that we saw in Chapters 4 and 6. It has mass $0$, it is a boson (any number of photons can occupy the same mode), and the number of photons is not conserved.

(The calculation of $\hat{\bm{E}}\times\hat{\bm{B}}$ proceeds as in the case of the energy: only the products of modes with the same $\bm{k}$ survive. In the classical case, substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:60-EBexp} into $\bm{E}\times\bm{B}$ gives $2\varepsilon_0V\omega\abs{A}^2\,\bm{k}$ (using $\bm{e}\times(\bm{k}\times\bm{e})=\bm{k}$). This can be written as $\hbar\bm{k}\,\abs{a}^2$, regarding $\abs{a}^2=2\varepsilon_0V\omega\abs{A}^2/\hbar$ as the quantity corresponding to the number of photons.)

Discussion: Polarization, the spin of the photon, and the Coulomb interaction

The two basis states of polarization may be either the linear polarizations $\bm{e}_{\bm{k}1},\bm{e}_{\bm{k}2}$ or the circular polarizations $\bm{e}_{\pm}=(\bm{e}_{\bm{k}1}\pm i\bm{e}_{\bm{k}2})/\sqrt2$. A circularly polarized photon carries angular momentum $\pm\hbar$ along the direction of propagation ($m=\pm1$ of spin 1). The reason that no state with component $m=0$ along the direction of propagation exists is that the electromagnetic wave is transverse; this corresponds to the fact that a particle of mass $0$ has two polarizations (helicities) (treated in AD 09). Also, in the Coulomb gauge, the static Coulomb interaction between charges, $v(q)=e_0^2/(\varepsilon_0q^2)$ (Chapter 58), is included in $\hat{V}$, and the photon represents only the transverse electromagnetic field. The coupling between an electron and photons is obtained simply by replacing $\bm{A}$ in $\hat{H}_1=(e_0/m_{\mathrm e})\bm{A}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}$ of Chapter 52 by the operator $\hat{\bm{A}}$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Aop} (Sections 60.4 and 60.5).

When many modes are treated together, the sum over $\bm{k}$ is replaced by an integral. Since the values of $\bm{k}$ are arranged one per cube of side $2\pi/L$, we have $\sum_{\bm{k}}\to V\displaystyle\int\dfrac{\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3}$. The number of modes whose angular frequency lies in the range $\omega\sim\omega+\dd\omega$, including the two polarizations, is $2\times V\dfrac{4\pi k^2\dd k}{(2\pi)^3}$ ($k=\omega/c$) $=\dfrac{V\omega^2}{\pi^2c^3}\dd\omega$ (the same as the counting of the modes in a cavity in AD 03 53.2 (in Japanese); for example, for $V=1\ \mathrm{\mu m^3}$, there are only about $0.4$ modes near a wavelength of $500\ \mathrm{nm}$, within a wavelength range of width $1\ \mathrm{nm}$).

60.2.3 Vacuum fluctuations — the electric field fluctuates even when there is not a single photon

Let us examine the electric field of the vacuum $\ket{0}$ (the state in which the number of photons in every mode is $0$: $\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}\ket{0}=0$). For simplicity, we focus on a single mode $(\bm{k},\lambda)$, fix a position (phase $\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\omega t=\vartheta$), and write the single-mode term of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Eop} as

\begin{equation} \hat{E}=iE_{\mathrm{vac}}\left(\hat{a}\,e^{i\vartheta}-\hat{a}^\dagger e^{-i\vartheta}\right),\qquad E_{\mathrm{vac}}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega}{2\varepsilon_0V}} \label{eq:60-Evac} \end{equation}

(the component along the polarization direction). In the vacuum, $\hat{a}\ket{0}=0$ and $\bra{0}\hat{a}^\dagger=0$ give $\expval{\hat{E}}=\mel{0}{\hat{E}}{0}=0$. The expectation value of the square, however, is

\begin{equation} \expval{\hat{E}^2}=-E_{\mathrm{vac}}^2\mel{0}{\left(\hat{a}\,e^{i\vartheta}-\hat{a}^\dagger e^{-i\vartheta}\right)^2}{0}=+E_{\mathrm{vac}}^2\mel{0}{\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger}{0}=E_{\mathrm{vac}}^2=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2\varepsilon_0V} \label{eq:60-Evac2} \end{equation}

(the square of the bracket is $\hat{a}^2e^{2i\vartheta}-\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger-\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+(\hat{a}^\dagger)^2e^{-2i\vartheta}$; in the vacuum the expectation values of $\hat{a}^2$, $(\hat{a}^\dagger)^2$, and $\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}$ are $0$, and $\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger=\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+1$ gives $\mel{0}{\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger}{0}=1$. The sign of $(i)^2=-1$ and the sign of $-\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger$ cancel each other, leaving $+E_{\mathrm{vac}}^2$). That is, even in the vacuum the electric field has a fluctuation with mean $0$ and mean square $E_{\mathrm{vac}}^2$. This is the manifestation, in the electromagnetic field, of the fact that in the ground state of a harmonic oscillator $\expval{x}=0$ but $\expval{x^2}=\hbar/2m\omega\ne0$ (Chapter 13, Chapter 56).

Check: Relation to the zero-point energy

The zero-point energy of one mode is $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$. Of this, the electric-field energy is $\dfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}V\expval{\hat{E}^2}=\dfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}V\cdot\dfrac{\hbar\omega}{2\varepsilon_0V}=\dfrac{\hbar\omega}{4}$, which is exactly half (the other half is the magnetic-field energy). This corresponds to the potential energy of a classical oscillator, $\tfrac12m\omega^2\expval{x^2}=\hbar\omega/4$. Therefore, the value $E_{\mathrm{vac}}=\sqrt{\hbar\omega/2\varepsilon_0V}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Evac} is fixed by the fact that half of the zero-point energy appears as electric-field energy.

When there are $n$ photons, $\mel{n}{\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger+\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}}{n}=2n+1$, so $\expval{\hat{E}^2}=E_{\mathrm{vac}}^2(2n+1)$. In thermal equilibrium, with $n\to\bar{n}$, this becomes $E_{\mathrm{vac}}^2(2\bar{n}+1)$, and for a coherent state of amplitude $\alpha$, $\expval{\hat{E}}=-2E_{\mathrm{vac}}\Imag(\alpha e^{i\vartheta})$; that is, it becomes a classical wave of amplitude $E_0=2E_{\mathrm{vac}}\abs{\alpha}$. From the latter, the number of photons in a monochromatic wave of classical amplitude $E_0$ is $\abs{\alpha}^2=E_0^2/4E_{\mathrm{vac}}^2=\varepsilon_0VE_0^2/2\hbar\omega$, and the energy $\hbar\omega\abs{\alpha}^2=\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E_0^2V$ agrees with the average energy density $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E_0^2$ of classical electromagnetism multiplied by the volume $V$.

Example 60.4: Magnitude of the vacuum electric field

For a cavity of volume $V=1\ \mathrm{\mu m^3}$, find the vacuum electric field $E_{\mathrm{vac}}=\sqrt{\hbar\omega/2\varepsilon_0V}$ of the mode of wavelength $500\ \mathrm{nm}$. Also find the energy density and the photon number density of a classical plane wave of amplitude $1\ \mathrm{V/m}$, and compare them.

Solution. The photon energy is $\hbar\omega=hc/\lambda=1239.84\ \mathrm{eV\,nm}/500\ \mathrm{nm}=2.480\ \mathrm{eV}=3.973\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$, and $V=(10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m})^3=10^{-18}\ \mathrm{m^3}$, so

$$ E_{\mathrm{vac}}=\sqrt{\frac{3.973\times10^{-19}}{2\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times10^{-18}}}=\sqrt{2.243\times10^{10}}=1.50\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{V/m} $$

(the units are $\sqrt{\mathrm{J}/(\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}\,m^3})}=\sqrt{\mathrm{J/(F\,m^2)}}=\mathrm{V/m}$, since $\mathrm{J/F}=\mathrm{V^2}$). Next, the (time-averaged) energy density of a plane wave of amplitude $E_0=1\ \mathrm{V/m}$ is $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E_0^2=\tfrac12\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times1^2=4.43\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{J/m^3}$, and the photon number density is $4.43\times10^{-12}/3.973\times10^{-19}=1.1\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$. A volume of $1\ \mathrm{\mu m^3}$ contains only $1.1\times10^{-11}$ photons. That is, the electric field that one mode of one small cavity feels in the vacuum ($1.5\times10^5\ \mathrm{V/m}$) is much larger than the electric field of ordinary weak light ($1\ \mathrm{V/m}$). However, since $E_{\mathrm{vac}}\propto V^{-1/2}$, the larger the volume, the smaller the vacuum electric field per mode.

Caution: The vacuum electric field is the value for “$1$ mode”

Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Evac} is the fluctuation of the vacuum electric field produced by one mode. The fluctuation of the vacuum electric field at a single point in space is the contribution of all the modes, $\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}E_{\mathrm{vac},\bm{k}}^2$, which becomes arbitrarily large when modes up to high frequencies are added. This is the same difficulty as the divergence of the zero-point energy $\sum\tfrac12\hbar\omega$; in actual measurements, the sum is cut off naturally by, for example, the bandwidth of the light involved in the measurement. When a single mode is selected inside a resonator and made to interact with an atom (Section 60.4), $E_{\mathrm{vac}}$ appears directly.

60.2.4 Advanced Topic: Zero-point energy produces a force — the Casimir effect

The zero-point energy $\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}\tfrac12\hbar\omega_{\bm{k}}$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Hphoton} diverges as it stands, because there are infinitely many modes. If we add up modes of higher and higher frequency, it grows by as many orders of magnitude as we like. For example, even if we add up only the modes with wavelengths longer than $500\ \mathrm{nm}$ (wavenumbers smaller than $k_{\mathrm c}=2\pi/500\ \mathrm{nm}$), the energy density of the vacuum, from the counting of states ($V\omega^2\dd\omega/\pi^2c^3$ in Section 60.2.2), is as large as $\displaystyle\int_0^{k_{\mathrm c}}\frac{\hbar ck}{2}\cdot\frac{k^2}{\pi^2}\dd k=\frac{\hbar c\,k_{\mathrm c}^4}{8\pi^2}\approx10\ \mathrm{J/m^3}$ ($0.01\ \mathrm{J}$ per $1\ \mathrm{L}$). In our everyday life, however, only the difference from a reference (the vacuum) is meaningful for energy, and a uniform zero-point energy shows no effect.

If, however, the zero-point energy is different from place to place, the difference becomes a force. Place two flat plates of an ideal conductor in vacuum, parallel to each other, at separation $\ell$. Since the parallel component of the electric field is $0$ at the surface of a conductor, the modes of the electromagnetic field between the plates are restricted to those for which the component of the wavevector perpendicular to the plates has the values $k_z=n\pi/\ell$ ($n=0,1,2,\dots$; however, for $n=0$ there is only one polarization). The vacuum outside has no such restriction. The sum of the zero-point energy differs between the region between the plates and the region outside, and this difference (the divergent parts cancel each other, and a finite value remains) pulls the plates together. We quote the result that Casimir derived in 1948, omitting the derivation:

Formula 60.1: Casimir effect (parallel plates of an ideal conductor, quoted)

The energy per unit area between parallel plates of an ideal conductor with separation $\ell$ is $-\dfrac{\pi^2\hbar c}{720\,\ell^3}$, and the magnitude of the force per unit area (the pressure) is

\begin{equation} P=\frac{\pi^2\hbar c}{240\,\ell^4} \label{eq:60-Casimir} \end{equation}

The plates attract each other.

In the derivation of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Casimir}, the divergent sum must be regularized while taking the difference between the region between the plates and the region outside (a finite value is determined regardless of the method of regularization). Here we leave the details to textbooks on quantum field theory. The force is proportional to $\hbar c$ and vanishes as $\hbar\to0$ in classical theory, so it is a purely quantum force. In 1997, Lamoreaux measured this force, as the force between a sphere (a lens with a large radius of curvature) and a flat plate, over the range of separations $0.6$–$6\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, to an accuracy of about $5\ \%$ relative to theory (in the sphere–plate configuration, the expression for the magnitude of the force has a different coefficient from the parallel-plate expression, Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Casimir}, and the measured values are compared with theoretical values converted to the sphere–plate configuration).

Find the Casimir pressure between parallel plates of an ideal conductor with separation $\ell=100\ \mathrm{nm}$. What about $\ell=10\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $\ell=1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$?

Solution. $\hbar c=1.0546\times10^{-34}\times2.998\times10^{8}=3.162\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{J\,m}$. For $\ell=100\ \mathrm{nm}=10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m}$, $\ell^4=10^{-28}\ \mathrm{m^4}$, so

$$ P=\frac{\pi^2\times3.162\times10^{-26}}{240\times10^{-28}}=\frac{3.121\times10^{-25}}{2.4\times10^{-26}}=13.0\ \mathrm{Pa} $$

(the units are $\mathrm{J\,m/m^4}=\mathrm{J/m^3}=\mathrm{Pa}$). This is $1.3\times10^{-4}$ of atmospheric pressure, $1.0\times10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$, and is small. But since $P\propto\ell^{-4}$, for $\ell=10\ \mathrm{nm}$ it is $13.0\times10^4\ \mathrm{Pa}=1.3\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{Pa}$ (about $1.3$ atmospheres), and for $\ell=1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ it is $1.3\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Pa}$. When the plates come within a few tens of nm, the attractive force becomes comparable to atmospheric pressure.

Application: “Sticking” in MEMS and NEMS

In tiny mechanical parts (MEMS, NEMS), the gap between a movable part and a fixed electrode becomes several $100\ \mathrm{nm}$ down to several $10\ \mathrm{nm}$, and the Casimir force and the van der Waals force, which likewise originates in the quantum fluctuations of the vacuum, attract the parts and cause them to stick together and not come apart (stiction). Real metals are not ideal conductors, and corrections for finite conductivity, surface roughness, and temperature are necessary, so Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Casimir} is a rough guide.

60.3 Statistics of Photons and Phonons — Thermal Equilibrium, Coherent, and Fock

Both phonons and photons are quanta of a harmonic oscillator for each mode, so in a crystal or a cavity in thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$, each mode contains some number of quanta on average. In this section we derive the average number $\bar{n}$ per mode, rebuild Planck's radiation formula as “the number in one mode $\times$ the number of modes,” and then compare the three faces that the state of light can have even for the same average number: thermal, coherent, and Fock.

60.3.1 Number distribution and variance in thermal equilibrium — the Bose distribution with chemical potential $0$

In thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$, the probability that a mode (angular frequency $\omega$) contains $n$ quanta is proportional to the Boltzmann factor $e^{-E_n/k_{\mathrm B}T}$ ($E_n=(n+\tfrac12)\hbar\omega$; the zero-point energy is a common factor). As found in Chapter 56, Section 56.7, with $x=e^{-\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}$ ($0\lt x\lt1$),

\begin{equation} P(n)=\frac{x^n}{Z}=(1-x)\,x^n,\qquad Z=\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n=\frac{1}{1-x} \label{eq:60-thermalP} \end{equation}

holds (a geometric distribution). The average number and the variance can be found together by the method of generating them from $Z$. We have $\bar{n}=\sum_nnP(n)=\dfrac{1}{Z}x\dfrac{\dd Z}{\dd x}$, and since $Z=1/(1-x)$ gives $\dfrac{\dd Z}{\dd x}=\dfrac{1}{(1-x)^2}$,

\begin{equation} \bar{n}=\frac{x}{1-x}=\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}-1} \label{eq:60-nbar-th} \end{equation}

is the average number. For the variance, let $D=x\,\dd/\dd x$; then $\bar{n}=Z^{-1}DZ$ and $\expval{n^2}=Z^{-1}D^2Z$, so $\expval{n^2}-\bar{n}^2=Z^{-1}D^2Z-\left(Z^{-1}DZ\right)^2=D\left(Z^{-1}DZ\right)=D\bar{n}=x\dfrac{\dd\bar{n}}{\dd x}$ (we used $D(Z^{-1}DZ)=Z^{-1}D^2Z-Z^{-2}(DZ)^2$). Differentiating $\bar{n}=x/(1-x)$ gives $x\dfrac{\dd\bar{n}}{\dd x}=x\cdot\dfrac{1}{(1-x)^2}=\dfrac{x}{1-x}\cdot\dfrac{1}{1-x}$, and since $\dfrac{1}{1-x}=\bar{n}+1$,

\begin{equation} \expval{n^2}-\bar{n}^2=\bar{n}(\bar{n}+1)=\bar{n}+\bar{n}^2 \label{eq:60-var-th} \end{equation}

is the variance. When $\bar{n}$ is large, the variance is approximately the square of the average (the standard deviation is about the size of the average). The $\bar{n}$ term is the fluctuation when the quanta are present as independent particles (a Poisson distribution; the coherent state, which appears later), and the $\bar{n}^2$ term is the wavelike fluctuation arising from the interference of many waves. In 1909 Einstein showed that the energy fluctuation of blackbody radiation has these two terms, and argued that light has the properties of both a particle and a wave.

Why is the chemical potential $0$?

Eq. \eqref{eq:60-nbar-th} is the Bose–Einstein distribution $1/[e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/k_{\mathrm B}T}-1]$ with the chemical potential set to $\mu=0$. The particles of an ideal gas (electrons, atoms) have a fixed total number $N$, and $\mu$ adjusts the number, but the numbers of photons and phonons are not fixed. The walls emit and absorb photons, and a crystal emits and absorbs phonons thermally, so in thermal equilibrium the free energy with respect to the number is at its minimum: $\mu=\partial F/\partial N=0$ (AD 03, Chapter 53, Section 53.1 (in Japanese), Section 53.10 (in Japanese)). The fact that the particle number can change shows up in statistical mechanics as $\mu=0$.

60.3.2 Rebuilding Planck's formula

The energy density of thermal radiation filling a cavity is obtained by multiplying “the average energy per mode of angular frequency $\omega$, $\hbar\omega\,\bar{n}(\omega)$” by the number of modes per unit volume and per unit angular frequency, $\dfrac{\omega^2}{\pi^2c^3}$ (including the two polarizations; Section 60.2.2):

\begin{equation} u(\omega)=\hbar\omega\cdot\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}-1}\cdot\frac{\omega^2}{\pi^2c^3}=\frac{\hbar\,\omega^3}{\pi^2c^3}\,\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}-1} \label{eq:60-planck} \end{equation}

This is Planck's radiation formula. The average energy $\hbar\omega/(e^{\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}-1)$ that Planck derived in 1900 by assuming that the energy of the oscillators in the cavity walls is an integer multiple of $\hbar\omega$ (AD 02, Chapter 49, Section 49.4; this book, Chapter 3, Section 3.4) can now be read as “the average number $\bar{n}$ of photons in the mode times the energy $\hbar\omega$ of one photon.” The counting of the number of modes is the same as that done in AD 02, Section 49.5 and AD 03, Section 53.2 (in Japanese). The zero-point energy term diverges when summed and is independent of temperature, so it does not appear in the spectrum of thermal radiation.

The two limits of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-planck} correspond to the two limits of $\bar{n}$ (Figure 60.4(b)).

  • $\hbar\omega\ll k_{\mathrm B}T$: $\bar{n}\approx\dfrac{k_{\mathrm B}T}{\hbar\omega}-\tfrac12$, and the energy per mode is $\hbar\omega\bar{n}\approx k_{\mathrm B}T$ (classical equipartition). $u(\omega)\approx\dfrac{\omega^2k_{\mathrm B}T}{\pi^2c^3}$ (Rayleigh–Jeans). This is the regime in which many photons are in the mode, so it can be treated as a classical wave.
  • $\hbar\omega\gg k_{\mathrm B}T$: $\bar{n}\approx e^{-\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}\ll1$, and $u(\omega)\approx\dfrac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2c^3}e^{-\hbar\omega/k_{\mathrm B}T}$ (Wien). This is the particle-like regime, in which the question is whether or not the mode contains one photon.

The total energy density is $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty u(\omega)\dd\omega=\dfrac{\pi^2k_{\mathrm B}^4T^4}{15\hbar^3c^3}=\dfrac{4\sigma_{\mathrm{SB}}}{c}T^4$ (the Stefan–Boltzmann law; AD 03, Section 53.5 (in Japanese)), which at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ is $6.1\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{J/m^3}$, and the number density of photons is $\dfrac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}\left(\dfrac{k_{\mathrm B}T}{\hbar c}\right)^3=5.5\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$ (for the cosmic microwave background at $2.725\ \mathrm{K}$ it is $4.1\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, that is, about $410$ per $1\ \mathrm{cm^3}$).

This decomposition is also connected with Einstein's argument in Chapter 52. The radiation density $u_1(\omega)=\hbar\omega^3/\pi^2c^3$ obtained by setting $\bar{n}=1$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-planck} is the ratio of the Einstein coefficients, $A_{21}/B_{21}$ (Chapter 52, Section 52.5.2). That is, $A_{21}=B_{21}u_1(\omega)$: spontaneous emission occurs at the same rate as stimulated emission when each mode contains one photon. We will make clear the quantum-mechanical meaning of this “$1$” in Section 60.4.

On the other hand, the situation $\bar{n}\gg1$, in which a single mode contains many photons, occurs at low frequencies (radio waves and microwaves, where $\hbar\omega\ll k_{\mathrm B}T$) or when light is concentrated in a single mode, as in a laser. In thermal radiation of visible light, light behaves like particles (photons are counted one by one); in radio waves, it behaves like waves (a classical field).

Application: The cosmic microwave background and thermal radiation cameras

The cosmic microwave background (CMB) at $2.725\ \mathrm{K}$ is thermal radiation from the era when the universe was hot, cooled by the expansion; the peak of its spectrum is near wavelengths of $1$–$2\ \mathrm{mm}$ (around $160\ \mathrm{GHz}$). In this region $\bar{n}$ is $0.005$–$0.08$, and the particle picture is meaningful. In thermography and infrared sensors that detect the infrared radiation of around $10\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ emitted by an object at room temperature ($\bar{n}\approx8\times10^{-3}$), the fluctuation in the number of photons received by the detector (photon noise) determines the limit of sensitivity.

60.3.3 The three faces of the photon-number distribution — thermal, coherent, Fock

Even for the same average number of photons, the distribution of the number of photons ($P(n)$, the probability of finding $n$ photons) differs greatly depending on the kind of state of the light. Let us compare three representative states of one mode (Figure 60.4(a)).

  • Thermal state: light in thermal equilibrium (one mode of sunlight, of a light bulb, or of a fluorescent lamp). $P(n)=\dfrac{\bar{n}^n}{(1+\bar{n})^{n+1}}$ (a geometric distribution, Eq. \eqref{eq:60-thermalP}). The probability is highest at $n=0$ and decreases monotonically. The variance is $\bar{n}+\bar{n}^2$.
  • Coherent state $\ket{\alpha}$: ideal laser light (Chapter 56, Section 56.6). $P(n)=e^{-\abs{\alpha}^2}\dfrac{\abs{\alpha}^{2n}}{n!}$ (a Poisson distribution, $\bar{n}=\abs{\alpha}^2$). Variance $=\bar{n}$.
  • Fock (photon-number) state $\ket{n_0}$: a state with exactly $n_0$ photons (for example, $n_0=1$ for a single-photon source). $P(n)=\delta_{n,n_0}$. Variance $=0$.

The difference among the three states shows up not only in the variance but also in the probability that two photons are found at the same time, $g^{(2)}(0)=\dfrac{\expval{n(n-1)}}{\bar{n}^2}$. For the thermal state, $\expval{n(n-1)}=2\bar{n}^2$ and $g^{(2)}(0)=2$; for the coherent state, $\expval{n(n-1)}=\abs{\alpha}^4$ and the value is $1$ (common to Poisson distributions); for the Fock state $\ket{n_0}$, it is $n_0(n_0-1)/n_0^2=1-1/n_0$.

Example 60.7: Variances of three states with an average of two photons

For (1) a thermal state, (2) a coherent state, and (3) a Fock state $\ket{2}$ of a mode whose average photon number is $\bar{n}=2$, find $P(0)$, $P(2)$, the variance, and $g^{(2)}(0)$.

60.4 Interaction of Light and Matter — A Two-Level Atom and a Single Photon Mode

In Chapter 52, we treated light as a classical electromagnetic wave and calculated the rates at which an atom absorbs light and undergoes stimulated emission, and we determined the rate of spontaneous emission from Einstein's thermal-equilibrium argument. Now that we have quantized light, the exchange of energy between an atom and photons can be written with a single Hamiltonian. First, let us solve exactly the simplest case—two levels of the atom and one mode of the electromagnetic field.

60.4.1 The electric dipole interaction and the Jaynes–Cummings model

In the electric dipole approximation of Chapter 52, the interaction between an atom and the electromagnetic field is the coupling $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=-\hat{\bm{d}}\cdot\hat{\bm{E}}$ between the electric dipole moment operator $\hat{\bm{d}}$ of the atom ($\hat{\bm{d}}=-e_0\hat{\bm{r}}$ for an electron) and the electric field $\hat{\bm{E}}$ at the position of the atom (since the wavelength of light is much longer than the size of the atom, $e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\approx1$). Now, let $\hat{\bm{E}}$ be the quantized field operator of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Eop}.

Suppose the atom has only two levels: the ground state $\ket{\mathrm{g}}$ (energy $-\tfrac12\hbar\omega_0$) and the excited state $\ket{\mathrm{e}}$ ($+\tfrac12\hbar\omega_0$). The angular frequency of the transition is $\omega_0$. We define the operators corresponding to $\sigma_z$ and $\sigma_\pm$ by

\begin{equation} \hat{\sigma}_z=\ket{\mathrm{e}}\bra{\mathrm{e}}-\ket{\mathrm{g}}\bra{\mathrm{g}},\qquad\hat{\sigma}_+=\ket{\mathrm{e}}\bra{\mathrm{g}},\qquad\hat{\sigma}_-=\ket{\mathrm{g}}\bra{\mathrm{e}} \label{eq:60-sigma} \end{equation}

(the same algebra as the Pauli matrices; $\hat{\sigma}_+$ excites the atom and $\hat{\sigma}_-$ de-excites it). The energy of the atom alone is $\hat{H}_{\mathrm A}=\tfrac12\hbar\omega_0\hat{\sigma}_z$. The eigenstates of the atom have definite parity, and the dipole operator $\hat{\bm{d}}$ has odd parity, so the diagonal elements vanish, $\mel{\mathrm{e}}{\hat{\bm{d}}}{\mathrm{e}}=\mel{\mathrm{g}}{\hat{\bm{d}}}{\mathrm{g}}=0$, and only $\mel{\mathrm{e}}{\hat{\bm{d}}}{\mathrm{g}}$ remains (the selection rule of Chapter 52, Section 52.4). Let $d=\mel{\mathrm{e}}{\bm{e}\cdot\hat{\bm{d}}}{\mathrm{g}}$ be the component along the polarization direction of the light, and choose the phase of $\ket{\mathrm{e}}$ so that $d$ is real. Then $\bm{e}\cdot\hat{\bm{d}}=d\,(\hat{\sigma}_++\hat{\sigma}_-)$.

We consider only one mode of the electromagnetic field (angular frequency $\omega$, photon operator $\hat{a}$); a high-$Q$ resonator confines just this mode. Choosing the phase again (by multiplying $\hat{a}$ by a phase factor), we write the electric field at the position of the atom as $\hat{E}=E_{\mathrm{vac}}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)$ with $E_{\mathrm{vac}}=\sqrt{\hbar\omega/2\varepsilon_0V}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Evac}). (In a standing-wave resonator, the magnitude of the electric field at the position of the atom is determined by the value of the mode function. Here we simply use the traveling-wave magnitude $E_{\mathrm{vac}}$ as it is, which gives a rough estimate.) Then

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=-d\,E_{\mathrm{vac}}\left(\hat{\sigma}_++\hat{\sigma}_-\right)\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger\right)=-\hbar g\left(\hat{\sigma}_++\hat{\sigma}_-\right)\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger\right),\qquad g=\frac{d\,E_{\mathrm{vac}}}{\hbar} \label{eq:60-Hint} \end{equation}

holds. The quantity $g$ (which has the dimension of angular frequency) is the strength of the coupling between the atom and the vacuum electric field (Dimension check: $d\,E_{\mathrm{vac}}$ is $\mathrm{C\,m}\times\mathrm{V/m}=\mathrm{C\,V}=\mathrm{J}$, so $g=dE_{\mathrm{vac}}/\hbar$ is $\mathrm{J/(J\,s)}=\mathrm{s^{-1}}$). Expanding the product gives four terms. $\hat{\sigma}_+\hat{a}$ (the atom is excited by absorbing one photon) and $\hat{\sigma}_-\hat{a}^\dagger$ (the atom de-excites and emits one photon) are “resonant” terms, consistent with energy conservation. The remaining terms, $\hat{\sigma}_+\hat{a}^\dagger$ (the atom is excited and a photon is also added) and $\hat{\sigma}_-\hat{a}$, are terms that miss energy conservation by as much as about $\hbar(\omega_0+\omega)$. In the interaction picture (Chapter 50), $\hat{\sigma}_+(t)=\hat{\sigma}_+e^{i\omega_0t}$ and $\hat{a}(t)=\hat{a}\,e^{-i\omega t}$, so $\hat{\sigma}_+\hat{a}$ becomes $e^{i(\omega_0-\omega)t}$ (slowly varying near resonance) and $\hat{\sigma}_+\hat{a}^\dagger$ becomes $e^{i(\omega_0+\omega)t}$ (oscillating rapidly), and the rapid terms average out to zero. We therefore drop them; this is the rotating-wave approximation. For $g\ll\omega$ and near resonance ($\abs{\omega_0-\omega}\ll\omega$), it is an excellent approximation (in the numerical values of Section 60.4.3, $g/\omega\sim10^{-6}$).

Definition 60.4: The Jaynes–Cummings model

For a system consisting of a two-level atom and a single photon mode, the Hamiltonian after the rotating-wave approximation (the zero-point energy $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$ is omitted as a constant)

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_{\mathrm{JC}}=\tfrac12\hbar\omega_0\hat{\sigma}_z+\hbar\omega\,\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}-\hbar g\left(\hat{\sigma}_+\hat{a}+\hat{\sigma}_-\hat{a}^\dagger\right) \label{eq:60-JC} \end{equation}

is called the Jaynes–Cummings model (Jaynes and Cummings, 1963).

The terms $\hat{\sigma}_+\hat{a}$ and $\hat{\sigma}_-\hat{a}^\dagger$ do not change the “total number of excitations” $\hat{N}_{\mathrm{ex}}=\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+\hat{\sigma}_+\hat{\sigma}_-$, the sum of the number of photons and the number of excitations of the atom (one of them increases by one and the other decreases by one). Indeed, from $\comm{\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}}{\hat{\sigma}_+\hat{a}}=-\hat{\sigma}_+\hat{a}$ and $\comm{\hat{\sigma}_+\hat{\sigma}_-}{\hat{\sigma}_+\hat{a}}=+\hat{\sigma}_+\hat{a}$ we have $\comm{\hat{N}_{\mathrm{ex}}}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{JC}}}=0$. Therefore, $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{JC}}$ splits into small subspaces, one for each value of $\hat{N}_{\mathrm{ex}}$. The subspace with $\hat{N}_{\mathrm{ex}}=n+1$ is spanned by the two states $\{\ket{\mathrm{e},n},\ket{\mathrm{g},n+1}\}$ (the atom is excited and there are $n$ photons, or the atom is in the ground state and there are $n+1$ photons). The subspace $\hat{N}_{\mathrm{ex}}=0$ contains only $\ket{\mathrm{g},0}$, which is unaffected by the coupling.

60.4.2 The $2\times2$ problem — the matrix element $\hbar g\sqrt{n+1}$

Let us find the matrix of $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{JC}}$ in $\{\ket{\mathrm{e},n},\ket{\mathrm{g},n+1}\}$. The diagonal elements are $\mel{\mathrm{e},n}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{JC}}}{\mathrm{e},n}=\tfrac12\hbar\omega_0+n\hbar\omega$ and $\mel{\mathrm{g},n+1}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{JC}}}{\mathrm{g},n+1}=-\tfrac12\hbar\omega_0+(n+1)\hbar\omega$. From $\hat{a}\ket{n+1}=\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n}$ (Theorem 56.2 of Chapter 56), the off-diagonal element is

\begin{equation} \mel{\mathrm{e},n}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{JC}}}{\mathrm{g},n+1}=-\hbar g\,\mel{\mathrm{e}}{\hat{\sigma}_+}{\mathrm{g}}\,\mel{n}{\hat{a}}{n+1}=-\hbar g\sqrt{n+1} \label{eq:60-JCel} \end{equation}

(the $\hat{\sigma}_-\hat{a}^\dagger$ term does not give a matrix element from $\ket{\mathrm{e},n}$ to $\ket{\mathrm{g},n+1}$). Using the detuning $\Delta=\omega_0-\omega$, the matrix becomes the following.

\begin{equation} H_n=\left(n+\tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}+\hbar\begin{pmatrix}\Delta/2&-g\sqrt{n+1}\\-g\sqrt{n+1}&-\Delta/2\end{pmatrix} \label{eq:60-Hn} \end{equation}

Since this is the eigenvalue problem of a symmetric matrix of order two, the eigenvalues are $\left(n+\tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega\pm\dfrac{\hbar}{2}\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2(n+1)}$ (the square root of the sum of the squares of the difference of the diagonal elements, $\hbar\Delta$, and of the off-diagonal element, $-\hbar g\sqrt{n+1}$). At resonance ($\Delta=0$), only $-\hbar g\sqrt{n+1}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ remains in the matrix, and the eigenvalues and eigenvectors are as follows.

Theorem 60.3: Eigenstates of the resonant Jaynes–Cummings model

At resonance ($\omega_0=\omega$), the eigenstates and eigenenergies in the subspace in which the total number of excitations is $n+1$ (taking the energy $-\tfrac12\hbar\omega$ of $\ket{\mathrm{g},0}$ as the reference) are

\begin{equation} \ket{\pm,n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\ket{\mathrm{e},n}\mp\ket{\mathrm{g},n+1}\right),\qquad E_{\pm,n}=(n+1)\hbar\omega\pm\hbar g\sqrt{n+1} \label{eq:60-dressed} \end{equation}

and they are split by $2\hbar g\sqrt{n+1}$ (Figure 60.5). The energy of $\ket{\mathrm{g},0}$ stays at $0$ and does not move.

Without the coupling, $\ket{\mathrm{e},n}$ and $\ket{\mathrm{g},n+1}$ were degenerate at the same energy (at resonance, the energy when the atom is excited and there are $n$ photons equals the energy when the atom is in the ground state and there is one more photon). When there is an off-diagonal element between two degenerate states, the degeneracy is lifted, and we obtain combinations of the sum and the difference (“states in which the atom and the photon are mixed”; these are the dressed states). This has the same structure as the fact that two identical pendulums joined by a spring split into an in-phase vibration and an out-of-phase vibration, or as the bonding and antibonding orbitals of the hydrogen molecular ion (Chapter 39). The characteristic feature is that the magnitude of the splitting, $2\hbar g\sqrt{n+1}$, grows with the square root of the number of photons.

The splitting $2\hbar g$ for $n=0$ occurs even in a vacuum that contains no photons at all. An atom in the excited state couples to the electric field of the vacuum and splits into the two dressed states $\ket{\pm,0}$. This is the vacuum Rabi splitting; when it is observed spectroscopically, it appears as a spectrum split into two lines (it can be seen when $g$ is larger than the loss of the resonator; it was observed in cavity QED experiments in the 1990s and in the exciton–photon coupling in semiconductor microcavities).

60.4.3 Rabi oscillations, and collapse and revival due to the photon-number distribution

On resonance, the atom starts from the state $\ket{\mathrm{e},n}$, in which the atom is excited and there are $n$ photons. Since $\ket{\mathrm{e},n}=(\ket{+,n}+\ket{-,n})/\sqrt2$, the time evolution is obtained by multiplying the two eigenstates by the phase factors $e^{-iE_{\pm,n}t/\hbar}$, which gives

\begin{equation} \ket{\psi(t)}=e^{-i(n+\frac12)\omega t}\left[\cos\left(g\sqrt{n+1}\,t\right)\ket{\mathrm{e},n}+i\sin\left(g\sqrt{n+1}\,t\right)\ket{\mathrm{g},n+1}\right] \label{eq:60-Rabi-state} \end{equation}

(adding and subtracting the phase factors $e^{\mp ig\sqrt{n+1}t}$ of $\ket{+,n}$ and $\ket{-,n}$ gives the $\cos$ and the $i\sin$). The probability that the atom is in the excited state is

\begin{equation} P_{\mathrm e}(t)=\cos^2\left(g\sqrt{n+1}\,t\right)=\tfrac12\left[1+\cos\left(2g\sqrt{n+1}\,t\right)\right] \label{eq:60-Pe} \end{equation}

so the atom goes back and forth between the excited state and the ground state at the Rabi angular frequency $\Omega_n=2g\sqrt{n+1}$. The atom emits a photon and goes to the ground state ($\ket{\mathrm{g},n+1}$), then absorbs it again and returns to the excited state. This is called the quantum Rabi oscillation (the quantum version of the Rabi oscillation of Chapter 51, Section 51.3). For $n=0$, $P_{\mathrm e}(t)=\cos^2(gt)$ is the vacuum Rabi oscillation: in a cavity that contains no photons at all, an excited atom emits one photon (spontaneous emission) and then absorbs it again, repeating this over and over. Its period is $\pi/g$ (Figure 60.6(a)).

Intuition: Relation to the classical Rabi oscillation

In Chapter 51 we saw that when classical light of amplitude $E_0$ strikes an atom, the excitation probability oscillates at the Rabi angular frequency $\Omega=dE_0/\hbar$. When a quantized field contains $n$ photons, the corresponding classical amplitude is $E_0=2E_{\mathrm{vac}}\sqrt{n}$ (Section 60.2.3), so $dE_0/\hbar=2gn^{1/2}$, which agrees with $\Omega_n=2g\sqrt{n+1}$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Pe} for $n\gg1$. What is new in quantum theory is that the angular frequency involves $\sqrt{n+1}$ rather than $\sqrt{n}$—that is, that oscillation occurs even for $n=0$ (the vacuum)—and that “$+1$” is the spontaneous emission of the next section.

Example 60.8: Coupling strength in cavity QED

Light of wavelength $780\ \mathrm{nm}$ is confined, in just one mode, in a resonator of volume $V=1\ \mathrm{\mu m^3}$. Take the transition dipole of the atom to be $d=e_0a_0$, a rough measure of the size of the atom. Find (1) the vacuum electric field $E_{\mathrm{vac}}$, (2) the coupling strength $g$ and $g/2\pi$, (3) the vacuum Rabi splitting $2g/2\pi$, and (4) the period of $P_{\mathrm e}(t)$ when the system starts from $\ket{\mathrm{e},0}$.

Solution. (1) Since $\hbar\omega=1239.84/780=1.5895\ \mathrm{eV}=2.547\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$,

$$ E_{\mathrm{vac}}=\sqrt{\frac{2.547\times10^{-19}}{2\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times10^{-18}}}=\sqrt{1.438\times10^{10}}=1.20\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{V/m} $$

(this is smaller than the value $1.50\times10^5$ of Example 60.4 for a wavelength of $500\ \mathrm{nm}$ by an amount corresponding to the smaller photon energy). (2) $d=e_0a_0=1.602\times10^{-19}\times5.292\times10^{-11}=8.478\times10^{-30}\ \mathrm{C\,m}$.

$$ g=\frac{dE_{\mathrm{vac}}}{\hbar}=\frac{8.478\times10^{-30}\times1.199\times10^{5}}{1.0546\times10^{-34}}=9.64\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad\frac{g}{2\pi}=1.53\ \mathrm{GHz} $$

The angular frequency of the light is $\omega=2\pi c/\lambda=2.415\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so $g/\omega=4.0\times10^{-6}$, and the rotating-wave approximation is entirely satisfactory. (3) The vacuum Rabi splitting is $2g/2\pi=3.07\ \mathrm{GHz}$ (in energy, $2\hbar g=12.7\ \mu\mathrm{eV}$). (4) The period of $P_{\mathrm e}(t)=\cos^2(gt)$ is $\pi/g=3.26\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{s}=326\ \mathrm{ps}$. When the lifetime of the photon (the loss of the resonator) and the lifetime of spontaneous emission of the atom are longer than this—that is, when the $Q$ value of the resonator greatly exceeds $\omega/g\approx2.5\times10^5$—this back-and-forth can be seen many times. This is called strong coupling.

Application: Cavity QED and quantum information

Studies that confine one atom and one photon in a resonator with a high $Q$ value and directly observe the Rabi oscillation discussed above have been carried out with Rydberg atoms and microwave resonators (the group of Haroche and coworkers), with neutral atoms in optical cavities (the groups of Kimble, Rempe, and others), and so on. Haroche and Wineland received the 2012 Nobel Prize in Physics for methods of measuring and controlling individual quantum systems without destroying them. The same physics is also used with semiconductor quantum dots and microcavities and with superconducting circuits (artificial atoms and resonators), and it is applied to single-photon sources and to the readout and coupling of qubits.

When the light is initially in a coherent state—collapse and revival.

When the initial state is $\ket{\mathrm{e}}\otimes\ket{\alpha}$ (the atom is in the excited state and the light is in the coherent state $\ket{\alpha}$; the photon-number distribution is a Poisson distribution with mean $\bar{n}=\abs{\alpha}^2$), each component with $n$ photons, $P(n)=e^{-\bar{n}}\bar{n}^n/n!$, oscillates independently at its own Rabi angular frequency $\Omega_n=2g\sqrt{n+1}$. The observed excitation probability is their superposition,

\begin{equation} P_{\mathrm e}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty P(n)\cos^2\left(g\sqrt{n+1}\,t\right)=\frac12\left[1+\sum_{n=0}^\infty P(n)\cos\left(2g\sqrt{n+1}\,t\right)\right] \label{eq:60-Pe-coh} \end{equation}

At time $0$, the components for all $n$ are aligned at phase $0$, so $P_{\mathrm e}=1$. When $\bar{n}$ is large, $P(n)$ is concentrated at $n\approx\bar{n}\pm\sqrt{\bar{n}}$, so the Rabi angular frequency is spread around $2g\sqrt{\bar{n}}$ with a width of about $2g\times\dfrac{\sqrt{\bar{n}}}{2\sqrt{\bar{n}}}=g$ (the variation of $\sqrt{n+1}$ with $n$, $\dd\sqrt{n}/\dd n=1/2\sqrt{n}$, multiplied by the width $\sqrt{\bar{n}}$). Consequently, within a time $t_{\mathrm{c}}\sim1/g$ the phases of the components become scrambled, the sum cancels out, and the oscillation disappears and settles at $P_{\mathrm e}\approx\tfrac12$ (collapse).

But this is not the end. Since $\sqrt{n+1}$ changes with $n$ only discretely (the number of photons is an integer), the difference between the Rabi angular frequencies of adjacent $n$ is $\Omega_{n+1}-\Omega_n\approx g/\sqrt{\bar{n}}$, and they are almost equally spaced. At the time $t$ when the phase difference $(\Omega_{n+1}-\Omega_n)t$ between adjacent components reaches $2\pi$, the phases of all the components are aligned again. This time is

\begin{equation} t_{\mathrm{rev}}\approx\frac{2\pi}{g/\sqrt{\bar{n}}}=\frac{2\pi\sqrt{\bar{n}}}{g} \label{eq:60-trev} \end{equation}

and the oscillation revives there (revival). This is an approximation for $\bar{n}\gg1$ (the difference between the Rabi angular frequencies of adjacent $n$ was evaluated near $n=\bar{n}$). If we include the $+1$ of $\sqrt{n+1}$, the center of the peak comes near $t_{\mathrm{rev}}\approx2\pi\sqrt{\bar{n}+1}/g$. For $\bar{n}=25$, Eq. \eqref{eq:60-trev} gives $31.4/g$, whereas the center of the first peak obtained by numerically evaluating the sum in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Pe-coh} is $32.0/g$, a difference of about $2\ \%$. The revived peak then becomes gradually lower and broader, and it appears again at twice and three times that time (Figure 60.6(b)). Collapse and revival are a direct consequence of the discreteness of the number of photons (the quantization of the electromagnetic field). The collapse was shown by Cummings in 1965, and the revival was predicted by Eberly, Narozhny, and Sanchez-Mondragon in 1980. Both were observed by Rempe, Walther, and Klein in 1987 in a one-atom maser using a Rydberg atom and a microwave resonator. In the semiclassical theory of Chapter 51, in which light is a classical field, the Rabi oscillation never decays, and neither collapse nor revival appears.

Example 60.9: Collapse and revival times

For the resonator of Example 60.8 ($g=9.64\times10^9\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$), when the atom is in the excited state and the light is in a coherent state with mean $\bar{n}=25$, find the time scale $1/g$ required for the collapse and the time $t_{\mathrm{rev}}$ of the first revival. Also, what is the period, in ps, of the fastest component ($n=\bar{n}$) of the Rabi oscillation?

Solution. $1/g=1.04\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{s}=104\ \mathrm{ps}$. From Eq. \eqref{eq:60-trev}, $t_{\mathrm{rev}}=2\pi\sqrt{25}/g=2\pi\times5/(9.64\times10^9\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})=31.4/(9.64\times10^9)=3.26\ \mathrm{ns}$. Including the $+1$ of $\sqrt{n+1}$, $2\pi\sqrt{26}/g=32.0/(9.64\times10^9)=3.32\ \mathrm{ns}$ is close to the center of the peak found by numerical evaluation of the sum (about $3.3\ \mathrm{ns}$). The Rabi angular frequency is $\Omega_{25}=2g\sqrt{26}=2\times9.64\times10^9\times5.10=9.83\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and the period is $2\pi/\Omega=64\ \mathrm{ps}$. That is, the oscillation disappears within the first $100\ \mathrm{ps}$ or so (during a few Rabi oscillations) and revives at about $3.3\ \mathrm{ns}$ (the FWHM of the revived peak is about $7.4/g=0.77\ \mathrm{ns}$, and it contains somewhat more than 10 Rabi oscillations). For collapse and revival to be observable, the photons must remain in the resonator and the atom must remain in the excited state for a time of about $3\ \mathrm{ns}$.

Since it has the same coefficient as the absorption rate ($\propto n$), Einstein's relation $B_{12}=B_{21}$ (when there is no degeneracy) emerges as the symmetry of the matrix elements $\sqrt{n}$ and $\sqrt{n+1}$. The emitted photon goes into the same mode as the photons already there (same direction, same polarization, same phase). This is a property of bosons, $\hat{a}^\dagger\ket{n}=\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1}$ (the more particles already in a state, the more readily another enters it; the Bose enhancement of Chapter 57), and it is the principle of the laser.
  • Spontaneous emission (the $1$, independent of $n$): emission that occurs even in the vacuum with no photons at all ($n=0$). In classical electromagnetism, an atom in a place where the electromagnetic field is $0$ feels nothing and stays in the excited state. But the quantized electromagnetic field has fluctuations of magnitude $E_{\mathrm{vac}}$ even in the vacuum (Section 60.2.3), and these act on the excited atom and make it emit light. Spontaneous emission is stimulated emission by vacuum fluctuations.

In 1917 Einstein introduced the coefficient $A$ of spontaneous emission by an argument based on thermal equilibrium (Chapter 52, Section 52.5), but he could not determine why spontaneous emission occurs or what its rate is. In 1927 Dirac quantized the electromagnetic field, coupled it to the atom as a collection of harmonic oscillators, and derived the rate of spontaneous emission from first principles. This was the first success of quantized field theory.

60.4.5 Derivation of the spontaneous emission rate $A$ — from the quantized electromagnetic field

Let us find, by Fermi's golden rule (Chapter 51, Section 51.2), the rate at which an atom in the excited state $\ket{\mathrm{e}}$ in vacuum emits one photon and goes to the ground state $\ket{\mathrm{g}}$. This time we consider not a single mode but all the modes $(\bm{k},\lambda)$ of free space. The initial state is $\ket{\mathrm{e}}\otimes\ket{0}$ (no photons) and the final state is $\ket{\mathrm{g}}\otimes\ket{1_{\bm{k}\lambda}}$ (one photon in the mode $(\bm{k},\lambda)$); the final states are distributed continuously.

Derivation: Spontaneous emission rate $A=\dfrac{\omega_0^3\abs{\bm{d}}^2}{3\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c^3}$

(1) Matrix element. In $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=-\hat{\bm{d}}\cdot\hat{\bm{E}}$ we substitute Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Eop} for $\hat{\bm{E}}$, and only the $\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\lambda}^\dagger$ term of $\hat{\bm{E}}$ creates a photon. Since the magnitude of the phase factor is 1,

$$ \abs{\mel{\mathrm{g},1_{\bm{k}\lambda}}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}}{\mathrm{e},0}}^2=\abs{\bm{d}\cdot\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}}^2\,\frac{\hbar\omega_{\bm{k}}}{2\varepsilon_0V} $$

(here we let $\bm{d}=\mel{\mathrm{g}}{\hat{\bm{d}}}{\mathrm{e}}$ and took it to be a real vector; $\mel{1}{\hat{a}^\dagger}{0}=1$).

(2) The golden rule. $A=\dfrac{2\pi}{\hbar}\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}\abs{\mel{\mathrm{g},1_{\bm{k}\lambda}}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}}{\mathrm{e},0}}^2\,\delta\left(\hbar\omega_{\bm{k}}-\hbar\omega_0\right)$. We replace the sum over final states $\sum_{\bm{k}}$ by the integral $V\displaystyle\int\dfrac{k^2\dd k\,\dd\Omega}{(2\pi)^3}$ ($\dd\Omega$ is the solid angle of the direction of $\bm{k}$). The volume $V$ cancels the $1/V$ of the matrix element.

(3) Polarization sum and angular integration. For the two polarizations perpendicular to $\bm{k}$, $\sum_{\lambda=1,2}\abs{\bm{d}\cdot\bm{e}_{\bm{k}\lambda}}^2=\abs{\bm{d}}^2-\abs{\bm{d}\cdot\hat{\bm{k}}}^2=\abs{\bm{d}}^2\sin^2\theta$ ($\theta$ is the angle between $\bm{d}$ and $\bm{k}$; expand $\bm{d}$ in the orthogonal basis formed by $\hat{\bm{k}}$ and the two polarization vectors, and compare the norms). The integral over direction is $\displaystyle\int\sin^2\theta\,\dd\Omega=2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,\dd\theta=2\pi\cdot\dfrac43=\dfrac{8\pi}{3}$.

(4) Frequency integral. We have $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty k^2\,\delta\left(\hbar ck-\hbar\omega_0\right)\dd k=\dfrac{1}{\hbar c}\left(\dfrac{\omega_0}{c}\right)^2=\dfrac{\omega_0^2}{\hbar c^3}$. Putting the above together,

$$ A=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\cdot\frac{\hbar\omega_0\abs{\bm{d}}^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}\cdot\frac{8\pi}{3}\cdot\frac{\omega_0^2}{\hbar c^3}=\frac{\pi\omega_0\abs{\bm{d}}^2}{\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{1}{3\pi^2}\cdot\frac{\omega_0^2}{\hbar c^3}=\frac{\omega_0^3\abs{\bm{d}}^2}{3\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c^3} $$

(here $\dfrac{2\pi}{\hbar}\cdot\dfrac{\hbar\omega_0}{2\varepsilon_0}=\dfrac{\pi\omega_0}{\varepsilon_0}$ and $\dfrac{8\pi/3}{(2\pi)^3}=\dfrac{1}{3\pi^2}$).

∎

Formula 60.2: Spontaneous emission rate (from the quantized electromagnetic field)

\begin{equation} A=\frac{\omega_0^3\,\abs{\bm{d}}^2}{3\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c^3},\qquad\bm{d}=\mel{\mathrm{g}}{\hat{\bm{d}}}{\mathrm{e}} \label{eq:60-A} \end{equation}

The lifetime of the excited state is $\tau=1/A$.

Let us check the dimensions. $\omega_0^3\abs{\bm{d}}^2$ is $\mathrm{s^{-3}}\cdot\mathrm{C^2m^2}$, and the denominator $\varepsilon_0\hbar c^3$ is $\mathrm{C^2J^{-1}m^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{J\,s}\cdot\mathrm{m^3s^{-3}}=\mathrm{C^2m^2s^{-2}}$ (the unit of $\varepsilon_0$ is $\mathrm{F/m}=\mathrm{C^2J^{-1}m^{-1}}$), so the ratio is $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, which is indeed the dimension of a rate.

Eq. \eqref{eq:60-A} agrees exactly with $A_{21}=\omega^3\abs{\bm{d}_{12}}^2/3\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c^3$ of Chapter 52 (Formula 52.4 of Section 52.5.3). In Chapter 52, Einstein's thermal-equilibrium argument gave $A_{21}=(\hbar\omega^3/\pi^2c^3)B_{21}$, and we obtained the spontaneous emission rate by multiplying by $B_{21}=\pi\abs{\bm{d}_{12}}^2/3\varepsilon_0\hbar^2$, computed with the golden rule. This time we obtained the same result from the zero-point fluctuations of the electromagnetic field alone, without the thermal-equilibrium argument. Moreover, the Intuition in Chapter 52, Section 52.5.3, “spontaneous emission occurs at the same rate as stimulated emission when there is one photon in each mode,” corresponds to the fact that, of the emission rate $n+1$, the stimulated part $n$ and the spontaneous part $1$ are equal at $n=1$ ($A=B_{21}u_1(\omega)$).

Discussion: In vacuum the decay is irreversible, and inside a resonator the system goes back and forth

In free space the final states (the photon modes) are distributed continuously, and a photon emitted by an excited atom escapes far away and does not return. For a transition into this continuous spectrum, the golden rule gives an exponential decay $e^{-At}$ (irreversible decay) (Weisskopf and Wigner, 1930; their method is shown in the next box). On the other hand, when the atom is coupled to only a single mode inside a resonator, the photon returns and the excitation probability oscillates (reversible), as in Section 60.4.3. Thus, even for the same interaction, whether the result is decay or oscillation is decided by whether the final states are continuous or discrete. The fact that the spontaneous emission rate of an atom inside a resonator differs from that without a resonator (the density of modes changes), known as the Purcell effect, is used in the design of light-emitting materials and semiconductor lasers.

Derivation: The Weisskopf–Wigner method — obtaining exponential decay and a Lorentzian line from the amplitudes of the atom and the photon

The golden rule gives only the “rate” of a transition. Let us follow the state of the atom + photon system directly and show that the exponential decay $e^{-At/2}$ and the shape of the spectrum come out (the method announced in Chapter 52, Section 52.6). We expand the state as

$$ \ket{\psi(t)}=c_{\mathrm{e}}(t)\,e^{-i\omega_0t}\ket{\mathrm{e},0}+\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}c_{\bm{k}\lambda}(t)\,e^{-i\omega_{\bm{k}}t}\ket{\mathrm{g},1_{\bm{k}\lambda}} $$

(an atom in the excited state with no photon + an atom in the ground state with one photon). Writing the magnitude of the coupling as $g_{\bm{k}\lambda}=\mel{\mathrm{g},1_{\bm{k}\lambda}}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}}{\mathrm{e},0}/\hbar$ (taken to be real), the Schrödinger equation gives

$$ \dot{c}_{\bm{k}\lambda}=-i\,g_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,e^{i(\omega_{\bm{k}}-\omega_0)t}\,c_{\mathrm{e}},\qquad \dot{c}_{\mathrm{e}}=-i\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}g_{\bm{k}\lambda}\,e^{-i(\omega_{\bm{k}}-\omega_0)t}\,c_{\bm{k}\lambda} $$

(we ignore processes in which two or more photons are present, and the coupling between the ground state and the vacuum). Let $c_{\mathrm{e}}=1$ and $c_{\bm{k}\lambda}=0$ at $t=0$; integrating the first equation and substituting the result into the second, we obtain

$$ \dot{c}_{\mathrm{e}}(t)=-\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}g_{\bm{k}\lambda}^2\int_0^t e^{-i(\omega_{\bm{k}}-\omega_0)(t-t')}\,c_{\mathrm{e}}(t')\dd t' . $$

Here, because the modes of the final states are distributed continuously, the sum extends over a wide range of $\omega_{\bm{k}}$, and when $t-t'$ is longer than about the period of the light, the individual terms cancel one another, so that only the neighborhood of $t'\approx t$ contributes to the integral. We therefore put $c_{\mathrm{e}}(t')\approx c_{\mathrm{e}}(t)$ (the Weisskopf–Wigner approximation; it holds when the lifetime is much longer than the period of the light). From the real part of $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-i(\omega_{\bm{k}}-\omega_0)\tau}\dd\tau=\pi\delta(\omega_{\bm{k}}-\omega_0)-i\,\mathrm{P}\dfrac{1}{\omega_{\bm{k}}-\omega_0}$ (P is the principal value), we get

$$ \dot{c}_{\mathrm{e}}=-\frac{A}{2}\,c_{\mathrm{e}},\qquad A=2\pi\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}g_{\bm{k}\lambda}^2\,\delta(\omega_{\bm{k}}-\omega_0)=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\sum_{\bm{k}\lambda}\abs{\mel{\mathrm{g},1_{\bm{k}\lambda}}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}}{\mathrm{e},0}}^2\delta(\hbar\omega_{\bm{k}}-\hbar\omega_0) $$

The $A$ on the right-hand side is the same as the $A$ in the golden rule above. Hence $c_{\mathrm{e}}(t)=e^{-At/2}$, and the excitation probability is $\abs{c_{\mathrm{e}}}^2=e^{-At}$ (the principal-value part gives only a slight shift of the level (the Lamb shift), which we include in $\omega_0$).

Putting $c_{\mathrm{e}}(t)=e^{-At/2}$ into the first equation and letting $t\to\infty$, we find that the amplitude of the emitted photon is

$$ c_{\bm{k}\lambda}(\infty)=-i\,g_{\bm{k}\lambda}\int_0^\infty e^{\,[\,i(\omega_{\bm{k}}-\omega_0)-A/2\,]t'}\dd t'=\frac{-i\,g_{\bm{k}\lambda}}{A/2-i(\omega_{\bm{k}}-\omega_0)},\qquad \abs{c_{\bm{k}\lambda}(\infty)}^2=\frac{g_{\bm{k}\lambda}^2}{(\omega_{\bm{k}}-\omega_0)^2+A^2/4} $$

The spectrum of the photon as a function of its angular frequency $\omega$ is a Lorentzian line shape centered at $\omega_0$ with full width (full width at half maximum) $A$ (the natural linewidth is $\Delta\omega=A$; Chapter 52, Section 52.6). The exponential decay and the Lorentzian line are expressions of the same quantity $A$ in the language of time and of angular frequency, respectively.

60.5 Processes That Change the Particle Number — Absorption, Emission, Phonon Emission, and Raman Scattering

Let us organize the phenomena in which the numbers of photons and phonons change, in the language of the operators we have learned so far. If we multiply together, in a single term, the electron creation and annihilation operators $\hat{c}^\dagger$, $\hat{c}$ (Chapters 57 and 58), the photon operators $\hat{a}^\dagger$, $\hat{a}$, and the phonon operators $\hat{b}^\dagger$, $\hat{b}$, then “how the electron moves, and by how many the photons and phonons increase or decrease” is written directly in the form of the term.

60.5.1 Absorption and emission of light in semiconductors — creation and annihilation of electron–hole pairs

In Chapter 52, we expressed the coupling between electrons and light by $\hat{H}_1=(e_0/m_{\mathrm e})\bm{A}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}$, which came from minimal coupling. Now we replace $\bm{A}$ by the quantized field $\hat{\bm{A}}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Aop}). Since $\bm{A}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}$ is a one-body operator of the electron, we rewrite it in the form $\sum\mel{i}{t}{j}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$ of Chapter 58. We describe the electron states of a semiconductor by the Bloch states $\ket{\mathrm{v},\bm{k}}$ and $\ket{\mathrm{c},\bm{k}}$ of the valence band ($\mathrm{v}$) and the conduction band ($\mathrm{c}$) (Chapter 59, Section 59.4), and pick out only the transitions from the valence band to the conduction band, which gives

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma}\sum_{\bm{q}\lambda}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{q}\lambda}\;\hat{a}_{\bm{q}\lambda}\,\hat{c}_{\mathrm{c},\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\mathrm{v},\bm{k},\sigma}+\mathcal{M}_{\bm{q}\lambda}^*\;\hat{a}_{\bm{q}\lambda}^\dagger\,\hat{c}_{\mathrm{v},\bm{k},\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\mathrm{c},\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}\right) \label{eq:60-Hsemi} \end{equation}

Using the velocity-gauge expression of Chapter 52, the coupling constant is $\mathcal{M}_{\bm{q}\lambda}=\dfrac{e_0}{m_{\mathrm e}}\sqrt{\dfrac{\hbar}{2\varepsilon_0V\omega_{\bm{q}}}}\,\mel{\mathrm{c}}{\bm{e}_{\bm{q}\lambda}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}}{\mathrm{v}}$ (the matrix element on the right is the interband momentum matrix element), and $\sigma$ is the spin. Terms whose energies do not match (such as $\hat{a}\,\hat{c}_{\mathrm v}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\mathrm c}$) have been dropped in the rotating-wave approximation.

  • Absorption $\hat{a}\,\hat{c}_{\mathrm c}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\mathrm v}$: one photon is annihilated, one electron of the valence band is annihilated (leaving one hole in the valence band), and one electron is created in the conduction band. That is, photon $-1$, electron–hole pair $+1$ (Figure 60.7(a)). In Chapter 59 we learned that the annihilation of a valence-band electron, $\hat{c}_{\mathrm v,\bm{k}}$, can be read as the creation of a hole, $\hat{h}_{-\bm{k},-\sigma}^\dagger$ ($\hat{h}$ is the hole operator, not the Planck constant). With this reading, absorption is $\hat{a}\,\hat{c}_{\mathrm c,\bm{k}+\bm{q}}^\dagger\hat{h}_{-\bm{k},-\sigma}^\dagger$, that is, a term that creates one electron and one hole at the same time.
  • Light emission $\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\mathrm v}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\mathrm c}$: an electron in the conduction band falls into an empty seat (a hole) in the valence band, the electron–hole pair is annihilated, and one photon is born. Photon $+1$, electron–hole pair $-1$ (Figure 60.7(b)). The process by which a light-emitting diode (LED) glows is the spontaneous-emission part of this term (the $1$ in $n+1$ of Section 60.4.4). In a mode that already contains many photons ($n\gg1$), the stimulated-emission part ($n$) dominates, and this gives the semiconductor laser (Chapter 53).

In Fermi's golden rule (Chapter 51), the transition rate is determined by the squared matrix element and the energy-conserving $\delta$ function $\delta\left(E_{\mathrm c}(\bm{k})-E_{\mathrm v}(\bm{k})-\hbar\omega\right)$. The wavenumber of light, $\abs{\bm{q}}=2\pi/\lambda\sim10^7\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, is far smaller than the size of the Brillouin zone ($\sim10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$), so the transition occurs “vertically” from the valence band to the conduction band at almost the same $\bm{k}$ ($\bm{q}\approx0$; conservation of crystal momentum). Therefore the energy of absorption and emission is about the band gap $E_{\mathrm g}$: $\hbar\omega\approx E_{\mathrm g}$ (the direct transition of Section 53.4 in Chapter 53).

Example 60.11: Emission wavelength of an LED and the number of electrons per photon

(1) Find the emission wavelengths of GaN ($E_{\mathrm g}=3.4\ \mathrm{eV}$), blue InGaN (emission energy $2.76\ \mathrm{eV}$), and GaAs ($1.42\ \mathrm{eV}$). (2) When a blue LED of wavelength $449\ \mathrm{nm}$ gives an optical output of $1\ \mathrm{mW}$, find the number of photons emitted per second and the current that flows when the internal quantum efficiency is $100\ \%$.

Solution. (1) The energy of an emitted photon is the energy released when an electron–hole pair recombines, $\approx E_{\mathrm g}$. From $\lambda=hc/\hbar\omega=1239.84\ \mathrm{eV\,nm}/\hbar\omega$, GaN gives $1239.84/3.4=365\ \mathrm{nm}$ (ultraviolet), InGaN gives $1239.84/2.76=449\ \mathrm{nm}$ (blue), and GaAs gives $1239.84/1.42=873\ \mathrm{nm}$ (near-infrared). The color of the emission is determined by the band gap. In the blue InGaN LED, a quantum well is used in which a thin layer of InGaN is sandwiched between layers of GaN, and the emission energy shifts from $E_{\mathrm g}$ by the energies of the levels that arise from the confinement of electrons and holes in the well (the particle in a box of Chapter 10).

(2) The energy of one photon is $2.76\ \mathrm{eV}=2.76\times1.602\times10^{-19}=4.422\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$. Since $1\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{J}$ is emitted per second, the number of photons is $1\times10^{-3}/4.422\times10^{-19}=2.26\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Because one photon corresponds to the recombination of one electron–hole pair (internal quantum efficiency $100\ \%$), $2.26\times10^{15}$ electrons are injected per second, and the current is $I=e_0\times2.26\times10^{15}=1.602\times10^{-19}\times2.26\times10^{15}=3.6\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{A}=0.36\ \mathrm{mA}$.

60.5.2 Electron–phonon interaction — an electron emits and absorbs phonons

An electron moving in a crystal feels the potential $V(\bm{r}-\bm{R}_n)$ created by the atoms of the lattice. When an atom is displaced by $\bm{u}_n$ from its equilibrium position $\bm{R}_n$, the potential changes to $V(\bm{r}-\bm{R}_n-\bm{u}_n)\approx V(\bm{r}-\bm{R}_n)-\bm{u}_n\cdot\nabla V(\bm{r}-\bm{R}_n)$. The second term is the coupling between the electron and the lattice vibration, $\delta\hat{H}=-\sum_n\hat{\bm{u}}_n\cdot\nabla V(\bm{r}-\bm{R}_n)$. Let us rewrite this in the second-quantized form $\sum\mel{\bm{k}'}{\delta h}{\bm{k}}\,\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}$ (Chapter 58). For $\hat{\bm{u}}_n$ we substitute the expansion of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-u} (of the same form for each branch in three dimensions), $\hat{\bm{u}}_n=\sum_{\bm{q}}\bm{u}_{\bm{q}}\left(\hat{b}_{\bm{q}}\,e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{R}_n}+\hat{b}_{\bm{q}}^\dagger\,e^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{R}_n}\right)$ ($\bm{u}_{\bm{q}}=\sqrt{\hbar/2NM\omega_{\bm{q}}}\,\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}$, where $\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}$ is the polarization vector; the branch index has been omitted). When the electron's Bloch state $\ket{\bm{k}}$ is shifted by a lattice vector $\bm{R}_n$, it acquires the phase $e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}_n}$, so we can write $\mel{\bm{k}'}{\nabla V(\bm{r}-\bm{R}_n)}{\bm{k}}=e^{i(\bm{k}-\bm{k}')\cdot\bm{R}_n}\,\bm{v}_{\bm{k}'\bm{k}}$ ($\bm{v}_{\bm{k}'\bm{k}}$ is the matrix element of the gradient of the potential of a single atom and is independent of $n$). The sum over $n$ is then $\sum_ne^{\pm i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{R}_n}e^{i(\bm{k}-\bm{k}')\cdot\bm{R}_n}$, which, for the same reason as in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-orth}, equals $N$ only when $\bm{k}'=\bm{k}\pm\bm{q}$ (up to a reciprocal lattice vector) and equals $0$ otherwise. That is, $\hat{b}_{\bm{q}}$ is accompanied by scattering with $\bm{k}'=\bm{k}+\bm{q}$, and $\hat{b}_{\bm{q}}^\dagger$ by scattering with $\bm{k}'=\bm{k}-\bm{q}$. If we relabel $\bm{q}\to-\bm{q}$ in the latter, $\bm{k}'=\bm{k}+\bm{q}$ is accompanied by $\hat{b}_{-\bm{q}}^\dagger$, so we obtain a term of the following form.

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_{\mathrm{ep}}=\sum_{\bm{k},\bm{q},\sigma}\mathcal{M}_{\bm{q}}\;\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k},\sigma}\left(\hat{b}_{\bm{q}}+\hat{b}_{-\bm{q}}^\dagger\right) \label{eq:60-Hep} \end{equation}

$\mathcal{M}_{\bm{q}}$ is the coupling constant: it is the coefficient $\sqrt{\hbar/2NM\omega_{\bm{q}}}$ of the zero-point amplitude in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-u}, multiplied by the matrix element $\bm{v}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{e}_{\bm{q}}$ of the force that the electron feels from the displacement of the ions (apart from the sign). (For acoustic phonons $\mathcal{M}_{\bm{q}}\propto\sqrt{q}$, and for LO phonons in polar crystals $\mathcal{M}_{\bm{q}}\propto1/q$.) $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}$ is the scattering of the electron's wavenumber from $\bm{k}$ to $\bm{k}+\bm{q}$, that is, a part of the density operator $\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}$ of Chapter 58. This scattering of the electron is accompanied by phonon operators.

  • $\hat{b}_{\bm{q}}$ (annihilates one phonon $\bm{q}$): the electron absorbs a phonon of momentum $\hbar\bm{q}$, and $\bm{k}\to\bm{k}+\bm{q}$.
  • $\hat{b}_{-\bm{q}}^\dagger$ (creates one phonon $-\bm{q}$): the electron emits a phonon of momentum $-\hbar\bm{q}$, and $\bm{k}\to\bm{k}+\bm{q}$ (conservation of momentum, $\bm{k}=(\bm{k}+\bm{q})+(-\bm{q})$; Figure 60.7(c)).

This term does not change the number of electrons (it has the form $\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}$), but it changes the number of phonons by one. By the golden rule, the transition rate is proportional to $n_{\bm{q}}$ for absorption and to $n_{\bm{q}}+1$ for emission (the same structure as in Table 60.3). Even when the temperature is absolute zero ($n_{\bm{q}}=0$), an electron can emit a phonon if it has enough energy (the $+1$, which corresponds to spontaneous emission), but it cannot absorb one. Only processes in which energy conservation, $\varepsilon_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}=\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\pm\hbar\omega_{\bm{q}}$, and momentum conservation (the difference of the wavenumbers is $\bm{q}$) hold simultaneously can occur.

Example 60.12: Relaxation of a hot electron in GaAs — how many LO phonons are emitted?

When light of $1.9\ \mathrm{eV}$ is shone on GaAs ($E_{\mathrm g}=1.42\ \mathrm{eV}$), how far above the bottom of the conduction band is the energy of the electron that is produced? Take the effective mass of the electron to be $m_{\mathrm e}^*=0.067\,m_{\mathrm e}$ and that of the heavy hole to be $m_{\mathrm{hh}}^*=0.45\,m_{\mathrm e}$, and neglect the momentum of the photon ($\bm{k}$ is conserved). When the electron falls to the bottom of the conduction band by emitting longitudinal optical (LO) phonons one after another ($\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}=36\ \mathrm{meV}$, which is about $290\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ when converted to a wavenumber), how many phonons does it emit?

Solution. The difference between the photon energy and $E_{\mathrm g}$, $1.9-1.42=0.48\ \mathrm{eV}$, becomes the sum of the kinetic energies of the electron and the hole (this partition is the same calculation as in Example 59.8 of Chapter 59). Since the bands are parabolic ($E=\hbar^2k^2/2m^*$) and the electron and the hole have equal wavenumbers (conservation of $\bm{k}$), the kinetic energy is divided in inverse proportion to the masses:

$$ E_{\mathrm e}=0.48\times\frac{m_{\mathrm{hh}}^*}{m_{\mathrm e}^*+m_{\mathrm{hh}}^*}=0.48\times\frac{0.45}{0.517}=0.418\ \mathrm{eV},\qquad E_{\mathrm h}=0.48\times\frac{0.067}{0.517}=0.062\ \mathrm{eV} $$

The electron receives $0.418\ \mathrm{eV}$. Each emission of one LO phonon costs $36\ \mathrm{meV}$, so $0.418/0.036=11.6$; that is, after emitting about $11$–$12$ phonons the electron has fallen to near the bottom of the conduction band. The hole accounts for only $0.062/0.036=1.7$ phonons' worth. Phonons convert the electron's energy into heat of the lattice. However, this is an idealized estimate. In real GaAs, there is an L valley at a height of about $0.3\ \mathrm{eV}$ above the bottom of the conduction band, and when the electron has as much as $0.4\ \mathrm{eV}$ to spare, intervalley scattering into the L valley also occurs. The valence band also contains light holes, and the bands deviate from parabolas. Here we considered only one valley of the parabolic band and the heavy hole, so this is only an order-of-magnitude estimate.

Application: The electron–phonon interaction as the origin of resistance and superconductivity

The electrical resistance of a metal arises because electrons are scattered by absorbing and emitting thermally excited phonons, so that their directions of propagation become randomized. At high temperatures ($k_{\mathrm B}T\gg\hbar\omega$), the phonon number $n_{\bm{q}}\approx k_{\mathrm B}T/\hbar\omega_{\bm{q}}$ is proportional to the temperature, so the resistance is also proportional to $T$ (this is why the resistance of a metal at room temperature increases almost linearly with temperature). At low temperatures the phonons freeze out, and the resistance decreases. On the other hand, the same interaction is also the source of superconductivity. A single virtual exchange in which one electron emits a phonon into the crystal lattice and another electron absorbs it produces an attraction between the electrons (a retarded attraction mediated by the distortion of the lattice), and this forms Cooper pairs (Related simulators: Cooper pair, superconductivity).

60.5.3 Raman scattering — Stokes is $\sqrt{n+1}$, anti-Stokes is $\sqrt{n}$

When monochromatic light (angular frequency $\omega_{\mathrm L}$) is shone on a material, most of the light is scattered at the same frequency (Rayleigh scattering), but a very small part becomes light whose frequency is slightly shifted. This shift is equal to the frequency $\Omega_{\mathrm v}$ of a lattice vibration or molecular vibration of the material. This is Raman scattering, which was discovered in 1928 by Raman and Krishnan (Raman received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930). Quantum-mechanically, it is a process in which, when one photon is scattered, one quantum of lattice vibration (a phonon) is gained or lost.

  • Stokes scattering: one phonon is created, and the photon energy decreases by the same amount: $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm S}=\hbar\omega_{\mathrm L}-\hbar\Omega_{\mathrm v}$.
  • Anti-Stokes scattering: one phonon that is already present is annihilated, and the photon energy increases by the same amount: $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{aS}}=\hbar\omega_{\mathrm L}+\hbar\Omega_{\mathrm v}$.

This scattering arises because the polarizability of the electrons depends on the positions of the atoms. Let the displacement of the phonon mode be $\hat{X}=X_{\mathrm{zp}}(\hat{b}+\hat{b}^\dagger)$ (with $X_{\mathrm{zp}}=\sqrt{\hbar/2M\Omega_{\mathrm v}}$, the same form as Eq. \eqref{eq:60-u}), and expand the polarizability as $\alpha_{\mathrm p}(\hat{X})=\alpha_{\mathrm p0}+\alpha_{\mathrm p}'\hat{X}$ (where $\alpha_{\mathrm p}'=\partial\alpha_{\mathrm p}/\partial X$). Then the dipole moment $\alpha_{\mathrm p}\bm{E}_{\mathrm L}$ induced by the electric field $\bm{E}_{\mathrm L}$ of the incident light consists of $\alpha_{\mathrm p0}\bm{E}_{\mathrm L}$ (Rayleigh scattering) plus the additional term $\alpha_{\mathrm p}'X_{\mathrm{zp}}(\hat{b}+\hat{b}^\dagger)\bm{E}_{\mathrm L}$. Written in terms of the photon operators, the effective interaction for the scattering is

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_{\mathrm R}\propto\hat{a}_{\mathrm{s}}^\dagger\hat{a}_{\mathrm{L}}\left(\hat{b}^\dagger+\hat{b}\right) \label{eq:60-HR} \end{equation}

(the incident photon $\hat{a}_{\mathrm L}$ is annihilated and one scattered photon $\hat{a}_{\mathrm s}^\dagger$ is created; as in the rotating-wave approximation, we keep the terms selected by energy conservation). Stokes scattering corresponds to the $\hat{b}^\dagger$ term and anti-Stokes scattering to the $\hat{b}$ term. When the phonon mode contains $n$ phonons, the matrix elements, from Theorem 56.2 of Chapter 56, are

\begin{equation} \text{Stokes:}\ \mel{n+1}{\hat{b}^\dagger}{n}=\sqrt{n+1},\qquad\text{anti-Stokes:}\ \mel{n-1}{\hat{b}}{n}=\sqrt{n} \label{eq:60-RamanEl} \end{equation}

The intensity of the scattering is proportional to the squared matrix element, and when we average over the distribution of phonons in thermal equilibrium (Eq. \eqref{eq:60-thermalP}), the Stokes intensity is proportional to $\bar{n}+1$ and the anti-Stokes intensity to $\bar{n}$. Therefore the intensity ratio (leaving out the factor that comes from the frequency of the scattered light) is, exactly,

\begin{equation} \frac{I_{\mathrm{aS}}}{I_{\mathrm S}}\ \propto\ \frac{\bar{n}}{\bar{n}+1}=e^{-\hbar\Omega_{\mathrm v}/k_{\mathrm B}T} \label{eq:60-Raman-ratio} \end{equation}

(the relation $\bar{n}/(\bar{n}+1)=e^{-x}$ follows from Eq. \eqref{eq:60-nbar-th}, which gives $\bar{n}=1/(e^{x}-1)$ and $\bar{n}+1=e^{x}/(e^{x}-1)$, where $x=\hbar\Omega_{\mathrm v}/k_{\mathrm B}T$). This is the Boltzmann factor itself: the anti-Stokes line presupposes that excited phonons exist thermally, so it becomes weaker at lower temperatures. The Stokes line occurs even when there are zero phonons, because $\sqrt{0+1}=1$.

In Chapter 56, Section 56.7.3, we found how this ratio varies with temperature (Example 56.16). Here we go further and add the correction for the frequency of the scattered light, to put the ratio into a form that can be used as a thermometer. As dipole radiation, the intensity of the scattered light is proportional to the fourth power of the angular frequency $\omega_{\mathrm s}$ of the scattered light (this is the case for a detector that measures the intensity as energy; for a photon-counting detector, the energy of one photon is proportional to $\omega_{\mathrm s}$, so the exponent is reduced by one and the correction is the third power; the temperature obtained depends on which of the two is used; see Example 60.13 (3)). Because the anti-Stokes line has the higher frequency, it is strengthened by that factor. Including the correction, the measured intensity ratio is

\begin{equation} \frac{I_{\mathrm{aS}}}{I_{\mathrm S}}=\left(\frac{\tilde{\nu}_{\mathrm L}+\tilde{\nu}}{\tilde{\nu}_{\mathrm L}-\tilde{\nu}}\right)^4e^{-\hbar\Omega_{\mathrm v}/k_{\mathrm B}T} \label{eq:60-Raman-full} \end{equation}

(here $\tilde{\nu}_{\mathrm L}$ is the wavenumber of the incident light and $\tilde{\nu}$ is the wavenumber of the Raman shift, both in $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$; to distinguish the wavenumber from the frequency $\nu=c\tilde{\nu}$, we put a tilde (a wavy line) on the wavenumber). Since the temperature can be determined from a measured intensity ratio, this is called a Raman thermometer (Figure 60.8).

Example 60.13: Raman scattering in Si — wavelengths, intensity ratio, and temperature

The optical phonon of Si (the wavenumber corresponding to $\Omega_{\mathrm v}$ is $520.5\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, and $\hbar\Omega_{\mathrm v}=64.53\ \mathrm{meV}$) is excited by a laser of wavelength $532\ \mathrm{nm}$. (1) Find the wavelengths of the Stokes line and the anti-Stokes line. (2) Find the intensity ratio $I_{\mathrm{aS}}/I_{\mathrm S}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Raman-full}) at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ and $400\ \mathrm{K}$. (3) When the measured intensity ratio is $0.15$, find the temperature of the sample.

Solution. (1) The wavenumber of the incident light is $\tilde{\nu}_{\mathrm L}=1/(532\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{cm})=18\,797.0\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. The Stokes line is at $18\,797.0-520.5=18\,276.5\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, with wavelength $1/18\,276.5\ \mathrm{cm}=547.15\ \mathrm{nm}$. The anti-Stokes line is at $18\,797.0+520.5=19\,317.5\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, with wavelength $517.67\ \mathrm{nm}$.

(2) The frequency correction factor is $\left(\dfrac{19\,317.5}{18\,276.5}\right)^4=1.0570^4=1.248$. At $300\ \mathrm{K}$, $\hbar\Omega_{\mathrm v}/k_{\mathrm B}T=64.53/25.85=2.496$ and $e^{-2.496}=0.0824$ ($\bar{n}=0.0898$, $\bar{n}/(\bar{n}+1)=0.0824$), so $I_{\mathrm{aS}}/I_{\mathrm S}=0.0824\times1.248=0.103$. At $400\ \mathrm{K}$, $k_{\mathrm B}T=34.47\ \mathrm{meV}$, $\hbar\Omega_{\mathrm v}/k_{\mathrm B}T=1.872$, and $e^{-1.872}=0.154$, so $0.154\times1.248=0.192$. When the temperature rises by $100\ \mathrm{K}$, the relative intensity of the anti-Stokes line nearly doubles.

(3) From $0.15=1.248\,e^{-x}$ we get $e^{-x}=0.1202$ and $x=-\ln0.1202=2.119$. Hence $T=\dfrac{\hbar\Omega_{\mathrm v}}{k_{\mathrm B}\,x}=\dfrac{64.53\ \mathrm{meV}}{0.08617\ \mathrm{meV/K}\times2.119}=353\ \mathrm{K}$ ($80\ {}^\circ\mathrm{C}$). Note that if we forget the frequency correction and put $0.15=e^{-x}$, then $x=1.897$ and $T=395\ \mathrm{K}$, an answer that differs by as much as $40\ \mathrm{K}$. Also, if we use the correction for a photon-counting detector (the third power), $\left(19\,317.5/18\,276.5\right)^3=1.181$, then $e^{-x}=0.15/1.181=0.1270$, and $x=2.064$ gives $T=64.53/(0.08617\times2.064)=363\ \mathrm{K}$, so the choice of correction changes the temperature by about $10\ \mathrm{K}$. With regard to the systematic error of the Raman thermometer, it is important to choose the correction that matches the type of detector.

Application: Evaluating materials by Raman spectroscopy

The positions (frequencies) of the lines in Raman scattering are determined by the strength of the bonds and the masses of the atoms, so they serve as a fingerprint of a substance. The phonon line of Si shifts in position when stress is applied, and when the temperature rises it broadens and moves to a lower wavenumber. Moreover, because the intensity ratio in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Raman-full} above serves as a thermometer, the temperature of a tiny spot illuminated by light can be measured without contact (this is used for semiconductor devices, where heating by the laser is a problem, and for measuring heat conduction). It is also widely used for evaluating carbon materials, such as the G band near $1580\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ of graphene and carbon nanotubes.

60.5.4 Conserved and nonconserved numbers

When we line up the terms obtained so far, the rule for conservation laws becomes clear. In the operators for photons and phonons, $\hat{a}$ or $\hat{b}$ appears alone, as in Eqs. \eqref{eq:60-Hsemi}, \eqref{eq:60-Hep}, and \eqref{eq:60-HR}. The electron operators, on the other hand, appear with creation and annihilation in pairs, as in $\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}$. This difference determines the conservation of number.

That the number of electrons is conserved is equivalent to the statement that $\hat{H}$ is invariant under the transformation $\hat{c}_i\to e^{i\theta}\hat{c}_i$ ($\hat{c}_i^\dagger\to e^{-i\theta}\hat{c}_i^\dagger$), which multiplies the operators of all the electrons by the same phase (the $\mathrm{U}(1)$ symmetry). In a pair $\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}$ the phases cancel. This symmetry corresponds to the conservation of charge (Noether's theorem). On the other hand, if we let $\hat{a}\to e^{i\theta}\hat{a}$, then $\hat{a}\,\hat{c}_{\mathrm c}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\mathrm v}$ is multiplied by $e^{i\theta}$ and is not invariant. That is, photons have no corresponding symmetry, and their number is not conserved. Note that if the absorption of light merely moves an electron from the valence band to the conduction band, the total number of electrons does not change (the electron–hole pair is the result of the motion of an electron). The only process that changes the number of electrons is the pair production of an electron and a positron (Part XI), and even there (number of electrons) $-$ (number of positrons) is conserved.

Even when numbers are not conserved, the conservation of energy and (crystal) momentum holds in each process. In the absorption in Figure 60.7(a), $\hbar\omega=E_{\mathrm c}-E_{\mathrm v}$, and in the phonon emission in (c), $\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}=\varepsilon_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}+\hbar\omega_{\bm{q}}$ and $\bm{k}=(\bm{k}+\bm{q})+(-\bm{q})$. The more conserved numbers there are, the more finely the state space splits into small pieces (the $\hat{N}_{\mathrm{ex}}$ of the Jaynes–Cummings model is an example), but in real systems containing photons and phonons, the main constraints are not numbers but energy and momentum.

Summary scheme: How to “read” the Hamiltonian

The three terms in this section all have the same structure: “electron movement ($\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}$) $\times$ creation or annihilation of a photon or phonon.” (i) $\hat{a}\,\hat{c}_{\mathrm c}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\mathrm v}$: the electron absorbs a photon and goes to the upper band. (ii) $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}\hat{b}_{-\bm{q}}^\dagger$: the electron is scattered, and the number of phonons increases by one. (iii) $\hat{a}_{\mathrm s}^\dagger\hat{a}_{\mathrm L}\hat{b}^\dagger$: the light is scattered, and the number of phonons increases by one. In every term, the matrix element contains $\sqrt{n}$ (annihilation) or $\sqrt{n+1}$ (creation), and averaging $n$ over thermal equilibrium gives $\bar{n}$ and $\bar{n}+1$. Second quantization is a language that writes the diverse processes in which the particle number changes in a unified form, as products of operators.

60.6 An Oscillator Driven by an External Force — Coherent States and an Introduction to Linear Response

In this last section we examine a harmonic oscillator acted on by an external force. This tells us why the coherent states learned in Chapter 56 are important. A classical force turns a harmonic oscillator into a coherent state. In addition, the shapes of the emission and absorption spectra of materials (the Franck–Condon principle, the Huang–Rhys factor) can be understood with this simple model. Furthermore, the response of an oscillator to an external force is an introduction to the linear response learned in Part IX (Chapter 54) and to the companion volume “Linear Response Theory” (AD 08).

60.6.1 A harmonic oscillator under a constant force — exactly solvable

A constant force $F$ acts on a harmonic oscillator of mass $m$ and angular frequency $\omega$. The potential energy gains an additional term $-F\hat{x}$, so $\hat{H}=\dfrac{\hat{p}^2}{2m}+\tfrac12m\omega^2\hat{x}^2-F\hat{x}$. Using $\hat{x}=x_{\mathrm{zp}}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)$ ($x_{\mathrm{zp}}=\sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}$; Chapter 56) and dropping the constant $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$, we have

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=\hbar\omega\,\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+G\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger\right),\qquad G=-F\,x_{\mathrm{zp}} \label{eq:60-Hdrive} \end{equation}

Here $G$ is a (signed) coupling strength with the dimension of energy (the unit of $F\,x_{\mathrm{zp}}$ is $\mathrm{N\,m}=\mathrm{J}$), and it is a different quantity from $g$ (an angular frequency) in Section 60.4. Also, the $\alpha$ in this section is the amplitude of a coherent state, and it is a different quantity from the polarizability $\alpha_{\mathrm p}$ in Section 60.5.3 and from the fine-structure constant. This Hamiltonian has the first-order terms $\hat{a}$ and $\hat{a}^\dagger$ and is no longer just $\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}$, but it can be solved exactly by completing the square.

Derivation: Completing the square and the displaced annihilation operator

Since $\hbar\omega\left(\hat{a}^\dagger+\dfrac{G}{\hbar\omega}\right)\left(\hat{a}+\dfrac{G}{\hbar\omega}\right)=\hbar\omega\,\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+G\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger\right)+\dfrac{G^2}{\hbar\omega}$, $\hat{H}$ can be rewritten as follows.

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=\hbar\omega\,\tilde{a}^\dagger\tilde{a}-\frac{G^2}{\hbar\omega},\qquad\tilde{a}=\hat{a}-\alpha,\quad\alpha=-\frac{G}{\hbar\omega}=\frac{F\,x_{\mathrm{zp}}}{\hbar\omega} \label{eq:60-Hsq} \end{equation}

Since $\alpha$ is real (a c-number), $\comm{\tilde{a}}{\tilde{a}^\dagger}=\comm{\hat{a}}{\hat{a}^\dagger}=1$, so $\tilde{a}$ and $\tilde{a}^\dagger$ also satisfy the algebra of annihilation and creation operators. Therefore, by exactly the same argument as in Chapter 56, the eigenvalues of the number operator $\tilde{N}=\tilde{a}^\dagger\tilde{a}$ are $n=0,1,2,\dots$, and the eigenstate $\ket{n}_{\sim}$ is built by applying $\tilde{a}^\dagger$ $n$ times. The eigenenergy is $\hbar\omega n-G^2/\hbar\omega$.

∎

Theorem 60.4: A harmonic oscillator under a constant force

The eigenenergies of $\hat{H}=\hbar\omega\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+G(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)$ are

\begin{equation} E_n=n\hbar\omega-\frac{G^2}{\hbar\omega}\qquad(n=0,1,2,\dots) \label{eq:60-En-drive} \end{equation}

and the ground state is the coherent state $\ket{\alpha}$ with eigenvalue $\alpha=-G/\hbar\omega$ of $\hat{a}$. The average number of quanta (of the original oscillator) in this ground state is

\begin{equation} \expval{\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}}=\abs{\alpha}^2=\frac{G^2}{(\hbar\omega)^2}=\frac{F^2}{2m\hbar\omega^3} \label{eq:60-nforce} \end{equation}

and the number follows a Poisson distribution.

The ground-state condition $\tilde{a}\ket{\psi_0}=0$ is the same as $\hat{a}\ket{\psi_0}=\alpha\ket{\psi_0}$, which is precisely the definition of a coherent state (Definition 56.3). The last equality of Eq. \eqref{eq:60-nforce} follows from $G=-Fx_{\mathrm{zp}}$ and $x_{\mathrm{zp}}^2=\hbar/2m\omega$: $G^2/(\hbar\omega)^2=F^2\hbar/(2m\omega)/(\hbar\omega)^2=F^2/(2m\hbar\omega^3)$. Let us check this classically. The expectation value of the position is $\expval{\hat{x}}=2x_{\mathrm{zp}}\Real\alpha=2x_{\mathrm{zp}}^2F/\hbar\omega=F/m\omega^2$, which coincides with the classical equilibrium position $x_0=F/m\omega^2$ (the balance between the force and the spring force). The lowering of the energy, $-G^2/\hbar\omega=-F^2/2m\omega^2$, is also equal to the minimum value $-F^2/2m\omega^2$ of the classical potential $\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2-Fx$. In other words, the ground state of an oscillator under a constant force is a wave packet of the same shape (Gaussian) whose equilibrium position is displaced by $x_0$. Seen in terms of the quanta of the original oscillator, it becomes “a state containing an average of $\abs{\alpha}^2=x_0^2/4x_{\mathrm{zp}}^2$ quanta (a coherent state).” This can be read as saying that the classical force has created a large number of quanta with aligned phases.

Example 60.14: A force that displaces the equilibrium position of a CO molecule

The stretching vibration of the CO molecule can be regarded as a harmonic oscillator with reduced mass $\mu=6.856\ \mathrm{u}$ and frequency $\nu=64.2\ \mathrm{THz}$ (wavenumber $2143\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, $\omega=2\pi\nu=4.03\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$). (1) Find the width of the zero-point vibration, $x_{\mathrm{zp}}=\sqrt{\hbar/2\mu\omega}$. (2) Find the constant force $F$ that displaces the equilibrium internuclear distance by $2x_{\mathrm{zp}}$, and confirm that $\expval{\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-nforce} is then $1$. (3) What is $\expval{\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}}$ for a force that displaces the equilibrium position by $x_{\mathrm{zp}}$?

Solution. (1) $\mu=6.856\times1.6605\times10^{-27}=1.1384\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$. Since $x_{\mathrm{zp}}^2=\dfrac{\hbar}{2\mu\omega}=\dfrac{1.0546\times10^{-34}}{2\times1.1384\times10^{-26}\times4.034\times10^{14}}=1.148\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, we get $x_{\mathrm{zp}}=3.39\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m}=3.4\ \mathrm{pm}$ (about $3\ \%$ of the CO bond length, $113\ \mathrm{pm}$).

(2) The displacement of the equilibrium position is $x_0=F/\mu\omega^2=2x_{\mathrm{zp}}=6.77\ \mathrm{pm}$, so $F=\mu\omega^2x_0=1.1384\times10^{-26}\times(4.034\times10^{14})^2\times6.77\times10^{-12}=1.26\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{N}=12.6\ \mathrm{nN}$ (a magnitude that can be handled with an atomic force microscope). Substituting into Eq. \eqref{eq:60-nforce} gives $\dfrac{F^2}{2\mu\hbar\omega^3}=\dfrac{(1.255\times10^{-8})^2}{2\times1.1384\times10^{-26}\times1.0546\times10^{-34}\times(4.034\times10^{14})^3}=\dfrac{1.575\times10^{-16}}{1.576\times10^{-16}}=1.00$, which is indeed $1$ (in general, $\expval{\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}}=x_0^2/4x_{\mathrm{zp}}^2$).

(3) If $x_0=x_{\mathrm{zp}}$, then $\expval{\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}}=x_{\mathrm{zp}}^2/4x_{\mathrm{zp}}^2=\dfrac14$. When the equilibrium position is displaced by just the width of the zero-point vibration, the average number of quanta is $1/4$.

60.6.2 Applications to materials — the Franck–Condon principle and the Huang–Rhys factor

The shapes of the absorption and emission spectra of light-emitting materials can be understood with the results above. At a luminescence center in a crystal (a color center, a dopant ion of a phosphor, a complex), when an electron is excited, the equilibrium positions of the surrounding atoms are displaced by $Q_0$ because of the change in bond strength. Taking out one mode of the lattice vibration, let the Hamiltonian when the electron is in the ground state be $\hat{H}_{\mathrm g}=\hbar\omega\hat{b}^\dagger\hat{b}$, and that when it is in the excited state be $\hat{H}_{\mathrm e}=E_{\mathrm{e}0}+\hbar\omega\hat{b}^\dagger\hat{b}+G(\hat{b}+\hat{b}^\dagger)$. The Hamiltonian of the excited state has the same form as Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Hdrive}, which corresponds to the statement that “the excitation of the electron exerts a force on the oscillator.” Therefore, the vibrational levels of the excited state are the eigenstates $\ket{m}_{\sim}$ (having $m$ quanta) of the operator $\tilde{b}=\hat{b}-\alpha$, in which $\hat{b}$ is displaced by $\alpha$, with $\alpha=-G/\hbar\omega$.

Definition 60.5: Huang–Rhys factor

The square of the magnitude of the displacement of $\hat{b}$ caused by the shift of the equilibrium position of the excited state,

\begin{equation} S=\abs{\alpha}^2=\frac{G^2}{(\hbar\omega)^2}=\frac{M\omega\,Q_0^2}{2\hbar}\qquad\left(Q_0=\text{displacement from equilibrium},\ M=\text{mass of the mode}\right) \label{eq:60-S} \end{equation}

is called the Huang–Rhys factor (Huang and Rhys, 1950). It is the average number of phonons generated along with an electronic transition.

The behavior of absorption can be understood as follows. Absorption of light occurs before the nuclei move (the Franck–Condon principle, Chapter 53, Section 53.5.2), so the lattice vibration remains in the vibrational ground state $\ket{0}$ of the ground state, and only the electron is excited. When $\ket{0}$ is expanded in the vibrational levels $\ket{m}_{\sim}$ of the excited state, $\ket{0}$ is a coherent state with respect to the excited-state operator $\tilde{b}$, with eigenvalue $\hat{b}\ket{0}=0$, that is, $\tilde{b}\ket{0}=-\alpha\ket{0}$ (amplitude $-\alpha$). By the expansion of a coherent state (Formula 56.2), the probability of a transition to the level $m$ of the excited state (the Franck–Condon factor) is

\begin{equation} P_m=\abs{{}_{\sim}\!\braket{m}{0}}^2=e^{-S}\frac{S^m}{m!}\qquad(m=0,1,2,\dots) \label{eq:60-FC} \end{equation}

a Poisson distribution (Figure 60.10). This is the same as the result derived in Chapter 53, Section 53.5.2. The absorption lines appear, corresponding to the vibrational levels $m$, as a series of lines of intensity $P_m$ at the energies $E_0+m\hbar\omega$ ($E_0$ is the energy of the transition between the vibrational ground states of the ground and excited states). In emission (which starts from the vibrational ground state of the excited state), symmetrically, a series of intensity $P_m$ appears at the energies $E_0-m\hbar\omega$ (Figure 60.9).

  • Zero-phonon line: the line with $m=0$ (a transition in which no phonon is emitted or absorbed), with intensity $P_0=e^{-S}$. When $S$ is large (the displacement of the lattice caused by the excitation is large), the zero-phonon line weakens rapidly.
  • Mean energy: Since the mean of the Poisson distribution is $\bar{m}=S$, the mean energy of the absorption line is $E_0+S\hbar\omega$, and that of the emission is $E_0-S\hbar\omega$. The difference between the peak positions of absorption and emission (the Stokes shift) is $2S\hbar\omega$. The reason the emission is shifted to the lower-energy side of the absorption is that the excited lattice emits on average $S$ phonons and loses the energy $S\hbar\omega$ before it emits light.
  • $S$ and materials: When $S\ll1$, the zero-phonon line dominates and becomes a sharp line (the R lines of ruby are sharp because ${}^2E$ and ${}^4A_2$ both have the $t_{2g}^3$ electron configuration, so the bond length hardly changes, that is, $S$ is small; Chapter 48). When $S$ is larger than 1, the result is a broad band in which many vibrational lines overlap (the absorption bands of the d–d transitions of transition-metal complexes are broad because the $e_g$ electrons weaken the bonds and $S$ is large; Chapter 53). In real materials, many modes of the lattice vibrations contribute, so the shape of the band is a superposition of Poisson-distribution series.

Example 60.15: The vibrational series and the Stokes shift for Huang–Rhys factor $S=2$

For a luminescence center with mode energy $\hbar\omega=30\ \mathrm{meV}$ and Huang–Rhys factor $S=2$, find the intensities $P_m$ of the lines $m=0,1,\dots,5$, the fraction of the zero-phonon line, and the Stokes shift. Also find the fraction of the zero-phonon line and the Stokes shift for $S=5$.

Solution. $S=2$: Since $e^{-2}=0.1353$, $P_m=0.1353\times2^m/m!$, and $P_0=0.1353$, $P_1=0.2707$, $P_2=0.2707$, $P_3=0.1804$, $P_4=0.0902$, $P_5=0.0361$ (and then $P_6=0.0120$, …; the sum of all of them is $1$). The zero-phonon line is $13.5\ \%$. The Stokes shift is $2S\hbar\omega=4\times30=120\ \mathrm{meV}$. $S=5$: the zero-phonon line is $e^{-5}=0.0067$ ($0.7\ \%$), and the Stokes shift is $2\times5\times30=300\ \mathrm{meV}=0.30\ \mathrm{eV}$, so the absorption band and the emission band are as much as $0.3\ \mathrm{eV}$ apart, and the emission is hardly reabsorbed (favorable for a phosphor). The peak of the distribution lies at $m\approx5$ ($P_4=P_5=0.1755$).

60.6.3 Forces that change with time — the Heisenberg equation and coherent states

When the force depends on time, we solve $\hat{H}(t)=\hbar\omega\,\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+f(t)\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger\right)$ ($f(t)=-F(t)\,x_{\mathrm{zp}}$ is a function with the dimension of energy). Instead of following the time evolution of the state vector, we solve the equation for the operator $\hat{a}(t)$ in the Heisenberg picture (Chapter 50, Section 50.3); because it is linear, it can be solved easily.

Derivation: The exact solution for $\hat{a}(t)$

In Heisenberg's equation of motion $\dfrac{\dd\hat{a}}{\dd t}=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}\comm{\hat{H}}{\hat{a}}$, we use $\comm{\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}}{\hat{a}}=-\hat{a}$ and $\comm{\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger}{\hat{a}}=\comm{\hat{a}^\dagger}{\hat{a}}=-1$. Since these give $\comm{\hat{H}}{\hat{a}}=-\hbar\omega\hat{a}-f(t)$, we obtain

\begin{equation} \frac{\dd\hat{a}}{\dd t}=-i\omega\,\hat{a}-\frac{i}{\hbar}f(t) \label{eq:60-aeom} \end{equation}

Here the second term on the right-hand side, $-if(t)/\hbar$, is not an operator but a c-number (an ordinary number). Taking $\hat{a}(0)=\hat{a}$ at $t=0$, the solution is

\begin{equation} \hat{a}(t)=\hat{a}\,e^{-i\omega t}+\alpha(t),\qquad\alpha(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^tf(t')\,e^{-i\omega(t-t')}\dd t' \label{eq:60-at} \end{equation}

That this is indeed the solution can be checked by substituting $\dfrac{\dd\alpha}{\dd t}=-\dfrac{i}{\hbar}f(t)-i\omega\,\alpha(t)$ (differentiation of the upper limit of the integral, and of the $t$ dependence of the integrand) into Eq. \eqref{eq:60-aeom}. Also, $\alpha(0)=0$.

∎

Eq. \eqref{eq:60-at} says, “$\hat{a}(t)$ is the solution $\hat{a}\,e^{-i\omega t}$ in the absence of the force plus the c-number $\alpha(t)$”; the effect of the force is packed into a single function $\alpha(t)$. If the initial state is the vacuum $\ket{0}$ ($\hat{a}\ket{0}=0$), then $\hat{a}(t)\ket{0}=\left(\hat{a}\,e^{-i\omega t}+\alpha(t)\right)\ket{0}=\alpha(t)\ket{0}$, so the Heisenberg-picture state $\ket{0}$ is an eigenstate of $\hat{a}(t)$ (with eigenvalue $\alpha(t)$). That is, in the Schrödinger picture the state at time $t$ is, apart from an overall phase, the coherent state $\ket{\alpha(t)}$, and the average number of quanta is $\abs{\alpha(t)}^2$.

Theorem 60.5: A state produced by a classical force is a coherent state

If a classical force $F(t)$ is applied to a harmonic oscillator that is initially in the vacuum, the state at time $t$ is always the coherent state $\ket{\alpha(t)}$ (apart from an overall phase), and the amplitude $\alpha(t)$ is given by Eq. \eqref{eq:60-at}. The distribution of the number of quanta is a Poisson distribution with mean $\abs{\alpha(t)}^2$.

This holds for light as well. A classical current (the current in an antenna, or the macroscopic polarization current in a laser medium) acts on each mode of the electromagnetic field as a force $f(t)$. The reason is that the coupling $-\int\bm{j}\cdot\hat{\bm{A}}\,\dd^3r$ between the current and $\hat{\bm{A}}$ (which is proportional to $\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Aop}) has the same form as Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Hdrive}. Therefore, light emitted by a classical current is a coherent state (Glauber). This is why we treated laser light as a coherent state in Chapter 56, Section 56.6. Thermal light, on the other hand, is emitted independently by a great many atoms (the current has a random phase), so it is a statistical mixture of coherent states.

As a special case, if a constant force $f(t)=G$ is suddenly applied at $t=0$, we obtain $\alpha(t)=-\dfrac{i}{\hbar}G\dfrac{1-e^{-i\omega t}}{i\omega}=\alpha_{\mathrm s}\left(1-e^{-i\omega t}\right)$ ($\alpha_{\mathrm s}=-G/\hbar\omega$ is the static value in Eq. \eqref{eq:60-Hsq}). The amplitude goes around the new equilibrium value $\alpha_{\mathrm s}$, tracing a circle of radius $\abs{\alpha_{\mathrm s}}$. The number of quanta is $\abs{\alpha}^2=2\abs{\alpha_{\mathrm s}}^2\,(1-\cos\omega t)$, whose time average is $2\abs{\alpha_{\mathrm s}}^2$, twice the value $\abs{\alpha_{\mathrm s}}^2$ obtained when the force is applied slowly. The phenomenon in which a short pulse of light excites the lattice vibrations of a crystal all at once with the same phase (coherent phonons) is an example of this sudden force.

Resonant driving: The magnitude of the coherent state grows in proportion to time

When a force $f(t)=\hbar\Omega_0\cos\omega t$ ($\Omega_0$ has the dimension of angular frequency) drives the oscillator at resonance, $\omega_{\mathrm d}=\omega$, Eq. \eqref{eq:60-at} gives $\alpha(t)=-i\Omega_0\displaystyle\int_0^t\cos\omega t'\,e^{-i\omega(t-t')}\dd t'=-i\Omega_0e^{-i\omega t}\left[\dfrac{t}{2}+\dfrac{e^{2i\omega t}-1}{4i\omega}\right]$, and for $\omega t\gg1$ we have $\abs{\alpha(t)}\approx\Omega_0t/2$, which grows in proportion to time. The number of quanta is $\Omega_0^2t^2/4$, which increases as the square of the time. This corresponds to the classical resonance in which “the amplitude grows in proportion to time” (Exercise 60.8).

60.6.4 Linear response — the response of the position to an external force $\chi(\omega)$

In Example 54.3 of Chapter 54, we obtained the response function of the quantum harmonic oscillator from the Kubo formula. Here we derive the same response function by a different route, from the exact solution, Eq. \eqref{eq:60-at}, of the Heisenberg equation. From Eq. \eqref{eq:60-at}, let us find the expectation value $\expval{\hat{x}}(t)$ of the position under an external force $F(t)$. Since $\hat{x}=x_{\mathrm{zp}}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)$ and $\expval{\hat{a}(t)}=\alpha(t)$ in the vacuum, $\expval{\hat{x}}(t)=2x_{\mathrm{zp}}\Real\alpha(t)$. Putting $f=-Fx_{\mathrm{zp}}$ into Eq. \eqref{eq:60-at} gives $\alpha(t)=\dfrac{ix_{\mathrm{zp}}}{\hbar}\displaystyle\int_0^tF(t')e^{-i\omega(t-t')}\dd t'$, and its real part is given by $\Real\left[ie^{-i\omega(t-t')}\right]=\sin\omega(t-t')$, so that

\begin{equation} \expval{\hat{x}}(t)=\frac{2x_{\mathrm{zp}}^2}{\hbar}\int_0^tF(t')\sin\omega(t-t')\dd t'=\frac{1}{m\omega}\int_0^tF(t')\sin\omega(t-t')\dd t' \label{eq:60-xresp} \end{equation}

(here $2x_{\mathrm{zp}}^2/\hbar=1/m\omega$). This is equal to the solution of the classical equation of motion $m\ddot{x}=-m\omega^2x+F(t)$ starting from rest at $t=0$ (the form using the Green's function $\sin\omega t/m\omega$; AD 03, Chapter 7, Section 7.4). $\hbar$ has dropped out. Now we distinguish the natural angular frequency of the oscillator, $\omega_0$, from the angular frequency of the external force, $\omega$, and write Eq. \eqref{eq:60-xresp} in the form of a “weighted sum of past forces”:

\begin{equation} \expval{\hat{x}}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}K(t-t')\,F(t')\dd t',\qquad K(\tau)=\theta(\tau)\frac{\sin\omega_0\tau}{m\omega_0} \label{eq:60-K} \end{equation}

Here $K(\tau)$ is the magnitude by which an impulsive force ($F(t')=\delta(t')$) applied a time $\tau$ earlier moves the present position (a causal response function; it is $0$ for $\tau\lt0$). The response to a monochromatic force $F(t)=F_0e^{-i\omega t}$ can be written $\expval{\hat{x}}=\chi(\omega)F_0e^{-i\omega t}$, and $\chi(\omega)$ is the Fourier transform of $K$ in time (with time kernel $e^{+i\omega\tau}$, the same convention as in Part IX). Attaching the convergence factor $\omega\to\omega+i\eta$ ($\eta\to+0$; the force was turned on gradually in the past), we have

\begin{equation} \chi(\omega)=\int_0^\infty K(\tau)\,e^{i(\omega+i\eta)\tau}\dd\tau=\frac{1}{m\omega_0}\cdot\frac{\omega_0}{\omega_0^2-(\omega+i\eta)^2}\ \xrightarrow{\ \eta\to+0\ }\ \frac{1}{m\left(\omega_0^2-\omega^2\right)} \label{eq:60-chi} \end{equation}

Here the integral is evaluated using $\sin\omega_0\tau=(e^{i\omega_0\tau}-e^{-i\omega_0\tau})/2i$ and $\int_0^\infty e^{i(\Omega+i\eta)\tau}\dd\tau=i/(\Omega+i\eta)$, which give $\tfrac12\left[\dfrac{1}{\omega+\omega_0}-\dfrac{1}{\omega-\omega_0}\right]=\dfrac{\omega_0}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2}$.

Formula 60.3: The mechanical susceptibility of a harmonic oscillator

When a force $F(t)=F_0e^{-i\omega t}$ is applied to a harmonic oscillator of natural angular frequency $\omega_0$ and mass $m$, the proportionality coefficient of the position response $\expval{\hat{x}}=\chi(\omega)F$ is

\begin{equation} \chi(\omega)=\frac{1}{m\left(\omega_0^2-\omega^2\right)} \label{eq:60-chi-final} \end{equation}

As $\omega\to0$, $\chi(0)=1/m\omega_0^2=1/k_{\mathrm{sp}}$ (the reciprocal of the spring constant $k_{\mathrm{sp}}=m\omega_0^2$; Hooke's law). For $\omega\lt\omega_0$ the oscillator is displaced in the same direction as the force, for $\omega\gt\omega_0$ in the opposite direction, and at $\omega=\omega_0$ it resonates and diverges (in reality it becomes finite because of damping).

Example 60.16: Response below and above resonance — in the same direction as the force, or in the opposite direction?

An oscillating force $F_0e^{-i\omega t}$ of magnitude $F_0=1\ \mathrm{nN}$ is applied to a Cu-type Einstein oscillator ($M=63.55\ \mathrm{u}$, $\omega_0/2\pi=4.5\ \mathrm{THz}$), at $\omega/2\pi=2.25\ \mathrm{THz}$ (below resonance) and at $\omega/2\pi=9\ \mathrm{THz}$ (above resonance). (1) Find the static response $\chi(0)$ and the displacement due to a static force of $1\ \mathrm{nN}$. (2) Find $\chi(\omega)$ and the magnitude of the displacement at the two frequencies, and compare them with the static response (in magnitude and direction). (3) Explain, from the classical equation of motion, why the displacement is opposite to the force above resonance.

Solution. (1) $M=63.55\times1.660\,539\times10^{-27}=1.0553\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $\omega_0=2\pi\times4.5\times10^{12}=2.827\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so the spring constant is $k_{\mathrm{sp}}=M\omega_0^2=1.0553\times10^{-25}\times(2.827\times10^{13})^2=84.4\ \mathrm{N/m}$. The static response is $\chi(0)=1/k_{\mathrm{sp}}=1.185\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m/N}$, and the displacement due to a force of $1\ \mathrm{nN}$ is $1.185\times10^{-2}\times10^{-9}=1.185\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m}=11.9\ \mathrm{pm}$.

(2) At $\omega=\omega_0/2$ ($2.25\ \mathrm{THz}$), $\omega_0^2-\omega^2=\omega_0^2\left(1-\tfrac14\right)=0.75\,\omega_0^2$, so by Eq. \eqref{eq:60-chi-final}, $\chi=\dfrac{1}{0.75\,M\omega_0^2}=\dfrac43\chi(0)=1.58\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m/N}$. The displacement amplitude is $15.8\ \mathrm{pm}$, which is $4/3$ times the static value, in the same direction as the force. At $\omega=2\omega_0$ ($9\ \mathrm{THz}$), $\omega_0^2-\omega^2=\omega_0^2(1-4)=-3\,\omega_0^2$, so $\chi=-\dfrac{1}{3M\omega_0^2}=-\tfrac13\chi(0)=-3.95\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m/N}$. The displacement amplitude is $3.95\ \mathrm{pm}$, which is $1/3$ of the static value; the sign is negative, that is, the displacement is opposite to the force (out of phase).

(3) Putting $x=\chi F_0e^{-i\omega t}$ into the equation of motion $M\ddot{x}=-M\omega_0^2x+F_0e^{-i\omega t}$ gives $-M\omega^2\chi=-M\omega_0^2\chi+1$, so $\chi=1/[M(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)]$. When the force changes slowly ($\omega\ll\omega_0$), the spring force $-M\omega_0^2x$ balances the force $F$, so $x\approx F/M\omega_0^2$ and the oscillator moves in the same direction. When the force changes quickly ($\omega\gg\omega_0$), the restoring force of the spring cannot keep up, the inertia term $M\ddot{x}=-M\omega^2x$ takes the leading role, and $x\approx-F/M\omega^2$. In a motion determined by inertia alone, the acceleration $\ddot{x}=F/M$ is in the same direction as the force, but in a motion oscillating at angular frequency $\omega$ we have $\ddot{x}=-\omega^2x$, so the displacement $x$ is opposite to the acceleration, that is, opposite to the force. The fact that the polarization of the electrons in a dielectric becomes out of phase with the electric field when the frequency of light rises above the resonance is also a response function of the same form.

In this calculation, neither $\hbar$ nor the state of the oscillator (vacuum or thermal equilibrium) enters the answer. The reason is that the commutator of $\hat{x}(\tau)$ in the Heisenberg picture is a c-number, not an operator: from $\hat{x}(\tau)=x_{\mathrm{zp}}\left(\hat{a}\,e^{-i\omega_0\tau}+\hat{a}^\dagger e^{i\omega_0\tau}\right)$ we get

\begin{equation} \comm{\hat{x}(\tau)}{\hat{x}(0)}=x_{\mathrm{zp}}^2\left(e^{-i\omega_0\tau}-e^{i\omega_0\tau}\right)=-2ix_{\mathrm{zp}}^2\sin\omega_0\tau,\qquad \frac{i}{\hbar}\comm{\hat{x}(\tau)}{\hat{x}(0)}=\frac{\sin\omega_0\tau}{m\omega_0} \label{eq:60-comm} \end{equation}

That is, the response function is $K(\tau)=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}\theta(\tau)\,\expval{\comm{\hat{x}(\tau)}{\hat{x}(0)}}$; the response function is the expectation value of the commutator, at different times, of the quantity $\hat{x}$ to which the external force is applied. This is precisely the Kubo formula of Chapter 54 (here we have derived from the Heisenberg equation the same result as Example 54.3 of Chapter 54). For the harmonic oscillator the commutator is a c-number, so the result is the same in any state, and the response is exactly linear in the external force (the quantum result agrees completely with the result of the classical equation of motion). In a general system the commutator becomes an operator, its expectation value depends on the state, and higher-order responses also appear.

Caution: Difference from the sign convention of AD 08

This book writes the coupling to the external force in the physical form $\hat{H}_1=-F(t)\,\hat{x}$ (the work done by the force $F$ on the position $x$; $-\hat{\bm{d}}\cdot\bm{E}$ has the same form) and defines $\expval{\hat{x}}=\chi F$. Then the harmonic oscillator's $\chi(\omega)=1/[m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)]$ is positive in the static limit (the oscillator moves in the direction of the force). The companion volume “Linear Response Theory” (AD 08; Chapter 7, Derivation of the Kubo Formula) writes the coupling to the external field as $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{ext}}=+f(t)\,\hat{B}$ (the convention of Fetter and Walecka), defines the retarded response function as $\chi^{\mathrm R}_{AB}(t)=-\dfrac{i}{\hbar}\theta(t)\expval{\comm{\hat{A}(t)}{\hat{B}(0)}}$, and sets $\expval{\hat{A}}=\chi^{\mathrm R}f$. This corresponds to $\hat{A}=\hat{B}=\hat{x}$ and $f=-F$, so for the harmonic oscillator $\chi^{\mathrm R}_{xx}(\omega)=-1/[m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)]$, which appears to have the opposite sign to the mechanical susceptibility. Both describe the same physics, and they are related by $\chi_{\mathrm{mech}}=-\chi^{\mathrm R}_{xx}$. When reading AD 08, check the sign of the coupling ($-F\hat{x}$ or $+f\hat{x}$). The same caution was given in the box in Chapter 54, Section 54.2.

60.6.5 Bridge to Part IX and AD 08

We summarize what we have obtained in this section in the following three points. (i) A classical force puts a harmonic oscillator into a (non-spreading) coherent state. (ii) The response of the oscillator can be written as the expectation value of the commutator of the quantity coupled to the external force (the simplest example of the Kubo formula; Chapter 54, Section 54.2). (iii) For a harmonic system ($\hat{H}$ is a quadratic expression in the operators), the linear response is exact, and quantum theory and classical theory agree. The response function of a general many-body system (the dielectric function or magnetic susceptibility of the electron gas) is obtained by replacing $\hat{x}$ in this $K(\tau)$ by a density or spin operator and taking the expectation value in the ground state (the Fermi sea). The tools for that calculation are the retarded Green's function (Chapter 55) and the language of second quantization (Chapters 56–59). Linear response theory (AD 08) uses these to treat the screening in an electron gas, plasmons, magnetism, and the response of superconductors (for the response function of a classical forced oscillator, see Chapter 3; for the derivation of the Kubo formula, see Chapter 7). The field quantization of photons and phonons (this chapter) is also the starting point of elementary particle physics (AD 09 (in Japanese)): a free field $=$ a collection of harmonic oscillators.

60.7.2 Summary of Part X — the three pillars of second quantization

The five chapters from Chapter 56 through this chapter can be summarized in a single story. In the language of wave functions (first quantization), we can describe only the motion of a system with a fixed number $N$ of particles. The language that makes the number of particles a variable is second quantization, and it rests on the following three pillars.

  1. Occupation-number representation (Chapter 57): a state is specified by how many particles occupy each orbital (mode), $\ket{n_1,n_2,\dots}$. The indistinguishability of particles is built into the representation from the start.
  2. Operator algebra (Chapters 56 and 57): all states and operators can be written using only $\hat{a}^\dagger$ ($\hat{c}^\dagger$), which creates one particle, and $\hat{a}$ ($\hat{c}$), which annihilates one. Bosons obey the commutation relation $\comm{\hat{a}}{\hat{a}^\dagger}=1$ (any number of particles can occupy a mode; the matrix elements are $\sqrt{n}$ and $\sqrt{n+1}$), and fermions obey the anticommutation relation $\acomm{\hat{c}}{\hat{c}^\dagger}=1$ (the Pauli exclusion principle becomes the single line $(\hat{c}^\dagger)^2=0$).
  3. Field operators and the many-body Hamiltonian (Chapters 58 and 59): with the one-body operator $\sum t_{ij}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$, the two-body operator $\tfrac12\sum v_{ijkl}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$, and the field operator $\hat{\psi}(\bm{r})$, the electron gas, the Hubbard model, and the Hartree–Fock approximation can all be written in the same form.

In this chapter we saw that this language shows its power in “processes in which the number of particles changes.” The absorption and emission of light, the emission of phonons by electrons, and Raman scattering are each written as a single term in which $\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}$ is multiplied by $\hat{a}$ or $\hat{b}$. In the language of wave functions, the domain of the wave function changes every time the number of particles changes, so such processes cannot be treated (Chapter 56, Section 56.1).

On to the next part and the companion volumes. In Part XI (Relativistic Quantum Mechanics), we will see that the problem of the negative-energy solutions of the Klein–Gordon equation and the Dirac equation is resolved by field quantization (electron–positron pair production, a world in which the number of particles changes). The quantization of the photon field $\hat{\bm{A}}$ seen in this chapter is its starting point. The companion volume “Linear Response Theory” (AD 08) applies the response function $\chi(\omega)$ and the Kubo formula of Section 60.6 of this chapter to electron gases and magnetic materials. “Elementary Particle Physics (in Japanese)” (AD 09) starts from free fields $=$ collections of harmonic oscillators (phonons $\hat{b}_{\bm{q}}$, photons $\hat{a}_{\bm{k}}$) and calculates the processes of particle creation and annihilation with Feynman diagrams. “Solid-State Physics” (AD 06) treats the dispersion and specific heat of phonons (Chapter 13 (in Japanese), Chapter 14 (in Japanese)) and the optical absorption of semiconductors (Chapter 17 (in Japanese)).
