Chapter 63The Nonrelativistic Limit of the Dirac Equation — Pauli Equation, g=2, Fine Structure of Hydrogen, and Relativistic Effects in Heavy Elements

The Dirac equation of the previous chapter was an equation for a free electron. A real electron, however, is found in the Coulomb field of an atomic nucleus or in a magnetic field applied from outside. In this chapter we place the Dirac equation in an electromagnetic field and examine what comes out under the condition that the speed $v$ of the electron is much smaller than the speed of light $c$. The answer is the Schrödinger equation, which we already know, with several correction terms added. Moreover, these correction terms automatically contain, with their coefficients included, things that until now we have “added by hand as experimental facts”: the $g=2$ of the magnetic moment of the electron spin, and the factor $\tfrac12$ in the spin–orbit interaction (the Thomas factor). As long as we start from the Schrödinger equation, we have no choice but to add spin afterward (Chapter 23). The Dirac equation contains it from the beginning.

The route is as follows. First, we introduce the electromagnetic field into the Dirac equation by “minimal coupling” and check the invariance under gauge transformations (Section 63.1). Next, eliminating the smaller two of the four components gives the Pauli equation satisfied by the upper two components (the large components), and $g=2$ can be read off from its coefficients. For the Landau levels of an electron in a uniform magnetic field, we also see that, thanks to $g=2$, the zero-point energies cancel exactly (Section 63.2). To go one step further and obtain the corrections up to $1/c^2$, we need a systematic procedure called the Foldy–Wouthuysen transformation, and as its result the mass-velocity term, the Darwin term, and the spin–orbit term appear (Sections 63.3 and 63.4). Applying these three terms to the hydrogen atom gives fine-structure levels determined by $j$ alone, and these agree completely with the expansion of the result of solving the Dirac equation exactly (Section 63.5). Finally, in heavy elements with a large nuclear charge $Z$, the speed of the 1s electron reaches 50–70% of the speed of light and the relativistic effects become dramatically large; we learn that the yellow color of gold, the fact that mercury is a liquid at room temperature, and even most of the electromotive force of the lead-acid battery are explained by relativity (Section 63.6).

The content of this chapter is the foundation of the “scalar-relativistic treatment,” “spin–orbit coupling,” and “Dirac–Kohn–Sham equation” that appear in the companion volumes “Computational Materials Science” (AD 05), “Solid-State Physics” (AD 06), and “Density Functional Theory” (AD 07). Moreover, in the chapters that follow, “Negative-Energy Solutions and Antiparticles” (Chapter 64) and “A Bridge to Quantum Field Theory” (Chapter 65), the deviation from the $g=2$ obtained in this chapter ($g-2$) and the Lamb shift reappear as the first tests of quantum electrodynamics.

63.1 The Dirac Equation in an Electromagnetic Field — Minimal Coupling and Gauge Invariance

63.1.1 How to include the electromagnetic field: Minimal coupling

When a particle of charge $q$ and mass $m$ moves in an electromagnetic field, classical mechanics requires us to distinguish two kinds of quantity that go by the name of the particle's “momentum.” One is the kinetic momentum, which corresponds to $m\bm{v}$ (mass × velocity); the other is the canonical momentum $\bm{p}$ of the Hamiltonian formalism. The two are related through the vector potential $\bm{A}$ by $m\bm{v}=\bm{p}-q\bm{A}$ (Introductory University Physics, Chapter 58, Section 58.4 (in Japanese), Section 58.8 (in Japanese)). The Hamiltonian of a nonrelativistic particle, with the potential energy $V=q\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$ due to the electric potential $\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$ added, is

\begin{equation} H=\frac{(\bm{p}-q\bm{A})^2}{2m}+q\varphi_{\mathrm{el}} \label{eq:63-Hclass} \end{equation}

(the Lorentz force $q(\bm{E}+\bm{v}\times\bm{B})$ follows from this Hamiltonian; see Introductory University Physics, Chapter 39, Section 39.4 (in Japanese)). The situation is the same for a relativistic particle: the total energy and the momentum are replaced by $E\to E-q\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$ and $\bm{p}\to\bm{p}-q\bm{A}$ (in four-vector notation, $p^\mu\to p^\mu-qA^\mu$ with $A^\mu=(\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}/c,\bm{A})$). When we move on to quantum mechanics, we turn this into a replacement of operators.

Definition 63.1: Minimal coupling

When a particle of charge $q$ is in an electromagnetic field (vector potential $\bm{A}$, electric potential $\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$), the operators in the free-particle equation are replaced as follows.

\begin{equation} \hat{\bm{p}}\ \to\ \hat{\bm{\pi}}\equiv\hat{\bm{p}}-q\bm{A},\qquad i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\ \to\ i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-q\varphi_{\mathrm{el}} \label{eq:63-mincoup} \end{equation}

$\hat{\bm{\pi}}$ is called the operator of the kinetic momentum. The replacement does not include adding any new term that represents the coupling to the electromagnetic field. The coupling is determined entirely by this replacement alone.

Applying Eq. \eqref{eq:63-mincoup} to the Dirac equation of the previous chapter, $i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi=(c\,\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}+\beta mc^2)\psi$, we obtain

\begin{equation} i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=\Bigl[c\,\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}+\beta mc^2+V\Bigr]\psi,\qquad V\equiv q\varphi_{\mathrm{el}} \label{eq:63-dirac-em} \end{equation}

This is the Dirac equation in an electromagnetic field. $V(\bm{r},t)$ is the potential energy that a particle of charge $q$ has in the electric potential $\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$, and the Hamiltonian on the right-hand side is the Hermitian operator $c\,\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}+\beta mc^2+V$ (each component of $\hat{\bm{\pi}}$ is a Hermitian operator, and $V$ is a real function). Therefore, as in the previous chapter, the probability density $\rho=\psi^\dagger\psi$ is nonnegative, and the continuity equation $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\bm{j}=0$ ($\bm{j}=c\,\psi^\dagger\bm{\alpha}\psi$) holds.

This chapter deals with the electron. The charge of the electron is $q=-e_0$ ($e_0\gt0$ is the elementary charge), so

\begin{equation} \hat{\bm{\pi}}=\hat{\bm{p}}+e_0\bm{A},\qquad V=-e_0\varphi_{\mathrm{el}} \label{eq:63-electron} \end{equation}

For an electron in the Coulomb field of an atomic nucleus (charge $+Ze_0$), $\bm{A}=\bm{0}$ and $\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}=Ze_0/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r)$, and using the fine-structure constant $\alpha=e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c)$ we have

\begin{equation} V(r)=-\frac{Ze_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,r}=-\frac{Z\alpha\hbar c}{r} \label{eq:63-Vcoul} \end{equation}

This is the same as the equation in Chapter 61. In what follows, the mass $m$ is the electron mass $m_{\mathrm e}$ (we omit the subscript).

Why “minimal”? — The term Pauli added by hand is not needed here

More complicated couplings are conceivable. For example, one could arbitrarily add to $\hat{H}$ a term proportional to $\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}$. Minimal coupling is the simplest rule: it brings in the electromagnetic field only through the replacement of the momentum and the time derivative, without adding any such term. When the electromagnetic field and the spin are put into the Schrödinger equation, one first sets $\hat{\bm{p}}\to\hat{\bm{\pi}}$, and then Pauli (1927), on the assumption that “the electron has the magnetic moment $-g\mu_{\mathrm B}\hat{\bm{S}}/\hbar$,” added the Zeeman energy $\mu_{\mathrm B}\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}$ by hand (Chapter 23, Section 23.9). In the Dirac equation, this term, with its coefficient, comes out automatically from minimal coupling alone (Section 63.2). This is one of the reasons the Dirac equation is called “the equation for the electron.”

Sorting out the notation: Many similar symbols appear in this chapter

Many similar symbols appear in this chapter, so we sort them out first.

  • $\alpha_x,\alpha_y,\alpha_z$ and $\beta$ are the Dirac matrices ($\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_x,\alpha_y,\alpha_z)$, with subscripts). They are different quantities from the spin functions $\alpha=\ket{\uparrow}$ and $\beta=\ket{\downarrow}$, from the resonance integral $\beta$, and from the fine-structure constant $\alpha=e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c)\approx1/137$. So that they can be told apart by context, the Dirac matrices are always written with a boldface $\bm{\alpha}$ or with a subscript, $\alpha_i$, and the fine-structure constant appears in the forms “$Z\alpha$” and “$\alpha^2$.”
  • $\psi_A,\psi_B$: the upper two components (the large component) and the lower two components (the small component) of the four-component spinor. Each is a two-component column vector.
  • $\chi_s$: a constant two-component spinor ($\chi_\uparrow=(1,0)^{\mathsf T}$, $\chi_\downarrow=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}$). It is different from the spin orbital $\chi_i$ (Part VII) and from the gauge function $\chi(\bm{r},t)$ (in this section).
  • $\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$: the electric potential (it carries the subscript el). It is different from the azimuthal angle $\varphi$ and from the orbital $\phi$. $V=q\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$ is the potential energy due to the electric potential.
  • $\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z)$: the Pauli matrices. $\Sigma_k=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_k&0\\0&\sigma_k\end{pmatrix}$: the spin matrices extended to four components (previous chapter). $\hat{\bm{S}}=\hbar\bm{\sigma}/2$.
  • $E=mc^2+\varepsilon$: the total energy $E$, including the rest energy, and the nonrelativistic energy $\varepsilon$.

63.1.2 Block form of the stationary state

We consider the case in which the electromagnetic field does not depend on time ($\bm{A}$ and $\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$ do not contain $t$). Substituting the stationary state $\psi(\bm{r},t)=\psi(\bm{r})\,e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ into Eq. \eqref{eq:63-dirac-em}, we have $i\hbar\,\partial_t\to E$, and hence $(E-V)\psi=(c\,\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}+\beta mc^2)\psi$. We split this into 2×2 blocks, using Dirac's standard representation from the previous chapter (Section 62.2; it is also called the Dirac–Pauli representation), $\alpha_i=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma_i\\\sigma_i&0\end{pmatrix}$, $\beta=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{1}&0\\0&-\mathbb{1}\end{pmatrix}$, and the four-component spinor $\psi=(\psi_A,\psi_B)^{\mathsf T}$. Since $\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}\\\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}&0\end{pmatrix}$,

$$(E-V)\begin{pmatrix}\psi_A\\\psi_B\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}mc^2&c\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}\\c\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}&-mc^2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_A\\\psi_B\end{pmatrix}$$

Writing the upper and lower components separately and rearranging, we obtain

\begin{align} (E-V-mc^2)\,\psi_A&=c\,(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})\,\psi_B, \label{eq:63-blockA}\\ (E-V+mc^2)\,\psi_B&=c\,(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})\,\psi_A \label{eq:63-blockB} \end{align}

The factor $E-V-mc^2$ on the left-hand side of the first equation corresponds to the “kinetic energy” $\varepsilon-V$, with the rest energy subtracted. The factor $E-V+mc^2$ in the second equation becomes a large number, $\approx2mc^2$, if $\varepsilon\ll mc^2$. This asymmetry is the reason the small component can be eliminated in the next section. The block-form equations of Section 62.2.4 of the previous chapter are the case $\hat{\bm{\pi}}\to\hat{\bm{p}}$, $V=0$.

63.1.3 Gauge invariance

The electric and magnetic fields are determined from the potentials by $\bm{E}=-\nabla\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}-\partial_t\bm{A}$ and $\bm{B}=\nabla\times\bm{A}$, but the potentials themselves are not unique. Using an arbitrary scalar function $\chi(\bm{r},t)$ (the gauge function),

\begin{equation} \bm{A}\to\bm{A}'=\bm{A}+\nabla\chi,\qquad\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}\to\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}'=\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}-\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t} \label{eq:63-gauge-pot} \end{equation}

Even if the potentials are transformed in this way, $\bm{E}$ and $\bm{B}$ do not change ($\nabla\times\nabla\chi=\bm{0}$, $-\nabla(-\partial_t\chi)-\partial_t\nabla\chi=\bm{0}$). This is called a gauge transformation (in Introductory University Physics, Chapter 58, Section 58.5 (in Japanese) we wrote it as the transformation of the four-potential, $A_\mu\to A_\mu-\partial_\mu\chi$). Since physical predictions should be determined by $\bm{E}$ and $\bm{B}$ alone, the equations of quantum mechanics must express “the same physics” under this transformation. The following theorem shows that, for this purpose, it suffices to change the phase of the wave function at the same time.

Theorem 63.1: Gauge invariance of the Dirac equation

Let $\psi$ be a solution of the Dirac equation in an electromagnetic field, Eq. \eqref{eq:63-dirac-em}. If, together with the gauge transformation in Eq. \eqref{eq:63-gauge-pot}, we multiply by a phase,

\begin{equation} \psi\ \to\ \psi'=e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\,\psi \label{eq:63-gauge-psi} \end{equation}

then $\psi'$ is a solution of the Dirac equation for the transformed potentials $\bm{A}',\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}'$. That is, the form of the equation does not change.

Proof

What we need are the following two relations. Let $\bm{A}'=\bm{A}+\nabla\chi$. Acting with $\hat{\bm{p}}=-i\hbar\nabla$ on the phase factor $e^{iq\chi/\hbar}$, we find from the product rule

$$-i\hbar\nabla\bigl(e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\psi\bigr)=e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\bigl(q\,\psi\nabla\chi-i\hbar\nabla\psi\bigr)=e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\bigl(\hat{\bm{p}}+q\nabla\chi\bigr)\psi$$

Therefore, acting on $\psi'$ with the transformed kinetic momentum $\hat{\bm{\pi}}'=\hat{\bm{p}}-q\bm{A}'=\hat{\bm{p}}-q\bm{A}-q\nabla\chi$, we obtain

$$\hat{\bm{\pi}}'\psi'=e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\bigl(\hat{\bm{p}}+q\nabla\chi-q\bm{A}-q\nabla\chi\bigr)\psi=e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\,\hat{\bm{\pi}}\psi$$

The $\nabla\chi$ terms cancel each other, and only $\hat{\bm{\pi}}$ remains. The time part is the same. Since $i\hbar\,\partial_t(e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\psi)=e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\bigl(-q\,\partial_t\chi\,\psi+i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi\bigr)$, using $V'=q\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}'=V-q\,\partial_t\chi$ we have

$$\Bigl(i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-V'\Bigr)\psi'=e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\Bigl(-q\,\partial_t\chi+i\hbar\,\partial_t-V+q\,\partial_t\chi\Bigr)\psi=e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\Bigl(i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-V\Bigr)\psi$$

In other words, under $\psi\to e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\psi$, $\hat{\bm{\pi}}$ and $i\hbar\partial_t-V$ merely come out of the phase factor and do not change their form. If we write Eq. \eqref{eq:63-dirac-em} as $(i\hbar\,\partial_t-V)\psi=(c\,\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}+\beta mc^2)\psi$, then for $\psi'$ both the left-hand and the right-hand side are just the original ones multiplied by the phase factor $e^{iq\chi/\hbar}$ (the matrices $\bm{\alpha},\beta$ commute with the phase factor). Since the phase factor can be removed from both sides, $\psi'$ satisfies the Dirac equation for the transformed potentials.

∎

Since the phase factor $e^{iq\chi/\hbar}$ has absolute value 1, neither the probability density $\rho=\psi^\dagger\psi$ nor the probability current $\bm{j}=c\,\psi^\dagger\bm{\alpha}\psi$ changes under a gauge transformation. The phase of the wave function itself is not observable; the only things that can be observed are phase differences (interference) and gauge-invariant quantities. The Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\partial_t\psi=[\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2/2m+V]\psi$ is also invariant under the same gauge transformation, for the same reason.

Intuition: The freedom of phase demands the electromagnetic field

This theorem can also be read in the opposite direction. The Dirac equation of a free particle is invariant under the transformation $\psi\to e^{i\theta}\psi$ ($\theta$ is a constant), which changes the phase of the wave function simultaneously over all of spacetime. Suppose we demand that the phase $\theta$ be allowed to take a different value at each place and time, $\theta(\bm{r},t)=q\chi(\bm{r},t)/\hbar$ (that is, that local phase transformations be allowed). Then the derivative $\partial_\mu\psi$ contains an extra term coming from $\partial_\mu\theta$, so to preserve the form of the equation we need a “partner field” $A_\mu$ that cancels $\partial_\mu\theta$. That is the electromagnetic potential. The view of modern quantum field theory is that the existence of the electromagnetic field is a consequence of local phase freedom (local $U(1)$ symmetry) (Related simulator: Relativistic-QM explanation §4.1. For the development as a quantum field theory, see Chapter 65 and “Elementary Particle Physics” (AD 09 (in Japanese))).

63.1.4 Correspondence with the covariant form

In the previous chapter we wrote the Dirac equation in covariant form as $(i\hbar\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu-mc)\psi=0$. The covariant form with the electromagnetic field is obtained by replacing the partial derivative $\partial_\mu$ with the covariant derivative

\begin{equation} D_\mu=\partial_\mu+\frac{iq}{\hbar}A_\mu\qquad\Bigl(A_\mu=\bigl(\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}/c,\,-\bm{A}\bigr)\Bigr) \label{eq:63-covD} \end{equation}

which gives $i\hbar\gamma^\mu D_\mu\psi=mc\,\psi$. Let us check that this is the same as Eq. \eqref{eq:63-dirac-em}. We use the metric $(+,-,-,-)$ and $x^\mu=(ct,\bm{x})$, so that $\partial_0=\partial_t/c$, $\partial_i=\partial/\partial x^i$ ($i=1,2,3$), $A_0=\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}/c$, and $A_i=-A^i$ ($A^i$ is a component of $\bm{A}$). We also use $\gamma^0=\beta$ and $\gamma^i=\beta\alpha_i$.

Derivation: From the covariant form to Eq. \eqref{eq:63-dirac-em}

The time component is $D_0=\dfrac1c\Bigl(\partial_t+\dfrac{iq\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}}{\hbar}\Bigr)$, so $i\hbar\gamma^0D_0\psi=\dfrac{\beta}{c}\bigl(i\hbar\,\partial_t-q\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}\bigr)\psi$. The spatial components are $D_i=\partial_i-iqA^i/\hbar$, so $i\hbar D_i=i\hbar\partial_i+qA^i$. Here $\hat{p}_i=-i\hbar\partial_i$, so $i\hbar\partial_i=-\hat{p}_i$, and $i\hbar D_i=-(\hat{p}_i-qA^i)=-\hat{\pi}_i$. Therefore $i\hbar\gamma^iD_i\psi=-\gamma^i\hat{\pi}_i\psi$. Putting these together, the covariant form reads

$$\frac{\beta}{c}\bigl(i\hbar\,\partial_t-V\bigr)\psi-\gamma^i\hat{\pi}_i\,\psi=mc\,\psi$$

Multiply both sides by $c\beta$ from the left. Since $\beta^2=1$ and $\beta\gamma^i=\beta\beta\alpha_i=\alpha_i$, we obtain

$$\bigl(i\hbar\,\partial_t-V\bigr)\psi-c\,\alpha_i\hat{\pi}_i\psi=\beta mc^2\,\psi,\qquad\text{that is}\qquad i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi=\bigl[c\,\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}+\beta mc^2+V\bigr]\psi$$

which agrees with Eq. \eqref{eq:63-dirac-em}.

∎

Example 63.1: Covariant derivative and gauge transformation: deriving $A_\mu\to A_\mu-\partial_\mu\chi$

Under the local phase transformation of the wave function $\psi\to\psi'=e^{iq\chi(x)/\hbar}\psi$ ($\chi$ is a function of spacetime), find how $A_\mu$ must transform so that the covariant derivative $D_\mu\psi=(\partial_\mu+iqA_\mu/\hbar)\psi$ picks up only the same phase factor, $D'_\mu\psi'=e^{iq\chi/\hbar}D_\mu\psi$. Also show that the result agrees with the time component $\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}\to\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}-\partial_t\chi$ and the spatial component $\bm{A}\to\bm{A}+\nabla\chi$.

Solution. The transformed covariant derivative is $D'_\mu\psi'=(\partial_\mu+iqA'_\mu/\hbar)\bigl(e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\psi\bigr)$. By the product rule we obtain

$$D'_\mu\psi'=e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\Bigl(\partial_\mu\psi+\frac{iq}{\hbar}(\partial_\mu\chi)\,\psi+\frac{iq}{\hbar}A'_\mu\psi\Bigr)=e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\Bigl(\partial_\mu+\frac{iq}{\hbar}\bigl(A'_\mu+\partial_\mu\chi\bigr)\Bigr)\psi$$

For this to equal $e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\bigl(\partial_\mu+iqA_\mu/\hbar\bigr)\psi$, we need $A'_\mu+\partial_\mu\chi=A_\mu$; that is, we must have

$$A'_\mu=A_\mu-\partial_\mu\chi$$

Now we read this in components. For $\mu=0$: $A_0=\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}/c$ and $\partial_0\chi=\partial_t\chi/c$, so $\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}'/c=\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}/c-\partial_t\chi/c$, that is, $\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}'=\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}-\partial_t\chi$. For $\mu=i$: $A_i=-A^i$, so $-A'^i=-A^i-\partial_i\chi$, that is, $A'^i=A^i+\partial_i\chi$, which in vector form is $\bm{A}'=\bm{A}+\nabla\chi$. Both agree with Eq. \eqref{eq:63-gauge-pot}. Dimension check: for $q\chi/\hbar$ to be dimensionless, the unit of $\chi$ must be $\mathrm{J\,s/C}=\mathrm{V\,s}$ (Wb), and the unit of $\nabla\chi$, $\mathrm{V\,s/m}$, agrees with the unit of $\bm{A}$.

63.2 Large and Small Components — The Pauli Equation and $g=2$

63.2.1 Eliminating the small component

The block forms in the previous section, Eqs. \eqref{eq:63-blockA} and \eqref{eq:63-blockB}, are simultaneous equations for $\psi_A$ and $\psi_B$. If we solve the second equation for $\psi_B$, we can express $\psi_B$ in terms of $\psi_A$. Since $E-V+mc^2$ is a function of position (and is real), we need only multiply by its reciprocal:

\begin{equation} \psi_B=\frac{1}{E-V+mc^2}\;c\,(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})\,\psi_A \label{eq:63-psiB-exact} \end{equation}

(First apply $\hat{\bm{\pi}}$, and then multiply by the function of position $1/(E-V+mc^2)$.) Substituting this into Eq. \eqref{eq:63-blockA} gives the equation for $\psi_A$ alone,

$$(E-V-mc^2)\,\psi_A=c\,(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})\,\frac{1}{E-V+mc^2}\,c\,(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})\,\psi_A$$

So far this is an exact equation that contains no approximation, but the unknown eigenvalue $E$ appears in the equation (in the denominator).

When the electron is slow—that is, when the nonrelativistic energy $\varepsilon=E-mc^2$ and the potential energy $V$ are sufficiently small compared with the rest energy ($\abs{\varepsilon-V}\ll mc^2$)—the denominator can be approximated as $E-V+mc^2=2mc^2+(\varepsilon-V)\approx2mc^2$. Then Eq. \eqref{eq:63-psiB-exact} becomes

\begin{equation} \psi_B\ \approx\ \frac{\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}}{2mc}\,\psi_A \label{eq:63-psiB} \end{equation}

The magnitude of $\hat{\bm{\pi}}\,\psi_A$ is of the order of the magnitude of the kinetic momentum $m\bm{v}$, so $\abs{\psi_B}/\abs{\psi_A}\sim mv/(2mc)$, that is, the ratio is

\begin{equation} \frac{\abs{\psi_B}}{\abs{\psi_A}}\sim\frac{v}{2c} \label{eq:63-ratio} \end{equation}

(This is the same as the ratio $pc/(E_p+mc^2)\approx v/2c$ for the plane-wave solution in the previous chapter; Section 62.5.3.) This is why $\psi_A$ is called the large component and $\psi_B$ the small component. The contribution to the normalization is $\abs{\psi_B}^2/\abs{\psi_A}^2\sim(v/2c)^2$, which is very small, $1.3\times10^{-5}$, for the 1s electron of the hydrogen atom ($v\approx\alpha c\approx c/137$). For core electrons of atoms with a large nuclear charge, however, it cannot be neglected (Section 63.6).

63.2.2 Computing $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2$

Substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:63-psiB} into the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-blockA} and using $\varepsilon=E-mc^2$, we obtain

\begin{equation} (\varepsilon-V)\,\psi_A\ \approx\ \frac{(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2}{2m}\,\psi_A \label{eq:63-psiA-lead} \end{equation}

Now we compute the operator $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2$ on the right-hand side. This is the most important calculation in this section. The only ingredient is the product formula for the Pauli matrices.

Formula 63.1: Products of the Pauli matrices

The Pauli matrices $\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z$ (with indices $i,j,k=1,2,3$) satisfy the following product relation (Chapter 23, Section 23.7, Chapter 24, Section 24.8).

\begin{equation} \sigma_i\sigma_j=\delta_{ij}\,\mathbb{1}+i\sum_{k}\epsilon_{ijk}\,\sigma_k \label{eq:63-sigprod} \end{equation}

Here $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is the Levi-Civita symbol ($\epsilon_{123}=1$; it changes sign when two indices are exchanged, and it is $0$ if any index appears twice). For example, $\sigma_x\sigma_y=i\sigma_z$, $\sigma_y\sigma_x=-i\sigma_z$, and $\sigma_x^2=\mathbb{1}$.

We square $\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}=\sum_i\sigma_i\hat{\pi}_i$. The Pauli matrices $\sigma_i$ are constant matrices and do not act on position or on differentiation, so they commute with the operator $\hat{\pi}_j$ ($\sigma_i\hat{\pi}_j=\hat{\pi}_j\sigma_i$). However, the order of $\hat{\pi}_i$ and $\hat{\pi}_j$ must be preserved.

\begin{align} (\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2&=\sum_{i,j}\sigma_i\sigma_j\,\hat{\pi}_i\hat{\pi}_j =\sum_{i,j}\Bigl(\delta_{ij}+i\sum_k\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k\Bigr)\hat{\pi}_i\hat{\pi}_j \notag\\ &=\sum_i\hat{\pi}_i\hat{\pi}_i+i\sum_k\sigma_k\sum_{i,j}\epsilon_{ijk}\,\hat{\pi}_i\hat{\pi}_j =\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2+i\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bigl(\hat{\bm{\pi}}\times\hat{\bm{\pi}}\bigr) \label{eq:63-sigpi-step} \end{align}

In the last step we used the component of the vector product, $(\hat{\bm{\pi}}\times\hat{\bm{\pi}})_k=\sum_{i,j}\epsilon_{kij}\hat{\pi}_i\hat{\pi}_j$ ($\epsilon_{ijk}=\epsilon_{kij}$). For an ordinary vector, $\bm{a}\times\bm{a}=\bm{0}$, but for operators $\hat{\pi}_i\hat{\pi}_j$ and $\hat{\pi}_j\hat{\pi}_i$ are not necessarily equal; since $\epsilon_{kij}$ is antisymmetric in $i,j$, we have

$$(\hat{\bm{\pi}}\times\hat{\bm{\pi}})_k=\frac12\sum_{i,j}\epsilon_{kij}\bigl(\hat{\pi}_i\hat{\pi}_j-\hat{\pi}_j\hat{\pi}_i\bigr)=\frac12\sum_{i,j}\epsilon_{kij}\,\comm{\hat{\pi}_i}{\hat{\pi}_j}$$

so it can be written in terms of commutators. Let us find the commutator of the $\hat{\pi}_i$ with one another. With $\hat{p}_i=-i\hbar\,\partial_i$, for any function $f$ we have $\comm{\hat{p}_i}{A_j}f=-i\hbar\,\partial_i(A_jf)+i\hbar\,A_j\partial_if=-i\hbar\,(\partial_iA_j)\,f$, so $\comm{\hat{p}_i}{A_j}=-i\hbar\,\partial_iA_j$. Therefore (noting that $\comm{\hat{p}_i}{\hat{p}_j}=0$ and $\comm{A_i}{A_j}=0$)

\begin{align} \comm{\hat{\pi}_i}{\hat{\pi}_j}&=\comm{\hat{p}_i-qA_i}{\hat{p}_j-qA_j}=-q\comm{\hat{p}_i}{A_j}-q\comm{A_i}{\hat{p}_j} =-q\bigl(-i\hbar\,\partial_iA_j\bigr)-q\bigl(i\hbar\,\partial_jA_i\bigr) \notag\\ &=i\hbar q\bigl(\partial_iA_j-\partial_jA_i\bigr)=i\hbar q\sum_l\epsilon_{ijl}B_l \label{eq:63-pipi} \end{align}

This gives the commutator (we used $\comm{A_i}{\hat{p}_j}=-\comm{\hat{p}_j}{A_i}=i\hbar\,\partial_jA_i$. The last step uses the components of $\bm{B}=\nabla\times\bm{A}$, for example $B_z=\partial_xA_y-\partial_yA_x$; University Mathematics, Chapter 19, Section 19.4). Using this,

$$(\hat{\bm{\pi}}\times\hat{\bm{\pi}})_k=\frac12\sum_{i,j}\epsilon_{kij}\,i\hbar q\sum_l\epsilon_{ijl}B_l=\frac{i\hbar q}{2}\sum_l\Bigl(\sum_{i,j}\epsilon_{kij}\epsilon_{ijl}\Bigr)B_l=\frac{i\hbar q}{2}\sum_l2\delta_{kl}B_l=i\hbar q\,B_k$$

This is the result (we used $\sum_{i,j}\epsilon_{kij}\epsilon_{lij}=2\delta_{kl}$). That is, $\hat{\bm{\pi}}\times\hat{\bm{\pi}}=i\hbar q\,\bm{B}$, and substituting this into Eq. \eqref{eq:63-sigpi-step} gives $i\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot(i\hbar q\bm{B})=-q\hbar\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}$.

Theorem 63.2: Identity for $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2$

\begin{equation} (\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2=\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2-q\hbar\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B},\qquad\hat{\bm{\pi}}\times\hat{\bm{\pi}}=i\hbar q\,\bm{B},\qquad\bm{B}=\nabla\times\bm{A} \label{eq:63-sigpi2} \end{equation}

$\bm{A}$ may be any vector potential (it may depend on position and time).

Why does the magnetic field appear? The components of the kinetic momentum no longer commute

For a free particle ($\bm{A}=\bm{0}$), $\hat{\bm{p}}\times\hat{\bm{p}}=\bm{0}$ and $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}})^2=\hat{\bm{p}}^2$. The spin does not affect the kinetic energy (in Section 62.2.4 of the previous chapter we used $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{p})^2=p^2$). With a magnetic field, however, the $x$ and $y$ components of the kinetic momentum no longer commute ($\comm{\hat{\pi}_x}{\hat{\pi}_y}=i\hbar qB_z\ne0$). As a result, a term $-q\hbar\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}$, in which the anticommutativity of the matrices ($\sigma_x\sigma_y=-\sigma_y\sigma_x$) and the commutator are combined, survives in the square of $\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}$. The coupling between spin and magnetic field is a by-product of this “noncommutativity.”

Example 63.2: Computing $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2$ for a uniform magnetic field in components. The Zeeman energy and ESR

(1) For a uniform magnetic field $\bm{B}=(0,0,B)$ with vector potential $\bm{A}=\tfrac12\bm{B}\times\bm{r}=(-By/2,\,Bx/2,\,0)$, write down $\hat{\pi}_x,\hat{\pi}_y,\hat{\pi}_z$, and compute $\comm{\hat{\pi}_x}{\hat{\pi}_y}$ and $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2$ in components. (2) Find the electron's Zeeman energy $\mu_{\mathrm B}B$ at $B=1\ \mathrm{T}$, the absorption frequency $2\mu_{\mathrm B}B/h$ (in $\mathrm{GHz}$) when the electron spin flips, and the resonance field for $X$-band ESR ($9.5\ \mathrm{GHz}$) with $g=2.0023$.

Solution. (1) We have $\hat{\pi}_x=\hat{p}_x-qA_x=\hat{p}_x+\tfrac12qBy$, $\hat{\pi}_y=\hat{p}_y-qA_y=\hat{p}_y-\tfrac12qBx$, and $\hat{\pi}_z=\hat{p}_z$. For the commutator, noting that $\comm{\hat{p}_x}{x}=-i\hbar$ and $\comm{y}{\hat{p}_y}=i\hbar$, we get

$$\comm{\hat{\pi}_x}{\hat{\pi}_y}=\comm{\hat{p}_x}{-\tfrac12qBx}+\comm{\tfrac12qBy}{\hat{p}_y}=-\tfrac12qB(-i\hbar)+\tfrac12qB(i\hbar)=i\hbar qB$$

which agrees with $i\hbar q\epsilon_{xyz}B_z=i\hbar qB$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:63-pipi}. We also have $\comm{\hat{\pi}_x}{\hat{\pi}_z}=\comm{\hat{\pi}_y}{\hat{\pi}_z}=0$ ($\hat{\pi}_z=\hat{p}_z$ acts neither on $x,y$ nor on $\hat{p}_x,\hat{p}_y$). Next we expand $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2=\bigl(\sigma_x\hat{\pi}_x+\sigma_y\hat{\pi}_y+\sigma_z\hat{\pi}_z\bigr)^2$. The diagonal terms give $\sigma_x^2\hat{\pi}_x^2+\sigma_y^2\hat{\pi}_y^2+\sigma_z^2\hat{\pi}_z^2=\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2$. Among the off-diagonal terms, the $(x,y)$ pair gives $\sigma_x\sigma_y\hat{\pi}_x\hat{\pi}_y+\sigma_y\sigma_x\hat{\pi}_y\hat{\pi}_x=i\sigma_z\hat{\pi}_x\hat{\pi}_y-i\sigma_z\hat{\pi}_y\hat{\pi}_x=i\sigma_z\comm{\hat{\pi}_x}{\hat{\pi}_y}=i\sigma_z(i\hbar qB)=-\hbar qB\,\sigma_z$, while the $(x,z)$ and $(y,z)$ pairs have commutators equal to $0$, so they vanish, also using $\sigma_x\sigma_z+\sigma_z\sigma_x=0$ (anticommutation). Hence

$$(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2=\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2-\hbar qB\,\sigma_z=\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2-q\hbar\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}$$

which agrees with Theorem 63.2.

(2) For the electron, $q=-e_0$, and the Zeeman term is $-(q\hbar/2m)\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}=+\mu_{\mathrm B}\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}$ (Section 63.2.4, which follows), so for $\bm{B}\parallel z$ the eigenvalues are $\pm\mu_{\mathrm B}B$. Since $\mu_{\mathrm B}=9.274\,010\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/T}=5.788\,382\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/T}$, at $B=1\ \mathrm{T}$ we get $\mu_{\mathrm B}B=57.88\ \mu\mathrm{eV}$. The spacing between the two levels $\pm\mu_{\mathrm B}B$ is $2\mu_{\mathrm B}B=115.77\ \mu\mathrm{eV}$, and the frequency of the absorbed electromagnetic wave is $\nu=2\mu_{\mathrm B}B/h=2\times9.274\,010\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/T}\,/\,6.626\,070\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}=2.799\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{Hz/T}=27.99\ \mathrm{GHz/T}$ (microwaves). For $X$ band at $9.5\ \mathrm{GHz}$, the resonance condition $h\nu=g\mu_{\mathrm B}B$ of a free electron with $g=2.0023$ gives $B=\dfrac{h\nu}{g\mu_{\mathrm B}}=\dfrac{9.5\times10^9\times6.626\,070\times10^{-34}}{2.0023\times9.274\,010\times10^{-24}}\ \mathrm{T}=0.3390\ \mathrm{T}$ (with $g=2$ one obtains $0.3394\ \mathrm{T}$; the difference of $0.1\ \%$ is due to the deviation of $g$ from $2$).

63.2.3 The Pauli equation

Using Theorem 63.2 in Eq. \eqref{eq:63-psiA-lead}, we obtain $(\varepsilon-V)\psi_A=\bigl[\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2-q\hbar\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}\bigr]\psi_A/2m$, that is, $\varepsilon\,\psi_A=\bigl[\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2/2m+V-(q\hbar/2m)\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}\bigr]\psi_A$. The nonrelativistic energy $\varepsilon$ corresponds to $i\hbar\,\partial_t$ of the time-dependent equation (the time dependence after the rest-energy phase $e^{-imc^2t/\hbar}$ has been removed from the wave function). In this way, we obtain the following equation.

Theorem 63.3: Pauli equation

As the nonrelativistic limit (lowest order) of the Dirac equation in an electromagnetic field, the two-component wave function $\psi_{\mathrm P}$ (the Pauli spinor) satisfies the following equation.

\begin{equation} i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi_{\mathrm P}}{\partial t}=\Bigl[\frac{(\hat{\bm{p}}-q\bm{A})^2}{2m}+q\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}-\frac{q\hbar}{2m}\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}\Bigr]\psi_{\mathrm P} \label{eq:63-pauli} \end{equation}

For the electron ($q=-e_0$), the last term of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-pauli} is $+\dfrac{e_0\hbar}{2m}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}$. At this lowest order, $\psi_{\mathrm P}$ is just the large component $\psi_A$ itself. As we will see in the next section, when we go on to order $1/c^2$, $\psi_{\mathrm P}$ is replaced by the quantity obtained by adding to $\psi_A$ a correction of $\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2/8m^2c^2$ (in order to keep the normalization correct). The Pauli equation was proposed by Pauli in 1927 by “assuming” that the electron has a spin magnetic moment and adding it to the Schrödinger equation. When Dirac derived the four-component equation in 1928, it turned out that Pauli's equation emerges as its nonrelativistic limit without any assumption.

63.2.4 $g=2$ and the Bohr magneton

The energy of a particle with magnetic moment $\bm{\mu}$ in a magnetic flux density $\bm{B}$ is $-\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{B}$ (Introductory University Physics, Chapter 38, Section 38.6 (in Japanese)). The magnetic moment of a particle with spin $\hat{\bm{S}}=\hbar\bm{\sigma}/2$ is defined by means of a number $g$, the $g$ factor, as

\begin{equation} \bm{\mu}=g\,\frac{q}{2m}\,\hat{\bm{S}} \label{eq:63-gdef} \end{equation}

For a classical circular current (a rotating charge), the orbital angular momentum $\bm{L}$ and the magnetic moment are related by $\bm{\mu}_l=(q/2m)\bm{L}$ ($g=1$, Chapter 23, Section 23.1), so $g$ is a number that expresses “how many times larger the spin magnetic moment is than in the case of orbital motion.” Using $\hbar\bm{\sigma}=2\hat{\bm{S}}$, the last term of the Pauli equation is

$$-\frac{q\hbar}{2m}\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}=-\frac{q}{m}\,\hat{\bm{S}}\cdot\bm{B}=-\Bigl(2\cdot\frac{q}{2m}\hat{\bm{S}}\Bigr)\cdot\bm{B}$$

Comparing this with $-\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{B}$, the $g$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:63-gdef} can be read as

\begin{equation} g=2 \label{eq:63-g2} \end{equation}

Within the framework of the Schrödinger equation, the value $g=2$ for the spin had to be put in by hand “as an experimental fact,” from the Stern–Gerlach experiment and the anomalous Zeeman effect (Chapter 23, Section 23.2). The Dirac equation derives it from minimal coupling alone.

Derivation: The orbital magnetic moment has $g=1$ (for comparison)

We take the vector potential of a uniform magnetic field $\bm{B}$ to be $\bm{A}=\tfrac12\bm{B}\times\bm{r}$ (one can check that $\nabla\times\bm{A}=\bm{B}$ and $\nabla\cdot\bm{A}=0$). Since $\nabla\cdot\bm{A}=0$, we have $\hat{\bm{p}}\cdot\bm{A}=\bm{A}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}$, so $\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2=\hat{\bm{p}}^2-2q\,\bm{A}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}+q^2\bm{A}^2$. By the property of the scalar triple product, $\bm{A}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}=\tfrac12(\bm{B}\times\bm{r})\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}=\tfrac12\bm{B}\cdot(\bm{r}\times\hat{\bm{p}})=\tfrac12\bm{B}\cdot\hat{\bm{L}}$, and therefore

$$\frac{\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2}{2m}=\frac{\hat{\bm{p}}^2}{2m}-\frac{q}{2m}\,\bm{B}\cdot\hat{\bm{L}}+\frac{q^2}{8m}(\bm{B}\times\bm{r})^2$$

The second term is the coupling to the orbital magnetic moment $\bm{\mu}_l=(q/2m)\hat{\bm{L}}$ ($g_l=1$), and the third term is a diamagnetic term proportional to the square of the magnetic field. The Zeeman Hamiltonian including both spin and orbit is, for an arbitrary charge $q$, $-\dfrac{q}{2m}(\hat{\bm{L}}+2\hat{\bm{S}})\cdot\bm{B}$, and for the electron it is $+\dfrac{\mu_{\mathrm B}}{\hbar}(\hat{\bm{L}}+2\hat{\bm{S}})\cdot\bm{B}$ (the same as in Chapter 28, Section 28.5).

∎

Let us collect the numbers for the electron. The magnetic moment of the electron is $\bm{\mu}=-g\,\mu_{\mathrm B}\,\hat{\bm{S}}/\hbar$, and the Bohr magneton is

\begin{equation} \mu_{\mathrm B}=\frac{e_0\hbar}{2m_{\mathrm e}}=9.274\,010\,0783\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/T}=5.788\,381\,8060\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/T}=13.996\,245\ \mathrm{GHz/T} \label{eq:63-muB} \end{equation}

(the last value is $\mu_{\mathrm B}/h$). Because of the negative charge, the magnetic moment points in the direction opposite to the spin. In this book, the spin arrow $\uparrow$ denotes $s_z=+\hbar/2$ (see the following Caution).

Caution: The meaning of “↑” — different from “parallel to the magnetic field” in Introductory University Physics

In this book, the spin arrow $\uparrow$ means “the $z$ component of the spin is $+\hbar/2$” (the eigenvalue $+\hbar/2$ of $\hat{S}_z$; the spin function $\alpha=\ket{\uparrow}$). Because the magnetic moment of the electron is opposite in direction to the spin, in a magnetic field $\bm{B}=B\bm{e}_z$ an electron with $\uparrow$ has its magnetic moment pointing in the $-z$ direction (antiparallel to the field), and its energy is higher, $+\mu_{\mathrm B}B$. The energy of an electron with $\downarrow$ is lower, $-\mu_{\mathrm B}B$ (of the $\pm\mu_{\mathrm B}B$ in Example 63.2, the $+$ belongs to $\uparrow$). In Law 51.3 of the companion volume AD 03 (Introductory University Physics, Chapter 51, Section 51.4 (in Japanese)), the “$\uparrow$” in $E_\uparrow=-\mu B$, $E_\downarrow=+\mu B$ means that the magnetic moment is parallel to the magnetic field; translated into the spin of the electron, it corresponds to the $\downarrow$ of this book. In either notation the physics is the same: the state of lower energy has a magnetic moment parallel to the magnetic field.

The experimentally measured $g$ factor of the electron is $g_{\mathrm e}=2.002\,319\,304\ldots$, which deviates from Dirac's prediction $g=2$ by only about $0.12\ \%$. This deviation is due to the radiative correction of quantum electrodynamics, in which the electron interacts with the photons (virtual photons) that it emits itself, and Schwinger (1948) calculated the lowest-order correction as $g/2=1+\alpha/2\pi+\cdots=1.00116\ldots$ (Chapter 65). The proton ($g_{\mathrm p}=5.586$; its magnetic moment in units of the nuclear magneton is $2.793\,\mu_{\mathrm N}$) and the neutron ($g_{\mathrm n}=-3.826$) deviate greatly from $g=2$. This is because the proton and the neutron are not point particles but have an internal structure made of quarks; the very fact that the uncharged neutron has a magnetic moment is a manifestation of that internal structure (treated in Elementary Particle Physics, AD 09, Chapter 7, Section 7.2 (in Japanese) and elsewhere). The electron's $g=2$ and its nonrelativistic limit are also derived in elementary particle physics (AD 09, Chapter 9, Section 9.3 (in Japanese)).

Example 63.3: Magnitude of the small component: the 1s electron in hydrogen and gold

Using the speed $v\approx Z\alpha c$ (the speed for $n=1$ in the Bohr model), the magnitude of the small component of a 1s electron can be estimated from Eq. \eqref{eq:63-ratio} as $\abs{\psi_B}/\abs{\psi_A}\sim Z\alpha/2$. Compute this estimate for hydrogen ($Z=1$), gold ($Z=79$), and uranium ($Z=92$), and compare it with the exact ratio $\sqrt{(1-\gamma)/(1+\gamma)}$ ($\gamma=\sqrt{1-(Z\alpha)^2}$) for the 1s solution, which will be derived in Section 63.5, and with the weight $(1-\gamma)/2$. ($\alpha=1/137.036$)

Solution. Hydrogen: $Z\alpha/2=7.2974\times10^{-3}/2=3.649\times10^{-3}$. For the exact ratio, from $\gamma=\sqrt{1-(7.2974\times10^{-3})^2}=0.999\,973$ we get $\sqrt{(1-\gamma)/(1+\gamma)}=\sqrt{2.663\times10^{-5}/1.999\,973}=3.649\times10^{-3}$, which agrees with the estimate to four digits. The weight is $(1-\gamma)/2=1.33\times10^{-5}$ ($=(3.649\times10^{-3})^2$, approximately).

Gold: $Z\alpha=0.5765$, and the estimate is $Z\alpha/2=0.288$. Exactly, $\gamma=\sqrt{1-0.3323}=0.8171$, and the ratio is $\sqrt{0.1829/1.8171}=0.3173$, which is about 10% larger than the estimate. The weight is $(1-\gamma)/2=0.0915$ (9.1%). Uranium: $Z\alpha=0.6714$, estimate $0.336$, exact ratio $0.3856$ ($\gamma=0.7411$), weight $0.129$ (12.9%). The estimate is accurate for $Z\alpha\ll1$, and as $Z\alpha$ approaches $1$ it underestimates the magnitude of $\psi_B$ (because the denominator $E-V+mc^2$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:63-psiB-exact} becomes smaller than $2mc^2$). In hydrogen the small component is only $10^{-5}$ of the whole, whereas in gold and uranium it reaches roughly one-tenth.

63.2.5 An electron in a uniform magnetic field — Landau levels and the disappearance of the zero-point energy due to $g=2$

The most basic application of the Pauli equation is an electron in a uniform magnetic field $\bm{B}=B\bm{e}_z$. Here there is no electric potential ($V=0$), and the charge is $q=-e_0$. The Hamiltonian is $\hat{H}=\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2/2m+\mu_{\mathrm B}B\,\sigma_z$ ($g=2$), and the orbital part ($\hat{\pi}_x,\hat{\pi}_y$) and the spin part ($\sigma_z$) can be diagonalized independently. The orbital part is the quantization of the electron's circular motion (cyclotron motion), namely the Landau levels.

Derivation: Landau levels of the Pauli equation

From Theorem 63.2 (or Eq. \eqref{eq:63-pipi}), $\comm{\hat{\pi}_x}{\hat{\pi}_y}=i\hbar qB=-i\hbar e_0B$. This is a commutation relation of the same form as $\comm{x}{\hat{p}}=i\hbar$, and it can be solved just like the harmonic oscillator (Chapter 13, Section 13.8). We define the ladder operators as

$$\hat{a}=\frac{\hat{\pi}_x-i\hat{\pi}_y}{\sqrt{2\hbar e_0B}},\qquad\hat{a}^\dagger=\frac{\hat{\pi}_x+i\hat{\pi}_y}{\sqrt{2\hbar e_0B}}$$

Then $\comm{\hat{a}}{\hat{a}^\dagger}=\dfrac{i\comm{\hat{\pi}_x}{\hat{\pi}_y}-i\comm{\hat{\pi}_y}{\hat{\pi}_x}}{2\hbar e_0B}=\dfrac{2i(-i\hbar e_0B)}{2\hbar e_0B}=1$. Also, $(\hat{\pi}_x-i\hat{\pi}_y)(\hat{\pi}_x+i\hat{\pi}_y)=\hat{\pi}_x^2+\hat{\pi}_y^2+i\comm{\hat{\pi}_x}{\hat{\pi}_y}=\hat{\pi}_x^2+\hat{\pi}_y^2+\hbar e_0B$, and the left-hand side is $2\hbar e_0B\,\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger$, so

$$\hat{\pi}_x^2+\hat{\pi}_y^2=2\hbar e_0B\,\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger-\hbar e_0B=\hbar e_0B\,(2\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+1)$$

(using $\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger=\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+1$). With the cyclotron angular frequency $\omega_c=e_0B/m$, this reads $\dfrac{\hat{\pi}_x^2+\hat{\pi}_y^2}{2m}=\hbar\omega_c\left(\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+\tfrac12\right)$. Therefore (including the free motion $\hat{p}_z^2/2m$ in the $z$ direction) we have

$$\hat{H}=\hbar\omega_c\left(\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+\tfrac12\right)+\frac{\hat{p}_z^2}{2m}+\mu_{\mathrm B}B\,\sigma_z,\qquad\mu_{\mathrm B}B=\frac{e_0\hbar}{2m}B=\frac{\hbar\omega_c}{2}$$

The last equality is a consequence of $g=2$. The eigenvalues of $\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}$ are $n_L=0,1,2,\dots$, while $\sigma_z=\pm1$, so for $p_z=0$ we obtain

\begin{equation} E=\hbar\omega_c\Bigl(n_L+\frac12\Bigr)+\sigma_z\,\frac{\hbar\omega_c}{2}=\hbar\omega_c\Bigl(n_L+\frac{1+\sigma_z}{2}\Bigr)\equiv n\,\hbar\omega_c \label{eq:63-landau} \end{equation}

Here we have set $n=n_L+(1+\sigma_z)/2$. For $\sigma_z=-1$ (spin $\downarrow$) we have $n=n_L$, and for $\sigma_z=+1$ (spin $\uparrow$) we have $n=n_L+1$.

∎

Theorem 63.4: Landau levels of the Pauli equation ($g=2$)

The eigenvalues ($p_z=0$) of the Pauli equation for an electron in a uniform magnetic field $B$ are

\begin{equation} E_n=n\,\hbar\omega_c\quad(n=0,1,2,\dots),\qquad\omega_c=\frac{e_0B}{m} \end{equation}

The level $n=0$ is nondegenerate (only spin $\downarrow$ with $n_L=0$), and each level with $n\ge1$ is twofold degenerate (spin $\downarrow$ with $n_L=n$, and spin $\uparrow$ with $n_L=n-1$). What we have counted here as “degeneracy” is only the combinations of spin and $n_L$. Each level has, in addition, an enormous degeneracy associated with the position of the center of the circular motion (the Caution following Figure 63.2).

The Landau levels $(n_L+\tfrac12)\hbar\omega_c$ of the orbital motion alone have a zero-point energy $\tfrac12\hbar\omega_c$, just as the harmonic oscillator does. However, the spin energy $\pm\hbar\omega_c/2$ for $g=2$ is exactly the same size as that, so in the lowest state with spin $\downarrow$ the zero-point energy is completely canceled and $E=0$ (Figure 63.2). If $g$ were not $2$, the levels would not line up so neatly. For the actual electron, $g=2.0023$, so the levels deviate from $n\hbar\omega_c$ by about $\pm0.0006\,\hbar\omega_c$, depending on the direction of the spin.

Caution: Landau levels have an enormous degeneracy due to the position of the center of the circular motion

The “1-fold” and “2-fold” in Figure 63.2 merely count the combinations of spin and $n_L$. An actual Landau level also has, separately from that, an enormous degeneracy associated with the position of the center of the circular motion (the guiding center). The radius of the circular motion of an electron in a magnetic field is of the order of the magnetic length $l_B=\sqrt{\hbar/(e_0B)}$, and the guiding-center coordinates $X,Y$ are canonically conjugate to each other, like $x$ and $\hat{p}_x$ ($\abs{\comm{X}{Y}}=l_B^2$), so they cannot be determined simultaneously; hence only one state can be distinguished per area $2\pi l_B^2$ of the $X$–$Y$ plane. Therefore, the number of states in a single Landau level (with the spin direction specified) per unit area is given by

\begin{equation} \frac{1}{2\pi l_B^2}=\frac{e_0B}{2\pi\hbar}=\frac{e_0B}{h} \label{eq:63-LLdeg} \end{equation}

For $B=10\ \mathrm{T}$, $l_B=8.11\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $e_0B/h=2.42\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}}$ ($2.42\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{cm^{-2}}$). In a sample of area $S$, $e_0BS/h$ states line up in a single level. The stronger the magnetic field, the larger the degeneracy, and the more electrons a single level can hold.

Application: Cyclotron resonance and the quantum Hall effect — experiments on Landau levels

For an electron in a solid, replacing the mass $m$ by the effective mass $m^*$ gives $\omega_c=e_0B/m^*$ (for the effective mass, see Chapter 41). For conduction electrons in GaAs, $m^*\approx0.067\,m_{\mathrm e}$, so at $B=10\ \mathrm{T}$ we have $\hbar\omega_c=1.158\ \mathrm{meV}/0.067=17.3\ \mathrm{meV}$ (about $200\ \mathrm{K}$), roughly 15 times larger than for a free electron. The phenomenon in which far-infrared light matching this spacing is absorbed is cyclotron resonance, and $m^*$ can be measured from the resonance frequency.

When a two-dimensional electron system formed at a heterojunction interface (such as GaAs/AlGaAs) is placed at low temperature and in a strong magnetic field, the energy gap $\hbar\omega_c$ between Landau levels comes into play. Let $n_s$ be the areal density of electrons. By Eq. \eqref{eq:63-LLdeg}, when $n_s=N\,e_0B/h$ ($N$ is an integer), exactly $N$ levels are filled and the Fermi level lies between levels. Then the longitudinal resistance becomes $0$, and the Hall resistance forms a flat part (a plateau) at the value $h/(Ne_0^2)$, which is independent of the material. This is the integer quantum Hall effect (von Klitzing, 1980). The value $h/e_0^2=25\,812.807\ \Omega$ is used as a resistance standard. In real samples, the plateaus persist over a wide range of magnetic field because of localized states due to impurities. The Landau levels of graphene proportional to $\sqrt{B}$ (the limit of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-dirac-landau} below with the mass set to $0$) are also seen in the same kind of experiment.

In the case of the Dirac equation, an exact solution is possible with all four components kept. The method that follows is simply to square the Hamiltonian.

Derivation: Exact Landau levels of the Dirac equation

For a static magnetic field with $V=0$ and $\bm{B}=B\bm{e}_z$, we square $\hat{H}=c\,\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}+\beta mc^2$. Since $\alpha_i\beta+\beta\alpha_i=0$ and $\hat{\bm{\pi}}$ commutes with $\beta$, the cross term $mc^3(\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}\,\beta+\beta\,\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})=0$, and $\hat{H}^2=c^2(\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2+m^2c^4$. Using $\alpha_i\alpha_j=\delta_{ij}+i\epsilon_{ijk}\Sigma_k$ from the previous chapter ($\Sigma_k$ is the 4×4 matrix with $\sigma_k$ as its diagonal blocks), exactly the same calculation as for Eq. \eqref{eq:63-sigpi-step} gives $(\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2=\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2-q\hbar\,\bm{\Sigma}\cdot\bm{B}$. For the electron, $-q\hbar\bm{\Sigma}\cdot\bm{B}=+e_0\hbar B\,\Sigma_z$, and from the earlier derivation $\hat{\pi}_x^2+\hat{\pi}_y^2=\hbar e_0B(2\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+1)$, so we have

$$\hat{H}^2=m^2c^4+c^2\hat{p}_z^2+c^2\hbar e_0B\bigl(2\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+1+\Sigma_z\bigr)=m^2c^4+c^2\hat{p}_z^2+2nc^2\hbar e_0B$$

where $n=n_L+(1+\Sigma_z)/2$ and $\Sigma_z=\pm1$. From the eigenvalue $E^2$ of $\hat{H}^2$, we obtain $E=\pm\sqrt{\cdots}$.

∎

\begin{equation} E_n=\pm\sqrt{m^2c^4+c^2p_z^2+2n\hbar e_0Bc^2}\qquad(n=0,1,2,\dots) \label{eq:63-dirac-landau} \end{equation}

These are the exact Landau levels of the Dirac equation, and, as the derivation above shows, $n=0$ is nondegenerate and $n\ge1$ is twofold degenerate (for each $p_z$). Taking the nonrelativistic limit $n\hbar\omega_c\ll mc^2$ at $p_z=0$, we have $2n\hbar e_0Bc^2=2n\hbar\omega_c\,mc^2$ from $e_0B=m\omega_c$, and we obtain

$$E_n-mc^2=mc^2\Bigl(\sqrt{1+\frac{2n\hbar\omega_c}{mc^2}}-1\Bigr)=n\hbar\omega_c-\frac{(n\hbar\omega_c)^2}{2mc^2}+\cdots$$

The first term is the result of the Pauli equation, and the second term is the relativistic correction. In the massless limit ($mc^2\to0$, $c\to v_{\mathrm F}$), $E_n\propto\sqrt{nB}$, which leads to the $\sqrt{B}$ law of the Landau levels of graphene (Chapter 65).

Example 63.4: Landau levels at $B=10\ \mathrm{T}$ and the relativistic correction

At $B=10\ \mathrm{T}$, (1) find $\hbar\omega_c$ in $\mathrm{meV}$ and verify that it equals $2\mu_{\mathrm B}B$. (2) Estimate, for $n=1$, the difference between the levels $n\hbar\omega_c$ ($n=0,1,2,3$) of the Pauli equation and the kinetic energy $E_n-mc^2$ of the exact Dirac levels.

Solution. (1) We find $\omega_c=e_0B/m_{\mathrm e}=1.602\,177\times10^{-19}\times10/9.109\,384\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}=1.7588\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\hbar\omega_c=1.054\,572\times10^{-34}\times1.7588\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{J}=1.8548\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{J}=1.1577\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}=1.158\ \mathrm{meV}$. On the other hand, $2\mu_{\mathrm B}B=2\times5.788\,382\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/T}\times10\ \mathrm{T}=1.1577\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}$, so $\hbar\omega_c=2\mu_{\mathrm B}B$ ($g=2$) is confirmed. The levels are equally spaced at $0,\,1.158,\,2.315,\,3.473\ \mathrm{meV}$ ($n=0,1,2,3$). The level $n=0$ is nondegenerate, and $n=1,2,3$ are twofold degenerate.

(2) The relativistic correction is $-(n\hbar\omega_c)^2/2mc^2$, and for $n=1$ it is $-(1.1577\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV})^2/(2\times510\,999\ \mathrm{eV})=-1.31\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{eV}$. Compared with the level spacing of $1.158\ \mathrm{meV}$, this is only $10^{-9}$ times as large, an extremely small amount. For $n=2,3$, being proportional to $n^2$, it becomes $-5.2\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $-1.2\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{eV}$. Even at $10\ \mathrm{T}$, with the magnetic fields obtainable in the laboratory the electron's motion is nonrelativistic, and the Pauli equation is sufficient.

63.3 The Foldy–Wouthuysen Transformation and the Spin–Orbit Interaction — The Thomas Factor of $\tfrac12$

63.3.1 Why a further stage of approximation is needed

In the previous section we eliminated the small component as $\psi_B\approx\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}\,\psi_A/2mc$ and obtained the lowest-order Pauli equation. However, corrections of order $(v/c)^2$, such as the fine structure of the hydrogen atom, are not contained in this lowest-order equation. To find the corrections, it seems that we need only carry the expansion of the denominator $E-V+mc^2=2mc^2+(\varepsilon-V)$ one step further, but when we actually try it we run into two difficulties.

  • The magnitude of the small component $\psi_B$ is $v/2c$ times that of $\psi_A$, and its contribution to the normalization is $(v/2c)^2$. Since the total probability is $\int(\psi_A^\dagger\psi_A+\psi_B^\dagger\psi_B)\,\dd^3r=1$, $\psi_A$ alone is not normalized and contains a correction of order $(v/c)^2$. If we expand the equation for the large component alone as it stands, a non-Hermitian operator appears, and it is difficult to treat the problem as an eigenvalue problem.
  • The denominator of the exact equation contains the unknown $E$, so the expanded result depends on $E$. The equation that determines the energy ends up depending on the energy itself.

The transformation given by Foldy and Wouthuysen (1950) resolves these difficulties systematically. Its idea is to “make only the operators that connect the large and small components smaller and smaller, one after another, by unitary transformations.” Since a unitary transformation preserves probability, the normalization problem does not arise, the Hamiltonian remains Hermitian, and the energy dependence also disappears.

63.3.2 Even operators and odd operators

We divide 4×4 operators into two kinds according to whether they mix the upper two components and the lower two components.

Definition 63.2: Even and odd operators

A 4×4 matrix operator $\hat{X}$ is called an even operator (block-diagonal: it does not mix the upper two components with the lower two) if it commutes with $\beta$ ($\hat{X}\beta=\beta\hat{X}$), and an odd operator (block off-diagonal: it mixes the upper and lower components) if it anticommutes with it ($\hat{X}\beta=-\beta\hat{X}$).

The Dirac Hamiltonian in an electromagnetic field splits into even and odd parts as follows (since $\beta=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbb{1},-\mathbb{1})$, the term containing $\bm{\alpha}$ is block off-diagonal).

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=\beta mc^2+\hat{\mathcal{E}}+\hat{\mathcal{O}},\qquad\hat{\mathcal{E}}=V,\qquad\hat{\mathcal{O}}=c\,\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}} \label{eq:63-EO} \end{equation}

$\beta mc^2$ and $\hat{\mathcal{E}}=V$ are even, and $\hat{\mathcal{O}}$ is odd ($\alpha_i\beta=-\beta\alpha_i$, and $\hat{\bm{\pi}}$ commutes with $\beta$). In what follows we consider a static electromagnetic field ($\bm{A},\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$ independent of time) and abbreviate $M\equiv mc^2$.

The rules for products of even and odd operators are as follows: (even)(even) = even, (odd)(odd) = even, and (even)(odd) and (odd)(even) = odd. Also, the commutator $\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}$ of an odd operator and an even operator is odd, and the square $\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2$ of an odd operator is even. The coupling between the large and small components is carried entirely by the odd operator $\hat{\mathcal{O}}$.

63.3.3 The procedure of the transformation

Consider the unitary transformation $\psi'=e^{w}\psi$, where $w=\dfrac{\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{2M}$ ($w$ is anti-Hermitian: $(\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}})^\dagger=\hat{\mathcal{O}}\beta=-\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}$. Hence $e^{w}$ is unitary. In the convention that writes a unitary transformation as $e^{iS}$ with $S$ Hermitian, this corresponds to $S=-iw$). For a static field, the transformed Hamiltonian is $\hat{H}'=e^{w}\hat{H}e^{-w}$. This can be expanded by Hadamard's formula (the commutator expansion)

\begin{equation} e^{w}\hat{H}e^{-w}=\hat{H}+\comm{w}{\hat{H}}+\frac{1}{2!}\comm{w}{\comm{w}{\hat{H}}}+\frac{1}{3!}\comm{w}{\comm{w}{\comm{w}{\hat{H}}}}+\cdots \label{eq:63-hadamard} \end{equation}

(let $F(u)=e^{uw}\hat{H}e^{-uw}$; then $\dd F/\dd u=\comm{w}{F}$, so expanding $F$ in a Taylor series about $u=0$ and setting $u=1$, the $k$th term is $1/k!$ times the $k$-fold commutator). Since $w\propto1/M$ is small, this expansion can be organized order by order in $1/M$. First we find the basic commutators that we will need. We use $\beta^2=1$ and $\hat{\mathcal{O}}\beta=-\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}$.

  • $\comm{w}{\beta M}=\dfrac{1}{2}\comm{\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\beta}=\dfrac12\bigl(\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}\beta-\beta\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}\bigr)=\dfrac12\bigl(-\hat{\mathcal{O}}-\hat{\mathcal{O}}\bigr)=-\hat{\mathcal{O}}$.
  • $\comm{w}{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}=\dfrac{1}{2M}\bigl(\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}\hat{\mathcal{E}}-\hat{\mathcal{E}}\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}\bigr)=\dfrac{\beta}{2M}\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}$ ($\hat{\mathcal{E}}$ commutes with $\beta$).
  • $\comm{w}{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}=\dfrac{1}{2M}\bigl(\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2-\hat{\mathcal{O}}\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}\bigr)=\dfrac{1}{2M}\bigl(\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2+\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2\bigr)=\dfrac{\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2}{M}$.

Using these, we compute each term of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-hadamard} in turn, order by order in $1/M$. We also use the facts that $\comm{\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}'}=-\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\hat{\mathcal{O}}'}$ for an odd operator $\hat{\mathcal{O}}'$, and that $\comm{w}{\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2}=-\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3/M$ (which follows from $\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2=-\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3$ and $\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}=\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3$).

\begin{align} \comm{w}{\hat{H}}&=-\hat{\mathcal{O}}+\frac{\beta}{2M}\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}+\frac{\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2}{M}, \notag\\ \comm{w}{\comm{w}{\hat{H}}}&=-\frac{\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2}{M}-\frac{1}{4M^2}\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}}-\frac{\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3}{M^2}, \label{eq:63-comms}\\ \comm{w}{\comm{w}{\comm{w}{\hat{H}}}}&=\frac{\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3}{M^2}-\frac{\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4}{M^3}+\cdots,\qquad \comm{w}{\comm{w}{\comm{w}{\comm{w}{\hat{H}}}}}=\frac{\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4}{M^3}+\cdots \notag \end{align}

(The omitted $\cdots$ are odd operators of still higher order, $1/M^3$ or beyond, or terms of order $M^{-4}$ or beyond. The $-\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4/M^3$ in the triple commutator comes from the last term $-\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3/M^2$ of the double commutator: $\comm{w}{-\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3/M^2}=-\dfrac{1}{2M^3}\comm{\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3}=-\dfrac{\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4}{M^3}$ (which follows from $\comm{\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3}=\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4-\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}=2\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4$). The $+\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4/M^3$ in the quadruple commutator comes from the first term $\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3/M^2$ of the triple commutator by the same calculation with the sign reversed.) Multiplying each term of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-hadamard} by the coefficients $1,\,1,\,\tfrac12,\,\tfrac16,\,\tfrac1{24}$ and adding, together with the terms of $\hat{H}=\beta M+\hat{\mathcal{E}}+\hat{\mathcal{O}}$, we obtain

\begin{equation} \hat{H}'=\beta\Bigl(M+\frac{\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2}{2M}-\frac{\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4}{8M^3}\Bigr)+\hat{\mathcal{E}}-\frac{1}{8M^2}\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}}+\hat{\mathcal{O}}' \label{eq:63-FW1} \end{equation}

The calculation of the coefficients along the way is as follows. The coefficient of $\hat{\mathcal{O}}$ cancels between $+1$ (the original term) and $-1$ (from $\comm{w}{\beta M}$). The coefficient of $\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2/M$ is $1-\tfrac12=\tfrac12$. The coefficient of $\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4/M^3$ comes from $-\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4/M^3$ in the triple commutator (coefficient $\tfrac1{3!}$) and $+\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4/M^3$ in the quadruple commutator (coefficient $\tfrac1{4!}$), giving $-\tfrac16+\tfrac1{24}=-\tfrac18$. The coefficient of the double commutator is $-\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac14=-\tfrac18$. The remaining odd operator is $\hat{\mathcal{O}}'=\dfrac{\beta}{2M}\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}-\dfrac{\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3}{3M^2}$ (the coefficient of $\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3$ is $-\tfrac12+\tfrac16=-\tfrac13$).

Intuition: Choosing $w$ — making the odd operator one step smaller

The odd operator of the original Hamiltonian is $\hat{\mathcal{O}}\sim c\,p$, whose magnitude is $v/c$ times $M=mc^2$. The transformed odd operator $\hat{\mathcal{O}}'$ consists of $[\hat{\mathcal{O}},\hat{\mathcal{E}}]/2M\sim c\,\hbar V'/M$ and $\hat{\mathcal{O}}^3/3M^2\sim c^3p^3/M^2$, and its magnitude is of order $M(v/c)^3$. It has become smaller by a factor of $(v/c)^2$ compared with the original $\hat{\mathcal{O}}\sim M(v/c)$. The choice $w=\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}/2M$ was made so that $\comm{w}{\beta M}=-\hat{\mathcal{O}}$ exactly cancels the original $\hat{\mathcal{O}}$. If we repeat the same procedure for $\hat{\mathcal{O}}'$, we can make the odd operator smaller still (a second transformation $w'=\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}'/2M$). Its effect is of order $\hat{\mathcal{O}}'^2/2M\sim c^{-4}$, so it can be neglected at the accuracy up to $1/c^2$ that we seek in this chapter. Therefore, if we discard the odd operator $\hat{\mathcal{O}}'$, $\hat{H}'$ consists of even operators only; that is, it is block-diagonal, with the upper two components and the lower two components independent of each other.

The upper two components ($\beta=+1$) of the block-diagonal $\hat{H}'$ are the main subject of this chapter.

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}=mc^2+\hat{\mathcal{E}}+\frac{\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2}{2mc^2}-\frac{\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4}{8m^3c^6}-\frac{1}{8m^2c^4}\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}} \label{eq:63-FW2} \end{equation}

The lower two components ($\beta=-1$) have the same form with different signs, and describe the negative-energy solutions (Chapter 64).

Relation between the large component $\psi_A$ and the Pauli spinor $\psi_{\mathrm P}$

The upper two components $\psi_{\mathrm P}$ after the FW transformation differ from the original large component $\psi_A$ only by the normalization correction. We find the upper two components of $\psi'=e^{w}\psi=\bigl(1+w+\tfrac12w^2+\cdots\bigr)\psi$. Since $w=\beta\hat{\mathcal{O}}/2M$, the contributions to the upper two components are as follows: the first-order term contributes $(\hat{\mathcal{O}}\psi)_A/2M=c\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}\,\psi_B/2M\approx(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2\psi_A/4m^2c^2$ (using Eq. \eqref{eq:63-psiB}), and the second-order term $\tfrac12w^2=-\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2/8M^2$ contributes $-(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2\psi_A/8m^2c^2$, so that we have

$$\psi_{\mathrm P}\simeq\Bigl(1+\frac{\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2}{8m^2c^2}\Bigr)\psi_A\qquad(\text{to lowest order }(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2\simeq\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2)$$

Indeed, the total probability $\int(\psi_A^\dagger\psi_A+\psi_B^\dagger\psi_B)\,\dd^3r\approx\int\psi_A^\dagger\bigl(1+\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2/4m^2c^2\bigr)\psi_A\,\dd^3r$ is equal to $\int\psi_{\mathrm P}^\dagger\psi_{\mathrm P}\,\dd^3r$, because $\bigl(1+\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2/8m^2c^2\bigr)^2\simeq1+\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2/4m^2c^2$. The Pauli spinor $\psi_{\mathrm P}$ is simply normalized as $\int\psi_{\mathrm P}^\dagger\psi_{\mathrm P}\,\dd^3r=1$ with accuracy up to $1/c^2$.

63.3.4 Writing the even operator explicitly — computing the double commutator

Substituting $\hat{\mathcal{E}}=V$ and $\hat{\mathcal{O}}=c\,\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}$ into Eq. \eqref{eq:63-FW2}, we find each term explicitly.

(i) $\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2$ and $\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4$. Using the algebra $\alpha_i\alpha_j=\delta_{ij}+i\epsilon_{ijk}\Sigma_k$ of the previous chapter ($\Sigma_k$ is the 4×4 matrix with $\sigma_k$ in each of its two diagonal blocks), exactly the same calculation as in Eqs. \eqref{eq:63-sigpi-step} and \eqref{eq:63-sigpi2} gives $(\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2=\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2-q\hbar\,\bm{\Sigma}\cdot\bm{B}$. Therefore

$$\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2=c^2\bigl(\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2-q\hbar\,\bm{\Sigma}\cdot\bm{B}\bigr),\qquad \frac{\hat{\mathcal{O}}^2}{2mc^2}=\frac{\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2}{2m}-\frac{q\hbar}{2m}\bm{\Sigma}\cdot\bm{B}$$

and, since $\bm{\Sigma}\to\bm{\sigma}$ in the upper two components, the right-hand side of the Pauli equation comes out directly. The fourth power is $\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4=c^4\bigl(\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2-q\hbar\,\bm{\Sigma}\cdot\bm{B}\bigr)^2$, which gives $\dfrac{\hat{\mathcal{O}}^4}{8m^3c^6}=\dfrac{\bigl(\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2-q\hbar\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}\bigr)^2}{8m^3c^2}$. When there is no magnetic field (or it is weak), this is $\hat{\bm{\pi}}^4/8m^3c^2$, and the cross term containing the magnetic field, $-q\hbar\{\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}\}/8m^3c^2$, is a relativistic correction of relative order $(v/c)^2$ to the Zeeman energy $\mu_{\mathrm B}B$ (the relativistic correction to the Zeeman effect). In this chapter we retain only $\hat{\bm{\pi}}^4/8m^3c^2$, which matters for the fine structure of atoms.

(ii) The double commutator $\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\hat{\mathcal{E}}}}$. This is the longest calculation in this section. For a function $V(\bm{r})$, we first find the inner commutator. Since $\comm{\hat{\pi}_i}{V}=-i\hbar\,\partial_iV$ ($\bm{A}$ is a function and so commutes with $V$), we have

$$\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{V}=c\sum_i\alpha_i\comm{\hat{\pi}_i}{V}=-i\hbar c\sum_i\alpha_i\,\partial_iV=-i\hbar c\,\bm{\alpha}\cdot\nabla V$$

Next we find the outer commutator. Let $f_i\equiv\partial_iV$. Paying attention to the order of the products,

\begin{align} \comm{\alpha_j\hat{\pi}_j}{\alpha_if_i}&=\alpha_j\alpha_i\,\hat{\pi}_jf_i-\alpha_i\alpha_j\,f_i\hat{\pi}_j =\alpha_j\alpha_i\bigl(f_i\hat{\pi}_j+\comm{\hat{\pi}_j}{f_i}\bigr)-\alpha_i\alpha_j\,f_i\hat{\pi}_j \notag\\ &=\bigl(\alpha_j\alpha_i-\alpha_i\alpha_j\bigr)f_i\hat{\pi}_j+\alpha_j\alpha_i\comm{\hat{\pi}_j}{f_i} \label{eq:63-dbl1} \end{align}

(we sum over $i,j$). First term: from $\alpha_i\alpha_j=\delta_{ij}+i\epsilon_{ijk}\Sigma_k$ we get $\alpha_j\alpha_i-\alpha_i\alpha_j=-2i\epsilon_{ijk}\Sigma_k$, so that $\sum_{i,j}\bigl(\alpha_j\alpha_i-\alpha_i\alpha_j\bigr)f_i\hat{\pi}_j=-2i\sum_k\Sigma_k\sum_{i,j}\epsilon_{kij}f_i\hat{\pi}_j=-2i\,\bm{\Sigma}\cdot(\nabla V\times\hat{\bm{\pi}})$. Second term: $\comm{\hat{\pi}_j}{f_i}=-i\hbar\,\partial_j\partial_iV$, and since $\partial_j\partial_iV$ is symmetric in $i,j$, the antisymmetric part $i\epsilon_{jik}\Sigma_k$ of $\alpha_j\alpha_i$ vanishes in the sum, and only the $\delta_{ji}$ part remains: $\sum_{i,j}\alpha_j\alpha_i\comm{\hat{\pi}_j}{f_i}=-i\hbar\,\nabla^2V$. Therefore we have

\begin{equation} \comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{V}}=c\cdot(-i\hbar c)\Bigl[-2i\,\bm{\Sigma}\cdot(\nabla V\times\hat{\bm{\pi}})-i\hbar\,\nabla^2V\Bigr] =-2\hbar c^2\,\bm{\Sigma}\cdot(\nabla V\times\hat{\bm{\pi}})-\hbar^2c^2\,\nabla^2V \label{eq:63-dblcomm} \end{equation}

(here $c\cdot(-i\hbar c)\cdot(-2i)=-2\hbar c^2$ and $c\cdot(-i\hbar c)\cdot(-i\hbar)=-\hbar^2c^2$). Multiplying by $-\dfrac{1}{8M^2}=-\dfrac{1}{8m^2c^4}$, we obtain

$$-\frac{1}{8m^2c^4}\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{\comm{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}{V}}=\frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\nabla^2V+\frac{\hbar}{4m^2c^2}\,\bm{\Sigma}\cdot\bigl(\nabla V\times\hat{\bm{\pi}}\bigr)$$

Putting all of the above together, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 63.5: Effective Hamiltonian up to $1/c^2$ (Foldy–Wouthuysen)

For a particle of charge $q$, mass $m$, and spin $\tfrac12$ in a static electromagnetic field, the Dirac equation reduces, up to and including terms of order $1/c^2$, to the equation $i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi_{\mathrm P}=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}\psi_{\mathrm P}$ for a two-component spinor $\psi_{\mathrm P}$, where (with $V=q\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$) the Hamiltonian is given by

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_{\mathrm{FW}}=mc^2+\frac{\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2}{2m}+V-\frac{q\hbar}{2m}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}\ \underbrace{-\ \frac{\hat{\bm{\pi}}^4}{8m^3c^2}}_{\text{mass-velocity term}}\ \underbrace{+\ \frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\nabla^2V}_{\text{Darwin term}}\ \underbrace{+\ \frac{\hbar}{4m^2c^2}\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bigl(\nabla V\times\hat{\bm{\pi}}\bigr)}_{\text{spin–orbit term}} \label{eq:63-HFW} \end{equation}

Since $\nabla V=q\nabla\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}=-q\bm{E}$ (electrostatic field $\bm{E}$), the last two terms can also be written as $-\dfrac{q\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\nabla\cdot\bm{E}$ and $-\dfrac{q\hbar}{4m^2c^2}\bm{\sigma}\cdot(\bm{E}\times\hat{\bm{\pi}})$. The cross terms involving the magnetic field (the cross terms of $\hat{\bm{\pi}}^4$ with $\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}$) have been omitted.

The first four terms on the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-HFW} make up the Pauli equation of the previous section (with the rest energy included). The next three terms are new corrections of order $1/c^2$, called the mass-velocity term, the Darwin term, and the spin–orbit term, respectively. They come out of the Dirac equation automatically. Each of them is a relativistic correction containing the square $(v/c)^2$ of the ratio of the particle's speed $v$ to the speed of light $c$, and none of them appears within the scope of the Schrödinger equation.

63.3.5 Spin–orbit interaction in a central force field $\xi(r)\,\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$

When an electron moves in a central-force potential $V(r)$, as an electron in an atom does, $\nabla V=\dfrac{1}{r}\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\,\bm{r}$, and if there is no external magnetic field, $\hat{\bm{\pi}}=\hat{\bm{p}}$. Therefore $\nabla V\times\hat{\bm{p}}=\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\,\bm{r}\times\hat{\bm{p}}=\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\,\hat{\bm{L}}$ (the order of the product $x_i\hat{p}_j$ ($i\ne j$) of components of the coordinate $\bm{r}$ and the momentum $\hat{\bm{p}}$ does not matter), and the spin–orbit term can be written as

$$\frac{\hbar}{4m^2c^2}\,\frac1r\frac{\dd V}{\dd r}\;\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{L}}=\frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\frac1r\frac{\dd V}{\dd r}\;\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\Bigl(\frac{\hbar\bm{\sigma}}{2}\Bigr)$$

where $\hat{\bm{S}}=\hbar\bm{\sigma}/2$.

Formula 63.2: Spin–orbit interaction in a central force field

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_{\mathrm{SO}}=\xi(r)\,\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}},\qquad\xi(r)=\frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\,\frac{1}{r}\frac{\dd V}{\dd r} \label{eq:63-HSO} \end{equation}

The $\tfrac12$ in the coefficient $\dfrac{1}{2m^2c^2}$ of $\xi(r)$ is the Thomas factor.

This is the same expression as Law 26.1 (Section 26.1.5), which we obtained in Chapter 26 from a heuristic argument. In the heuristic argument, the coupling of a magnetic moment with $g=2$ to the rest-frame magnetic field $\bm{B}'$ gave $1/(m^2c^2)$, and this had to be halved by the correction due to the Thomas precession. Here we have not considered the switch of rest frames or the Thomas precession at all. Simply expanding the Dirac equation up to $1/c^2$ made the factor $\tfrac12$ come out automatically. Next we look at the physical meaning of this $\tfrac12$.

63.3.6 Physical picture: The rest-frame magnetic field and Thomas precession

The electron moves with velocity $\bm{v}$ in the electrostatic field $\bm{E}$ of the atomic nucleus. To an observer moving along with the electron (the electron's rest frame), the electric and magnetic fields appear mixed, and for $v\ll c$ a magnetic field

\begin{equation} \bm{B}'=-\frac{\bm{v}\times\bm{E}}{c^2} \label{eq:63-Bprime} \end{equation}

acts on the electron (Introductory University Physics, Chapter 58, Section 58.7 (in Japanese); Chapter 26, Section 26.1). For a central force, since $\bm{E}=\dfrac{1}{e_0}\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\dfrac{\bm{r}}{r}$, we have $\bm{B}'=\dfrac{1}{e_0mc^2}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\bm{L}$, which points in the same direction as $\bm{L}$ (Figure 63.3). The coupling of the spin magnetic moment $\bm{\mu}=-g\mu_{\mathrm B}\hat{\bm{S}}/\hbar$ to this magnetic field is, for $g=2$, $-\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{B}'=\dfrac{1}{m^2c^2}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$; the coefficient $1/(m^2c^2)$ is exactly twice Dirac's result. This discrepancy was also a problem when Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit (1925) proposed the spin, and in 1926 Thomas resolved it by pointing out that the electron's rest frame is not an inertial frame (it is accelerating because of the orbital motion), and that the Lorentz transformations (boosts) connecting the rest frames at successive instants produce a rotation (Thomas precession, angular velocity $\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm T}\simeq\tfrac12\bm{a}\times\bm{v}/c^2$). This precession, in the opposite direction, cancels $\tfrac12$ of the spin precession.

Why $-\tfrac12$? The magnitude of the Thomas precession

We confirm the magnitude of the Thomas precession and the coefficient $-\tfrac12$ in three steps.

(1) The composition of two boosts includes a rotation. If we go to the rest frame moving with velocity $\bm{v}$ as seen from the laboratory frame, and then to a frame moving with a small velocity $\delta\bm{v}$ relative to that frame, the composition is a single boost to the velocity $\bm{v}+\delta\bm{v}$ multiplied by a small rotation (if $\delta\bm{v}\parallel\bm{v}$, no rotation appears). On a two-component spinor, a boost to a velocity $\bm{u}$ ($\abs{\bm{u}}\ll c$) acts, in terms of the rapidity $\eta\approx u/c$, as $e^{\eta\,\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\sigma}/2}\approx1+\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\sigma}/2c$ ($\bm{n}=\bm{u}/u$), and a rotation through an angle $\bm{\theta}$ acts as $1-i\bm{\theta}\cdot\bm{\sigma}/2$. Expanding the product $(1+\delta\bm{v}\cdot\bm{\sigma}/2c)(1+\bm{v}\cdot\bm{\sigma}/2c)$ with Formula 63.1, we are left with an antisymmetric term proportional to $\bm{\sigma}$, $\dfrac{i}{4c^2}\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot(\delta\bm{v}\times\bm{v})$. This corresponds to the rotation $-i\bm{\theta}\cdot\bm{\sigma}/2$, so the magnitude of the rotation angle is

$$\abs{\bm{\theta}}=\frac{\abs{\bm{v}\times\delta\bm{v}}}{2c^2}$$

(This is where the “1/2” comes from.)

(2) The rest frame of an accelerating electron rotates little by little. Each time the velocity of the electron changes by $\delta\bm{v}=\bm{a}\,\delta t$ during a time $\delta t$, the rest frame rotates through the angle of (1). Therefore the orientation of the electron's rest frame, relative to the laboratory frame, rotates with angular velocity

$$\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm T}\simeq\frac{1}{2c^2}\,\bm{a}\times\bm{v}$$

This is the Thomas precession. For a circular orbit, $\bm{a}$ points toward the center, so $\bm{a}\times\bm{v}$ points in the opposite direction to $\bm{L}$, and the rest frame rotates in the direction opposite to the orbital rotation.

(3) For a central force, compare the coefficients. Let $m$ be the mass of the electron and $V(r)$ the potential. The force is $-\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\dfrac{\bm{r}}{r}$, so the acceleration is $\bm{a}=-\dfrac{1}{m}\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\dfrac{\bm{r}}{r}$, and from $\bm{r}\times\bm{v}=\bm{L}/m$ we get $\bm{a}\times\bm{v}=-\dfrac{1}{m^2}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\bm{L}$. Hence

$$\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm T}=-\frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\frac1r\frac{\dd V}{\dd r}\,\bm{L}$$

On the other hand, a spin with the $g=2$ magnetic moment $\bm{\mu}=-(e_0/m)\hat{\bm{S}}$ in the rest-frame magnetic field $\bm{B}'$ precesses, under the torque $\bm{\mu}\times\bm{B}'$, as $\dd\hat{\bm{S}}/\dd t=\bm{\omega}_B\times\hat{\bm{S}}$. Its angular velocity is $\bm{\omega}_B=\dfrac{e_0}{m}\bm{B}'=\dfrac{1}{m^2c^2}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\bm{L}$ (we used $\bm{B}'$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-Bprime}), and the energy corresponds to $\bm{\omega}_B\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}=-\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{B}'$. Then $\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm T}=-\tfrac12\bm{\omega}_B$, so the Thomas precession cancels exactly half of the spin precession. The angular velocity of the spin precession seen in the laboratory frame is $\bm{\omega}_B+\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm T}=\tfrac12\bm{\omega}_B$, and the spin–orbit energy is

$$\tfrac12\bm{\omega}_B\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}=\frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\frac1r\frac{\dd V}{\dd r}\,\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}=\xi(r)\,\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$$

which agrees with the result of the Dirac equation (Formula 63.2).

For a general $g$ factor, the coupling to the rest-frame magnetic field is $\tfrac{g}{2}\cdot\dfrac{1}{m^2c^2}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ and the contribution of the Thomas precession is $-\tfrac12\cdot\dfrac{1}{m^2c^2}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$, so the total coefficient is $\dfrac{g-1}{2m^2c^2}$. For $g=2$ it is $\dfrac{1}{2m^2c^2}$ (Chapter 26, Section 26.1.4). The result of the Dirac equation can be rephrased as correctly including both $g=2$ and this Thomas correction “simultaneously.” Using the experimental $g_{\mathrm e}=2.0023$, the coefficient is $(g-1)/2=0.50116$, which is larger by $0.23\ \%$ (this is one reason why the measured splitting of hydrogen $2\mathrm{p}$ is about $0.2\ \%$ larger than the Dirac value; Section 63.5).

Example 63.5: The rest-frame magnetic field felt by an electron in a Bohr orbit, and the magnitude of the spin–orbit energy

For the electron in the $n=1$ orbit (radius $a_0$, speed $v=\alpha c$) of the Bohr model of the hydrogen atom, find (1) the electric field $E$ of the nucleus and the rest-frame magnetic field $B'=vE/c^2$, and (2) the relation between $\mu_{\mathrm B}B'$ and $\alpha^4m_{\mathrm e}c^2/2$. (3) The splitting of hydrogen $2\mathrm{p}$, $\tfrac32\zeta_{2p}=\alpha^4m_{\mathrm e}c^2/32$, is what fraction of the quantity in (2)? (4) How is this explained in terms of $\expval{ r^{-3}}$?

Solution. (1) $E=\dfrac{e_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a_0^2}=\dfrac{1.602\,177\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}}{4\pi\times8.854\,188\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m}\times(5.291\,772\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m})^2}=5.142\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{V/m}$. The speed is $v=\alpha c=7.2974\times10^{-3}\times2.99792\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}=2.188\times10^6\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Since $\bm{v}\perp\bm{E}$, $B'=\dfrac{vE}{c^2}=\dfrac{(2.188\times10^6)(5.142\times10^{11})}{(2.99792\times10^8)^2}\ \mathrm{T}=12.5\ \mathrm{T}$. A magnetic field far exceeding that of a laboratory electromagnet (a few T) is produced “on its own” inside the atom.

(2) $\mu_{\mathrm B}B'=5.788\,382\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/T}\times12.52\ \mathrm{T}=7.245\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV}=0.7245\ \mathrm{meV}$. On the other hand, $\alpha^4m_{\mathrm e}c^2=1.449\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}$, so half of it is $0.7245\ \mathrm{meV}$, and the two agree. This is not an accident. We can compute $\mu_{\mathrm B}B'=\dfrac{e_0\hbar}{2m}\cdot\dfrac{\alpha c\cdot e_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a_0^2c^2}=\dfrac{\hbar}{2m}\cdot\dfrac{\alpha\cdot\alpha\hbar c}{a_0^2c}=\dfrac{\alpha^2\hbar^2}{2ma_0^2}=\dfrac{\alpha^2E_{\mathrm h}}{2}=\dfrac{\alpha^4mc^2}{2}$ (using $e_0^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0=\alpha\hbar c$ and $E_{\mathrm h}=\hbar^2/ma_0^2=\alpha^2mc^2$). The same quantity is also $\xi(a_0)\hbar^2=\dfrac{\alpha\hbar^3}{2m^2c\,a_0^3}=\dfrac{\alpha^4mc^2}{2}$ (using $1/a_0^3=(mc\alpha/\hbar)^3$).

(3) The $2\mathrm{p}$ splitting is $\tfrac32\zeta_{2p}=\alpha^4mc^2/32$ (Section 63.3.7), which is $1/16$ of the quantity $\alpha^4mc^2/2$ in (2). (4) Since $\xi(r)\propto r^{-3}$, the spin–orbit energy is proportional to the average $\expval{ r^{-3}}$. For $2\mathrm{p}$, $\expval{ r^{-3}}=1/(24a_0^3)$ (Formula 17.7), which is only $1/24$ of the value $1/a_0^3$ for the circular orbit at $r=a_0$. Since the $2\mathrm{p}$ electron is, on average, as far as $5a_0$ from the nucleus, the rest-frame magnetic field is small. We have $\zeta_{2p}=\xi(a_0)\hbar^2\cdot a_0^3\expval{ r^{-3}}_{2p}=\dfrac{0.7245\ \mathrm{meV}}{24}=30.19\ \mu\mathrm{eV}$, so $\tfrac32\zeta_{2p}=45.28\ \mu\mathrm{eV}$, which corresponds to $\dfrac{3}{2\cdot24}=\dfrac{1}{16}$ of the quantity in (2).

63.3.7 The spin–orbit coupling constant $\zeta_{nl}$ of hydrogen-like atoms

For a hydrogen-like atom (nuclear charge $+Ze_0$), $V=-Z\alpha\hbar c/r$, so $\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}=\dfrac{Z\alpha\hbar c}{r^3}$ and $\xi(r)=\dfrac{Z\alpha\hbar}{2m^2c\,r^3}$. As a first-order perturbation, we denote the radial average of $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{SO}}$ by $\zeta_{nl}\equiv\hbar^2\expval{\xi}_{nl}$. Using $\expval{ r^{-3}}_{nl}=\dfrac{Z^3}{n^3\,l(l+\frac12)(l+1)\,a_0^3}$ ($l\ge1$; Formula 17.7) and $1/a_0^3=(mc\alpha/\hbar)^3$, all the factors of $\hbar$ cancel and we obtain

$$\zeta_{nl}=\frac{Z\alpha\hbar^3}{2m^2c}\cdot\frac{Z^3}{n^3l(l+\frac12)(l+1)}\Bigl(\frac{mc\alpha}{\hbar}\Bigr)^3=\frac{Z^4\alpha^4mc^2}{2n^3\,l\bigl(l+\tfrac12\bigr)(l+1)}$$

This is the result.

Formula 63.3: Spin–orbit coupling constant and splitting of a hydrogen-like atom

\begin{equation} \zeta_{nl}=\frac{mc^2\,(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3\,l\bigl(l+\tfrac12\bigr)(l+1)}\qquad(l\ge1),\qquad \hat{H}_{\mathrm{SO}}\ \to\ \zeta_{nl}\,\frac{\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}}{\hbar^2} \label{eq:63-zeta} \end{equation}

The eigenvalues of $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}=\tfrac12\bigl[\hat{\bm{J}}^2-\hat{\bm{L}}^2-\hat{\bm{S}}^2\bigr]$ are $\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2}\bigl[j(j+1)-l(l+1)-\tfrac34\bigr]$, that is, $l\hbar^2/2$ for $j=l+\tfrac12$ and $-(l+1)\hbar^2/2$ for $j=l-\tfrac12$, so the level shifts and the splitting are

\begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{SO}}(j=l\pm\tfrac12)=\begin{cases}+\tfrac{l}{2}\,\zeta_{nl}\\[1mm]-\tfrac{l+1}{2}\,\zeta_{nl}\end{cases},\qquad \Delta E_{\mathrm{SO}}=\bigl(l+\tfrac12\bigr)\zeta_{nl}=\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3\,l(l+1)} \label{eq:63-SOsplit} \end{equation}

This $\zeta_{nl}$ is the same as that in Theorem 26.1 of Chapter 26 ($\zeta_{nl}=\alpha^2E_{\mathrm h}Z^4/[2n^3l(l+\tfrac12)(l+1)]$; setting $E_{\mathrm h}=\alpha^2mc^2$ makes it agree with Eq. \eqref{eq:63-zeta}). In Chapter 26 we derived $\xi(r)$ from an estimate of the “rest-frame magnetic field”; in this chapter we started from the Dirac equation and derived $\xi(r)$ including the Thomas factor $\tfrac12$ (Sections 63.3.5–63.3.6). Since $\zeta_{nl}\propto mc^2(Z\alpha)^4$ is proportional to $Z^4$, and $Z\alpha=v_1/c$ ($v_1$ is a rough measure of the speed of a 1s electron), it grows rapidly for heavy elements. The numerical values for hydrogen $2\mathrm{p}$, $\zeta=30.19\ \mu\mathrm{eV}$ and the splitting $10.95\ \mathrm{GHz}=0.3652\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, are given in Table 26.1 and Example 26.2 of Chapter 26.

63.3.8 Toward materials: The $^3P$ splitting of Group 14 atoms and its $Z$ dependence

Convert the $^3P_2-{}^3P_0$ splittings in Table 63.1 (C: $43.4$, Si: $223.2$, Ge: $1410$, Sn: $3427.7$, Pb: $10650.3\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$) to meV, and find the factor of increase from C to Pb and the slope $d\ln\Delta/d\ln Z$ of the log–log plot. Solution. Since $1\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}=hc/e_0\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{eV}=1.239\,842\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV}=0.123\,984\ \mathrm{meV}$, we have C: $43.4\times0.123\,984=5.38\ \mathrm{meV}$, Si: $27.67\ \mathrm{meV}$, Ge: $174.8\ \mathrm{meV}$, Sn: $425.0\ \mathrm{meV}$, and Pb: $1320.5\ \mathrm{meV}=1.32\ \mathrm{eV}$. If the $Z^4$ law of hydrogen-like atoms were obeyed, the splitting would increase by a factor of $(82/6)^4=3.5\times10^4$ from C to Pb, but the actual factor is $245$. The reasons are as follows. (i) The valence electrons ($np$) of Group 14 feel the effective nuclear charge $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ left after the core electrons have shielded the nuclear charge, so the splitting is proportional to $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}^4$ rather than $Z^4$ (Chapter 18). (ii) We have $\expval{ r^{-3}}\propto n^{-3}$, and the farther down the periodic table the atom lies (the larger the principal quantum number $n$), the more the electron spreads outward and the smaller $\expval{ r^{-3}}$ becomes. (iii) Since $\xi(r)\propto r^{-3}$ is determined by the behavior very near the nucleus, the degree to which the valence electron “penetrates” near the nucleus also matters. Because of these effects, the actual growth is roughly $Z^2$, much more gradual than $Z^4$. Even so, the splitting increases by more than two orders of magnitude from C to Pb. Note also that for Pb the Landé interval rule ($2:1$) is badly violated. This is because the spin–orbit interaction becomes comparable to the electrostatic interaction between electrons, and $LS$ coupling breaks down (approaching $jj$ coupling); this discussion is found in Table 34.3 and Example 34.4 of Chapter 34 (Sections 34.2, 34.4).

Application: The valence band of semiconductors and lead-based perovskites — spin–orbit splitting determines the properties of materials

In crystals as well, the top of the valence band, which originates from the atomic $p$ orbitals, splits into two bands, $j=\tfrac32$ and $j=\tfrac12$, through the spin–orbit interaction. The splitting $\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}}$ is about $44\ \mathrm{meV}$ in Si, about $0.3\ \mathrm{eV}$ in Ge, about $0.34\ \mathrm{eV}$ in GaAs, and about $0.8\ \mathrm{eV}$ in InSb; the heavier the constituent atoms, the larger it is. In lead halide perovskites (solar-cell materials) and in PbTe, which contain lead (Pb), the strong spin–orbit splitting of the Pb $6p$ orbitals (the $^3P_2-{}^3P_0$ splitting of the free atom is $1.32\ \mathrm{eV}$, which gives $\zeta_{6p}\approx0.9\ \mathrm{eV}$ when converted to a one-electron coupling constant; the $^2P_{3/2}-{}^2P_{1/2}$ splitting of the $6p$ state of $\mathrm{Pb^+}$ is about $1.75\ \mathrm{eV}$; both are NIST values) makes the bottom of the conduction band a band derived from the $j=\tfrac12$ states of $6p$, and the band gap is lowered substantially. The strength of the spin–orbit coupling thus determines the band-edge structure and the optical properties of semiconductors (Chapter 26, Section 26.7. The $\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ in Solid-State Physics (AD 06, Chapter 20 (in Japanese)) also starts from Eq. \eqref{eq:63-HSO} of this chapter).

63.4 The Mass-Velocity Term and the Darwin Term — The Kinetic-Energy Correction and the Upward Shift of s Levels

Of the $1/c^2$ corrections in Theorem 63.5, the terms that come after the spin–orbit term are the mass-velocity term $-\hat{\bm{p}}^4/8m^3c^2$ and the Darwin term $\dfrac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\nabla^2V$. For a hydrogen-like atom ($\bm{A}=\bm{0}$, $V=-Z\alpha\hbar c/r$), we compute the first-order perturbation due to these terms. On the solutions $\psi_{nlm}$ of the Schrödinger equation, the mass-velocity term, the Darwin term, and the spin–orbit term all commute with $\hat{\bm{L}}^2$, $\hat{\bm{J}}^2$, and $\hat{J}_z$, so the matrix elements between degenerate states of the same $n$ are $0$ if $l$, $j$, or $m_j$ differ (Chapter 28, Section 28.2). Therefore, just as in nondegenerate first-order perturbation theory, it suffices to find the expectation value in each state.

63.4.1 The mass-velocity term

Expanding the relativistic kinetic energy $\sqrt{m^2c^4+p^2c^2}-mc^2$ for $p\ll mc$ gives

$$\sqrt{m^2c^4+p^2c^2}-mc^2=\frac{p^2}{2m}-\frac{p^4}{8m^3c^2}+\cdots$$

(See Introductory University Physics, Chapter 57, Section 57.6 (in Japanese) and this book, Chapter 61, Section 61.3.) The second term is the mass-velocity term $-\hat{\bm{p}}^4/8m^3c^2$. Even for the same momentum, a fast particle has a kinetic energy smaller than $p^2/2m$ because of the relativistic increase in mass. This correction is always negative, and the larger the kinetic energy (the closer the electron is to the nucleus and the faster it moves), the more the energy is lowered.

Derivation: The expectation value of the mass-velocity term for a hydrogen-like atom

For an eigenstate $\psi$ of $\hat{H}_0=\hat{\bm{p}}^2/2m+V$, $\hat{\bm{p}}^2\psi=2m(E_n-V)\psi$ holds. Since $\hat{\bm{p}}^2$ is Hermitian, we have

$$\expval{\hat{\bm{p}}^4}=\braket{\psi}{\hat{\bm{p}}^2\hat{\bm{p}}^2\psi}=\braket{\hat{\bm{p}}^2\psi}{\hat{\bm{p}}^2\psi}=4m^2\expval{(E_n-V)^2}=4m^2\bigl[E_n^2-2E_n\expval{ V}+\expval{ V^2}\bigr]$$

(We use the form $\braket{\hat{\bm{p}}^2\psi}{\hat{\bm{p}}^2\psi}$ in order to avoid the problem that, because an $s$-state $\psi$ has a cusp at the origin ($e^{-Zr/a_0}$), a $\delta$ function appears in $\hat{\bm{p}}^4\psi$; Exercise 63.5.) For a hydrogen-like atom, $E_n=-\dfrac{(Z\alpha)^2mc^2}{2n^2}$, $\expval{ V}=-Z\alpha\hbar c\expval{ r^{-1}}=-\dfrac{(Z\alpha)^2mc^2}{n^2}=2E_n$ (with $\expval{ r^{-1}}=Z/n^2a_0$ of Formula 17.7), and $\expval{ V^2}=(Z\alpha\hbar c)^2\expval{ r^{-2}}=\dfrac{(Z\alpha)^4m^2c^4}{n^3(l+\frac12)}$ (from $\expval{ r^{-2}}=Z^2/[n^3(l+\frac12)a_0^2]$ and $\hbar c/a_0=\alpha mc^2$), so we have

$$\frac{\expval{\hat{\bm{p}}^4}}{4m^2}=E_n^2-4E_n^2+\expval{ V^2}=-3E_n^2+\expval{ V^2}=(Z\alpha)^4m^2c^4\Bigl[\frac{1}{n^3(l+\frac12)}-\frac{3}{4n^4}\Bigr]$$

Multiplying both sides by $-\dfrac{4m^2}{8m^3c^2}=-\dfrac{1}{2mc^2}$ gives the expectation value of the mass-velocity term.

∎

Formula 63.4: The expectation value of the mass-velocity term

\begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{MV}}=\Bigl\langle-\frac{\hat{\bm{p}}^4}{8m^3c^2}\Bigr\rangle_{nl}=-\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3}\Bigl[\frac{1}{l+\frac12}-\frac{3}{4n}\Bigr] \label{eq:63-EMV} \end{equation}

The smaller $l$ is, the deeper (the more negative) the value becomes. In an $s$ state ($l=0$) the electron has a large probability of being near the nucleus and is fast there, so the mass-velocity term is the largest.

63.4.2 The Darwin term

The Darwin term $\dfrac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\nabla^2V$ contains the Laplacian of the potential (the average “curvature” of the potential). For the Coulomb potential, the electrostatic potential $\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$ satisfies Poisson's equation $\nabla^2\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}=-\rho/\varepsilon_0$ (Introductory University Physics, Chapter 36, Section 36.7 (in Japanese)), and for the point charge $+Ze_0$ at the origin $\rho=Ze_0\,\delta^3(\bm{r})$, so from $V=-e_0\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$ we obtain

\begin{equation} \nabla^2V=\frac{Ze_0^2}{\varepsilon_0}\,\delta^3(\bm{r})=4\pi Z\alpha\hbar c\;\delta^3(\bm{r}) \label{eq:63-lapV} \end{equation}

(Here $e_0^2/\varepsilon_0=4\pi\cdot e_0^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0=4\pi\alpha\hbar c$.) That is, the Darwin term is a $\delta$ function, proportional to the probability (density) of the electron being at the position of the nucleus. For a state with $l\ge1$ we have $\psi(\bm{0})=0$, so the term is $0$, and only $s$ states are affected. The value of $\psi_{ns}(\bm{0})$ follows from $R_{n0}(0)^2=4Z^3/(n^3a_0^3)$ and $\abs{Y_0^0}^2=1/4\pi$: $\abs{\psi_{ns}(\bm{0})}^2=\dfrac{Z^3}{\pi n^3a_0^3}$ (Chapter 16, Section 16.2). Therefore

$$E_{\mathrm{D}}=\frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\cdot4\pi Z\alpha\hbar c\cdot\frac{Z^3}{\pi n^3a_0^3}=\frac{Z^4\alpha\,\hbar^3}{2m^2c\,n^3a_0^3}=\frac{Z^4\alpha^4mc^2}{2n^3}\qquad\Bigl(\frac{1}{a_0^3}=\frac{m^3c^3\alpha^3}{\hbar^3}\Bigr)$$

is obtained.

Formula 63.5: The expectation value of the Darwin term

\begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{D}}=\Bigl\langle\frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\nabla^2V\Bigr\rangle_{nl}=\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3}\,\delta_{l0} \label{eq:63-ED} \end{equation}

Only $s$ states are shifted, in the positive direction (upward).

Intuition: The electron is not a point but is blurred over about a Compton wavelength

The physical meaning of the Darwin term is that the electron does not exist as a “point” at the position $\bm{r}$ but is blurred over a range of about the Compton wavelength $\bar{\lambda}_{\mathrm C}=\hbar/mc=386\ \mathrm{fm}$ (it oscillates rapidly: Zitterbewegung; Chapter 64). The average potential felt by the blurred electron can be expanded, with $\delta\bm{r}$ a small displacement of the electron's position, as $\expval{ V(\bm{r}+\delta\bm{r})}=V(\bm{r})+\tfrac16\expval{\delta r^2}\nabla^2V+\cdots$ (the term of second order in an isotropic displacement). Comparing this with the Darwin term $\dfrac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\nabla^2V$ gives $\expval{\delta r^2}=\tfrac34\bar{\lambda}_{\mathrm C}^2$, so the displacement is of the order of $\bar{\lambda}_{\mathrm C}$ in magnitude (Welton's picture). The Coulomb potential bends sharply very near the nucleus ($r\lesssim\bar{\lambda}_{\mathrm C}$), so only the $s$ states, which have a nonzero probability of the electron being at the position of the nucleus, have their average potential made shallower by the blurring, and their energy rises. The Bohr radius $a_0=5.3\times10^4\ \mathrm{fm}$ is about 137 times $\bar{\lambda}_{\mathrm C}$ ($a_0=\bar{\lambda}_{\mathrm C}/\alpha$), and the effect is very small, of order $(Z\alpha)^4$ (Related simulator: explanation of Elementary Particles §6.2).

Caution: The mass-velocity and Darwin terms can be used only in first-order perturbation theory

For a point-charge nucleus, $\hat{\bm{p}}^4$ and $\nabla^2V\propto\delta^3(\bm{r})$ are operators that are singular at the origin, and if they are used in perturbation theory of second order and higher, the integrals diverge. The $1/c^2$ corrections are treated either by first-order perturbation theory or by a regularized method (in computational chemistry, two-component methods such as ZORA, Douglas–Kroll–Hess, and X2C are used. A general introduction to the Dirac equation and spinors is given in AD 05, Chapter 7, Section 7.10). For hydrogen, one can check the results by solving the Dirac equation exactly, as in Section 63.5.

Example 63.8: Hydrogen $1\mathrm{s}$: mass-velocity term $-0.9057\ \mathrm{meV}$, Darwin term $+0.7245\ \mathrm{meV}$

For the hydrogen $1\mathrm{s}$ state, (1) find the mass-velocity term from $\expval{\hat{\bm{p}}^4}=4m^2\expval{(E-V)^2}$. (2) Find the Darwin term, and compare the total with the difference between Dirac's exact $1\mathrm{s}$ level $E=mc^2\bigl(\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}-1\bigr)$ and the Bohr level.

Solution. (1) In atomic units ($m=\hbar=a_0=1$, with the unit of energy $E_{\mathrm h}$) we have $E=-\tfrac12$, $V=-1/r$, $\expval{ r^{-1}}=1$, and $\expval{ r^{-2}}=2$, so $\expval{(E-V)^2}=\expval{(1/r-\tfrac12)^2}=\expval{ r^{-2}}-\expval{ r^{-1}}+\tfrac14=2-1+\tfrac14=\tfrac54$ and $\expval{\hat{\bm{p}}^4}=4\cdot\tfrac54=5$ (in units of $m^2E_{\mathrm h}^2$). Then $E_{\mathrm{MV}}=-\dfrac{\expval{\hat{\bm{p}}^4}}{8m^3c^2}=-\dfrac58\dfrac{E_{\mathrm h}^2}{mc^2}=-\dfrac58\alpha^2E_{\mathrm h}=-\dfrac58\alpha^4mc^2$ (since $E_{\mathrm h}=\alpha^2mc^2$). Because $\alpha^4mc^2=1.449\,04\ \mathrm{meV}$, we get $E_{\mathrm{MV}}=-0.9057\ \mathrm{meV}$ (this agrees with Formula 63.4 for $n=1$, $l=0$: $-\tfrac12(2-\tfrac34)=-\tfrac58$).

(2) The Darwin term, from Formula 63.5 with $n=1$, is $E_{\mathrm{D}}=+\tfrac12\alpha^4mc^2=+0.7245\ \mathrm{meV}$. The spin–orbit term is $0$ for $l=0$. The total is $-0.9057+0.7245=-0.1811\ \mathrm{meV}$. Dirac's exact level is $mc^2\bigl(\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}-1\bigr)=-13.605\,874\ \mathrm{eV}$, and the Bohr level (for the electron mass, with the nucleus held fixed) is $-\tfrac12\alpha^2mc^2=-13.605\,693\ \mathrm{eV}$; the difference is $-0.1811\ \mathrm{meV}$, which agrees with the sum of the three terms. Relativistic effects deepen the $1\mathrm{s}$ level by $0.18\ \mathrm{meV}$, that is, by $1.3\times10^{-5}$ times the Bohr energy.

Example 63.9: Hydrogen $n=2$: degeneracy of $2\mathrm{s}_{1/2}$ and $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$

For the $2\mathrm{s}_{1/2}$, $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$, and $2\mathrm{p}_{3/2}$ levels of hydrogen with $n=2$, tabulate the three corrections in units of $\alpha^4mc^2$, and verify that the totals are $-\tfrac{5}{128}$, $-\tfrac{5}{128}$, and $-\tfrac{1}{128}$. Why does degeneracy occur in the sum of three corrections that differ in character?

Solution. From Formula 63.4 with $n=2$: $E_{\mathrm{MV}}=-\tfrac{1}{16}\bigl[\tfrac{1}{l+1/2}-\tfrac38\bigr]$. For $l=0$: $-\tfrac1{16}\cdot\tfrac{13}{8}=-\tfrac{13}{128}$, and for $l=1$: $-\tfrac1{16}\bigl(\tfrac23-\tfrac38\bigr)=-\tfrac1{16}\cdot\tfrac{7}{24}=-\tfrac{7}{384}$. From Formula 63.5, the Darwin term is $+\tfrac1{16}$ for $2\mathrm{s}$ only. For the spin–orbit term, using $\zeta_{2p}=\tfrac1{48}$ (Formula 63.3) in Eq. \eqref{eq:63-SOsplit}, we get $2\mathrm{p}_{3/2}$: $+\tfrac12\zeta=+\tfrac{1}{96}$, and $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$: $-\zeta=-\tfrac{1}{48}$. Totals: $2\mathrm{s}_{1/2}$: $-\tfrac{13}{128}+\tfrac{8}{128}=-\tfrac{5}{128}$. $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$: $-\tfrac{7}{384}-\tfrac{8}{384}=-\tfrac{15}{384}=-\tfrac{5}{128}$. $2\mathrm{p}_{3/2}$: $-\tfrac{7}{384}+\tfrac{4}{384}=-\tfrac{3}{384}=-\tfrac{1}{128}$. Converting these using $1.449\,04\ \mathrm{meV}$ and $350.38\ \mathrm{GHz}$ as the units gives the values in parentheses in Table 63.2.

Each of the three corrections applies according to the “degree” to which the electron is near the nucleus (mass-velocity term: the kinetic energy is large $\Rightarrow l$ is small; Darwin term: the electron is at the position of the nucleus $\Rightarrow s$ only; spin–orbit term: $\expval{ r^{-3}}$ is large $\Rightarrow$ large for small $l$). That a relation depending only on $j$ (the same value for $j=l\pm\tfrac12$) holds among three corrections that at first sight have nothing to do with one another is not an accident: it is because $\kappa$ and $-\kappa$ are degenerate in the Dirac equation for the Coulomb potential (the algebraic check is given in Theorem 63.6, and the reason is stated after Theorem 63.7 in Section 63.5.2).

63.5 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen — Agreement with Dirac's Exact Solution

63.5.1 The sum of the three corrections is determined by $j$ alone

To the levels $E_n=-\tfrac12(Z\alpha)^2mc^2/n^2$ of a hydrogen-like atom we add the three corrections of the preceding sections (the mass-velocity term $E_{\mathrm{MV}}$, the Darwin term $E_{\mathrm{D}}$, and the spin–orbit term $E_{\mathrm{SO}}$). All three corrections are proportional to $u_n\equiv\dfrac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3}$, so we write them in units of this quantity. From Formulas 63.4, 63.5, and 63.3, we obtain

\begin{align} E_{\mathrm{MV}}&=-u_n\Bigl[\frac{1}{l+\frac12}-\frac{3}{4n}\Bigr],\qquad E_{\mathrm{D}}=+u_n\,\delta_{l0}, \notag\\ E_{\mathrm{SO}}\bigl(j=l+\tfrac12\bigr)&=\frac{l}{2}\zeta_{nl}=\frac{u_n}{(2l+1)(l+1)},\qquad E_{\mathrm{SO}}\bigl(j=l-\tfrac12\bigr)=-\frac{l+1}{2}\zeta_{nl}=-\frac{u_n}{l(2l+1)} \label{eq:63-terms} \end{align}

(here $\zeta_{nl}=u_n/[l(l+\frac12)(l+1)]$ was used).

Theorem 63.6: The fine structure is determined by $j$ alone

The sum $E_{\mathrm{MV}}+E_{\mathrm{D}}+E_{\mathrm{SO}}$ of the $1/c^2$ corrections of a hydrogen-like atom is, for both $j=l\pm\tfrac12$,

\begin{equation} E^{(4)}_{nj}=-\frac{mc^2(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3}\Bigl[\frac{1}{j+\frac12}-\frac{3}{4n}\Bigr] \label{eq:63-E4} \end{equation}

It does not depend on $l$; it depends only on $j$.

Proof

We split into three cases and add the terms of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-terms}. Below, we take $u_n$ as the unit, and $\dfrac{3}{4n}$ remains common to all cases.

(i) $j=l+\tfrac12$, $l\ge1$. Here $E_{\mathrm{D}}=0$, and since $\dfrac{1}{l+1/2}=\dfrac{2}{2l+1}=\dfrac{2(l+1)}{(2l+1)(l+1)}$, we have

$$-\frac{2(l+1)}{(2l+1)(l+1)}+\frac{1}{(2l+1)(l+1)}=-\frac{2l+1}{(2l+1)(l+1)}=-\frac{1}{l+1}=-\frac{1}{j+\frac12}\qquad(j+\tfrac12=l+1)$$

That is, adding the spin–orbit term $+\dfrac{1}{(2l+1)(l+1)}$ to the mass-velocity term $-\dfrac{1}{l+1/2}$ gives $-\dfrac{1}{j+1/2}$.

(ii) $j=l-\tfrac12$, $l\ge1$. Since $\dfrac{1}{l+1/2}=\dfrac{2l}{l(2l+1)}$ and the spin–orbit term is $-\dfrac{1}{l(2l+1)}$, we have

$$-\frac{2l}{l(2l+1)}-\frac{1}{l(2l+1)}=-\frac{2l+1}{l(2l+1)}=-\frac1l=-\frac{1}{j+\frac12}\qquad(j+\tfrac12=l)$$

(iii) $l=0$, $j=\tfrac12$. There is no spin–orbit term, but there is the Darwin term $+1$. We have $-\dfrac{1}{1/2}+1=-2+1=-1=-\dfrac{1}{j+1/2}$ ($j+\tfrac12=1$).

In all three cases the result is $u_n\Bigl[-\dfrac{1}{j+1/2}+\dfrac{3}{4n}\Bigr]$, and Eq. \eqref{eq:63-E4} is proved.

∎

In this proof, substituting $l=0$ into the spin–orbit expression $u_n/[(2l+1)(l+1)]$ for $j=l+\tfrac12$ in (i) gives $u_n$, which coincides exactly with the Darwin term $+u_n$ in (iii). That is, for an $s$ state ($l=0$), the Darwin term makes a contribution of exactly the same magnitude in place of the missing spin–orbit term, so that a single formula covers $l=0$ as well. From Theorem 63.6, the total energy of hydrogen is

\begin{equation} E_{nj}=mc^2\Bigl[1-\frac{(Z\alpha)^2}{2n^2}-\frac{(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3}\Bigl(\frac{1}{j+\frac12}-\frac{3}{4n}\Bigr)+\cdots\Bigr] \label{eq:63-Enj-expand} \end{equation}

This includes the rest energy $mc^2$ (it is the formula of Chapter 61 with $l$ replaced by $j$). Since the levels are determined by $n$ and $j$, $2\mathrm{s}_{1/2}$ and $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$ remain degenerate, and only $2\mathrm{p}_{3/2}$ lies above them. For $n=3$, the levels split into three: $3\mathrm{s}_{1/2}=3\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$, $3\mathrm{p}_{3/2}=3\mathrm{d}_{3/2}$, and $3\mathrm{d}_{5/2}$. For any $n$, two states with the same $n$ and $j$ but different $l=j\pm\tfrac12$ are degenerate (only the state with $l=n-1$ and $j=n-\tfrac12$ has no partner). This degeneracy is a property peculiar to the Coulomb potential, and it does not hold in shielded many-electron atoms (such as the $3\mathrm{s}$ and $3\mathrm{p}$ levels of alkali atoms; Chapter 18, Section 18.5).

63.5.2 Advanced Topic: The exact solution of the Dirac equation

In fact, the Dirac equation for the Coulomb potential can be solved exactly, without perturbation theory (Darwin and Gordon, 1928). The result contains the whole expansion of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-Enj-expand} in powers of $(Z\alpha)$, and it takes a form that depends only on $j$. The derivation is the four-component, spin-including version of the procedure for solving the Schrödinger equation of the hydrogen atom (Chapter 15). Since it is long, this section may be skipped as an “advanced topic.” The result is stated as Theorem 63.7.

Advanced Topic: Radial equations of the Dirac equation in a central force field and the exact eigenvalues

Step 1. Separation of the angular part. In a central force field, the total angular momentum $\hat{\bm{J}}=\hat{\bm{L}}+\hbar\bm{\sigma}/2$ and its $z$ component are conserved (Section 62.6 of the previous chapter). We therefore use the two-component spin-angular functions $\Omega_{\kappa m_j}(\theta,\varphi)$ (simultaneous eigenfunctions of $\hat{J}^2,\hat{J}_z,\hat{L}^2$, obtained by combining $Y_l^{m_l}$ and the spin function $\chi_s$ with Clebsch–Gordan coefficients; Chapter 25). We define the quantum number $\kappa$ by $\kappa=-(l+1)$ for $j=l+\tfrac12$ and $\kappa=+l$ for $j=l-\tfrac12$ (so that $\abs{\kappa}=j+\tfrac12$). The following two properties hold.

\begin{equation} \Bigl(\frac{\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{L}}}{\hbar}+1\Bigr)\Omega_{\kappa m_j}=-\kappa\,\Omega_{\kappa m_j},\qquad (\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{e}_r)\,\Omega_{\kappa m_j}=-\Omega_{-\kappa m_j} \label{eq:63-Omega} \end{equation}

(Here $\bm{e}_r=\bm{r}/r$ is the unit vector in the radial direction. The first relation follows from the fact that $\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{L}}/\hbar=2\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}/\hbar^2$ equals $l$ for $j=l+\tfrac12$ and $-(l+1)$ for $j=l-\tfrac12$ (Chapter 25). As for the second relation, $\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{e}_r$ is an operator that is a scalar under rotations, leaves $j,m_j$ unchanged, reverses only the parity, and takes $l$ to $l'=2j-l$, and the relation is a phase convention compatible with $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{e}_r)^2=1$.)

The sign of the second relation can be checked with the simplest example. For $\kappa=-1$ ($\mathrm{s}_{1/2}$, $l=0$) and $m_j=+\tfrac12$, we have $\Omega_{-1,1/2}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}(1,0)^{\mathsf T}$, and multiplying by $\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{e}_r=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\,e^{-i\varphi}\\\sin\theta\,e^{i\varphi}&-\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ gives $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{e}_r)\Omega_{-1,1/2}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta\,e^{i\varphi})^{\mathsf T}$. On the other hand, the state with $\kappa=+1$ ($\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$, $l=1$) and $m_j=+\tfrac12$ can be written with Clebsch–Gordan coefficients (Condon–Shortley convention, Chapter 25) as $-\sqrt{\tfrac13}\,Y_1^0\,\alpha+\sqrt{\tfrac23}\,Y_1^1\,\beta$, and substituting $Y_1^0=\sqrt{3/4\pi}\cos\theta$ and $Y_1^1=-\sqrt{3/8\pi}\sin\theta\,e^{i\varphi}$ gives $\Omega_{+1,1/2}=-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta\,e^{i\varphi})^{\mathsf T}$. Therefore $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{e}_r)\Omega_{-1,1/2}=-\Omega_{+1,1/2}$, and the second relation of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-Omega} holds. (For other $\kappa,m_j$ as well, it holds with the same sign if the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients are taken in the same convention. This sign determines the signs in the radial equations below.)

Let the wave function be

\begin{equation} \psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_A\\\psi_B\end{pmatrix}=\frac1r\begin{pmatrix}G(r)\,\Omega_{\kappa m_j}\\[1mm] i\,F(r)\,\Omega_{-\kappa m_j}\end{pmatrix} \label{eq:63-ansatz} \end{equation}

Here $G,F$ are real functions of the radial coordinate, and the normalization condition is $\int_0^\infty(G^2+F^2)\,\dd r=1$.

Step 2. Radial representation of $\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}$. Multiply both sides of the identity $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{e}_r)(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}})=\bm{e}_r\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}+i\bm{\sigma}\cdot(\bm{e}_r\times\hat{\bm{p}})=\bm{e}_r\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}+\dfrac{i}{r}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{L}}$ (the same as in Theorem 63.2, the calculation of Formula 63.1) from the left by $\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{e}_r$ and use $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{e}_r)^2=1$. This gives $\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}=(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{e}_r)\Bigl[-i\hbar\dfrac{\partial}{\partial r}+\dfrac{i\hbar}{r}\dfrac{\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{L}}}{\hbar}\Bigr]$ ($\bm{e}_r\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}=-i\hbar\,\partial_r$). Acting on the product of a radial function $f(r)$ and $\Omega_{\kappa m_j}$ and using the first relation of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-Omega}, we obtain, with $f=G/r$,

$$\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}\Bigl[\frac{G}{r}\Omega_{\kappa m_j}\Bigr]=(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{e}_r)\Omega_{\kappa m_j}\cdot(-i\hbar)\Bigl[\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\frac{G}{r}+\frac{\kappa+1}{r}\frac{G}{r}\Bigr]=\frac{i\hbar}{r}\Bigl(G'+\frac{\kappa}{r}G\Bigr)\Omega_{-\kappa m_j}$$

(we used $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd r}\dfrac{G}{r}=\dfrac{G'}{r}-\dfrac{G}{r^2}$ and the second relation of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-Omega}). Replacing $\kappa\to-\kappa$, we also obtain $\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}}\Bigl[\dfrac{F}{r}\Omega_{-\kappa m_j}\Bigr]=\dfrac{i\hbar}{r}\Bigl(F'-\dfrac{\kappa}{r}F\Bigr)\Omega_{\kappa m_j}$.

Step 3. The radial equation. We substitute these into the block forms, Eqs. \eqref{eq:63-blockA} and \eqref{eq:63-blockB} ($\bm{A}=\bm{0}$, $\hat{\bm{\pi}}=\hat{\bm{p}}$). Both sides of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-blockB} are proportional to $\Omega_{-\kappa m_j}$, which gives $(E-V+mc^2)\dfrac{iF}{r}=c\,\dfrac{i\hbar}{r}\Bigl(G'+\dfrac{\kappa}{r}G\Bigr)$. Both sides of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-blockA} are proportional to $\Omega_{\kappa m_j}$, which gives $(E-V-mc^2)\dfrac{G}{r}=c\cdot i\cdot\dfrac{i\hbar}{r}\Bigl(F'-\dfrac{\kappa}{r}F\Bigr)=-\dfrac{c\hbar}{r}\Bigl(F'-\dfrac{\kappa}{r}F\Bigr)$. Rearranging, we obtain

\begin{align} \frac{\dd G}{\dd r}+\frac{\kappa}{r}G&=\frac{E+mc^2-V}{\hbar c}\,F, \label{eq:63-radG}\\ \frac{\dd F}{\dd r}-\frac{\kappa}{r}F&=-\frac{E-mc^2-V}{\hbar c}\,G \label{eq:63-radF} \end{align}

These are the radial equations of the Dirac equation in a central force field (the signs are those obtained when the phase convention of $\Omega_{\kappa m_j}$ is taken as in Eq. \eqref{eq:63-Omega}). For the Coulomb potential $V=-Z\alpha\hbar c/r$, the radial equations become

$$G'+\frac{\kappa}{r}G=\Bigl(k_++\frac{Z\alpha}{r}\Bigr)F,\qquad F'-\frac{\kappa}{r}F=\Bigl(k_--\frac{Z\alpha}{r}\Bigr)G$$

for a bound state ($\abs{E}\lt mc^2$), where $k_\pm=(mc^2\pm E)/\hbar c$ and $\Lambda=\sqrt{k_+k_-}=\sqrt{m^2c^4-E^2}/\hbar c$.

Step 4. Asymptotic form. For $r\to\infty$ the $1/r$ terms drop out, leaving $G'\approx k_+F$ and $F'\approx k_-G$. From these, $G''=k_+k_-G=\Lambda^2G$, so we choose the decaying solution $G,F\sim e^{-\Lambda r}$. For $r\to0$ the $1/r$ terms dominate, and putting $G=g\,r^\gamma$ and $F=f\,r^\gamma$ gives $(\gamma+\kappa)g=Z\alpha\,f$ and $(\gamma-\kappa)f=-Z\alpha\,g$. Eliminating $g,f$ from the two relations gives $\gamma^2-\kappa^2=-(Z\alpha)^2$, that is,

\begin{equation} \gamma=\sqrt{\kappa^2-(Z\alpha)^2} \label{eq:63-gamma} \end{equation}

(the positive root, for which the wave function is well behaved at the origin). For the states with $\abs{\kappa}=1$ ($1\mathrm{s}_{1/2}$, $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$, etc.), $\gamma$ is real for $Z\alpha\lt1$, that is, for $Z\lt137$. This $\gamma$ is the exponent of the radial function at the origin, and it is a different quantity from the Lorentz factor $\gamma_{\mathrm L}$ of Chapter 61 and from the Dirac $\gamma^\mu$ matrices (see the box in Section 61.1.6).

Step 5. Series solution. Matching the ratio $F/G=-\sqrt{k_-/k_+}$ at $r\to\infty$, we put

$$G=\sqrt{k_+}\,e^{-\Lambda r}r^\gamma\bigl[\mathcal{P}(r)+\mathcal{Q}(r)\bigr],\qquad F=-\sqrt{k_-}\,e^{-\Lambda r}r^\gamma\bigl[\mathcal{P}(r)-\mathcal{Q}(r)\bigr]$$

(where $\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}$ are functions of $r$; writing the solution in the sum-and-difference form makes the coefficients come out neatly later, when we take the sum and the difference of the two equations). The substitution proceeds as follows. By the product rule, $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd r}\bigl[e^{-\Lambda r}r^\gamma(\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{Q})\bigr]=e^{-\Lambda r}r^\gamma\Bigl[\mathcal{P}'+\mathcal{Q}'-\Lambda(\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{Q})+\dfrac{\gamma}{r}(\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{Q})\Bigr]$. Substituting into the first equation of Step 3, $G'+\dfrac{\kappa}{r}G=\Bigl(k_++\dfrac{Z\alpha}{r}\Bigr)F$, and dividing both sides by the common factors $e^{-\Lambda r}r^\gamma$ and $\sqrt{k_+}$, the right-hand side becomes $-\sqrt{k_-/k_+}\,\bigl(k_++Z\alpha/r\bigr)(\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{Q})=-\Bigl(\Lambda+\dfrac{Z\alpha}{r}\sqrt{\dfrac{k_-}{k_+}}\Bigr)(\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{Q})$ (since $k_+\sqrt{k_-/k_+}=\sqrt{k_+k_-}=\Lambda$). The term $-\Lambda(\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{Q})$ on the left-hand side and the term $+\Lambda(\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{Q})$ that appears when the right-hand side is moved over combine into $-2\Lambda\mathcal{Q}$. The second equation, $F'-\dfrac{\kappa}{r}F=\Bigl(k_--\dfrac{Z\alpha}{r}\Bigr)G$, is treated in the same way if we divide by $-\sqrt{k_-}$ and use $k_-\sqrt{k_+/k_-}=\Lambda$, and we obtain

\begin{align} \mathcal{P}'+\mathcal{Q}'-2\Lambda\mathcal{Q}+\frac{(\gamma+\kappa)(\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{Q})+Z\alpha\sqrt{k_-/k_+}\,(\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{Q})}{r}&=0, \label{eq:63-step5a}\\ \mathcal{P}'-\mathcal{Q}'+2\Lambda\mathcal{Q}+\frac{(\gamma-\kappa)(\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{Q})-Z\alpha\sqrt{k_+/k_-}\,(\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{Q})}{r}&=0 \label{eq:63-step5b} \end{align}

Now take the sum and the difference of these two equations. From $k_\pm=(mc^2\pm E)/\hbar c$ and $\Lambda=\sqrt{m^2c^4-E^2}/\hbar c$, we have

$$\sqrt{\frac{k_-}{k_+}}-\sqrt{\frac{k_+}{k_-}}=\frac{k_--k_+}{\Lambda}=-\frac{2E}{\sqrt{m^2c^4-E^2}},\qquad\sqrt{\frac{k_-}{k_+}}+\sqrt{\frac{k_+}{k_-}}=\frac{k_-+k_+}{\Lambda}=\frac{2mc^2}{\sqrt{m^2c^4-E^2}}$$

Let $\nu\equiv\dfrac{Z\alpha E}{\sqrt{m^2c^4-E^2}}$ and $\eta\equiv\dfrac{Z\alpha\,mc^2}{\sqrt{m^2c^4-E^2}}$ (so that $\eta^2-\nu^2=(Z\alpha)^2$); then $Z\alpha\bigl(\sqrt{k_-/k_+}-\sqrt{k_+/k_-}\bigr)=-2\nu$ and $Z\alpha\bigl(\sqrt{k_-/k_+}+\sqrt{k_+/k_-}\bigr)=2\eta$. In the sum of Eqs. \eqref{eq:63-step5a} and \eqref{eq:63-step5b}, $\mathcal{Q}'$ and $\pm2\Lambda\mathcal{Q}$ cancel, leaving $2\mathcal{P}'+\bigl[2(\gamma-\nu)\mathcal{P}+2(\kappa-\eta)\mathcal{Q}\bigr]/r=0$, while the difference gives $2\mathcal{Q}'-4\Lambda\mathcal{Q}+\bigl[2(\kappa+\eta)\mathcal{P}+2(\gamma+\nu)\mathcal{Q}\bigr]/r=0$. Multiplying each by $r/2$ and taking $\rho=2\Lambda r$ as the variable ($r\,\dd/\dd r=\rho\,\dd/\dd\rho$, $2\Lambda r=\rho$), the equations become

\begin{align} \rho\frac{\dd\mathcal{P}}{\dd\rho}+(\gamma-\nu)\mathcal{P}+(\kappa-\eta)\mathcal{Q}&=0, \label{eq:63-seriesA}\\ \rho\frac{\dd\mathcal{Q}}{\dd\rho}-\rho\mathcal{Q}+(\gamma+\nu)\mathcal{Q}+(\kappa+\eta)\mathcal{P}&=0 \label{eq:63-seriesB} \end{align}

Substituting $\mathcal{P}=\sum_kp_k\rho^k$ and $\mathcal{Q}=\sum_kq_k\rho^k$ and comparing the coefficients of $\rho^k$, we obtain

$$(k+\gamma-\nu)\,p_k+(\kappa-\eta)\,q_k=0,\qquad(k+\gamma+\nu)\,q_k-q_{k-1}+(\kappa+\eta)\,p_k=0$$

For $k=0$, the condition for the two equations to have a nontrivial solution $p_0,q_0$ is the vanishing of the coefficient determinant, $(\gamma-\nu)(\gamma+\nu)-(\kappa-\eta)(\kappa+\eta)=\gamma^2-\kappa^2+(\eta^2-\nu^2)=\gamma^2-\kappa^2+(Z\alpha)^2=0$, which agrees with $\gamma$ of Step 4 (so there is no contradiction). If the series does not terminate, it behaves as $\mathcal{Q}\sim e^{\rho}$ ($q_k/q_{k-1}\to1/k$), so $G,F\sim e^{+\Lambda r}$ diverge and cannot be normalized. For the series to terminate at order $k=n_r$ ($p_k=q_k=0$ for $k\gt n_r$), the second equation at $k=n_r+1$ requires $q_{n_r}=0$, and the first equation at $k=n_r$ then gives $(n_r+\gamma-\nu)p_{n_r}=0$. Since $p_{n_r}\ne0$, we have

\begin{equation} \nu=\frac{Z\alpha E}{\sqrt{m^2c^4-E^2}}=n_r+\gamma\qquad(n_r=0,1,2,\dots) \label{eq:63-quant} \end{equation}

Note that for $n_r=0$ we have $q_0=0$, and the second equation at $k=0$ gives $(\kappa+\eta)p_0=0$; that is, $\kappa=-\eta\lt0$ ($j=l+\tfrac12$) is required. States with $\kappa\gt0$ require $n_r\ge1$. Let us solve Eq. \eqref{eq:63-quant} for $E$. From $(Z\alpha)^2E^2=(n_r+\gamma)^2(m^2c^4-E^2)$ we get $E^2\bigl[(Z\alpha)^2+(n_r+\gamma)^2\bigr]=(n_r+\gamma)^2m^2c^4$, and the solution with $E\gt0$ is

$$E=\frac{mc^2}{\sqrt{1+(Z\alpha)^2/(n_r+\gamma)^2}}$$

Using the principal quantum number $n=n_r+\abs{\kappa}$ ($n_r=n-j-\tfrac12$), we can write $n_r+\gamma=n-\abs{\kappa}+\sqrt{\kappa^2-(Z\alpha)^2}=n-\bigl[(j+\tfrac12)-\sqrt{(j+\tfrac12)^2-(Z\alpha)^2}\bigr]=n-\delta_j$.

Theorem 63.7: The exact Dirac levels of a hydrogen-like atom

The bound-state energies (including the rest energy) of the Dirac equation for an electron in the Coulomb field of a nuclear charge $Ze_0$ (a point charge) are

\begin{equation} E_{nj}=mc^2\left[1+\frac{(Z\alpha)^2}{(n-\delta_j)^2}\right]^{-1/2},\qquad \delta_j=j+\frac12-\sqrt{\Bigl(j+\frac12\Bigr)^2-(Z\alpha)^2} \label{eq:63-Dirac-Enj} \end{equation}

($n=1,2,\dots$, $j=\tfrac12,\tfrac32,\dots,n-\tfrac12$). The levels are determined by $n$ and $j$ alone, and the two states with $l=j\pm\tfrac12$ are degenerate (the level with $l=n-1$, $j=n-\tfrac12$ consists of a single state).

Why does the energy depend only on $j$? In the derivation, the energy contains $\kappa$ only through $\gamma=\sqrt{\kappa^2-(Z\alpha)^2}$ and $n_r=n-\abs{\kappa}$. Both are functions of $\abs{\kappa}=j+\tfrac12$ only. Therefore the two states with the same $j$, $l=j-\tfrac12$ ($\kappa=-(j+\tfrac12)$) and $l=j+\tfrac12$ ($\kappa=+(j+\tfrac12)$), have equal energies even though their parities are opposite. This is the reason why the levels do not depend on $l$ and are determined by $j$ alone (the states with $n_r=0$ have only $\kappa\lt0$ and have no partner). This degeneracy between $\kappa$ and $-\kappa$ is peculiar to the Coulomb potential, and it does not hold for potentials of other forms (for example, a shielded many-electron atom).

This formula has the same form as the fine-structure formula that Sommerfeld derived in 1916 from an elliptical-orbit model (the Bohr–Sommerfeld model) in which special relativity was incorporated into Bohr's quantum theory (in Sommerfeld's formula, in the place corresponding to $j+\tfrac12$, there appeared the azimuthal quantum number $k=1,\dots,n$, which expresses the angular momentum of the orbit). In an era when spin was not yet known, the formula was, in effect, correctly reproducing the effect of the spin–orbit interaction “by accident,” through the relativistic motion of the orbit. The Dirac equation gave that formula a physical basis.

Let us expand Eq. \eqref{eq:63-Dirac-Enj} in powers of $(Z\alpha)^2$. From $\delta_j\approx\dfrac{(Z\alpha)^2}{2(j+1/2)}$ we get $\dfrac{(Z\alpha)^2}{(n-\delta_j)^2}\approx\dfrac{(Z\alpha)^2}{n^2}+\dfrac{(Z\alpha)^4}{n^3(j+1/2)}$, and using $[1+x]^{-1/2}=1-\tfrac{x}{2}+\tfrac38x^2-\cdots$, we obtain

$$E_{nj}=mc^2\Bigl[1-\frac{(Z\alpha)^2}{2n^2}-\frac{(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3(j+\frac12)}+\frac{3(Z\alpha)^4}{8n^4}+\cdots\Bigr]=mc^2\Bigl[1-\frac{(Z\alpha)^2}{2n^2}-\frac{(Z\alpha)^4}{2n^3}\Bigl(\frac{1}{j+\frac12}-\frac{3}{4n}\Bigr)+\cdots\Bigr]$$

which agrees with Eq. \eqref{eq:63-Enj-expand}. The values obtained by expanding the exact result of the Dirac equation up to $(Z\alpha)^4$ agree with the sum of the three terms obtained by perturbation theory, for every $n=1,2,3,\dots$ and all $(l,j)$.

63.5.3 Comparison with experiment — the $n=2$ levels and the Lamb shift

Let us follow the $n=2$ levels of the hydrogen atom as we add the correction terms one at a time (Figure 63.5). In the Bohr levels, $2\mathrm{s}$ and $2\mathrm{p}$ (eight states) are degenerate. The mass-velocity term lowers the level with the smaller $l$ more (for $2\mathrm{s}$: $-35.6\ \mathrm{GHz}$, for $2\mathrm{p}$: $-6.4\ \mathrm{GHz}$). The Darwin term raises only $2\mathrm{s}$, by $+21.9\ \mathrm{GHz}$. The spin–orbit term splits $2\mathrm{p}$ into $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$ ($-7.3\ \mathrm{GHz}$) and $2\mathrm{p}_{3/2}$ ($+3.65\ \mathrm{GHz}$); as a result $2\mathrm{s}_{1/2}$ and $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$ become degenerate (Dirac's result), and $2\mathrm{p}_{3/2}$ lies $10.95\ \mathrm{GHz}$ above them. Experiment showed that yet one more stage of correction is needed.

The Dirac equation predicted $2\mathrm{p}_{3/2}-2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}=10.95\ \mathrm{GHz}$ to within $0.2\ \%$ of the experimental value $10.969\ \mathrm{GHz}$ (the small difference is the sum of the electron's $g$ factor being $2.0023$ ($+0.23\ \%$), the recoil correction due to the finite mass of the atomic nucleus, other radiative corrections, and so on). On the other hand, the degeneracy of $2\mathrm{s}_{1/2}$ and $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$ was something the Dirac equation predicted exactly. For a long time, neither spectroscopists nor theorists doubted this.

Then, in 1947, Lamb and Retherford found, in an experiment using microwaves, that $2\mathrm{s}_{1/2}$ lies about $1000\ \mathrm{MHz}$ above $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$ (later precise measurements showed the spacing to be $1057.8\ \mathrm{MHz}$ ($4.37\ \mu\mathrm{eV}$, $0.0353\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$)). What they made use of is that the $2\mathrm{s}_{1/2}$ state is metastable. Because the electric dipole transition $2\mathrm{s}\to1\mathrm{s}$ is forbidden ($\Delta l=\pm1$ is required; Chapter 52), the lifetime of $2\mathrm{s}$ is long, about $0.12\ \mathrm{s}$, whereas $2\mathrm{p}$ falls to $1\mathrm{s}$ in about $1.6\ \mathrm{ns}$. By directing microwaves at a stream of metastable hydrogen atoms and observing how the metastable atoms decrease through the $2\mathrm{s}\to2\mathrm{p}$ transition, they measured the small energy difference between $2\mathrm{s}$ and $2\mathrm{p}$. This is the Lamb shift, and it is evidence that the degeneracy of the Dirac equation (an electron with a point charge and spin) is broken.

Immediately after this discovery, Bethe (1947), in a nonrelativistic calculation of the interaction between the electron and the electromagnetic field (photons), estimated that the raising of $2\mathrm{s}$ is about $1040\ \mathrm{MHz}$, and showed that this is close to the experimental value. Subsequently, the renormalization theory of Sin-Itiro Tomonaga, Schwinger, Feynman, and others (quantum electrodynamics, QED) gave a precise explanation of the Lamb shift. The main cause is the effect of the electron reabsorbing the virtual photons it emits itself (the electron's self-energy), which amounts to a more accurate evaluation of how the electron's position is smeared out, as in the Darwin term. The effect of virtual electron–positron pairs forming in vacuum and shielding the charge of the nucleus (vacuum polarization) also makes a smaller contribution. The deviation of the electron's $g$ factor from $2$ ($g-2$) is also a radiative correction of the same quantum electrodynamics (Chapter 65) (Related simulator: explanation of Elementary Particles §6.3). Incidentally, the hyperfine structure of $1\mathrm{s}$ and $2\mathrm{s}$ (the interaction with the nuclear spin; the $21\ \mathrm{cm}$ hydrogen line is a transition of this $1\mathrm{s}$ hyperfine structure) is treated in elementary particle physics (AD 09, Chapter 7, Section 7.2 (in Japanese)) as part of the four layers of hydrogen. Example 63.10: Size of the fine structure of the Lyman-$\alpha$ line

The Lyman-$\alpha$ line of hydrogen ($1\mathrm{s}\to2\mathrm{p}$, wavelength $\lambda=121.567\ \mathrm{nm}$) splits into two lines, corresponding to $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$ and $2\mathrm{p}_{3/2}$. (1) From the difference of the wavenumbers of the two lines, $\Delta\tilde{\nu}=0.3652\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, find the wavelength difference $\Delta\lambda=\lambda^2\Delta\tilde{\nu}$ and the resolution $\lambda/\Delta\lambda$ required to separate the two lines. (2) Find the Doppler broadening (full width at half maximum $\lambda\sqrt{8k_{\mathrm B}T\ln2/(Mc^2)}$, where $M$ is the mass of a hydrogen atom) of hydrogen atoms at room temperature ($300\ \mathrm{K}$), and state whether the two lines can be separated.

Solution. (1) We have $\lambda=121.567\ \mathrm{nm}=1.21567\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{cm}$, and $\Delta\lambda=\lambda^2\Delta\tilde{\nu}=(1.21567\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{cm})^2\times0.3652\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}=5.397\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{cm}=0.540\ \mathrm{pm}$. The resolution is $\lambda/\Delta\lambda=121.567\ \mathrm{nm}/0.540\ \mathrm{pm}=2.25\times10^5$. (2) With $T=300\ \mathrm{K}$ and $M=1.6735\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $\sqrt{8k_{\mathrm B}T\ln2/(Mc^2)}=\sqrt{8\times1.3806\times10^{-23}\times300\times0.6931/(1.6735\times10^{-27}\times(2.998\times10^8)^2)}=1.236\times10^{-5}$, and multiplying by $\lambda$ gives $\Delta\lambda_{\mathrm D}=1.50\ \mathrm{pm}$. This is about three times the separation of the two lines, $0.54\ \mathrm{pm}$, so the Lyman-$\alpha$ line of hydrogen gas at room temperature cannot be separated into two lines because of the Doppler broadening, and it looks like a single line. To resolve it, we need either to cool the atoms ($0.76\ \mathrm{pm}$ at $77\ \mathrm{K}$, $0.27\ \mathrm{pm}$ at $10\ \mathrm{K}$) or to use a method that is free from Doppler broadening (saturated absorption or two-photon spectroscopy).

63.5.4 The exact 1s state — shape and small component

Dirac's exact solution tells us not only the energy but also the form of the wave function. Let us write down the simplest case, $1\mathrm{s}_{1/2}$ ($n=1$, $\kappa=-1$, $n_r=0$). In the series of the previous section, since $n_r=0$, we have $\mathcal{Q}=0$ and $\mathcal{P}=p_0$ (a constant). Therefore $G=\sqrt{k_+}\,p_0\,e^{-\Lambda r}r^\gamma$ and $F=-\sqrt{k_-}\,p_0\,e^{-\Lambda r}r^\gamma$, with $\gamma=\sqrt{1-(Z\alpha)^2}$, and the energy is $E=\gamma\,mc^2$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:63-Dirac-Enj} with $n=1$, $j=\tfrac12$). Then, from $\hbar c\Lambda=\sqrt{m^2c^4-E^2}=mc^2\sqrt{1-\gamma^2}=mc^2\,Z\alpha$, we get $\Lambda=Z\alpha\,mc/\hbar=Z/a_0$.

Theorem 63.8: The exact $1\mathrm{s}_{1/2}$ state

With $\gamma=\sqrt{1-(Z\alpha)^2}$, the following holds:

\begin{equation} E_{1s}=\gamma\,mc^2,\qquad G(r)=N\,r^\gamma e^{-Zr/a_0},\qquad F(r)=-\sqrt{\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}}\;G(r) \label{eq:63-1s} \end{equation}

Here $F/G$ is a constant independent of $r$, so the small component has the same form as the large component. The normalized radial density is $P(r)=G^2+F^2=\dfrac{(2Z/a_0)^{2\gamma+1}}{\Gamma(2\gamma+1)}\,r^{2\gamma}e^{-2Zr/a_0}$.

We used $F/G=-\sqrt{k_-/k_+}=-\sqrt{(mc^2-E)/(mc^2+E)}=-\sqrt{(1-\gamma)/(1+\gamma)}$. The normalization follows from $\int_0^\infty r^{2\gamma}e^{-2\Lambda r}\dd r=\Gamma(2\gamma+1)/(2\Lambda)^{2\gamma+1}$ and $G^2+F^2=G^2\bigl[1+\tfrac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\bigr]$. For $Z\alpha\to0$ ($\gamma\to1$), $P(r)=4(Z/a_0)^3r^2e^{-2Zr/a_0}$, and we return to the radial distribution of the 1s orbital of the Schrödinger equation (Chapter 17). From Theorem 63.8, the following quantities are determined (Figure 63.6).

  • Weight of the small component: $\dfrac{F^2}{G^2+F^2}=\dfrac{1-\gamma}{2}$ (the weight of the large component is $\dfrac{1+\gamma}{2}$). It is $1.3\times10^{-5}$ for hydrogen, $0.91\ \%$ for iron, $3.0\ \%$ for silver, $9.1\ \%$ for gold, and $12.9\ \%$ for uranium.
  • Most probable radius: the maximum of $P(r)$ is at the maximum of $r^{2\gamma}e^{-2\Lambda r}$, that is, at $r=\gamma/\Lambda=\gamma a_0/Z$. It shrinks to $\gamma$ times the Schrödinger value $a_0/Z$.
  • Mean radius: $\expval{ r}=\dfrac{\Gamma(2\gamma+2)}{2\Lambda\,\Gamma(2\gamma+1)}=\dfrac{(2\gamma+1)\,a_0}{2Z}$. It shrinks to $\dfrac{2\gamma+1}{3}$ times the Schrödinger value $\dfrac{3a_0}{2Z}$.

The $Z\alpha=1$ wall: What about elements with $Z\gt137$?

In Eq. \eqref{eq:63-gamma}, $\gamma=\sqrt{\kappa^2-(Z\alpha)^2}$ becomes imaginary for states with $\abs{\kappa}=1$ ($1\mathrm{s}_{1/2}$, $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$, and so on) when $Z\alpha\gt1$, that is, $Z\gt137$. For a point-charge nucleus, when $\gamma$ becomes imaginary the solution $r^\gamma$ near the origin oscillates, and the energy $E_{nj}$ cannot be determined (for the 1s state at $Z=137$, $E=\gamma mc^2\approx11.7\ \mathrm{keV}$, and the binding energy is about $499\ \mathrm{keV}$). This is the limitation of the idealization in which the nucleus is regarded as a point charge with no size. A real atomic nucleus has a finite size, and inside the nucleus the potential does not diverge at the origin but becomes flat, so the 1s level can be defined even for $Z\gt137$. It has been calculated that, as $Z$ increases, the 1s level keeps going down and reaches the negative-energy continuum states ($E=-mc^2$) at $Z\approx173$. Beyond that, the 1s level dives into the Dirac sea (Chapter 64), and it becomes possible for electron–positron pairs to be created from the vacuum. $Z\alpha\sim1$ is a rough indication of where the picture of a point charge and the one-body Dirac equation reaches its limit.

63.6 Relativistic Effects in Heavy Elements and Materials — The Color of Gold, Mercury, the Lead-Acid Battery, and Spin–Orbit Coupling

For hydrogen, the fine structure is only $5\times10^{-5}$ times the Bohr level. Relativity has nothing at all to do with the chemistry of hydrogen. But as the nuclear charge increases, the quantity $(Z\alpha)^2$ that sets the size of the correction grows rapidly. In this section we use Dirac's exact solutions (Theorems 63.7 and 63.8) to estimate the magnitude of the relativistic effects on the core electrons of heavy elements, and to see how these effects show up in the color and melting point of materials, the voltage of batteries, and electronic structure.

63.6.1 A table of $Z\alpha$ — at what fraction of the speed of light does a 1s electron move?

A rough measure of the speed of a 1s electron is $v\approx Z\alpha c$ (the Bohr model; Chapter 17, Section 17.1). Therefore $Z\alpha$ is the $v/c$ of the 1s electron, and $(Z\alpha)^2$ is a rough measure of the size of the relativistic correction. For hydrogen $v/c=0.73\ \%$, whereas for gold it is $0.58$ and for uranium $0.67$, reaching more than half the speed of light. The exact Dirac 1s state (Theorem 63.8) contains the exponent $\gamma=\sqrt{1-(Z\alpha)^2}$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:63-gamma}. This is the reciprocal of the Lorentz factor $\gamma_{\mathrm L}=1/\sqrt{1-(Z\alpha)^2}$ of Chapter 61, Table 61.1 ($\gamma=1/\gamma_{\mathrm L}$); for Au it is $1/1.224=0.8171$. Using this $\gamma$, the most probable radius shrinks to $\gamma$ times and the mean radius to $(2\gamma+1)/3$ times the nonrelativistic value, and the weight of the small component is $(1-\gamma)/2$.

Intuition: The electron's “getting heavier” effect shrinks the Bohr radius

The Bohr radius is $a_0=4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2/(me_0^2)$, which is inversely proportional to the electron mass $m$. If we suppose that the relativistic mass increase raises the mass of a fast-moving electron to $\gamma_{\mathrm L}m$ ($\gamma_{\mathrm L}=1/\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}$), the radius shrinks to $1/\gamma_{\mathrm L}$ times its original value. For a 1s electron $v/c=Z\alpha$, so the factor of shrinkage is $\sqrt{1-(Z\alpha)^2}=\gamma$, which agrees exactly with the ratio of the most probable radii of the exact Dirac 1s state. This simple picture captures well the fact that an $s$ electron moves fast very close to the nucleus, becomes heavier, and shrinks.

63.6.2 The 1s binding energy — the depth of the inner shell of an atom

The total energy of the 1s level is $E_{1s}=\gamma mc^2$, and the binding energy (the depth below the rest energy) is

\begin{equation} B_{1s}=mc^2\bigl(1-\gamma\bigr)=mc^2\Bigl[1-\sqrt{1-(Z\alpha)^2}\Bigr]=\frac{Z^2}{2}\alpha^2mc^2\Bigl[1+\frac{(Z\alpha)^2}{4}+\cdots\Bigr] \label{eq:63-B1s} \end{equation}

where $\tfrac12\alpha^2mc^2=13.606\ \mathrm{eV}$ is the Rydberg energy. The nonrelativistic Bohr value $Z^2\times13.606\ \mathrm{eV}$ is multiplied by the factor $1+(Z\alpha)^2/4$.

The measured K absorption edge is $80.7\ \mathrm{keV}$ for Au and $115.6\ \mathrm{keV}$ for U, considerably smaller than the Dirac values for the hydrogen-like ions, $93.5$ and $132.3\ \mathrm{keV}$. This is because the nuclear charge seen by the 1s electron of a neutral atom is shielded by the other electrons, so the binding is weaker. If we consider only the shielding by the other electron of the K shell, the effective nuclear charge is not $Z$ but $Z-1$ (Moseley's law; Chapter 15, Section 15.6). However, even the Dirac formula $mc^2(1-\gamma)$ for the hydrogen-like ion with $Z-1$ gives $90.9\ \mathrm{keV}$ for Au ($Z-1=78$) and $128.9\ \mathrm{keV}$ for U ($Z-1=91$), which still exceed the measured values. This is because the shielding by the outer electrons also acts, and the effective nuclear charge becomes even smaller than $Z-1$. The measured values are also shifted slightly by the size of the nucleus and by QED corrections (the Lamb shift of the 1s level of hydrogen-like uranium is about $0.5\ \mathrm{keV}$).

63.6.3 Three kinds of relativistic effects

The relativistic effects in heavy elements are classified into the following three kinds.

  • Direct effect: The $s$ orbitals and $p_{1/2}$ orbitals penetrate close to the nucleus and move fast there. Because of the mass-velocity term and the Darwin term, these orbitals contract and are stabilized (the energy is lowered).
  • Indirect effect: When the inner $s$ and $p$ orbitals contract, they shield the nuclear charge more effectively. As a result, the outer $d$ and $f$ orbitals, whose effective attraction to the nucleus is weakened, expand and are destabilized (the energy is raised). Since $d$ and $f$ orbitals do not penetrate close to the nucleus, they are hardly affected by the direct effect, and the indirect effect is larger.
  • Spin–orbit splitting: Orbitals with $l\ge1$ split into $j=l\pm\tfrac12$ (Section 63.3). The splitting increases rapidly in proportion to $Z^4$.

Computational methods that incorporate these effects include the “scalar-relativistic” approximation, which includes only the mass-velocity term and the Darwin term; two-component methods that also include the spin–orbit interaction (ZORA, Douglas–Kroll–Hess, X2C); and four-component methods that solve the Dirac equation as it stands (Dirac–Hartree–Fock, Dirac–Kohn–Sham). In the core-electron part of the pseudopotentials and PAW used in first-principles calculations of materials, the relativistic effects are built in beforehand (for density functional theory, see AD 07 (in Japanese); for a general introduction to the Dirac equation and spinors, see AD 05, Chapter 7, Section 7.10). Related simulator: Relativistic-QM explanation §4.4, the relativistic free-electron gas (§6), and the relativistic version of the Thomas–Fermi model (§8)).

63.6.4 The color of gold

Silver shines white, and gold shines yellow. Both are noble metals with a filled $d$ shell ($\mathrm{Ag}$: $4d^{10}5s^1$, $\mathrm{Au}$: $5d^{10}6s^1$), and although their electron configurations are of the same type, their colors differ. The color of a metal is determined by the minimum energy of the electronic transitions that absorb light. Let us go through this step by step.

① The valence electrons of a metal form bands (energy bands; Chapter 41) in the crystal. The band of $s$ electrons (the $s$ band) is filled up to the Fermi level, and below it lies the band of $d$ electrons (the $d$ band). The main process that absorbs light is the interband transition, which excites an electron at the top of the $d$ band into an empty $s$ ($p$) state just above the Fermi level. We call the minimum energy required for this transition the threshold $E_{\mathrm{th}}$. Photons with energy larger than $E_{\mathrm{th}}$ are absorbed.

② The photon energy of visible light ($400$–$700\ \mathrm{nm}$) is $1.77$–$3.10\ \mathrm{eV}$. If $E_{\mathrm{th}}$ lies inside this range, the side with energy higher than $E_{\mathrm{th}}$ (blue to violet) is absorbed and the lower side (red to yellow) is reflected, so the reflected light is tinged with yellow or red.

③ If $E_{\mathrm{th}}$ is larger than $3.10\ \mathrm{eV}$, no photon of visible light can cause an interband transition, and none is absorbed. The reflected light does not change color, and the metal has a white metallic luster.

In gold, this threshold is about $2.4\ \mathrm{eV}$ (wavelength about $517\ \mathrm{nm}$), which lies inside the band of visible light, so blue to green light is absorbed. In silver, the threshold is about $3.7$–$3.9\ \mathrm{eV}$ (wavelength about $320$–$335\ \mathrm{nm}$, ultraviolet), so visible light is not absorbed and silver looks white. The cause of this difference is the magnitude of the relativistic effect in gold ($Z=79$). The $6s$ orbital contracts and is stabilized, and the $5d$ orbital expands through an indirect effect and is destabilized, so the gap between $5d$ and $6s$ becomes smaller than it would be if relativity were ignored. Band-structure calculations have shown that, when relativity is switched off, the interband transition energy of gold becomes as large as that of silver, and gold too would look white like silver (review: Pyykkö and Desclaux, 1979). The threshold of copper is about $2.1\ \mathrm{eV}$, lower still, and copper absorbs green to blue and takes on a reddish color. Related simulator: explanation of Elementary Particles (§2.2–2.3: speed of a 1s electron $v=Z\alpha c$, the color of gold and mercury).

This effect, which overlaps with the lanthanide contraction, appears commonly in the chemistry of the heavy elements of the sixth period (the inert-pair effect, by which Tl$^+$, Pb$^{2+}$, and Bi$^{3+}$ are stable).

63.6.6 The voltage of the lead-acid battery

The lead-acid battery, widely used as an automobile battery, consists of $\mathrm{Pb}$ and $\mathrm{PbO_2}$ electrodes immersed in dilute sulfuric acid, and its electromotive force is about $2.1\ \mathrm{V}$ per cell, higher than that of most aqueous batteries (about $1.2$–$1.5\ \mathrm{V}$). The first-principles calculation of Ahuja et al. (Phys. Rev. Lett. 106, 018301 (2011)) reported that about $1.7\ \mathrm{V}$ of this $2.1\ \mathrm{V}$ comes from relativistic effects (if relativity is switched off, the electromotive force is only about $0.4\ \mathrm{V}$) (the numbers are the values in the paper). The explanation is that, because the $6s$ orbital of Pb contracts and is stabilized by relativity, $\mathrm{PbO_2}$, which is Pb(IV), becomes a strong oxidizing agent (it readily accepts electrons), and the potential difference from metallic lead, Pb(0), becomes large. The lead-acid battery can also be called an example of an application of relativistic quantum mechanics.

63.6.7 Materials in which the spin–orbit interaction plays the leading role

The spin–orbit interaction plays the leading role in determining the electronic states of materials containing heavy elements. The $\xi(r)\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ derived in this chapter is the starting point of the following topics in materials science (the details are treated in the companion volumes; here we only announce the names).

  • 5d transition-metal oxides: In $\mathrm{Ir^{4+}}$ ($5d^5$) of $\mathrm{Sr_2IrO_4}$, strong spin–orbit coupling splits $t_{2g}$ into $j_{\mathrm{eff}}=\tfrac32$ and $\tfrac12$, and the $j_{\mathrm{eff}}=\tfrac12$ band becomes a Mott insulator because of electron–electron repulsion (Kim et al., 2008).
  • Topological insulators: In $\mathrm{Bi_2Se_3}$ and similar materials, the spin–orbit interaction causes “band inversion,” and massless Dirac electrons (Chapter 65) appear on the surface.
  • Rashba effect: At interfaces and surfaces without inversion symmetry, the spin–orbit interaction splits the electron bands in spin according to the wavenumber.
  • Magnetic anisotropy: The property that the magnetic moment of a magnet tends to point in a particular direction (magnetic anisotropy) also has its origin in the spin–orbit interaction (Solid-State Physics (AD 06, Chapter 20 (in Japanese))).

To treat the inner shells of heavy elements and the chemistry of superheavy elements, one must solve the four-component Dirac equation. Density functional theory also uses the relativistic Dirac–Kohn–Sham equation (AD 07 (in Japanese)). In the next chapter (Chapter 64), we examine the meaning of the negative-energy solutions of the Dirac equation and follow the path that leads to the prediction of the antiparticle (the positron).

63.7.1 Summary

  • Minimal coupling: With $\hat{\bm{p}}\to\hat{\bm{\pi}}=\hat{\bm{p}}-q\bm{A}$ and $i\hbar\partial_t\to i\hbar\partial_t-q\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$ we obtain the Dirac equation in an electromagnetic field, $i\hbar\partial_t\psi=[c\,\bm{\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}+\beta mc^2+V]\psi$ ($V=q\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$). For the electron, $q=-e_0$ ($\hat{\bm{\pi}}=\hat{\bm{p}}+e_0\bm{A}$). The form is unchanged under the gauge transformation $\bm{A}\to\bm{A}+\nabla\chi$, $\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}\to\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}-\partial_t\chi$, $\psi\to e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\psi$. The covariant form is $i\hbar\gamma^\mu D_\mu\psi=mc\,\psi$, $D_\mu=\partial_\mu+iqA_\mu/\hbar$.
  • Large and small components: $(E-V-mc^2)\psi_A=c\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}\,\psi_B$, $(E-V+mc^2)\psi_B=c\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}\,\psi_A$. The small component is $\psi_B=c\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}\,\psi_A/(E-V+mc^2)\approx\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}}\,\psi_A/2mc$, and its magnitude is $v/2c$ times that of the large component.
  • The Pauli equation and $g=2$: From $\sigma_i\sigma_j=\delta_{ij}+i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k$ and $\hat{\bm{\pi}}\times\hat{\bm{\pi}}=i\hbar q\bm{B}$ we get $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2=\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2-q\hbar\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}$. To lowest order, $i\hbar\partial_t\psi_{\mathrm P}=[\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2/2m+q\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}-(q\hbar/2m)\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{B}]\psi_{\mathrm P}$. Comparing with $\bm{\mu}=g(q/2m)\hat{\bm{S}}$ gives $g=2$. For the electron, $\bm{\mu}=-g\mu_{\mathrm B}\hat{\bm{S}}/\hbar$ with $\mu_{\mathrm B}=e_0\hbar/2m_{\mathrm e}=9.274\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J/T}=57.88\ \mu\mathrm{eV/T}=13.996\ \mathrm{GHz/T}$. An electron ($q=-e_0$) is in a uniform magnetic field $\bm{B}=B\bm{e}_z$, and the vector potential is $\bm{A}=(-By/2,\,Bx/2,\,0)$. (1) Write down $\hat{\pi}_x,\hat{\pi}_y,\hat{\pi}_z$ and find $\comm{\hat{\pi}_x}{\hat{\pi}_y}$. (2) Compute $(\bm{\sigma}\cdot\hat{\bm{\pi}})^2$ in components and show that it equals $\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2+e_0\hbar B\,\sigma_z$. (3) Expand $\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2/2m$ and show that it becomes $\dfrac{\hat{\bm{p}}^2}{2m}+\dfrac{e_0B}{2m}\hat{L}_z+\dfrac{e_0^2B^2}{8m}(x^2+y^2)$, and verify that the part of the Pauli equation that is first order in the magnetic field is $\dfrac{\mu_{\mathrm B}B}{\hbar}\bigl(\hat{L}_z+2\hat{S}_z\bigr)$.

Hint: $\hat{\pi}_x=\hat{p}_x+e_0A_x$, and so on. $\comm{\hat{p}_x}{x}=-i\hbar$. For (3), use $\hat{\bm{\pi}}^2=\hat{\bm{p}}^2+e_0(\hat{\bm{p}}\cdot\bm{A}+\bm{A}\cdot\hat{\bm{p}})+e_0^2\bm{A}^2$ and $x\hat{p}_y-y\hat{p}_x=\hat{L}_z$.

Compare the predicted splitting of hydrogen $2\mathrm{p}$ (with the Thomas factor ignored and with it included) with the experimental value $10.969\ \mathrm{GHz}$, and describe the role of the Thomas factor. What happens to the splitting if $g=2.0023$ is used?
