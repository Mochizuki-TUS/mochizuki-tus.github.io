Appendix BPhysical Constants, Units, Symbols, and Index of Terms

The language of quantum mechanics is made up of symbols such as $\hbar$ (h-bar), $\hat{H}$ (Hamiltonian), and $\ket{\psi}$ (ket), of units such as $\mathrm{eV}$ (electron volt) and $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (wavenumber), and of hundreds of terms. Moreover, the same letter may stand for different quantities in different parts ($\alpha$ is a spin function, the fine-structure constant, and also the exponent of a Gaussian function), and the notation may also differ in the companion volumes. This appendix is a “quick reference” that lets you look all of these up in one place. When you come across an unfamiliar letter, unit, or word while reading the main text, please open this appendix first. Every entry comes with its pronunciation and a one-line meaning, together with a link to the chapter and section where the main text first explains it properly. If you follow the link back, you can reread the definition and the examples.

In high-school and university physics, many people have probably memorized values such as “$h=6.6\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$.” In quantum mechanics, the relations among the constants (for example, $a_0=\hbar/(m_{\mathrm e}c\alpha)$ and $E_{\mathrm h}=\alpha^2m_{\mathrm e}c^2$) come into view, and you also start to wonder how accurately each value is determined. In the table in B.1, we distinguish the constants that became defined values with zero uncertainty in the 2019 revision of the SI (International System of Units) from the constants that are measured experimentally. B.2 contains a conversion table for the energy units used in the atomic world ($\mathrm{eV}$, $E_{\mathrm h}$, $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, $\mathrm{K}$, and so on), together with atomic units, natural units, and the correspondence with Gaussian units.

B.1 Table of Physical Constants

The values are the 2018 CODATA (Committee on Data for Science and Technology) recommended values (the sources are given in B.7). Those marked defined value are constants that the May 2019 revision of the SI decided to “fix at that value,” and they carry no error (they are not quantities determined by measurement; rather, the meter and the kilogram are determined from these values). A “value determined from the defined values” is a quantity that can be calculated exactly from a combination of defined values (for example, $R=N_{\mathrm A}k_{\mathrm B}$); in this book we show its first several digits and indicate the continuation with $\ldots$. The thin spaces separating the digits of the numerical values are for readability and do not affect the values. Relations among the constants — fewer numbers to remember

Almost all the constants of the atomic world can be written as combinations of $e_0$, $\hbar$, $c$, $m_{\mathrm e}$, and $\varepsilon_0$. If you know the following relations, you do not have to memorize many values one by one.

$$ \alpha=\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c},\qquad a_0=\frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{m_{\mathrm e}e_0^2}=\frac{\hbar}{m_{\mathrm e}c\,\alpha},\qquad E_{\mathrm h}=\frac{\hbar^2}{m_{\mathrm e}a_0^2}=\alpha^2m_{\mathrm e}c^2, $$ $$ R_\infty=\frac{\alpha^2m_{\mathrm e}c}{2h},\qquad \mu_{\mathrm B}=\frac{e_0\hbar}{2m_{\mathrm e}},\qquad \lambda_{\mathrm C}=\frac{h}{m_{\mathrm e}c},\qquad r_{\mathrm e}=\alpha^2a_0,\qquad c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\mu_0}} . $$

For example, $a_0=\hbar/(m_{\mathrm e}c\alpha)=\bar{\lambda}_{\mathrm C}/\alpha=3.8616\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm{m}\times137.036=5.2918\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m}$ and $E_{\mathrm h}=\alpha^2m_{\mathrm e}c^2=(1/137.036)^2\times0.511\,00\ \mathrm{MeV}=27.211\ \mathrm{eV}$, so the scale of the atom follows from nothing but the rest energy of the electron and $\alpha$. The speed of the 1s electron in hydrogen is $\alpha c=2.1877\times10^6\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (about $1/137$ of the speed of light), so an electron in an atom moves at only a tiny fraction of the speed of light.

B.2.4 Atomic Units (Hartree Units)

The atomic units introduced in Chapter 33 form the system of units obtained by setting $\hbar=m_{\mathrm e}=e_0=4\pi\varepsilon_0=1$, which makes the units of mass, charge, action, and the electric constant equal to 1 at the same time. The results of quantum-chemical calculations (in Hartree units) are converted back to $\mathrm{eV}$ or $\mathrm{kJ/mol}$ with the conversions below. In the world of atomic units, the speed of light is $c=1/\alpha=137.036$, the Bohr magneton is $\mu_{\mathrm B}=1/2$, and the Coulomb force is simply $1/r^2$.

B.2.5 Natural Units $\hbar=c=1$

In elementary particle physics and relativistic quantum mechanics (Chapter 61), we use natural units, in which we set $\hbar=c=1$. Then mass, energy, and momentum all have the same unit (usually $\mathrm{MeV}$ or $\mathrm{GeV}$), and length and time are measured in the reciprocal of that unit. To restore $\hbar$ and $c$ in a formula, it suffices to match the dimensions (for example, a formula for a length takes the form $\hbar c/E$). The key to the conversion is $\hbar c=197.326\,980\,459\ldots\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm}$. As a rule, the formulas in this book keep $\hbar$ and $c$ explicit.

B.2.6 Correspondence Between Gaussian Units and SI

The companion volumes “Linear Response Theory (AD 08)” and “Solid-State Physics (AD 06),” among others, as well as older textbooks, write the formulas of electromagnetism in Gaussian units. The formulas in SI and in Gaussian units differ only in the choice of the unit of charge; the physics is the same. The most frequently used correspondence is the following: $$e^2_{\mathrm{Gauss}}\ \longleftrightarrow\ \frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\qquad(=\alpha\hbar c=1.439\,964\,548\ \mathrm{eV\,nm}).$$ In other words, whenever $e^2$ appears in a formula in Gaussian units, we simply replace it by $e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ in SI. In atomic units (B.2.4) we set $4\pi\varepsilon_0=1$, so the formulas take the same form as in Gaussian units.

Symbols with More Than One Meaning (Caution)

There are only about 100 symbols, even counting the Latin alphabet and the Greek letters together, whereas there are hundreds of physical quantities, so the same letter stands for different quantities in different parts, and sometimes even within the same chapter. For example, $\alpha$ is a function that describes the spin direction, the fine-structure constant, the exponent of a Gaussian function, and also a diagonal element in the Hückel method. Which meaning is intended is determined by the part and chapter in which the letter is used, its subscripts, and its units. The table below lists the letters that this book uses with more than one meaning, together with the section in which each meaning first appears (it collects the symbols listed in the tables of the individual parts).

Caution: How to tell apart different quantities that share the same letter

  1. Look at the subscripts: For many letters the convention is to distinguish the quantities by subscripts, as in $\alpha$ (spin function, fine-structure constant) and $\alpha_{\mathrm p}$ (polarizability), $\alpha_{\mathrm{abs}}$ (absorption coefficient), $\alpha_i$ (Dirac matrices). For $\sigma$ (spin variable) and $\sigma_x$ (Pauli matrix), the matrix always carries a subscript.
  2. Look at the typeface: Letters are distinguished by context and typeface, as in $J$ (exchange integral, total angular momentum) and $\mathcal{J}$ (superexchange coupling constant), or $\Gamma$ (irreducible representation) and the $\Gamma$ point (the center of $k$-space). $\phi$ (orbital, trial function) and $\varphi$ (azimuthal angle) are different letterforms.
  3. Look at the units: $\mu\ [\mathrm{kg}]$ is the reduced mass, $\mu\ [\mathrm{J/T}]$ is the magnetic moment, and $\mu_0\ [\mathrm{N/A^2}]$ is the vacuum permeability. If the units are written as well, there is no confusion.
  4. Look at the note at the beginning of the chapter: In Chapter 20, where the meaning of $\lambda$ changes, in Chapter 58, where $t$ takes three different meanings, and elsewhere, the text states explicitly, “In this chapter we denote … by …”.
