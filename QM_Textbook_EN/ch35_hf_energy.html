Chapter 35Energy in the Hartree–Fock Approximation — Ground and Excited States, Coulomb and Exchange Integrals

In Chapter 33, we decided to approximate the wave function of a system with two or more electrons by a Slater determinant $\Phi$, built by lining up one-electron states (spin orbitals). The job of this chapter is to actually compute the energy $E=\mel{\Phi}{\hat{H}}{\Phi}$ of that $\Phi$, without skipping a single line. Once the energy can be computed, the variational principle (Chapter 30) lets us search for “which set of orbitals is the best approximation” (the Hartree–Fock equations of the next chapter, Chapter 36, are exactly that). Moreover, once the energies of excited states can be computed, we can also estimate the color of the light that a material absorbs or emits (the energy of an electronic transition). This chapter also puts the electron configurations, term symbols, and Hund's rules that we saw in Chapter 34 (Many-Electron Atoms) on a firm footing, in the language of the energy of a Slater determinant.

The result of the calculation is easy to grasp. For every electron pair, the energy of a Slater determinant is built from just the following three kinds of quantities. The first is the one-electron energy $h_{aa}$ (the kinetic energy of an electron and its attraction to the atomic nucleus). The second is the Coulomb integral $U_{ab}$: the electrostatic repulsion energy between two electron clouds; a quantity of the same form also appears in classical electromagnetism (AD 03, Chapter 36, Section 36.8 (in Japanese), the potential produced by a continuously distributed charge). The third is the exchange integral $J_{ab}$: it has no counterpart in classical physics, and is a quantity that arises from the fact that “identical electrons are indistinguishable” together with the Pauli exclusion principle. $J_{ab}$ takes a positive value, and lowers by $J_{ab}$ the energy of only those electron pairs whose spins are parallel. From this single point follow the explanations of Hund's rules (the more the spins are aligned and the electrons are placed in separate orbitals, the more stable the state) and of the energy difference between the singlet and the triplet of the helium atom. The magnetism of materials, such as the ferromagnetism of iron, also has this exchange interaction as its starting point.

In this chapter, we decide “which terms vanish and which terms survive” when the determinant is expanded, using just two rules. (i) Orthogonality of the spin functions, $\int\alpha^*\beta\,\dd\sigma=0$. (ii) Orthogonality of different orbitals, $\int\psi_1^*\psi_2\,\dd\bm{r}=0$. We check this criterion term by term for the ground state, the singly excited state, and the doubly excited state of the two-electron system, and finally extend it to the general case of $N$ electrons (the Slater–Condon rules). We will also see that the theoretical limit (25%) of the light-emission efficiency of organic EL (OLED) and the materials design of thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) are directly tied to the magnitude of the $J_{ab}$ found here.

35.1 The Two-Electron System in Spin Orbitals — Notation, Level Diagrams, and the Hamiltonian

35.1.1 What are we computing? — Starting from the two-electron system

Consider a system with two electrons (a helium atom, a hydrogen molecule, a quantum dot confining two electrons, and so on). We keep the positions of the atomic nuclei fixed (the Born–Oppenheimer approximation; Chapter 33, Section 33.2). Suppose that the one-electron orbitals $\psi_1,\psi_2,\dots$ are prepared, numbered in order of increasing energy, and that they are orthogonal to one another and normalized: $\int\psi_a^*\psi_b\,\dd\bm{r}=\delta_{ab}$. The task of this chapter is to write down how to place two electrons in these orbitals as a Slater determinant that satisfies the Pauli exclusion principle, and to find its energy expectation value. Since the determinant is $2\times2$, its expansion has only two terms, and we can write out the intermediate steps without skipping a single line. The general case of $N$ electrons is treated in Section 35.7.

The calculation here is the first step of the Hartree–Fock approximation. The Hartree–Fock approximation means approximating a many-electron wave function by a single Slater determinant. In the first half of this chapter (Sections 35.2–35.6), we take the orbitals $\psi_a$ to be arbitrary (merely orthogonal to one another and normalized) and construct the energy expression for a single Slater determinant. The Hartree–Fock equations (Chapter 36) are the result of optimizing the orbitals by the variational principle, and we denote the energy of the Slater determinant of the optimal orbitals by $E_{\mathrm{HF}}$ (orbital optimization is first put to use in Brillouin's theorem, Section 35.5.4). Whatever the orbitals are, computing the energy expectation value $E=\mel{\Phi}{\hat{H}}{\Phi}$ yields, in addition to the one-electron energy, two kinds of terms arising from the Coulomb repulsion between electrons—the Coulomb term, which expresses the repulsion between charge clouds, and the exchange term, which originates from the Pauli exclusion principle. We check below, one term at a time, what each of these two terms is.

35.1.2 Spin orbitals $\chi_a$, $\bar{\chi}_a$ and the level diagram

The state of an electron is expressed as the product of a spatial function $\psi_a(\bm{r})$ and a spin function. The spin functions are $\alpha(\sigma)$, for which the $z$ component of the spin is $+\hbar/2$ (spin up $\uparrow$, also written $\ket{\uparrow}$), and $\beta(\sigma)$, for which it is $-\hbar/2$ (spin down $\downarrow$, also written $\ket{\downarrow}$) (Chapter 23, Section 23.5). For the coordinate of an electron, we combine the spatial coordinate $\bm{r}$ and the spin variable $\sigma=\pm\tfrac12$ and write $\bm{x}=(\bm{r},\sigma)$; an integral over $\bm{x}$ means taking the integral over the spatial coordinate $\bm{r}$ together with the sum over the spin variable $\sigma$ (below we write $\int\dd\bm{x}=\int\dd\bm{r}\int\dd\sigma$).

Definition 35.1: Spin orbitals $\chi_a$, $\bar{\chi}_a$

The one-electron states obtained by multiplying a spatial orbital $\psi_a(\bm{r})$ by a spin function,

\begin{equation} \chi_a(\bm{x})=\psi_a(\bm{r})\,\alpha(\sigma),\qquad \bar{\chi}_a(\bm{x})=\psi_a(\bm{r})\,\beta(\sigma) \label{eq:35-chi} \end{equation}

are called spin orbitals. The subscript $a=1,2,\dots$ corresponds to the quantum number of the orbital, and the orbitals are numbered in order of increasing energy. The bar over the letter ($\bar{\chi}$) indicates that the state has $\beta$ spin (down). From the same spatial orbital $\psi_a$, two spin orbitals, $\chi_a$ and $\bar{\chi}_a$, are made.

Written out separately for electron 1 and electron 2, Eq. \eqref{eq:35-chi} reads as follows:

$$ \begin{cases} \chi_1(\bm{x}_1)=\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\,\alpha(\sigma_1)\\ \bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)=\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\,\beta(\sigma_1) \end{cases} \qquad \begin{cases} \chi_1(\bm{x}_2)=\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\,\alpha(\sigma_2)\\ \bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2)=\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\,\beta(\sigma_2) \end{cases} $$

Electron 1 and electron 2 simply substitute their own coordinates into the same function $\chi_1$, and nothing other than the subscript of the coordinate marks which electron is which.

The spin functions have the following orthogonality under integration (summation) over the spin variable:

\begin{equation} \int\alpha^*(\sigma)\alpha(\sigma)\,\dd\sigma=\int\beta^*(\sigma)\beta(\sigma)\,\dd\sigma=1,\qquad \int\alpha^*(\sigma)\beta(\sigma)\,\dd\sigma=\int\beta^*(\sigma)\alpha(\sigma)\,\dd\sigma=0 \label{eq:35-spin} \end{equation}

(Since $\alpha(\sigma)$ is a function that equals 1 only at $\sigma=+\tfrac12$ and $\beta(\sigma)$ is a function that equals 1 only at $\sigma=-\tfrac12$, $\alpha^*\beta$ is 0 at every $\sigma$, and so the sum is also 0.) This orthogonality is the first rule that decides whether a term vanishes in the calculations below. Similarly, for the orbitals we use $\int\psi_a^*\psi_b\,\dd\bm{r}=\delta_{ab}$ (the second rule). The inner product of two spin orbitals is the product of these two rules:

\begin{equation} \braket{\chi_a}{\chi_b}=\braket{\psi_a}{\psi_b}\cdot1=\delta_{ab},\qquad \braket{\chi_a}{\bar{\chi}_b}=\braket{\psi_a}{\psi_b}\cdot\underbrace{\int\alpha^*\beta\,\dd\sigma}_{0}=0 \label{eq:35-chiorth} \end{equation}

Which spin orbitals the two electrons occupy is represented by a level diagram. A horizontal line is a spatial orbital $\psi_a$ (the lower the line, the lower the energy), and an arrow is an electron; an up arrow represents $\alpha$ spin and a down arrow represents $\beta$ spin. Figure 35.1 shows two states in which two electrons are placed in two orbitals, $\psi_1$ (lower) and $\psi_2$ (higher). On the left is the ground state $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ (g stands for ground), in which both electrons are in the lower orbital $\psi_1$; on the right is the doubly excited state $\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}$ (ex stands for excited), in which both electrons have been raised to the higher orbital $\psi_2$. At most two electrons, with spins in opposite directions, can occupy the same orbital (the Pauli exclusion principle).

Shorthand (vertical bars). Below, we write the two-electron Slater determinant (the convention of Chapter 33: rows are electrons, columns are orbitals) by enclosing in vertical bars the spin orbitals placed in the columns:

\begin{equation} \Phi=\lvert\chi_a\chi_b\rvert\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \begin{vmatrix} \chi_a(\bm{x}_1)&\chi_b(\bm{x}_1)\\ \chi_a(\bm{x}_2)&\chi_b(\bm{x}_2) \end{vmatrix} \label{eq:35-bar} \end{equation}

This is a notation that indicates which spin orbitals are placed in the columns of the determinant, and it serves to distinguish subscripted $\Phi$'s such as $\Phi_{\mathrm g}=\lvert\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1\rvert$. The wave function itself is, as in Chapter 33, the determinant (or the expanded expression, as in Section 35.1.3). Also, the two-electron product $\mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{v}{\chi_c\chi_d}$ in a matrix element (Section 35.3) is a different notation, without vertical bars.

35.1.3 Expanding the Slater determinant of the ground state

The ground state $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ is the state in which the two electrons are in the spin orbitals $\chi_1$ (spin up in the lower level) and $\bar{\chi}_1$ (spin down in the lower level). Writing the Slater determinant in the convention of Chapter 33 (rows are electrons, columns are orbitals, coefficient $1/\sqrt{N!}$), we have

\begin{equation} \Phi_{\mathrm g}(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \begin{vmatrix} \chi_1(\bm{x}_1)&\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)\\ \chi_1(\bm{x}_2)&\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2) \end{vmatrix} =\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\,\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2)-\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)\,\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\Bigr] \label{eq:35-Phig} \end{equation}

(The $2\times2$ determinant is “upper left × lower right − upper right × lower left.”) Below, we also write the state represented by this function as $\ket{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}$. Substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:35-chi} and separating the spin functions from the orbital functions, we obtain

\begin{align} \Phi_{\mathrm g} &=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\alpha(\sigma_1)\,\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\beta(\sigma_2)-\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\beta(\sigma_1)\,\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\alpha(\sigma_2)\Bigr] \notag\\ &=\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\alpha(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2)-\alpha(\sigma_2)\beta(\sigma_1)\Bigr] \label{eq:35-Phig2} \end{align}

In the second line, we used the fact that the two terms have the same spatial part $\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)$ to factor it out (the spin functions are numbers that commute with each other, so their order can be interchanged: $\beta(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2)=\alpha(\sigma_2)\beta(\sigma_1)$).

Intuition: Space is symmetric, spin is antisymmetric

The spatial part $\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Phig2} does not change when electrons 1 and 2 are interchanged (symmetric). The spin part $\bigl[\alpha(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2)-\alpha(\sigma_2)\beta(\sigma_1)\bigr]/\sqrt2$ changes sign under the interchange (antisymmetric). This is none other than the singlet of two spin-$\tfrac12$ particles (total spin $S=0$) (Chapter 25, Section 25.8). Since “symmetric × antisymmetric = antisymmetric,” the Pauli exclusion principle is satisfied. Two electrons in the same orbital have no choice but to form a singlet with spins in opposite directions.

Example 35.1: Normalization of $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$

Verify, by carrying out the integrals, that $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Phig2} satisfies $\braket{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}=1$. Here $\psi_1$ is normalized.

Solution. Since the spatial part and the spin part are in product form, the integral also becomes a product:

$$ \braket{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}} =\underbrace{\int\lvert\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\rvert^2\dd\bm{r}_1}_{1}\ \underbrace{\int\lvert\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\rvert^2\dd\bm{r}_2}_{1}\cdot\frac12\iint\Bigl\lvert\alpha(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2)-\beta(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2)\Bigr\rvert^2\dd\sigma_1\dd\sigma_2 . $$

We expand the integrand of the spin integral. Using $\lvert A-B\rvert^2=\lvert A\rvert^2+\lvert B\rvert^2-A^*B-AB^*$ and letting $A=\alpha(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2)$ and $B=\beta(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2)$, we have

$$ \iint\lvert A\rvert^2=\int\lvert\alpha\rvert^2\dd\sigma_1\int\lvert\beta\rvert^2\dd\sigma_2=1\cdot1,\qquad \iint\lvert B\rvert^2=\int\lvert\beta\rvert^2\dd\sigma_1\int\lvert\alpha\rvert^2\dd\sigma_2=1\cdot1, $$ $$ \iint A^*B=\underbrace{\int\alpha^*(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_1)\dd\sigma_1}_{0}\cdot\int\beta^*(\sigma_2)\alpha(\sigma_2)\dd\sigma_2=0,\qquad \iint AB^*=0 . $$

Therefore the spin integral is $1+1-0-0=2$, and $\braket{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}=1\cdot1\cdot\tfrac12\cdot2=1$. The coefficient $1/\sqrt2$ plays the following role: because the overlap of the two terms is 0 (they are orthogonal), dividing by 2 normalizes the function.

35.1.4 The Hamiltonian and the one-electron and two-electron terms

The Hamiltonian of a two-electron system around a single atomic nucleus (nuclear charge $Z$) is given, in atomic units (Chapter 33, Section 33.1), by

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=\underbrace{-\frac12\nabla_1^2-\frac{Z}{r_1}}_{\hat{h}_1}\ \underbrace{-\frac12\nabla_2^2-\frac{Z}{r_2}}_{\hat{h}_2}\ +\ \underbrace{\frac{1}{r_{12}}}_{v} \qquad(r_i=\lvert\bm{r}_i\rvert,\ \ r_{12}=\lvert\bm{r}_1-\bm{r}_2\rvert) \label{eq:35-Ham} \end{equation}

This is for a helium-type system with two electrons: $Z=1$ gives the hydride ion $\mathrm{H^-}$, $Z=2$ gives the helium atom, and $Z=3$ gives $\mathrm{Li^+}$. The Hamiltonian consists of three kinds of terms: the kinetic energy ($-\tfrac12\nabla^2$), the attraction to the nucleus ($-Z/r$), and the Coulomb repulsion between the electrons ($1/r_{12}$). In atomic units, the unit of energy is the hartree $E_{\mathrm h}$ and the unit of length is the Bohr radius $a_0$, and $1/r_{12}$ corresponds to $e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r_{12})$ in the SI system of units. For molecules and solids, we need only replace the term for the attraction to the nucleus by a general one-electron potential $V_{\mathrm{ext}}(\bm{r})$. We therefore let the Hamiltonian of a single electron alone be

\begin{equation} \hat{h}(\bm{r})=-\frac12\nabla^2+V_{\mathrm{ext}}(\bm{r})\qquad(\text{for an atom, }V_{\mathrm{ext}}(\bm{r})=-Z/r) \label{eq:35-h} \end{equation}

The repulsion $1/r_{12}$ between two electrons is written with the same symbol $v$ as in Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Ham}, and inside the bra and ket of an integral it is abbreviated $v\equiv1/r_{12}$ ($\mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{v}{\chi_c\chi_d}$ is an integral with the two-electron operator $v$ sandwiched in, and it is something different from the one-electron potential $V_{\mathrm{ext}}$). The one-electron operator is written with a hat, $\hat{h}$ ($\hat{h}_1,\hat{h}_2$), as in Chapters 33 and 34. The two-electron term $v$ is the operation of multiplying by the function of the coordinates $1/r_{12}$, and since it is used as a name inside the integral symbol, it carries no hat, as in Chapters 36 and 37.

Caution: $\hat{h}_1$ and $\hat{h}_2$ are “$\hat{h}(\bm{r}_1)$ and $\hat{h}(\bm{r}_2)$”

In Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Ham}, $\hat{h}_1,\hat{h}_2$ are not separate operators: they are the same operator $\hat{h}$ with its variable set to the coordinate $\bm{r}_1$ of electron 1 and the coordinate $\bm{r}_2$ of electron 2, that is, $\hat{h}_1=\hat{h}(\bm{r}_1)$ and $\hat{h}_2=\hat{h}(\bm{r}_2)$. Therefore, for example, $\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}_1}{\chi_1}$ and $\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}_2}{\chi_1}$ have the same value, differing only in the name of the integration variable (the value of an integral does not change when the integration variable is renamed).

What matters here is that, in the Hamiltonian of Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Ham}, the spin variable $\sigma$ does not appear (we ignore terms that act on the spin, such as the spin–orbit interaction). Hence, when each term of $\hat{H}$ is applied to the wave function, the spin function simply “passes through,” and the spin integral is just an inner product. Concretely, we write the one-electron and two-electron integrals as follows, and compute the spin part separately from the orbital part.

Definition 35.2: Notation for one-electron and two-electron integrals

For spin orbitals $\chi_a,\chi_b,\chi_c,\chi_d$ (with spin functions $s_a,s_b,s_c,s_d$), we define

\begin{align} h_{ab}&\equiv\mel{\chi_a}{\hat{h}}{\chi_b}=\int\chi_a^*(\bm{x})\,\hat{h}(\bm{r})\,\chi_b(\bm{x})\,\dd\bm{x}=\mel{\psi_a}{\hat{h}}{\psi_b}\,\braket{s_a}{s_b}, \label{eq:35-hab}\\ \mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\chi_c\chi_d}&\equiv\iint\chi_a^*(\bm{x}_1)\,\chi_b^*(\bm{x}_2)\,\frac{1}{r_{12}}\,\chi_c(\bm{x}_1)\,\chi_d(\bm{x}_2)\,\dd\bm{x}_1\dd\bm{x}_2 =\mel{\psi_a\psi_b}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\psi_c\psi_d}\,\braket{s_a}{s_c}\braket{s_b}{s_d} \label{eq:35-brk} \end{align}

In the bra (ket), the first $\chi$ corresponds to the coordinate of electron 1 and the second $\chi$ to the coordinate of electron 2 ($a,c$ belong to electron 1 and $b,d$ to electron 2). Here $\braket{s}{s'}$ is the inner product of spin functions and takes the values in Table 35.1. The integral $\mel{\psi_a\psi_b}{1/r_{12}}{\psi_c\psi_d}$ involving orbitals only is obtained by replacing $\chi$ with $\psi$ and turning the integral over $\bm{x}$ into an integral over $\bm{r}$.

By Eqs. \eqref{eq:35-hab} and \eqref{eq:35-brk}, whether each term that appears when the determinant is expanded is zero is decided by the following two-step test.

  1. Spin test: for each electron, are the spin functions on the bra side and on the ket side the same (Table 35.1)? If the spin functions differ for even one electron, the term is 0.
  2. Orbital test: is the orbital integral of a term that survived the spin test zero? In particular, a factor involving only a single electron, $\braket{\psi_a}{\psi_b}$ ($a\ne b$), is 0 by orbital orthogonality.

In the sections that follow, we carry out this test once for each term.

35.2 The Energy Expectation Value of the Ground State

35.2.1 Splitting the expectation value into three parts

The energy expectation value of the ground state is the integral in which $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Phig} is sandwiched by $\hat{H}$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Ham}. Writing the complex conjugate of Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Phig} on the left and the function itself on the right, we obtain

\begin{align} \mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}} =\iint\dd\bm{x}_1\dd\bm{x}_2\ \frac12&\Bigl[\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_1)\bar{\chi}_1^*(\bm{x}_2)-\bar{\chi}_1^*(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_2)\Bigr]\notag\\ &\times\Bigl(\hat{h}_1+\hat{h}_2+\frac{1}{r_{12}}\Bigr)\Bigl[\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2)-\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\Bigr] \label{eq:35-Eg0} \end{align}

Since the three operators in the parentheses are added, we also split the expectation value into three parts and compute them one at a time:

$$ \mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}=\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{h}_1}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}+\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{h}_2}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}+\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}} . $$

Each part is a sum of integrals of four terms, obtained by multiplying the two square brackets together. Each term splits into a product of an “integral over electron 1” and an “integral over electron 2,” so it suffices to apply the two-step test (spin, orbital) of Section 35.1.4 to each term.

35.2.2 The one-electron term $\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{h}_1}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}$

Since $\hat{h}_1$ acts only on the coordinate $\bm{r}_1$ of electron 1, it does not act on the wave function of electron 2. Multiplying out the two square brackets of Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Eg0} into four terms, we get

\begin{align} \mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{h}_1}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}=\frac12\Bigl[ &\underbrace{\int\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_1)\hat{h}_1\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\dd\bm{x}_1}_{\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_1}}\int\bar{\chi}_1^*(\bm{x}_2)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2)\dd\bm{x}_2 +\underbrace{\int\bar{\chi}_1^*(\bm{x}_1)\hat{h}_1\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)\dd\bm{x}_1}_{\mel{\bar{\chi}_1}{\hat{h}}{\bar{\chi}_1}}\int\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_2)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\dd\bm{x}_2\notag\\ &-\int\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_1)\hat{h}_1\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)\dd\bm{x}_1\underbrace{\int\bar{\chi}_1^*(\bm{x}_2)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\dd\bm{x}_2}_{0} -\int\bar{\chi}_1^*(\bm{x}_1)\hat{h}_1\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\dd\bm{x}_1\underbrace{\int\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_2)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2)\dd\bm{x}_2}_{0}\Bigr] \label{eq:35-h1exp} \end{align}

Let us test each of the four terms.

  • Term 1: the factor for electron 2 is $\int\bar{\chi}_1^*\bar{\chi}_1\dd\bm{x}_2=\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_1}\braket{\beta}{\beta}=1$. The factor for electron 1 is $\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_1}=\mel{\psi_1}{\hat{h}}{\psi_1}\braket{\alpha}{\alpha}=h_{11}$.
  • Term 2: similarly, the factor for electron 2 is 1, and the factor for electron 1 is $\mel{\bar{\chi}_1}{\hat{h}}{\bar{\chi}_1}=\mel{\psi_1}{\hat{h}}{\psi_1}\braket{\beta}{\beta}=h_{11}$. The factors for electron 1 in Terms 1 and 2 have the same value (because $\chi_1$ and $\bar{\chi}_1$ have the same spatial part $\psi_1$, and $\hat{h}$ does not act on the spin).
  • Terms 3 and 4: the factor for electron 2 is $\int\bar{\chi}_1^*\chi_1\dd\bm{x}_2=\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_1}\int\beta^*\alpha\,\dd\sigma_2=0$ (and $\int\alpha^*\beta\,\dd\sigma_2=0$). Because the spin integral of the $\alpha^*\beta$ type is 0, these terms vanish. (The factor for electron 1 is also 0, since $\int\alpha^*\beta\,\dd\sigma_1=0$.)

From the above, we obtain

\begin{equation} \mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{h}_1}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}=\frac12h_{11}+\frac12h_{11}=h_{11}, \qquad h_{11}\equiv\mel{\psi_1}{\hat{h}}{\psi_1}=\int\psi_1^*(\bm{r})\,\hat{h}(\bm{r})\,\psi_1(\bm{r})\,\dd\bm{r} \label{eq:35-h1g} \end{equation}

The term for electron 2, $\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{h}_2}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}$, leads to the same calculation with only the roles of electrons 1 and 2 interchanged (when we rename $\bm{x}_1\leftrightarrow\bm{x}_2$, $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ merely changes sign, and the expectation value does not change):

$$ \mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{h}_2}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}=\frac12\int\psi_1^*(\bm{r}_2)\hat{h}_2\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\dd\bm{r}_2+\frac12\int\psi_1^*(\bm{r}_2)\hat{h}_2\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\dd\bm{r}_2=h_{11} . $$

In other words, the one-electron term is the sum $2h_{11}$ obtained when each of the two electrons is regarded as having the one-electron energy $h_{11}$ in the orbital $\psi_1$.

35.2.3 The two-electron term $\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{1/r_{12}}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}$

Since the electron–electron repulsion $1/r_{12}$ depends on both $\bm{r}_1$ and $\bm{r}_2$, it cannot be split into a product over the electrons as the one-electron term could. Even so, we can multiply out the two square brackets into four terms and carry out the spin integral first in each term.

\begin{align} \mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}=\frac12\iint\dd\bm{x}_1\dd\bm{x}_2\Bigl[ &\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\frac{1}{r_{12}}\bar{\chi}_1^*(\bm{x}_2)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2) +\bar{\chi}_1^*(\bm{x}_1)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)\frac{1}{r_{12}}\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_2)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\notag\\ &-\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_1)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)\frac{1}{r_{12}}\bar{\chi}_1^*(\bm{x}_2)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2) -\bar{\chi}_1^*(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\frac{1}{r_{12}}\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_2)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2)\Bigr] \label{eq:35-vexp} \end{align}

For the factor for electron 1 and the factor for electron 2 in each term, we examine the spin integral.

  • Term 1: electron 1 gives $\chi_1^*\chi_1$ (spin $\alpha^*\alpha$, integral 1) and electron 2 gives $\bar{\chi}_1^*\bar{\chi}_1$ ($\beta^*\beta$, integral 1). The spin integral survives as 1, and only the orbital integral $\iint\lvert\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\rvert^2\dfrac{1}{r_{12}}\lvert\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\rvert^2\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2$ remains.
  • Term 2: electron 1 gives $\beta^*\beta$ and electron 2 gives $\alpha^*\alpha$, so this term also survives. The orbital integral is the same as that of Term 1.
  • Term 3: the spin of electron 1 is $\chi_1^*\bar{\chi}_1\to\alpha^*\beta$ and the spin of electron 2 is $\bar{\chi}_1^*\chi_1\to\beta^*\alpha$; both integrals are 0.
  • Term 4: electron 1 gives $\beta^*\alpha$ and electron 2 gives $\alpha^*\beta$, and the integral is 0.

Therefore Terms 3 and 4 (these two terms are the “exchange term” that arises from the antisymmetrization demanded by the Pauli exclusion principle) vanish because of the orthogonality of the spin functions. The surviving Terms 1 and 2 have the same value, so

\begin{equation} \mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}} =\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}-\underbrace{\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_1}}_{\text{because of the spin: }0} =\iint\lvert\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\rvert^2\frac{1}{r_{12}}\lvert\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\rvert^2\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2\equiv U_{11} \label{eq:35-vg} \end{equation}

In the first equality, we combined Terms 1 and 2 into the same integral $\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}{v}{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}$ by renaming electrons 1 and 2 ($\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1$), and likewise combined Terms 3 and 4 into $-\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}{v}{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_1}$. We call the first term of this expression the Coulomb term and the second term the exchange term. In the ground state, electron 1 is in $\chi_1$ ($\alpha$ spin) and electron 2 is in $\bar{\chi}_1$ ($\beta$ spin), so the exchange term vanishes through the spin integral $\braket{\alpha}{\beta}\braket{\beta}{\alpha}=0$. The surviving Coulomb term $U_{11}$ is the classical electrostatic repulsion energy between the charge clouds $\lvert\psi_1\rvert^2$.

35.2.4 The ground-state energy

We add the one-electron term $2h_{11}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:35-h1g} and its version for electron 2) and the two-electron term $U_{11}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:35-vg}).

\begin{align} \mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}} &=\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}_1}{\chi_1}+\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}_2}{\chi_1}+\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}-\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_1}\notag\\ &=h_{11}+h_{11}+U_{11}-0 \label{eq:35-Eg1} \end{align}

In the first line, $\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}_2}{\chi_1}$ is an integral over the coordinates of electron 2, and it has the same value as $\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}_1}{\chi_1}$ (they differ only in the name of the integration variable).

Theorem 35.1: Energy of the two-electron ground state $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$

The expectation value of the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}=\hat{h}_1+\hat{h}_2+1/r_{12}$ in the Slater determinant $\Phi_{\mathrm g}=\lvert\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1\rvert$, in which two electrons occupy the same spatial orbital $\psi_1$ with opposite spins, is

\begin{equation} E_{\mathrm g}=\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}=2h_{11}+U_{11} \label{eq:35-Eg} \end{equation}

where $h_{11}=\mel{\psi_1}{\hat{h}}{\psi_1}$ is the one-electron energy and $U_{11}=\mel{\psi_1\psi_1}{1/r_{12}}{\psi_1\psi_1}$ is the Coulomb integral between the charge clouds of the orbital $\psi_1$ (Section 35.3). The exchange term vanishes because of the spin.

In other words, the ground-state energy in the Hartree–Fock approximation is the “sum of the one-electron energies when the two electrons are independently in the orbital $\psi_1$” plus only the “classical electrostatic repulsion between the two electron clouds.” For two electrons with opposite spins, no quantum-mechanical correction (exchange term) appears.

Let us summarize this result as a way of viewing the energy in the Hartree–Fock approximation. The energy is made up of (i) the one-electron energy $h_{aa}$ of an orbital (kinetic energy and attraction to the nucleus), (ii) the Coulomb repulsion $U$ between charge clouds, and (iii) the exchange energy $-J$, which appears only for electron pairs with parallel spins. For two electrons with antiparallel spins, (iii) vanishes. The effect by which electrons avoid one another and thereby reduce their repulsion (electron correlation) is contained in none of these three (Section 35.7). The general $N$-electron expression that includes (iii) is derived in Eq. \eqref{eq:35-EN} in Section 35.7.1.

Example 35.2: The ground-state energy of the helium atom — using a hydrogen-like 1s orbital

Approximate the ground state of the helium atom ($Z=2$) by two electrons in the $Z=2$ hydrogen-like 1s orbital $\psi_{1\mathrm s}=\sqrt{Z^3/\pi}\,e^{-Zr}$. Find $h_{11}$, $U_{11}$, and $E_{\mathrm g}$ of Theorem 35.1 in units of $E_{\mathrm h}$ and in eV, and compare them with the exact value. You may use $\expval{1/r}=Z$ and $\expval{\hat{T}}=Z^2/2$ (for the hydrogen-like 1s orbital; Chapter 15) and $U_{11}=\tfrac58Z$ (Chapter 29, Section 29.2).

Solution. The expectation value of $\hat{h}(\bm{r})=-\tfrac12\nabla^2-Z/r$ is the sum of the kinetic energy and the attraction to the nucleus:

$$ h_{11}=\underbrace{\tfrac12Z^2}_{\expval{\hat{T}}}-Z\underbrace{Z}_{\expval{1/r}}=\tfrac12Z^2-Z^2=-\tfrac12Z^2 . $$

For $Z=2$, $h_{11}=-2\,E_{\mathrm h}$. The Coulomb integral is $U_{11}=\tfrac58\cdot2=\tfrac54=1.25\,E_{\mathrm h}$. Therefore, from Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Eg},

$$ E_{\mathrm g}=2h_{11}+U_{11}=2\times(-2)+1.25=-2.75\,E_{\mathrm h}=-2.75\times27.2114\ \mathrm{eV}=-74.83\ \mathrm{eV} . $$

The nonrelativistic exact value is $-2.9037\,E_{\mathrm h}=-79.01\ \mathrm{eV}$ (the experimental value is $-79.005\ \mathrm{eV}$), so this approximation is too high by about $4.2\ \mathrm{eV}$ (by the variational principle, an approximate energy is always higher than the exact value). If we adjust the spread of the orbital by replacing the nuclear charge with $Z'=Z-5/16=27/16$ (Chapter 31, Section 31.3), the energy becomes $-2.8477\,E_{\mathrm h}=-77.49\ \mathrm{eV}$, and in the Hartree–Fock approximation (Chapter 36), in which the shape of $\psi_1$ itself is optimized, it becomes $-2.8617\,E_{\mathrm h}=-77.87\ \mathrm{eV}$. Since Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Eg} holds whatever function $\psi_1$ is, we see that the choice of the orbital $\psi_1$ determines the accuracy of this approximation.

Caution: The names “correlation term” and “exchange term”

The Coulomb term $U_{11}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:35-vg} is sometimes called the “correlation term” (meaning that the positions of the two electrons are related to each other through the charge clouds). In quantum chemistry and condensed matter physics, however, electron correlation means the difference from the exact solution that the Hartree–Fock approximation misses, $E_{\mathrm{corr}}=E_{\mathrm{exact}}-E_{\mathrm{HF}}$ (Section 35.7). To avoid confusion, this book calls the two terms in Eq. \eqref{eq:35-vg} the Coulomb term ($U$) and the exchange term ($J$) (the general definition of the exchange term is given in Section 35.3), and uses the word “correlation” only for $E_{\mathrm{corr}}$. It is also standard to say that “the energy in the Hartree–Fock approximation includes the exchange energy but not the correlation energy.”

35.3 The Coulomb Integral $U$ and the Exchange Integral $J$ — Definitions, Properties, and Correspondence with Other Books

35.3.1 Definitions — the integral in which the electrons keep their orbitals and the integral in which they interchange them

In the calculation of Section 35.2, two kinds of two-electron integrals appeared. Let $\ket{\psi_p\psi_q}$ denote the state in which electron 1 is in the orbital $\psi_p$ and electron 2 is in the orbital $\psi_q$ (the first entry of the bra–ket refers to electron 1 and the second to electron 2). Then the two integrals differ in whether, for the bra $\bra{\psi_p\psi_q}$, the ket is $\ket{\psi_p\psi_q}$ (the electrons do not change orbitals) or $\ket{\psi_q\psi_p}$ (the electrons interchange orbitals).

Definition 35.3: Coulomb integral $U_{pq}$

For two spatial orbitals $\psi_p$ and $\psi_q$,

\begin{equation} U_{pq}\equiv\mel{\psi_p\psi_q}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\psi_p\psi_q} =\iint\underbrace{\psi_p^*(\bm{r}_1)\psi_p(\bm{r}_1)}_{\lvert\psi_p(\bm{r}_1)\rvert^2}\ \frac{1}{r_{12}}\ \underbrace{\psi_q^*(\bm{r}_2)\psi_q(\bm{r}_2)}_{\lvert\psi_q(\bm{r}_2)\rvert^2}\ \dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2 \label{eq:35-Udef} \end{equation}

is called the Coulomb integral.

Definition 35.4: Exchange integral $J_{pq}$

For the same two spatial orbitals,

\begin{equation} J_{pq}\equiv\mel{\psi_p\psi_q}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\psi_q\psi_p} =\iint\psi_p^*(\bm{r}_1)\psi_q(\bm{r}_1)\ \frac{1}{r_{12}}\ \psi_q^*(\bm{r}_2)\psi_p(\bm{r}_2)\ \dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2 \label{eq:35-Jdef} \end{equation}

is called the exchange integral.

Figure 35.2 depicts the difference between the two integrals in terms of which orbital each electron moves from and which orbital it moves to.

The two integrals are defined in the same form for the spin orbitals $\chi_a,\chi_b$ (spin functions $s_a,s_b$) as well:

\begin{equation} U_{ab}\equiv\mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\chi_a\chi_b},\qquad J_{ab}\equiv\mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\chi_b\chi_a} \label{eq:35-UJso} \end{equation}

Using Eq. \eqref{eq:35-brk} to separate the spin parts, we find that $U_{ab}$ is multiplied by the spin factor $\braket{s_a}{s_a}\braket{s_b}{s_b}=1$ and $J_{ab}$ by the spin factor $\braket{s_a}{s_b}\braket{s_b}{s_a}=\delta_{s_as_b}$, so

\begin{equation} U_{ab}=U_{pq},\qquad J_{ab}=\delta_{s_as_b}\,J_{pq}\qquad(\chi_a=\psi_ps_a,\ \chi_b=\psi_qs_b) \label{eq:35-UJspin} \end{equation}

That is, the Coulomb integral does not depend on the spin, and the exchange integral survives only when the spins are parallel (it is 0 if they are antiparallel). The reason that $\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}{v}{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_1}$ in Section 35.2 was 0 is that the spins of $\chi_1$ and $\bar{\chi}_1$ were antiparallel. In this book the orbital labels are written $1,2,p,q$ and the spin-orbital labels $a,b,c,\dots$ (the $a,b,c$ in the states $\Phi_a,\Phi_b,\Phi_c$ of Section 35.5 are state labels, distinct from the spin-orbital labels). For example, $U_{11}$, $U_{12}$, and $J_{12}$ are integrals for the orbitals $\psi_1,\psi_2$ (the same as $U_{\mathrm{1s2s}}$ and $J_{\mathrm{1s2s}}$ in Chapter 29).

Notation: Atomic units and SI units

Eqs. \eqref{eq:35-Udef} and \eqref{eq:35-Jdef} are written in atomic units. In the SI system of units, it suffices to replace $1/r_{12}$ by $e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r_{12})$, and Definition 29.3 in Chapter 29 is written in that form. Values are expressed in units of $E_{\mathrm h}=e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0a_0)$.

35.3.2 Physical meaning and properties

The Coulomb integral $U_{pq}$ has a clear classical meaning. The charge cloud of the electron in the orbital $\psi_p$ (electron density $\rho_p(\bm{r})=\lvert\psi_p(\bm{r})\rvert^2$) and the charge cloud $\rho_q(\bm{r})=\lvert\psi_q(\bm{r})\rvert^2$ of the orbital $\psi_q$ are both negatively charged “clouds,” and the electrostatic repulsion energy between the two clouds is the Coulomb energy between infinitesimal volume elements summed over all space—Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Udef} itself (the energy version of the potential produced by a continuous charge distribution in AD 03, Chapter 36, Section 36.8 (in Japanese)). Therefore $U_{pq}\gt0$.

The exchange integral $J_{pq}$ has no counterpart in classical physics. The integrand of Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Jdef} is made of the product of two different orbitals, $\rho_{pq}(\bm{r})\equiv\psi_p^*(\bm{r})\psi_q(\bm{r})$. This is called the overlap density (transition density). For $p\ne q$, $\rho_{pq}$ has positive and negative regions, and when integrated over all space they cancel by the orbital orthogonality $\int\rho_{pq}\,\dd\bm{r}=\braket{\psi_p}{\psi_q}=0$. For real orbitals, $J_{pq}=\iint\rho_{pq}(\bm{r}_1)\dfrac{1}{r_{12}}\rho_{pq}(\bm{r}_2)\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2$, which has the form of the self-repulsion energy of the signed charge distribution called the “overlap density.” Figure 35.3 shows, for two orbitals in a one-dimensional box of width $L$ ($\psi_n=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)$, $n=1,2$; Chapter 10), the charge clouds $\lvert\psi_1\rvert^2$ and $\lvert\psi_2\rvert^2$ and the overlap density $\rho_{12}=\psi_1\psi_2$.

The name comes from the fact that in Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Jdef} electron 1 and electron 2 exchange orbitals (relative to $\psi_p(\bm{r}_1)\psi_q(\bm{r}_2)$ in the bra, the ket has $\psi_q(\bm{r}_1)\psi_p(\bm{r}_2)$). Because of the indistinguishability of identical particles, we cannot distinguish “electron 1 is in $p$ and electron 2 is in $q$” from “electron 1 is in $q$ and electron 2 is in $p$,” and this integral arises from that fact.

Theorem 35.2: Properties of $U_{pq}$ and $J_{pq}$

For normalized spatial orbitals $\psi_p,\psi_q$, the following hold.

  1. $U_{pq}=U_{qp}\gt0$.
  2. $J_{pq}=J_{qp}$ is real, and $J_{pq}\ge0$.
  3. $U_{pp}=J_{pp}$.
  4. $U_{pq}\ge J_{pq}$ (equality holds only when $\psi_p$ and $\psi_q$ differ merely by a phase factor. For mutually orthogonal orbitals with $p\ne q$, equality does not hold, and $U_{pq}\gt J_{pq}$).

Proof

(1) The integrand $\lvert\psi_p(\bm{r}_1)\rvert^2\lvert\psi_q(\bm{r}_2)\rvert^2/r_{12}$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Udef} is nonnegative everywhere and is nonzero on some region. Interchanging the names of the integration variables $\bm{r}_1,\bm{r}_2$ gives $U_{pq}=U_{qp}$.

(3) Setting $p=q$, the integrand of Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Jdef} becomes $\lvert\psi_p(\bm{r}_1)\rvert^2\dfrac{1}{r_{12}}\lvert\psi_p(\bm{r}_2)\rvert^2$, which coincides with what is obtained by setting $q=p$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Udef}.

(2) Substituting into Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Jdef} the Fourier expansion of the Coulomb potential $\dfrac{1}{r_{12}}=\dfrac{1}{2\pi^2}\displaystyle\int\dfrac{\dd^3k}{k^2}\,e^{i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{r}_1-\bm{r}_2)}$ (used in Chapter 29, Section 29.5; its derivation is given in the “Math Tool” after this proof) and using $\tilde{\rho}_{pq}(\bm{k})\equiv\int\rho_{pq}(\bm{r})e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\dd\bm{r}$, we obtain, for $\rho_{pq}=\psi_p^*\psi_q$, $$ J_{pq}=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int\frac{\dd^3k}{k^2}\Bigl[\int\rho_{pq}(\bm{r}_1)e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}_1}\dd\bm{r}_1\Bigr]\Bigl[\int\rho_{pq}^*(\bm{r}_2)e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}_2}\dd\bm{r}_2\Bigr] =\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int\frac{\dd^3k}{k^2}\,\Bigl\lvert\int\rho_{pq}(\bm{r})e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\dd\bm{r}\Bigr\rvert^2\ \ge0 $$ (the second square bracket is the complex conjugate of the first, and the product is the square of the absolute value). The integrand is real and nonnegative, so $J_{pq}$ is also real and $\ge0$. That $J_{pq}=J_{qp}$ follows because $\rho_{qp}=\rho_{pq}^*$ gives the same expression.

(4) Consider the antisymmetric state $\Phi=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[A-B\bigr]\cdot(\text{spin})$, with $A=\psi_p(\bm{r}_1)\psi_q(\bm{r}_2)$ and $B=\psi_q(\bm{r}_1)\psi_p(\bm{r}_2)$, in which two electrons with the same spin occupy the orbitals $\psi_p,\psi_q$. The expectation value of the electron–electron repulsion, from the spatial part alone, is $$ \tfrac12\iint\frac{\lvert A-B\rvert^2}{r_{12}}\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2=\tfrac12\iint\frac{\lvert A\rvert^2+\lvert B\rvert^2-A^*B-AB^*}{r_{12}}\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2=\tfrac12\bigl(U_{pq}+U_{qp}-J_{pq}-J_{pq}^*\bigr)=U_{pq}-J_{pq} $$ (we used that $\iint\lvert A\rvert^2/r_{12}=U_{pq}$, $\iint\lvert B\rvert^2/r_{12}=U_{qp}$, and $\iint A^*B/r_{12}=\iint\psi_p^*(\bm{r}_1)\psi_q(\bm{r}_1)\dfrac{1}{r_{12}}\psi_q^*(\bm{r}_2)\psi_p(\bm{r}_2)=J_{pq}$). The left-hand side is nonnegative because $\lvert A-B\rvert^2\ge0$ and $1/r_{12}\gt0$. Therefore $U_{pq}-J_{pq}\ge0$. Equality holds only when $\lvert A-B\rvert^2$ is zero almost everywhere, that is, when $\psi_p(\bm{r}_1)\psi_q(\bm{r}_2)=\psi_q(\bm{r}_1)\psi_p(\bm{r}_2)$ (when $\psi_q=e^{i\theta}\psi_p$). For two orthogonal orbitals ($p\ne q$) this does not happen, so $U_{pq}\gt J_{pq}$.

Math Tool: The Fourier expansion of $1/r$

First we find the three-dimensional Fourier transform of the screened Coulomb potential $e^{-\mu r}/r$ ($\mu\gt0$). Taking the direction of $\bm{k}$ as the polar axis, $\displaystyle\int e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\dd\Omega=2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}e^{-ikr\cos\theta}\,\dd(\cos\theta)=4\pi\,\frac{\sin kr}{kr}$, so $$ \int\frac{e^{-\mu r}}{r}\,e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\dd\bm{r}=\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-\mu r}}{r}\cdot4\pi\frac{\sin kr}{kr}\,r^2\dd r=\frac{4\pi}{k}\int_0^\infty e^{-\mu r}\sin kr\,\dd r=\frac{4\pi}{k^2+\mu^2} $$ (the last integral is $\int_0^\infty e^{-\mu r}\sin kr\,\dd r=k/(k^2+\mu^2)$). The three-dimensional inverse Fourier transform carries the coefficient $(2\pi)^{-3}$, so $$ \frac{e^{-\mu r}}{r}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}\int\frac{4\pi}{k^2+\mu^2}\,e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\dd^3k=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int\frac{e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}}{k^2+\mu^2}\dd^3k . $$ The coefficient $1/(2\pi^2)=4\pi/(2\pi)^3$ is the ratio formed from this $4\pi$ and the $(2\pi)^{-3}$ of the inverse transform. Taking the limit $\mu\to+0$ gives $1/r=\dfrac{1}{2\pi^2}\displaystyle\int\dfrac{\dd^3k}{k^2}\,e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}$, which was used in the proof.

When this expansion is used for $J_{pq}$, the interchange of the order of integration is permitted as long as $\mu\gt0$, because the integrand is then absolutely integrable, and the same calculation as in (2) of the proof gives $J_{pq}^{(\mu)}=\dfrac{1}{2\pi^2}\displaystyle\int\dfrac{\dd^3k}{k^2+\mu^2}\lvert\tilde{\rho}_{pq}(\bm{k})\rvert^2\ge0$. Since $\lvert e^{-\mu r}/r\rvert\le1/r$, for ordinary orbitals for which $\iint\lvert\rho_{pq}(\bm{r}_1)\rvert\lvert\rho_{pq}(\bm{r}_2)\rvert/r_{12}$ is finite we have $J_{pq}^{(\mu)}\to J_{pq}$ as $\mu\to+0$, and the inequality $J_{pq}\ge0$ survives.

Property (4) becomes important in Section 35.6. It expresses a consequence of the Pauli exclusion principle: “the repulsion energy $U_{pq}-J_{pq}$ of two electrons with parallel spins is smaller than the $U_{pq}$ of two electrons with antiparallel spins (by the amount $J_{pq}\ge0$).”

35.3.3 A feel for the numbers — The hydrogen molecule and the helium atom

Let us get a feel for the magnitudes of $U$ and $J$. Between the hydrogen-like 1s and 2s orbitals ($Z=2$) of the helium atom, $U_{\mathrm{1s2s}}=\tfrac{17}{81}Z=0.4198\,E_{\mathrm h}$ ($11.42\ \mathrm{eV}$) and $J_{\mathrm{1s2s}}=\tfrac{16}{729}Z=0.0439\,E_{\mathrm h}$ ($1.19\ \mathrm{eV}$) (these were computed exactly with Formula 29.4 in Chapter 29, Section 29.5). The exchange integral is about one tenth of the Coulomb integral, which reflects that the overlap density is small because the two orbitals differ greatly in space (1s lies right next to the nucleus, 2s far away). The more similar the orbitals are, the larger the overlap density, and the closer $J$ comes to $U$ (in the limit $p=q$, $J_{pp}=U_{pp}$). Next, let us actually compute $U$ and $J$ for the hydrogen molecule, in which the two orbitals are spread out to about the same extent.

Example 35.3: $U$ and $J$ between the bonding orbital $\sigma_{\mathrm g}$ and the antibonding orbital $\sigma_{\mathrm u}$ of the hydrogen molecule $\mathrm{H_2}$

For the hydrogen molecule (internuclear distance $R=1.4\,a_0$), the atomic orbitals $\phi_A$, $\phi_B$ of the STO-3G minimal basis (the 1s orbitals of nuclei A and B, real functions) have the overlap integral $S=\braket{\phi_A}{\phi_B}=0.65932$, and the two-electron integrals (chemists' notation, in units of $E_{\mathrm h}$) are $$ (AA\vert AA)=0.77461,\quad(AA\vert BB)=0.56968,\quad(AA\vert AB)=0.44411,\quad(AB\vert AB)=0.29703 $$ (where $(AB\vert CD)=\iint\phi_A(\bm{r}_1)\phi_B(\bm{r}_1)\frac{1}{r_{12}}\phi_C(\bm{r}_2)\phi_D(\bm{r}_2)\,\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2$). For the molecular orbitals $\sigma_{\mathrm g}=(\phi_A+\phi_B)/\sqrt{2(1+S)}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm u}=(\phi_A-\phi_B)/\sqrt{2(1-S)}$ (g and u are the symbols for gerade (even) and ungerade (odd), and are distinct from the g (ground) of the ground state $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$), find the Coulomb integral $U_{\mathrm{gu}}$ and the exchange integral $J_{\mathrm{gu}}$ between them.

Solution. Let the densities of the atomic orbitals be $\rho_{AA}=\phi_A^2$, $\rho_{BB}=\phi_B^2$, $\rho_{AB}=\phi_A\phi_B$, and write the repulsion between two densities as $(f\vert g)=\iint f(\bm{r}_1)\frac{1}{r_{12}}g(\bm{r}_2)\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2$ (with $(f\vert g)=(g\vert f)$). To simplify the notation, let $A_0=(AA\vert AA)$, $B_0=(AA\vert BB)$, $D_0=(AB\vert AB)$. Since atoms A and B are of the same kind, $(BB\vert BB)=A_0$.

[i] $U_{\mathrm{gu}}$. The charge clouds of the two molecular orbitals are $$ \lvert\sigma_{\mathrm g}\rvert^2=\frac{\rho_{AA}+\rho_{BB}+2\rho_{AB}}{2(1+S)},\qquad \lvert\sigma_{\mathrm u}\rvert^2=\frac{\rho_{AA}+\rho_{BB}-2\rho_{AB}}{2(1-S)} $$ The product of the denominators is $4(1+S)(1-S)=4(1-S^2)$, and expanding the product of the numerators gives $(\rho_{AA}+\rho_{BB}+2\rho_{AB}\vert\rho_{AA}+\rho_{BB}-2\rho_{AB})=(\rho_{AA}+\rho_{BB}\vert\rho_{AA}+\rho_{BB})-4(\rho_{AB}\vert\rho_{AB})+\bigl[-2(\rho_{AA}+\rho_{BB}\vert\rho_{AB})+2(\rho_{AB}\vert\rho_{AA}+\rho_{BB})\bigr]$. The last square bracket is 0 because $(f\vert g)=(g\vert f)$. What remains are $(\rho_{AA}+\rho_{BB}\vert\rho_{AA}+\rho_{BB})=(AA\vert AA)+(BB\vert BB)+2(AA\vert BB)=2A_0+2B_0$ and $(\rho_{AB}\vert\rho_{AB})=D_0$, so $$ U_{\mathrm{gu}}=\frac{2A_0+2B_0-4D_0}{4(1-S^2)}=\frac{A_0+B_0-2D_0}{2(1-S^2)}=\frac{0.77461+0.56968-2\times0.29703}{2\,(1-0.43470)}=\frac{0.75023}{1.13060}=0.6636\,E_{\mathrm h}. $$

[ii] $J_{\mathrm{gu}}$. The overlap density is $\rho_{\mathrm{gu}}=\sigma_{\mathrm g}\sigma_{\mathrm u}=\dfrac{(\phi_A+\phi_B)(\phi_A-\phi_B)}{2\sqrt{1-S^2}}=\dfrac{\rho_{AA}-\rho_{BB}}{2\sqrt{1-S^2}}$, that is, “the charge cloud of atom A minus the charge cloud of atom B”: a distribution that is positive near nucleus A, negative near nucleus B, and zero in total (a shape resembling an electric dipole). The exchange integral is the self-repulsion of this distribution: $$ J_{\mathrm{gu}}=\frac{(\rho_{AA}-\rho_{BB}\vert\rho_{AA}-\rho_{BB})}{4(1-S^2)}=\frac{2A_0-2B_0}{4(1-S^2)}=\frac{A_0-B_0}{2(1-S^2)}=\frac{0.77461-0.56968}{1.13060}=\frac{0.20493}{1.13060}=0.1813\,E_{\mathrm h}\ (=4.93\ \mathrm{eV}). $$

Here we used $(\rho_{AA}-\rho_{BB}\vert\rho_{AA}-\rho_{BB})=(AA\vert AA)+(BB\vert BB)-2(AA\vert BB)=2A_0-2B_0$. We have $U_{\mathrm{gu}}=0.664\gt J_{\mathrm{gu}}=0.181\ge0$, which satisfies properties (1), (2), and (4) of Theorem 35.2. $J$ is as large as about 27% of $U$, larger than for the 1s–2s pair of helium (about 10%). This is because the two orbitals are of comparable size and the overlap density is large. (These are values for the minimal basis; in high-accuracy calculations the values change.)

35.4 Is the Ground State Unique? — $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ and $\Phi_{\mathrm g'}$

35.4.1 Question: Is $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ the only wave function?

In Section 35.2, we arranged the columns of the determinant for the ground state $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ in the order “$\chi_1$, $\bar{\chi}_1$.” However, whether we write the spin-up electron first or the spin-down electron first is merely a matter of the writer's preference; there is no physical reason for it. A question therefore arises: does the determinant whose columns are arranged in the order “$\bar{\chi}_1$, $\chi_1$” (in the level diagram, we draw a down arrow on the left and an up arrow on the right; Figure 35.4, right) represent another wave function for the same ground state? If so, the Hartree–Fock approximation would have several candidates for the “ground state,” and we would have to decide which one to choose. This question arises from the way we construct a Slater determinant, “by arranging the orbitals occupied by the electrons in columns.”

Let us denote the determinant with the columns interchanged by $\Phi_{\mathrm{g}'}$:

\begin{equation} \Phi_{\mathrm{g}'}(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \begin{vmatrix} \bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)&\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\\ \bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2)&\chi_1(\bm{x}_2) \end{vmatrix} =\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)-\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2)\Bigr] \label{eq:35-Phigp} \end{equation}

Comparing this with Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Phig}, we see that the two terms in the square brackets are interchanged, so the sign is reversed:

\begin{equation} \Phi_{\mathrm g}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2)-\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\Bigr]=-\Phi_{\mathrm{g}'} \label{eq:35-gsign} \end{equation}

This is a check, for $2\times2$, of the property of determinants that “interchanging two columns changes only the sign” (University Mathematics, Chapter 8, Section 8.5).

Theorem 35.3: Reordering columns and the Slater determinant

Slater determinants built from the same $N$ spin orbitals $\chi_1,\dots,\chi_N$ differ at most by a sign $\pm1$, in whatever order the columns are arranged. That is, for a rearrangement (permutation) $P$ of the columns, $$ \Phi(\chi_{P(1)},\dots,\chi_{P(N)})=\operatorname{sgn}(P)\,\Phi(\chi_1,\dots,\chi_N) $$ holds ($\operatorname{sgn}(P)=\pm1$: $+1$ for an even permutation, $-1$ for an odd permutation). Multiplying by $-1$, or changing the phase, does not change the state in quantum mechanics (neither the expectation value $\mel{\Phi}{\hat{O}}{\Phi}$ nor the probability density $\lvert\Phi\rvert^2$ changes). Therefore, once the set of spin orbitals is fixed, the state represented by the Slater determinant is determined uniquely.

A state is not “the wave function itself” but “the whole collection of the wave function multiplied by nonzero constants” (a ray). Therefore $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ and $\Phi_{\mathrm{g}'}$, for which Eq. \eqref{eq:35-gsign} holds, represent the same state, and there is only one two-electron ground state. What must be determined in the Hartree–Fock approximation is not “which arrangement” but only “which set of spin orbitals” (the set of occupied orbitals).

Discussion: The same holds even if it is not the ground state, and even if the energies are degenerate

This argument used only the property of reordering the columns of a determinant; it did not use the fact that the energies of $\chi_1,\bar{\chi}_1$ are low. Therefore the situation is the same even if the state is not the ground state, or even if the orbital energies of the two electrons are degenerate. A determinant built from the same set of spin orbitals is one state, regardless of the order of the columns. Conversely, if the sets differ, the states differ. For example, $\Phi_a=\lvert\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2\rvert$ and $\Phi_b=\lvert\bar{\chi}_1\chi_2\rvert$ in Section 35.5 have the same occupation of the two orbitals, but which spin is in which orbital differs, so they are different states (this has nothing to do with whether they are degenerate). Note also that in Part X (Second Quantization), a state is specified by “whether each spin orbital contains an electron” (the occupation numbers). The occupation-number representation is a way of writing that specifies only this “set,” so we do not have to think about the sign of the ordering of the columns from the start.

Example 35.4: Reordering the determinant of a three-electron system

For the $3\times3$ Slater determinant $\Phi_{abc}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt6}\det[\chi_j(\bm{x}_i)]$ built from three spin orbitals $\chi_a,\chi_b,\chi_c$, determine, for each of the six determinants obtained by rearranging the columns, which of $\pm\Phi_{abc}$ it equals. Also, determinants built from the same set of spin orbitals represent one state regardless of the arrangement (Theorem 35.3), so the number of distinct states equals the number of sets of spin orbitals that the electrons occupy. From the four spin orbitals formed from two spatial orbitals ($\psi_1,\psi_2$) and two kinds of spin ($\alpha,\beta$), how many distinct two-electron states (sets of two spin orbitals) can be made?

Solution. The sign of a rearrangement of the columns is determined by the parity of the number of interchanges (transpositions) needed to achieve it.

  • $(abc)$: unchanged (zero interchanges): $+\Phi_{abc}$.
  • $(bca)$: two interchanges, $(abc)\to(bac)\to(bca)$ (even permutation): $+\Phi_{abc}$.
  • $(cab)$: two interchanges, $(abc)\to(cba)\to(cab)$ (even permutation): $+\Phi_{abc}$.
  • $(acb)$, $(bac)$, $(cba)$: one interchange each (odd permutation): $-\Phi_{abc}$.

Therefore, the three even permutations give $+\Phi_{abc}$ and the three odd permutations give $-\Phi_{abc}$, and all six determinants represent the same state (for general $N$, of the $N!$ arrangements, half give $+$ and half give $-$). The number of states is “the number of ways of choosing, from the four spin orbitals, the two spin orbitals that the two electrons occupy,” $\binom{4}{2}=6$. Explicitly, they are the six states $\lvert\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1\rvert=\Phi_{\mathrm g}$, $\lvert\chi_2\bar{\chi}_2\rvert=\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}$, $\lvert\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2\rvert=\Phi_a$, $\lvert\bar{\chi}_1\chi_2\rvert=\Phi_b$, $\lvert\chi_1\chi_2\rvert=\Phi_c$, and $\lvert\bar{\chi}_1\bar{\chi}_2\rvert=\Phi_{\bar c}$, and these are all the states treated in Sections 35.5–35.6.

35.5 The Hartree–Fock Approximation for Excited States — Orthogonality and Off-Diagonal Elements

35.5.1 Singly excited states $\Phi_a$ and $\Phi_b$ and the overlap integral

Now that the ground-state energy has been found, we next treat the excited states in the Hartree–Fock approximation. We raise just one electron from the lower orbital $\psi_1$ to the upper orbital $\psi_2$. Since there are two combinations of the spin direction of the raised electron and the spin direction of the electron remaining below, two different states result (Figure 35.5).

  • $\Phi_a$: electrons with spin up ($\chi_1$) in the lower level $\psi_1$ and spin down ($\bar{\chi}_2$) in the upper level $\psi_2$.
  • $\Phi_b$: electrons with spin down ($\bar{\chi}_1$) in the lower level $\psi_1$ and spin up ($\chi_2$) in the upper level $\psi_2$.

Here the subscripts $a,b,c$ of $\Phi_a,\Phi_b$ (and of $\Phi_c$ in Section 35.5.5) are state labels and are unrelated to the numbers $a,b$ of general spin orbitals ($h_{aa}$, $U_{ab}$, $\Phi_a^r$ in Section 35.7, and so on).

Written as Slater determinants,

\begin{align} \Phi_a&=\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \begin{vmatrix} \chi_1(\bm{x}_1)&\bar{\chi}_2(\bm{x}_1)\\ \chi_1(\bm{x}_2)&\bar{\chi}_2(\bm{x}_2) \end{vmatrix} =\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\bar{\chi}_2(\bm{x}_2)-\bar{\chi}_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\Bigr], \label{eq:35-Phia}\\ \Phi_b&=\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \begin{vmatrix} \bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)&\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\\ \bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2)&\chi_2(\bm{x}_2) \end{vmatrix} =\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)-\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2)\Bigr] \label{eq:35-Phib} \end{align}

Unlike $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ and $\Phi_{\mathrm{g}'}$ of Section 35.4, $\Phi_a$ and $\Phi_b$ are not “dependent” but “independent.” That is, $\Phi_a\ne\pm\Phi_b$ (the sets of occupied spin orbitals differ).

Let us compute the overlap integral of these two states. Multiplying the two square brackets in Eqs. \eqref{eq:35-Phia} and \eqref{eq:35-Phib} and expanding into four terms, we obtain

\begin{align} \braket{\Phi_a}{\Phi_b}=\frac12\Bigl[ &\int\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_1)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)\dd\bm{x}_1\int\bar{\chi}_2^*(\bm{x}_2)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)\dd\bm{x}_2 +\int\bar{\chi}_2^*(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\dd\bm{x}_1\int\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_2)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2)\dd\bm{x}_2\notag\\ &-\int\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\dd\bm{x}_1\int\bar{\chi}_2^*(\bm{x}_2)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_2)\dd\bm{x}_2 -\int\bar{\chi}_2^*(\bm{x}_1)\bar{\chi}_1(\bm{x}_1)\dd\bm{x}_1\int\chi_1^*(\bm{x}_2)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)\dd\bm{x}_2\Bigr] \label{eq:35-ovab} \end{align}

The reasons why the two factors of each term vanish are summarized in Table 35.3.

Table 35.3: Checking the four terms of Eq. \eqref{eq:35-ovab} (spin orthogonality $\int\alpha^*\beta=0$, orbital orthogonality $\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_2}=0$)
TermFactor for electron 1Factor for electron 2Reason it vanishes
Term 1$\braket{\chi_1}{\bar{\chi}_1}$ ($\alpha^*\beta$)$\braket{\bar{\chi}_2}{\chi_2}$ ($\beta^*\alpha$)The spins are orthogonal (both factors are 0)
Term 2$\braket{\bar{\chi}_2}{\chi_2}$ ($\beta^*\alpha$)$\braket{\chi_1}{\bar{\chi}_1}$ ($\alpha^*\beta$)The spins are orthogonal (both factors are 0)
Term 3$\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_2}$ ($\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_2}$)$\braket{\bar{\chi}_2}{\bar{\chi}_1}$ ($\braket{\psi_2}{\psi_1}$)The orbitals are orthogonal (both factors are 0)
Term 4$\braket{\bar{\chi}_2}{\bar{\chi}_1}$ ($\braket{\psi_2}{\psi_1}$)$\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_2}$ ($\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_2}$)The orbitals are orthogonal (both factors are 0)

Therefore all four terms are 0 (each term has two factors that are 0), and we have

\begin{equation} \braket{\Phi_a}{\Phi_b}=0 \label{eq:35-ovab0} \end{equation}

This is, in a sense, a natural result: the overlap of wave functions in which electrons with different spin states (an interchange of $\alpha$ and $\beta$) occupy different orbitals ($\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$) is 0.

Math Tool: The overlap integral of two determinants (Löwdin's formula)

The four terms of Eq. \eqref{eq:35-ovab} have the same form as the expansion of a $2\times2$ determinant. In fact, for $\Phi=\lvert u_1u_2\rvert$ and $\Phi'=\lvert w_1w_2\rvert$ ($u_i,w_j$ are spin orbitals), the same expansion gives $$ \braket{\Phi}{\Phi'}=\tfrac12\Bigl[2\braket{u_1}{w_1}\braket{u_2}{w_2}-2\braket{u_1}{w_2}\braket{u_2}{w_1}\Bigr]=\begin{vmatrix}\braket{u_1}{w_1}&\braket{u_1}{w_2}\\ \braket{u_2}{w_1}&\braket{u_2}{w_2}\end{vmatrix} $$ (the terms come in pairs with equal values, because renaming electrons 1 and 2 turns one into the other). That is, the overlap integral is the determinant of the matrix of inner products of the spin orbitals (the same holds for general $N$; this is Löwdin's formula). In the present case, $\begin{vmatrix}\braket{\chi_1}{\bar{\chi}_1}&\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_2}\\ \braket{\bar{\chi}_2}{\bar{\chi}_1}&\braket{\bar{\chi}_2}{\chi_2}\end{vmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{vmatrix}=0$, and we can see directly that all the entries of the determinant are 0.

35.5.2 Matrix element of the Hamiltonian between $\Phi_a$ and $\Phi_b$

If the overlap integral is 0, is the energy matrix element $\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_b}$ also 0? Let us check. We sandwich each part of $\hat{H}=\hat{h}_1+\hat{h}_2+1/r_{12}$ between Eqs. \eqref{eq:35-Phia} and \eqref{eq:35-Phib} and carry out the same four-term expansion as in Section 35.5.1.

One-electron term. In the four terms of $\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{h}_1}{\Phi_b}$, $\hat{h}_1$ enters the factor for electron 1:

\begin{align} \mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{h}_1}{\Phi_b}=\frac12\Bigl[ &\underbrace{\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\bar{\chi}_1}}_{0}\underbrace{\braket{\bar{\chi}_2}{\chi_2}}_{0} +\underbrace{\mel{\bar{\chi}_2}{\hat{h}}{\chi_2}}_{0}\underbrace{\braket{\chi_1}{\bar{\chi}_1}}_{0}\notag\\ &-\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_2}\underbrace{\braket{\bar{\chi}_2}{\bar{\chi}_1}}_{0} -\mel{\bar{\chi}_2}{\hat{h}}{\bar{\chi}_1}\underbrace{\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_2}}_{0}\Bigr]=0 \label{eq:35-h1ab} \end{align}

Here Terms 1 and 2 vanish because in both factors the spins are orthogonal ($\alpha^*\beta$, $\beta^*\alpha$). In Terms 3 and 4, the factor for electron 1, $\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_2}=h_{12}$, is not necessarily 0 by itself, but the factor for electron 2 vanishes by the orthogonality of the orbitals ($\braket{\psi_2}{\psi_1}=0$, $\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_2}=0$), so each term as a whole is 0. For $\hat{h}_2$ of electron 2, renaming the electrons likewise gives $\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{h}_2}{\Phi_b}=0$.

Two-electron term. The four terms of $\mel{\Phi_a}{1/r_{12}}{\Phi_b}$ are

\begin{align} \mel{\Phi_a}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\Phi_b}=\frac12\Bigl[ &\underbrace{\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2}{v}{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_2}}_{\text{the spin of electron 1 }\alpha^*\beta\,\to\,0} +\underbrace{\mel{\bar{\chi}_2\chi_1}{v}{\chi_2\bar{\chi}_1}}_{\text{the spin of electron 1 }\beta^*\alpha\,\to\,0}\notag\\ &-\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2}{v}{\chi_2\bar{\chi}_1} -\mel{\bar{\chi}_2\chi_1}{v}{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_2}\Bigr] \label{eq:35-vab} \end{align}

Here each bra–ket sandwiches $v=1/r_{12}$, with the first $\chi$ referring to electron 1 and the second to electron 2. Terms 1 and 2 vanish because of the spin. In the surviving Terms 3 and 4, the spin factors become $\braket{\alpha}{\alpha}\braket{\beta}{\beta}=1$ (Term 3) and $\braket{\beta}{\beta}\braket{\alpha}{\alpha}=1$ (Term 4), and only the orbital integrals remain:

$$ \mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2}{v}{\chi_2\bar{\chi}_1}=\mel{\psi_1\psi_2}{v}{\psi_2\psi_1}=J_{12},\qquad \mel{\bar{\chi}_2\chi_1}{v}{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_2}=\mel{\psi_2\psi_1}{v}{\psi_1\psi_2}=J_{21}=J_{12} . $$

(The orbital integral has the form $\dfrac{1}{2}\displaystyle\iint\bigl[\psi_1^*(\bm{r}_1)\psi_2(\bm{r}_1)\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}\psi_2^*(\bm{r}_2)\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)+\psi_2^*(\bm{r}_1)\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}\psi_1^*(\bm{r}_2)\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)\bigr]$, which is exactly the integrand of Definition 35.4.) Therefore, we obtain

\begin{equation} \mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_b}=-\frac12\bigl(J_{12}+J_{12}\bigr)=-J_{12} =-\mel{\psi_1\psi_2}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\psi_2\psi_1}=-\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\chi_2\bar{\chi}_1} \label{eq:35-Hab} \end{equation}

All the one-electron terms vanish, and only the exchange term remains.

Caution: Even when the overlap is 0, the matrix element of the Hamiltonian is not necessarily 0

Interestingly, although $\braket{\Phi_a}{\Phi_b}=0$, we have $\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_b}=-J_{12}\ne0$. This is not a contradiction. That the overlap integral is 0 only means that the two states are orthogonal; it does not mean that the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$ does not mix the two states. $\hat{H}$ can “connect” the two states, and the off-diagonal element $-J_{12}$ expresses the strength with which the two states mix (the strength with which a degenerate level is split; the same structure as the two-level system of Chapter 28). In Section 35.6, this off-diagonal element produces the energy difference $2J_{12}$ between the singlet and the triplet.

Question: If the spacing between levels is large, does $J$ become small?

One is tempted to think, “If the energy spacing between the two orbitals becomes large, $1/r_{12}$ approaches 0, so $J$ should become small.” This is not correct, however. $1/r_{12}$ is a quantity determined by the distance between the two electrons and has no direct relation to the spacing between the levels. What determines $J_{12}$ is the magnitude and spread of the overlap density $\rho_{12}=\psi_1^*\psi_2$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Jdef}). Figure 35.6 is an example for the hydrogen molecule (minimal basis); it shows that as the internuclear distance $R$ is increased, the orbital energy difference $\varepsilon_{\mathrm u}-\varepsilon_{\mathrm g}$ between $\sigma_{\mathrm g}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm u}$ approaches 0, whereas the exchange integral $J_{\mathrm{gu}}$ in fact increases. For $R\to\infty$, $\rho_{\mathrm{gu}}=(\rho_{AA}-\rho_{BB})/(2\sqrt{1-S^2})\to(\phi_A^2-\phi_B^2)/2$, and the exchange integral approaches the self-repulsion of the 1s charge clouds of the two atoms, $(AA\vert AA)/2\approx0.39\,E_{\mathrm h}$. Even when the levels are degenerate (the spacing is 0), $J$ remains large.

35.5.3 Matrix elements between the ground state $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ and the singly excited state $\Phi_a$

Now we examine the overlap integral and the energy matrix element between the ground state $\Phi_{\mathrm g}=\lvert\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1\rvert$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Phig}) and the singly excited state $\Phi_a=\lvert\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2\rvert$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Phia}). $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ and $\Phi_a$ share the spin orbital $\chi_1$, and are related by the replacement of $\bar{\chi}_1$ (spin down in the lower level) by $\bar{\chi}_2$ (spin down in the upper level). In the level diagram (Figure 35.7), the spin-up electron ($\chi_1$) stays in $\psi_1$, and only the spin-down electron moves from $\psi_1$ to $\psi_2$.

Overlap integral. From Löwdin's formula (Section 35.5.1),

$$ \braket{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\Phi_a}=\begin{vmatrix}\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_1}&\braket{\chi_1}{\bar{\chi}_2}\\ \braket{\bar{\chi}_1}{\chi_1}&\braket{\bar{\chi}_1}{\bar{\chi}_2}\end{vmatrix} =\begin{vmatrix}1&0\\0&\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_2}\end{vmatrix}=1\cdot0-0\cdot0=0 . $$

$\braket{\chi_1}{\bar{\chi}_2}$ ($\alpha^*\beta$) and $\braket{\bar{\chi}_1}{\chi_1}$ ($\beta^*\alpha$) vanish because of the orthogonality of the spin functions, and $\braket{\bar{\chi}_1}{\bar{\chi}_2}=\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_2}=0$ is the orthogonality of the orbitals.

One-electron term. We write the four terms obtained by multiplying the two terms of the bra $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ by the two terms of the ket $\Phi_a$:

\begin{align} \mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{h}_1}{\Phi_a}=\frac12\Bigl[ &\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_1}\underbrace{\braket{\bar{\chi}_1}{\bar{\chi}_2}}_{\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_2}=0} -\underbrace{\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\bar{\chi}_2}}_{\alpha^*\beta\,\to\,0}\underbrace{\braket{\bar{\chi}_1}{\chi_1}}_{0}\notag\\ &-\underbrace{\mel{\bar{\chi}_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_1}}_{\beta^*\alpha\,\to\,0}\underbrace{\braket{\chi_1}{\bar{\chi}_2}}_{0} +\mel{\bar{\chi}_1}{\hat{h}}{\bar{\chi}_2}\underbrace{\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_1}}_{1}\Bigr] =\frac12h_{12} \label{eq:35-h1ga} \end{align}

Here Term 1 vanishes because of the factor for electron 2 (orbital orthogonality), and Terms 2 and 3 vanish because of spin. Only Term 4 survives: the factor for electron 1 is $\mel{\bar{\chi}_1}{\hat{h}}{\bar{\chi}_2}=\mel{\psi_1}{\hat{h}}{\psi_2}\equiv h_{12}$ ($\beta^*\beta=1$), and the factor for electron 2 is $\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_1}=1$. The $\hat{h}_2$ term gives the same value $\tfrac12h_{12}$, so the one-electron terms add up to $h_{12}$. If $\psi_1,\psi_2$ are eigenfunctions of the one-electron Hamiltonian $\hat{h}$, then $h_{12}=0$, but in general it is not 0.

Two-electron term. Similarly, we write four terms:

\begin{align} \mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\Phi_a}=\frac12\Bigl[ &\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}{v}{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2} -\underbrace{\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}{v}{\bar{\chi}_2\chi_1}}_{\text{electron 1 is }\alpha^*\beta\,\to\,0}\notag\\ &-\underbrace{\mel{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_1}{v}{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2}}_{\text{electron 1 is }\beta^*\alpha\,\to\,0} +\mel{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_1}{v}{\bar{\chi}_2\chi_1}\Bigr]. \label{eq:35-vga} \end{align}

Terms 2 and 3 vanish because of spin. In Term 1, the spin factor of electron 1 is $\braket{\alpha}{\alpha}=1$ and that of electron 2 is $\braket{\beta}{\beta}=1$, and the orbital integral $\mel{\psi_1\psi_1}{v}{\psi_1\psi_2}$ remains. In Term 4, too, the spin factor is $\braket{\beta}{\beta}\braket{\alpha}{\alpha}=1$, and the orbital integral $\mel{\psi_1\psi_1}{v}{\psi_2\psi_1}$ remains; this has the same value as Term 1 (by relabeling electrons 1 and 2). Let us define this value as

\begin{equation} X\equiv\mel{\psi_1\psi_1}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\psi_1\psi_2}=\iint\lvert\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\rvert^2\,\frac{1}{r_{12}}\,\psi_1^*(\bm{r}_2)\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)\,\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2 \label{eq:35-X} \end{equation}

Then the two-electron term is $\tfrac12(X+X)=X$. $X$ is the electrostatic potential that the charge cloud $\lvert\psi_1\rvert^2$ creates at the position of electron 2, averaged over the transition density $\psi_1^*\psi_2$. Since it does not have the form in which the electrons interchange orbitals (electron 1 goes $\psi_1\to\psi_1$, and only electron 2 goes $\psi_1\to\psi_2$), it is, strictly speaking, not an exchange integral. It is an integral of “mixed type,” of a form intermediate between the Coulomb integral and the exchange integral. Putting the above together,

\begin{equation} \mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a}=h_{12}+X . \label{eq:35-Hga} \end{equation}

Discussion: This matrix element is not an “exchange repulsion energy”

Looking at Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Hga}, one might be tempted to read it as “there is an exchange repulsion energy between $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ and $\Phi_a$” or “a state excited with the spin preserved is unstable.” But it cannot be read that way. (i) $h_{12}+X$ is not an exchange integral $J$. It is a quantity that changes sign when $\psi_2$ is replaced by $-\psi_2$ (that is, when the phase is changed), and it is not a quantity that expresses the “magnitude of an energy.” (ii) Whether a state is stable or unstable (whether its energy is high or low) is decided by comparing the diagonal elements $\mel{\Phi}{\hat{H}}{\Phi}$ (Section 35.6). The off-diagonal element is the quantity that decides whether the two states mix.

35.5.4 Brillouin's theorem

When does $h_{12}+X$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Hga} vanish? Here we meet an important theorem of the Hartree–Fock approximation.

Theorem 35.4: Brillouin's theorem (the two-electron case)

When the energy $E_{\mathrm g}=2h_{11}+U_{11}$ of $\Phi_{\mathrm g}=\lvert\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1\rvert$ is minimized with respect to the orbital $\psi_1$ (that is, when the best orbital $\psi_1$ of the Hartree–Fock approximation is chosen), this $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ does not mix, through the Hamiltonian, with the singly excited state $\Phi_a$:

\begin{equation} \mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a}=h_{12}+X=0 \label{eq:35-Bril} \end{equation}

($\psi_2$ is any orbital orthogonal to $\psi_1$. Also for a general $N$-electron system, the matrix element between the Hartree–Fock ground state and a singly excited state in which one electron has been moved from an occupied orbital to an empty orbital is 0.)

Derivation: Variation of the orbital, and the gradient in the direction of mixing $\Phi_a$ into $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$

We consider real orbitals. Take the normalized orbital obtained by mixing a little of the orthogonal orbital $\psi_2$ into the orbital $\psi_1$,

$$ \psi(\varepsilon)=\frac{\psi_1+\varepsilon\psi_2}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^2}}\qquad(\varepsilon\text{ is a small real number}) $$

and compute $E_{\mathrm g}=2h[\psi]+U[\psi]$ with it, then differentiate with respect to $\varepsilon$. The denominator comes from $\braket{\psi_1+\varepsilon\psi_2}{\psi_1+\varepsilon\psi_2}=1+\varepsilon^2$. The one-electron energy is

$$ h[\psi(\varepsilon)]=\frac{h_{11}+2\varepsilon h_{12}+\varepsilon^2h_{22}}{1+\varepsilon^2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\left.\frac{\dd h}{\dd\varepsilon}\right|_0=2h_{12} . $$

For the Coulomb integral, we expand the four factors of $\psi(\varepsilon)$ and keep terms through first order in $\varepsilon$. From the term $U_{11}$, in which four $\psi_1$ appear, and the four terms in which just one $\psi_2$ enters (all with the same value $X$; Eq. \eqref{eq:35-X}), we obtain

$$ U[\psi(\varepsilon)]=\frac{U_{11}+4\varepsilon X+O(\varepsilon^2)}{(1+\varepsilon^2)^2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\left.\frac{\dd U}{\dd\varepsilon}\right|_0=4X . $$

Therefore

\begin{equation} \left.\frac{\dd E_{\mathrm g}}{\dd\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=2\cdot2h_{12}+4X=4\,(h_{12}+X)=4\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a} \label{eq:35-grad} \end{equation}

If $\psi_1$ is the orbital that minimizes $E_{\mathrm g}$, then $\varepsilon=0$ is a minimum point and the gradient is 0. Hence $h_{12}+X=0$, and Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Bril} holds.

∎

In other words, $\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a}$ is the gradient that tells at what rate the ground-state energy changes when the occupied orbital $\psi_1$ is mixed with the empty orbital $\psi_2$ ($1/4$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:35-grad}). An orbital for which the gradient is not 0 is not yet the best one. This gradient $h_{12}+X$ equals the matrix element $\mel{\psi_1}{\hat{F}}{\psi_2}$ of the Fock operator $\hat{F}$, which we introduce in Chapter 36 (the Hartree–Fock equations express the same content as $\mel{\psi_a}{\hat{F}}{\psi_b}=0$ ($a$ occupied, $b$ empty), written in the form of an equation for the orbitals).

Example 35.5: The hydrogen-like 1s orbital of the helium atom is not a Hartree–Fock orbital

The hydrogen-like 1s orbital $\psi_1$ ($Z=2$) and 2s orbital $\psi_2$ ($Z=2$) of the helium atom ($Z=2$) are orthogonal to each other, and both are eigenfunctions of $\hat{h}=-\tfrac12\nabla^2-2/r$ ($h_{12}=0$). (a) Using the value of $X$, find $\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a}$ and confirm that $\psi_1$ is not a Hartree–Fock orbital. (b) How far does the energy drop for the orbital $\psi(\varepsilon)$ obtained by mixing $\psi_2$ into $\psi_1$? The integrals needed are $h_{11}=-2$, $h_{22}=-\tfrac12$, $U_{11}=\tfrac54$, $U_{12}=\tfrac{34}{81}$, $J_{12}=\tfrac{32}{729}$, $U_{22}=\tfrac{77}{256}$, $X=\mel{\psi_1\psi_1}{v}{\psi_1\psi_2}=\dfrac{8192\sqrt2}{64827}=0.17871$, and $Y=\mel{\psi_2\psi_2}{v}{\psi_1\psi_2}=0.017163$ (in units of $E_{\mathrm h}$; the first four integrals are the values from Chapter 29, and $X,Y$ are values obtained by computing integrals of spherically symmetric orbitals).

Solution. (a) Since $h_{12}=0$, Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Hga} gives $\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a}=0+X=0.1787\,E_{\mathrm h}$ ($4.86\ \mathrm{eV}$), which is not 0. Therefore, $\psi_1$ is not a Hartree–Fock orbital, and the gradient $\dd E_{\mathrm g}/\dd\varepsilon=4X=0.7148\,E_{\mathrm h}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:35-grad} is positive, so making $\varepsilon$ negative (mixing $\psi_2$ into $\psi_1$ out of phase) lowers the energy. Here the sign of $\psi_2$ (2s) has been chosen to be positive near the nucleus and negative far from it. Subtracting $\psi_2$ from $\psi_1$ reduces the amplitude near the nucleus and increases the amplitude in the outer region. That is, the orbital changes in the direction of spreading out (for $\varepsilon=-0.198$ in (b) below, the average distance $\expval{r}$ increases from $0.75\,a_0$ to $0.94\,a_0$).

(b) We compute $E_{\mathrm g}=2h+U$ for $\psi(\varepsilon)$. Here $h=\dfrac{h_{11}+\varepsilon^2h_{22}}{1+\varepsilon^2}$ ($h_{12}=0$). For $U$, we expand the four factors of $\psi(\varepsilon)$ and count the terms in which $\psi_2$ appears $k$ times. $k=0$: $U_{11}$ (one term). $k=1$: $X$ (four terms). $k=2$: $\mel{\psi_2\psi_1}{v}{\psi_2\psi_1}$ and $\mel{\psi_1\psi_2}{v}{\psi_1\psi_2}$, in which $\psi_2$ stands at the coordinate of the same electron in the bra and the ket, are the Coulomb integral $U_{12}$, two terms. The remaining four, $\mel{\psi_1\psi_1}{v}{\psi_2\psi_2}$, $\mel{\psi_2\psi_2}{v}{\psi_1\psi_1}$, $\mel{\psi_1\psi_2}{v}{\psi_2\psi_1}$, and $\mel{\psi_2\psi_1}{v}{\psi_1\psi_2}$, are the terms in which two of the $\psi$'s in the bra (or two in the ket, or the pair with the orbitals exchanged between electrons 1 and 2) are $\psi_2$; for real orbitals each of them is the self-repulsion of the overlap density $\rho_{12}$, that is, the exchange integral $J_{12}$ (the $\binom42=6$ ways of choosing split into $2+4$). $k=3$: $Y$ (four terms). $k=4$: $U_{22}$ (one term). Hence

$$ E_{\mathrm g}(\varepsilon)=\frac{2(h_{11}+\varepsilon^2h_{22})}{1+\varepsilon^2}+\frac{U_{11}+4X\varepsilon+(2U_{12}+4J_{12})\varepsilon^2+4Y\varepsilon^3+U_{22}\varepsilon^4}{(1+\varepsilon^2)^2}. $$

At $\varepsilon=0$ this gives $-4+1.25=-2.75\,E_{\mathrm h}$, and the gradient is $4X=0.7148$. Finding the minimum value numerically, we obtain $E_{\mathrm g}=-2.8236\,E_{\mathrm h}$ at $\varepsilon=-0.198$, which is lower by $0.0736\,E_{\mathrm h}$ ($2.0\ \mathrm{eV}$). Merely changing the shape of the orbital a little (spreading it outward) lowers the energy by this much. This direction agrees with what we saw in Example 35.2 and Exercise 35.1: the optimal effective nuclear charge is $Z'=27/16$, smaller than $Z=2$ (the orbital spreads out). The energy obtained here does not yet reach the energy $-2.8617\,E_{\mathrm h}$ of the Hartree–Fock approximation with the best single orbital (because only 2s has been mixed in), but $\psi_1$ has been improved in the direction indicated by Brillouin's theorem.

35.5.5 The excited state $\Phi_c$ with aligned spins

Finally, we consider the state $\Phi_c=\lvert\chi_1\chi_2\rvert$ in which the spin direction of the excited electron is not changed, so that both the lower level and the upper level hold one electron with spin up (Figure 35.8).

\begin{equation} \Phi_c=\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \begin{vmatrix} \chi_1(\bm{x}_1)&\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\\ \chi_1(\bm{x}_2)&\chi_2(\bm{x}_2) \end{vmatrix} =\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)-\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\Bigr] \label{eq:35-Phic} \end{equation}

We examine the quantities between this state and the ground state $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ using the same four-term expansion as before. The bra of $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ contains $\bar{\chi}_1$ ($\beta$ spin), whereas the spin orbitals $\chi_1,\chi_2$ in the ket of $\Phi_c$ both have $\alpha$ spin. Therefore, in every term of the expansion there is one factor containing $\bar{\chi}_1^*$, and the spin integral of that factor is $\int\beta^*\alpha\,\dd\sigma=0$.

  • Overlap integral: $\braket{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\Phi_c}=\begin{vmatrix}\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_1}&\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_2}\\ \braket{\bar{\chi}_1}{\chi_1}&\braket{\bar{\chi}_1}{\chi_2}\end{vmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{vmatrix}=0$ ($\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_2}=\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_2}=0$, and the bottom row is 0 because of the spin).
  • One-electron term: Of the four terms in $\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{h}_1}{\Phi_c}=\dfrac12\Bigl[\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_1}\braket{\bar{\chi}_1}{\chi_2}-\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_2}\braket{\bar{\chi}_1}{\chi_1}-\mel{\bar{\chi}_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_1}\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_2}+\mel{\bar{\chi}_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_2}\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_1}\Bigr]$, the second factor of Terms 1 and 2 is $\beta^*\alpha$ (0), and the first factor of Terms 3 and 4 is $\beta^*\alpha$ (0), so all of them vanish. Of these, $\braket{\bar{\chi}_1}{\chi_2}$ in Term 1 and $\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_2}$ in Term 3 also vanish because of the orbital orthogonality $\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_2}=0$. In Terms 2 and 4, however, the orbital factors ($h_{12}$ and $\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_1}=1$) are not 0, and only the spin orthogonality eliminates them. The difference from $\Phi_a$ lies precisely in this Term 4: in $\Phi_a$ the spin factor became $\beta^*\beta=1$, and $\tfrac12h_{12}$ remained.
  • Two-electron term: In the four terms of $\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{1/r_{12}}{\Phi_c}=\tfrac12\bigl[\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}{v}{\chi_1\chi_2}-\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1}{v}{\chi_2\chi_1}-\mel{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_1}{v}{\chi_1\chi_2}+\mel{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_1}{v}{\chi_2\chi_1}\bigr]$, electron 2 contains $\beta^*\alpha$ in Terms 1 and 2, and electron 1 does in Terms 3 and 4, so all of them vanish.

Therefore, in this case we have

\begin{equation} \braket{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\Phi_c}=0,\qquad\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_c}=0 \label{eq:35-Hgc} \end{equation}

The term-by-term calculation can also be replaced by the following single remark. Because the spin does not appear in $\hat{H}$, $\hat{H}$ commutes with the $z$ component of the total spin, $\hat{S}_z$. $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ is an eigenstate of $\hat{S}_z$ with eigenvalue $0$ (one electron with spin up and one with spin down), and $\Phi_c$ is an eigenstate with eigenvalue $+\hbar$ (two electrons with spin up); the matrix element between states with different eigenvalues is always 0 if $\hat{H}$ commutes with $\hat{S}_z$ (an operator that commutes with $\hat{S}_z$ does not change the eigenvalue of $\hat{S}_z$).

(By the same argument, $\Phi_{\bar{c}}=\lvert\bar{\chi}_1\bar{\chi}_2\rvert$ (two electrons with spin down, eigenvalue $-\hbar$) also does not mix with $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$.)

Whether the spin direction of the excited electron is kept or changed alters the relation to the ground state. Then which of $\Phi_a$ and $\Phi_c$ has the lower energy? That is a question of the diagonal elements, which we answer in Section 35.6.

35.6 Excited-State Energies and the Singlet–Triplet Splitting

35.6.1 The rule for diagonal elements — the Coulomb term always, the exchange term only for parallel spins

In Section 35.5 we examined the (off-diagonal) matrix elements between different states. The energy of an excited state itself is the diagonal element $\mel{\Phi}{\hat{H}}{\Phi}$, with the same state on both sides. From the result for the ground state (Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Eg1}) and the calculations up to Section 35.5, the following rules emerge.

  • The Coulomb term $U$ always appears, for every pair of electrons and for any combination of spins, when the bra and the ket are the same state (the initial and final states are the same, that is, for a diagonal element) (the repulsion between charge clouds does not depend on spin).
  • The exchange term $-J$ appears, in the direction of lowering the energy (stabilizing), only for a pair of electrons with the same spin direction and different orbitals (for antiparallel spins it vanishes because the spin integral is $\braket{\alpha}{\beta}=0$; it is impossible for parallel spins to occupy the same orbital, by the Pauli exclusion principle).

We extend this rule to the general case of $N$ electrons in Section 35.7. Here we verify it by expansion for three states of a two-electron system.

35.6.2 The energy of the doubly excited state $\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}$

The state in which both electrons occupy the upper orbital $\psi_2$ (Figure 35.1, right) has the form obtained from $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ by replacing $\chi_1,\bar{\chi}_1$ with $\chi_2,\bar{\chi}_2$:

\begin{equation} \Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \begin{vmatrix} \chi_2(\bm{x}_1)&\bar{\chi}_2(\bm{x}_1)\\ \chi_2(\bm{x}_2)&\bar{\chi}_2(\bm{x}_2) \end{vmatrix} =\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\bar{\chi}_2(\bm{x}_2)-\bar{\chi}_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)\Bigr] \label{eq:35-Phiex} \end{equation}

The calculation of Section 35.2 (read $\chi_1\to\chi_2$, $\psi_1\to\psi_2$) can be used as it is. The one-electron term is $\mel{\chi_2}{\hat{h}_1}{\chi_2}+\mel{\chi_2}{\hat{h}_2}{\chi_2}=2h_{22}$, and in the two-electron term the exchange term is 0 because of the spin (the electrons are antiparallel), leaving only the Coulomb term $U_{22}$:

\begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{ex}}=\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}} =\mel{\chi_2}{\hat{h}_1}{\chi_2}+\mel{\chi_2}{\hat{h}_2}{\chi_2}+\mel{\chi_2\bar{\chi}_2}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\chi_2\bar{\chi}_2} =2h_{22}+U_{22} \label{eq:35-Eex} \end{equation}

(The spin factor of $\mel{\chi_2\bar{\chi}_2}{v}{\bar{\chi}_2\chi_2}$ is $\braket{\alpha}{\beta}\braket{\beta}{\alpha}=0$.)

Example 35.6: The $2\mathrm{s}^2$ doubly excited state of the helium atom

Using Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Eex}, find the energy $E_{\mathrm{ex}}$ of the state in which both electrons of the helium atom ($Z=2$) occupy the hydrogen-like 2s orbital ($Z=2$). Here, for the hydrogen-like 2s orbital, $\expval{\hat{T}}=Z^2/8$, $\expval{1/r}=Z/4$, and $U_{22}=\tfrac{77}{512}Z$ (Chapter 29, Section 29.4). Also find the excitation energy from the ground state.

Solution. $h_{22}=\expval{\hat{T}}-Z\expval{1/r}=\dfrac{Z^2}{8}-\dfrac{Z^2}{4}=-\dfrac{Z^2}{8}$. For $Z=2$, $h_{22}=-\tfrac12\,E_{\mathrm h}$. $U_{22}=\tfrac{77}{512}\times2=\tfrac{77}{256}=0.3008\,E_{\mathrm h}$. Hence

$$ E_{\mathrm{ex}}=2h_{22}+U_{22}=2\times\Bigl(-\frac12\Bigr)+0.3008=-0.6992\,E_{\mathrm h}=-19.03\ \mathrm{eV} . $$

The excitation energy from the ground state ($-2.75\,E_{\mathrm h}$ in Example 35.2) is $-0.6992-(-2.75)=2.051\,E_{\mathrm h}=55.8\ \mathrm{eV}$. Because both electrons of this state are in the $n=2$ shell, it is higher than the state in which one electron has been pulled infinitely far from the nucleus (the state with one electron left in the 1s orbital of $\mathrm{He^+}$, with energy $-2\,E_{\mathrm h}$). Such a doubly excited state is an unstable state that can undergo autoionization, in which one electron gives energy to the other and flies out of the atom.

35.6.3 The energy of the singly excited state $\Phi_a$

We find the diagonal element of the singly excited state $\Phi_a=\lvert\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2\rvert$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Phia}). Expanding the one-electron term $\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{h}_1}{\Phi_a}$ into four terms, we obtain

\begin{align} \mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{h}_1}{\Phi_a}=\frac12\Bigl[ &\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_1}\braket{\bar{\chi}_2}{\bar{\chi}_2} +\mel{\bar{\chi}_2}{\hat{h}}{\bar{\chi}_2}\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_1}\notag\\ &-\underbrace{\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\bar{\chi}_2}}_{\alpha^*\beta\,\to\,0}\underbrace{\braket{\bar{\chi}_2}{\chi_1}}_{0} -\underbrace{\mel{\bar{\chi}_2}{\hat{h}}{\chi_1}}_{\beta^*\alpha\,\to\,0}\underbrace{\braket{\chi_1}{\bar{\chi}_2}}_{0}\Bigr] =\frac12\bigl(h_{11}+h_{22}\bigr) \label{eq:35-h1aa} \end{align}

Here Terms 3 and 4 vanish by the orthogonality of the spins, and from $\braket{\bar{\chi}_2}{\bar{\chi}_2}=\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_1}=1$, Terms 1 and 2 reduce to $\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_1}=h_{11}$ and $\mel{\bar{\chi}_2}{\hat{h}}{\bar{\chi}_2}=h_{22}$. Likewise, $\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{h}_2}{\Phi_a}$ takes the same value $\tfrac12(h_{11}+h_{22})$, after renaming the integration variables as in $\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}_1}{\chi_1}=\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}_2}{\chi_1}$. Therefore the one-electron terms total $h_{11}+h_{22}$ (since one electron sits in each of the two orbitals, this is the sum of the one-electron energies of the two orbitals).

Expanding the two-electron term into four terms in the same way, we obtain

\begin{align} \mel{\Phi_a}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\Phi_a}=\frac12\Bigl[ &\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2}{v}{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2} +\mel{\bar{\chi}_2\chi_1}{v}{\bar{\chi}_2\chi_1}\notag\\ &-\underbrace{\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2}{v}{\bar{\chi}_2\chi_1}}_{\alpha^*\beta\,\to\,0} -\underbrace{\mel{\bar{\chi}_2\chi_1}{v}{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2}}_{\beta^*\alpha\,\to\,0}\Bigr] =\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2}=U_{12} \label{eq:35-vaa} \end{align}

Here Terms 3 and 4 (the exchange terms) are 0, because the spin factor of electron 1 is $\braket{\alpha}{\beta}$ or $\braket{\beta}{\alpha}$. Terms 1 and 2 have the same value $U_{12}$. Putting these together,

\begin{equation} E_a=\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a}=h_{11}+h_{22}+U_{12} . \label{eq:35-Ea} \end{equation}

Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Ea} is the energy of the state in which the electrons are in separate orbitals with antiparallel spins; there is no exchange term, only the Coulomb term. The calculation for $\Phi_b$ is the same, merely interchanging $\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta$, and $\mel{\Phi_b}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_b}=h_{11}+h_{22}+U_{12}=E_a$.

35.6.4 The energy of the spin-aligned excited state $\Phi_c$ — the exchange term appears

Next we find the diagonal element of the spin-aligned excited state $\Phi_c=\lvert\chi_1\chi_2\rvert$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Phic}). The expansion has the same form as for $\Phi_a$; the difference is that, because the spins are aligned, the spin factor of every term becomes 1. The one-electron term is

$$ \mel{\Phi_c}{\hat{h}_1}{\Phi_c}=\frac12\Bigl[\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_1}\braket{\chi_2}{\chi_2}+\mel{\chi_2}{\hat{h}}{\chi_2}\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_1}-\mel{\chi_1}{\hat{h}}{\chi_2}\underbrace{\braket{\chi_2}{\chi_1}}_{0}-\mel{\chi_2}{\hat{h}}{\chi_1}\underbrace{\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_2}}_{0}\Bigr]=\frac12(h_{11}+h_{22}) $$

where the last two terms vanish by the orbital orthogonality $\braket{\psi_1}{\psi_2}=0$. The two-electron term is

$$ \mel{\Phi_c}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\Phi_c}=\frac12\Bigl[\mel{\chi_1\chi_2}{v}{\chi_1\chi_2}+\mel{\chi_2\chi_1}{v}{\chi_2\chi_1}-\mel{\chi_1\chi_2}{v}{\chi_2\chi_1}-\mel{\chi_2\chi_1}{v}{\chi_1\chi_2}\Bigr]=U_{12}-J_{12} $$

Here the spin factor of each of the four terms is $\braket{\alpha}{\alpha}\braket{\alpha}{\alpha}=1$, so the orbital integrals $U_{12}$ (two terms) and $J_{12}$ (two terms) remain as they are. (For $\Phi_a$ the last two terms vanished through the spin, whereas for $\Phi_c$ they do not.) Therefore,

\begin{equation} E_c=\mel{\Phi_c}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_c}=h_{11}+h_{22}+U_{12}-J_{12}=E_a-J_{12} \label{eq:35-Ec} \end{equation}

The state excited with the spins aligned is lower in energy than the state excited with the spins in opposite directions, by an amount equal to the exchange integral $J_{12}\ge0$ (Theorem 35.2). This is the effect of the exchange interaction: electrons with parallel spins avoid each other because of the Pauli exclusion principle (the Fermi hole, also called the exchange hole; Chapter 33), and the repulsion energy is lowered. Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Ec} shows that, when exciting, aligning the spin directions makes the state more stable. Note that it is not the values of $\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a}$ and $\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_c}$ of Section 35.5 themselves that speak of “stable” or “unstable”; stability is determined by the comparison of these diagonal elements.

35.6.5 Singlet and triplet — $E_{\mathrm S}=E_a+J_{12}$, $E_{\mathrm T}=E_a-J_{12}$

$\Phi_a$ and $\Phi_b$ both have the energy $E_a$ (they are degenerate), but by the result $\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_b}=-J_{12}$ of Section 35.5.2 they mix with each other. When two degenerate states mix through an off-diagonal element, the correct states are linear combinations of the two (degenerate perturbation theory; Chapter 28, Section 28.2). The $2\times2$ matrix of the Hamiltonian in the basis $\{\Phi_a,\Phi_b\}$ is

\begin{equation} \begin{pmatrix}\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a}&\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_b}\\ \mel{\Phi_b}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a}&\mel{\Phi_b}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_b}\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}E_a&-J_{12}\\-J_{12}&E_a\end{pmatrix} \label{eq:35-2x2} \end{equation}

The eigenvalues, from $\det\begin{pmatrix}E_a-\lambda&-J_{12}\\-J_{12}&E_a-\lambda\end{pmatrix}=(E_a-\lambda)^2-J_{12}^2=0$, are $\lambda=E_a\pm J_{12}$. The eigenvectors are $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$ for $\lambda=E_a+J_{12}$ and $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ for $\lambda=E_a-J_{12}$ (one can check that $\begin{pmatrix}E_a&-J\\-J&E_a\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}=(E_a+J)\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}$).

Derivation: These two eigenstates are the spin singlet and triplet

Substituting $\chi_a=\psi_a\alpha$ and $\bar{\chi}_a=\psi_a\beta$ into Eqs. \eqref{eq:35-Phia} and \eqref{eq:35-Phib}, we separate the spatial part and the spin part.

$$ \Phi_a=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)\,\alpha(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2)-\psi_2(\bm{r}_1)\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\,\beta(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2)\Bigr], $$ $$ \Phi_b=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)\,\beta(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2)-\psi_2(\bm{r}_1)\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)\,\alpha(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2)\Bigr]. $$

Taking the difference $\Phi_a-\Phi_b$, the coefficient of $\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)$ is $\alpha(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2)-\beta(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2)$ and the coefficient of $\psi_2(\bm{r}_1)\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)$ is $-\beta(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2)+\alpha(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2)$, so the same spin part can be factored out. In the sum $\Phi_a+\Phi_b$, the coefficients are $\alpha\beta+\beta\alpha$ and $-(\beta\alpha+\alpha\beta)$:

\begin{align} \Phi_{\mathrm S}&\equiv\frac{\Phi_a-\Phi_b}{\sqrt2}=\underbrace{\frac{\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)+\psi_2(\bm{r}_1)\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)}{\sqrt2}}_{\text{spatial: symmetric}}\ \underbrace{\frac{\alpha(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2)-\beta(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2)}{\sqrt2}}_{\text{spin: singlet (}S=0\text{)}},\label{eq:35-PhiS}\\ \Phi_{\mathrm T}&\equiv\frac{\Phi_a+\Phi_b}{\sqrt2}=\underbrace{\frac{\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)-\psi_2(\bm{r}_1)\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)}{\sqrt2}}_{\text{spatial: antisymmetric}}\ \underbrace{\frac{\alpha(\sigma_1)\beta(\sigma_2)+\beta(\sigma_1)\alpha(\sigma_2)}{\sqrt2}}_{\text{spin: triplet (}S=1,\ M_S=0\text{)}}.\label{eq:35-PhiT} \end{align}

The eigenvector $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$ corresponds to $\Phi_{\mathrm S}$, and $(1,1)/\sqrt2$ to $\Phi_{\mathrm T}$. Overall, the symmetries of the spatial part and the spin part are opposite, so both are antisymmetric under interchange of the electrons (satisfying the Pauli exclusion principle).

∎

From the above, the energies are

\begin{equation} E_{\mathrm S}=E_a+J_{12}\ \ (\text{singlet}),\qquad E_{\mathrm T}=E_a-J_{12}\ \ (\text{triplet}),\qquad E_{\mathrm S}-E_{\mathrm T}=2J_{12}\ge0 \label{eq:35-ST} \end{equation}

The quantity $E_c=E_a-J_{12}=E_{\mathrm T}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Ec} is the energy of the $M_S=+1$ component of the triplet, and it coincides with the energy $E_a-J_{12}$ of the $M_S=0$ component $\Phi_{\mathrm T}$. The three components of the triplet ($M_S=+1,0,-1$, which are $\Phi_c$, $\Phi_{\mathrm T}$, $\Phi_{\bar{c}}$, respectively) have the same energy, because the spin does not appear in $\hat{H}$. This also serves as a consistency check: the two results obtained from different determinants (the 2×2 diagonalization for $\Phi_a,\Phi_b$ and the diagonal element of $\Phi_c$) do not contradict each other.

Figure 35.9 shows how the levels of the determinants $\Phi_a,\Phi_b$ split into a singlet and a triplet.

Intuition: Why is the triplet lower?

The spatial part of the triplet, $[\psi_1(\bm{r}_1)\psi_2(\bm{r}_2)-\psi_2(\bm{r}_1)\psi_1(\bm{r}_2)]/\sqrt2$, gives probability 0 that the two electrons are at the same place $\bm{r}_1=\bm{r}_2$ (because it is antisymmetric). The electrons avoid one another, their average distance becomes larger, and the Coulomb repulsion weakens. The spatial part of the singlet (symmetric) gives a high probability that the electrons are at the same place, and the repulsion becomes stronger. This difference is the $\pm J_{12}$ in $U_{12}\pm J_{12}$. It is the simplest two-electron form of Hund's first rule (electrons in the same shell have lower energy the more their spins are aligned; Chapter 34). The numerical values of the distance between the electrons for two electrons in a box are in Chapter 33, Section 33.6.7, and the relation to Hund's rules is in Chapter 34, Section 34.6.1.

Example 35.7: Checking the singlet–triplet splitting with two electrons in a one-dimensional box

Two electrons in a one-dimensional box of width $L=5\,a_0$ occupy the orbitals $\psi_n=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)$, and the interaction between the electrons is $v(x_1,x_2)=1/\sqrt{(x_1-x_2)^2+d^2}$ ($d=0.5\,a_0$; this is $1/r_{12}$ softened for use in one dimension) (atomic units). The eigenvalues of $\hat{h}=-\tfrac12\,\dd^2/\dd x^2$ are $E_n=n^2\pi^2/(2L^2)$. The values of the integrals obtained by numerical integration are $U_{11}=1.0815$, $U_{22}=0.9356$, $U_{12}=0.9082$, and $J_{12}=0.3242\,E_{\mathrm h}$ (Figure 35.3). Find the energies of the six determinants, and $E_{\mathrm S}$ and $E_{\mathrm T}$.

Solution. $h_{11}=E_1=\pi^2/50=0.1974$ and $h_{22}=E_2=4\pi^2/50=0.7896\,E_{\mathrm h}$. Using the formulas in Table 35.5, we obtain

  • $E_{\mathrm g}=2h_{11}+U_{11}=0.3948+1.0815=1.4763$,
  • $E_{\mathrm{ex}}=2h_{22}+U_{22}=1.5791+0.9356=2.5147$,
  • $E_a=E_b=h_{11}+h_{22}+U_{12}=0.1974+0.7896+0.9082=1.8952$,
  • $E_c=E_{\bar{c}}=E_a-J_{12}=1.8952-0.3242=1.5710$.

From Eq. \eqref{eq:35-ST}, $E_{\mathrm S}=E_a+J_{12}=2.2194$ and $E_{\mathrm T}=E_a-J_{12}=1.5710$, and the splitting is $2J_{12}=0.6485\,E_{\mathrm h}$ ($17.6\ \mathrm{eV}$). We can also confirm that the triplet has the same value as $E_c$. (Because the electrons are confined in a narrow box of $L=5\,a_0\approx2.6$ Å, the exchange integral is large.)

35.6.6 The 1s2s states of the helium atom — The direction of the splitting is correct, but its magnitude is too large

We apply Eq. \eqref{eq:35-ST} to the 1s2s states of the helium atom ($Z=2$). The orbitals are the hydrogen-like 1s ($\psi_1$) and 2s ($\psi_2$) orbitals. We use the values $h_{11}=-2$, $h_{22}=-\tfrac12$ from Examples 35.2 and 35.6, and the integrals $U_{12}=\tfrac{34}{81}=0.41975$ and $J_{12}=\tfrac{32}{729}=0.04390\,E_{\mathrm h}$ from Chapter 29.

\begin{align*} E_a&=h_{11}+h_{22}+U_{12}=-2-0.5+0.41975=-2.0802\,E_{\mathrm h},\\ E_{\mathrm T}&=E_a-J_{12}=-2.1241\,E_{\mathrm h},\qquad E_{\mathrm S}=E_a+J_{12}=-2.0364\,E_{\mathrm h}, \end{align*}

The splitting is $2J_{12}=0.0878\,E_{\mathrm h}=2.39\ \mathrm{eV}$. Experimentally, the $2^3\mathrm{S}$ state of $\mathrm{He}$ lies $19.82\ \mathrm{eV}$ above the ground state and the $2^1\mathrm{S}$ state lies $20.62\ \mathrm{eV}$ above it; in total energy these are about $-2.175\,E_{\mathrm h}$ (triplet) and about $-2.146\,E_{\mathrm h}$ (singlet). Therefore, with the triplet below and the singlet above, the order is correctly reproduced, but the experimental splitting is $20.62-19.82=0.80\ \mathrm{eV}$, and the $2.39\ \mathrm{eV}$ of this model is about three times too large. (This is the same as the first-order perturbation result of Chapter 29. Here we derived the same formula from the diagonalization of Slater determinants.)

The main reason for the overestimate is the relaxation of the orbitals. The hydrogen-like 2s orbital is an orbital that feels the nuclear charge $Z=2$ in full, but a real 2s electron feels a weaker nuclear charge ($1\lt Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\lt2$), because the inner 1s electron shields the charge of the nucleus, and the 2s electron is spread out much more widely (Chapter 18). When the orbital spreads out, the overlap density $\rho_{12}$ with the 1s orbital becomes smaller, and the exchange integral $J_{12}$ drops sharply. Figure 35.10 shows $2J_{12}$ when the 1s orbital is fixed at $Z=2$ and the effective nuclear charge $\zeta$ of the 2s orbital is lowered from $2$ (the 2s orbital was orthogonalized to the 1s orbital). The value $2.39\ \mathrm{eV}$ at $\zeta=2$ (hydrogen-like) reaches the experimental value of $0.80\ \mathrm{eV}$ at $\zeta\approx1.6$. However, this $\zeta\approx1.6$ is merely a value that matches the experimental value; it is not the physical effective nuclear charge of the 2s electron. The one-parameter hydrogen-like 2s orbital is a model that cannot represent the spread of the orbital and the amplitude in its inner region at the same time, and this figure is meant for seeing the qualitative trend that “$J_{12}$ drops sharply when the orbital spreads out.” Hydrogen-like orbitals are sufficient for evaluating the direction of the energy difference, but evaluating its magnitude requires including the relaxation of the orbitals (Hartree–Fock calculations from Chapter 36 on, and calculations that include electron correlation).

35.6.7 Applications to materials — Efficiency of organic EL and thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF)

Application: $2J$ determines the light-emission efficiency of organic EL

An organic EL device (OLED) is a device in which holes injected from the anode and electrons injected from the cathode meet on a light-emitting molecule to form an exciton (an excited state), which then emits light and returns to the ground state. Because the spins of the electron and the hole are unrelated to each other, the four combinations of two spins $\tfrac12$ ($\alpha\alpha,\alpha\beta,\beta\alpha,\beta\beta$) are formed with equal probability. These four combinations split into one singlet state ($S=0$) and three triplet states ($S=1$, $M_S=+1,0,-1$), so excitons are generated in the ratio singlet : triplet $=1:3$ (25%:75%).

In an ordinary fluorescent molecule, only the singlet emits light (fluorescence) in the transition from the excited state back to the ground state (a transition that does not change the spin), while the triplet does not emit light and ends up as heat. For this reason, the upper bound of the internal quantum efficiency of a fluorescent OLED is 25%. In complexes containing heavy metals (Ir, Pt, and so on), the spin–orbit interaction (Chapter 26) is strong and light is emitted from the triplet as well (phosphorescence), so the upper bound becomes 100% (the phosphorescent OLED of Baldo et al. in 1998). Another solution is thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF). If the energy difference $\Delta_{\mathrm{ST}}=E_{\mathrm S}-E_{\mathrm T}$ between the singlet and the triplet is small, the triplet returns to the singlet through thermal energy at room temperature ($k_{\mathrm B}T\approx0.026\ \mathrm{eV}$) (reverse intersystem crossing) and emits light from there (Adachi laboratory, 2012).

According to Eq. \eqref{eq:35-ST}, $\Delta_{\mathrm{ST}}=2J$ ($J$ is the exchange integral between the two orbitals involved in the excitation, the HOMO and the LUMO). $J$ is the self-repulsion of the overlap density $\rho_{\mathrm{HL}}=\psi_{\mathrm H}^*\psi_{\mathrm L}$, and it is of second order in $\rho_{\mathrm{HL}}$ ($J\propto\rho_{\mathrm{HL}}^2$), so the more the spatial overlap between the HOMO and the LUMO is reduced, the more sharply $J$ drops. TADF materials are designed by separating the HOMO onto an electron-donor part and the LUMO onto an electron-acceptor part, and reducing the overlap through a twisted structure. Conversely, in a planar $\pi$-conjugated molecule in which the HOMO and the LUMO spread over the same molecular plane, the overlap density is large and $\Delta_{\mathrm{ST}}$ is large. This design guideline follows precisely from the fact that $J_{12}$ is the “self-repulsion of the overlap density” and not the spacing between levels (Section 35.5.2).

Example 35.8: How much does $\Delta_{\mathrm{ST}}$ decrease when the overlap is reduced?

In a certain planar $\pi$-conjugated molecule, the exchange integral between the HOMO and the LUMO is $J=0.30\ \mathrm{eV}$ ($\Delta_{\mathrm{ST}}=2J=0.60\ \mathrm{eV}$). Suppose the molecule is redesigned as a donor–acceptor type so that the magnitude of the overlap density $\rho_{\mathrm{HL}}$ becomes $1/3$ (without changing its shape). What happens to $J$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{ST}}$? Also, how does the Boltzmann factor $e^{-\Delta_{\mathrm{ST}}/k_{\mathrm B}T}$ at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ (a measure of how readily the triplet is raised to the singlet by thermal energy) change? Use $k_{\mathrm B}T=0.02585\ \mathrm{eV}$ ($300\ \mathrm{K}$).

Solution. Since $J=\iint\rho_{\mathrm{HL}}(\bm{r}_1)\dfrac{1}{r_{12}}\rho_{\mathrm{HL}}(\bm{r}_2)\dd\bm{r}_1\dd\bm{r}_2$ is of second order in $\rho_{\mathrm{HL}}$, the replacement $\rho_{\mathrm{HL}}\to\tfrac13\rho_{\mathrm{HL}}$ gives $J\to(\tfrac13)^2J=J/9$. Hence $J=0.30/9=0.0333\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{ST}}=2J=0.0667\ \mathrm{eV}$. The Boltzmann factor is $e^{-0.60/0.02585}=e^{-23.2}=8\times10^{-11}$ before the redesign and $e^{-0.0667/0.02585}=e^{-2.58}=0.076$ after it, an increase by a factor of about $10^9$. Before the redesign, the triplets can hardly return to the singlet by heat, but after the redesign a fraction of somewhat under one-tenth of them can return to the singlet (the efficiency of an actual TADF molecule is determined by many factors, such as the rate of intersystem crossing, and this estimate is only a guide).

35.7 The Energy of a General Slater Determinant — The Slater–Condon Rules and Electron Correlation

35.7.1 The energy of an $N$-electron Slater determinant

Let us line up the results for the two-electron system. $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ gives $2h_{11}+U_{11}$, $\Phi_a$ gives $h_{11}+h_{22}+U_{12}$, and $\Phi_c$ gives $h_{11}+h_{22}+U_{12}-J_{12}$. Each has the form “the sum of the one-electron energies $h_{aa}$ of the occupied spin orbitals” + “$U_{ab}$ for each electron pair” − “$J_{ab}$ for each pair of electrons with parallel spins.” This is not limited to two electrons. In Chapter 29, Section 29.5, we used the “sum over electron pairs of $U_{xy}-\delta_{s_xs_y}J_{xy}$” as a sum over the electron pairs of $(1\mathrm{s})^22\mathrm{s}$; let us now prove it for a Slater determinant of $N$ electrons.

Theorem 35.5: The energy of a Slater determinant

For the Slater determinant $\Phi$ built from $N$ spin orbitals $\chi_1,\dots,\chi_N$ that are orthogonal to one another and normalized, the expectation value of $\hat{H}=\sum_i\hat{h}(i)+\sum_{i\lt j}\dfrac{1}{r_{ij}}$ is

\begin{equation} E=\mel{\Phi}{\hat{H}}{\Phi}=\sum_{a=1}^Nh_{aa}+\sum_{a\lt b}\bigl(U_{ab}-J_{ab}\bigr)=\sum_ah_{aa}+\frac12\sum_{a,b}\bigl(U_{ab}-J_{ab}\bigr) \label{eq:35-EN} \end{equation}

(here $U_{ab}=\mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{v}{\chi_a\chi_b}$ and $J_{ab}=\mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{v}{\chi_b\chi_a}$; by Eq. \eqref{eq:35-UJspin}, $J_{ab}$ is nonzero only for pairs with parallel spins). In the second equality, we used the fact that $U_{aa}=J_{aa}$, so that the terms with $a=b$ cancel ($U_{aa}-J_{aa}=0$), to rewrite $\sum_{a\lt b}$ as $\tfrac12\sum_{a,b}$.

Proof: Expanding into a sum over permutations

Using the Hartree product $\Pi=\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)\cdots\chi_N(\bm{x}_N)$ and the rearrangement $\hat{P}$ of the electron coordinates (corresponding to a permutation $P$), the Slater determinant is $\Phi=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\sum_P(-1)^P\hat{P}\Pi$ ($(-1)^P$ is the sign of $P$).

[i] If $\hat{O}$ is invariant under interchange of electrons, then $\mel{\Phi}{\hat{O}}{\Phi}=\sum_R(-1)^R\mel{\Pi}{\hat{O}}{\hat{R}\Pi}$. $\hat{H}$ does not change under rearrangement of the electron coordinates (identical particles), so we may take $\hat{O}=\hat{H}$. Substituting $\Phi$, we obtain $$ \mel{\Phi}{\hat{O}}{\Phi}=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{P,Q}(-1)^{P+Q}\mel{\hat{P}\Pi}{\hat{O}}{\hat{Q}\Pi} =\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{P,Q}(-1)^{P+Q}\mel{\Pi}{\hat{P}^{-1}\hat{O}\hat{Q}}{\Pi} =\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{P,Q}(-1)^{P+Q}\mel{\Pi}{\hat{O}\,\hat{P}^{-1}\hat{Q}}{\Pi} $$ (since $\hat{P}$ is unitary, and $\hat{O}$ and $\hat{P}$ commute). Let $R=P^{-1}Q$. Then $(-1)^{P+Q}=(-1)^R$, and for each fixed $P$, as $Q$ varies, $R$ runs through all the permutations exactly once; hence the sum over $P$ gives a factor of $N!$, which cancels the $1/N!$ and yields the formula above.

[ii] The one-electron operator $\hat{h}(i)$. In $\hat{R}\Pi$, electron $k$ occupies the spin orbital $\chi_{R(k)}$ ($R(k)$ is the index to which $R$ sends $k$). We have $\mel{\Pi}{\hat{h}(i)}{\hat{R}\Pi}=\mel{\chi_i}{\hat{h}}{\chi_{R(i)}}\prod_{k\ne i}\braket{\chi_k}{\chi_{R(k)}}$, and by orthonormality the product is nonzero only if $R(k)=k$ for all $k\ne i$. Then only $i$ is left for $R(i)$ as well, so $R$ is the identity permutation. Therefore $\sum_i\mel{\Phi}{\hat{h}(i)}{\Phi}=\sum_i\mel{\chi_i}{\hat{h}}{\chi_i}=\sum_ah_{aa}$.

[iii] The two-electron operator $1/r_{ij}$. Similarly, for $\mel{\Pi}{v(i,j)}{\hat{R}\Pi}$ to be nonzero, we need $R(k)=k$ for every electron $k$ other than $i,j$; $R$ is then either the identity permutation or the transposition $(ij)$ that interchanges only $i$ and $j$. The identity permutation gives $\mel{\chi_i\chi_j}{v}{\chi_i\chi_j}=U_{ij}$, and the transposition $(ij)$ (sign $-1$) gives $-\mel{\chi_i\chi_j}{v}{\chi_j\chi_i}=-J_{ij}$. Summing over all electron pairs ($i\lt j$) gives $\sum_{i\lt j}(U_{ij}-J_{ij})$.

Adding [ii] and [iii], we obtain Eq. \eqref{eq:35-EN}.

∎

Eq. \eqref{eq:35-EN} is the general form of the energy in the Hartree–Fock approximation. Once the set of occupied spin orbitals is fixed, the energy is determined simply by computing the one-electron integrals $h_{aa}$, the Coulomb integrals $U_{ab}$, and the exchange integrals $J_{ab}$ and adding them up. In the companion volumes, AD 05, Chapter 9 (Slater–Condon rules, exchange energy) and AD 07, Chapter 5 ($E_{\mathrm{HF}}=\sum H+\tfrac12\sum(J-K)$, where $J$ and $K$ follow the Szabo–Ostlund notation) take this equation as their starting point.

Example 35.9: The energy of the lithium atom $(1\mathrm{s})^22\mathrm{s}$

Place the three electrons of the lithium atom ($Z=3$) in the hydrogen-like 1s ($Z=3$) and 2s ($Z=3$) orbitals as $\chi_1=1\mathrm{s}\,\alpha$, $\bar{\chi}_1=1\mathrm{s}\,\beta$, $\chi_2=2\mathrm{s}\,\alpha$. Find the energy using Eq. \eqref{eq:35-EN}. The required values are $h_{11}=-\tfrac92$, $h_{22}=-\tfrac98$, $U_{11}=\tfrac{15}{8}$, $U_{12}=\tfrac{17}{27}$, and $J_{12}=\tfrac{16}{243}\,E_{\mathrm h}$ ($Z=3$, hydrogen-like, using the “$Z$-scaling law” of Chapter 29).

Solution. There are three electron pairs. $(\chi_1,\bar{\chi}_1)$: the same 1s orbital with antiparallel spins, contribution $U_{11}$. $(\chi_1,\chi_2)$: 1s and 2s with parallel spins, contribution $U_{12}-J_{12}$. $(\bar{\chi}_1,\chi_2)$: antiparallel spins, contribution $U_{12}$. The one-electron terms are $h_{11}+h_{11}+h_{22}$. The total is

$$ E=2h_{11}+h_{22}+U_{11}+2U_{12}-J_{12} =-9-\frac98+\frac{15}{8}+\frac{34}{27}-\frac{16}{243}=-\frac{6859}{972}=-7.0566\,E_{\mathrm h}=-192.02\ \mathrm{eV} . $$

(Combining the fractions: $-9-\tfrac98+\tfrac{15}{8}=-8.25$, $\tfrac{34}{27}-\tfrac{16}{243}=\tfrac{306-16}{243}=\tfrac{290}{243}=1.19342$, and the sum $=-7.05658$.) The experimental total energy is $-7.478\,E_{\mathrm h}$ ($-203.5\ \mathrm{eV}$), so this model is about $11.5\ \mathrm{eV}$ too high (the same result as the first-order perturbation theory in Chapter 29, Section 29.5).

35.7.2 The Slater–Condon rules — matrix elements between different determinants

In Section 35.5, for the case of two electrons, we computed the matrix elements between different determinants one term at a time. Applying the same argument as in the proof (steps [i]–[iii] of Section 35.7.1) to two different determinants $\Phi$ and $\Phi'$ gives the result for general $N$ electrons. Because the permutation $R$ allows only “orbital differences of up to one electron for a one-electron operator, and of up to two electrons for a two-electron operator,” the following rules are derived (the Slater–Condon rules).

Theorem 35.6: The Slater–Condon rules

The matrix element of $\hat{H}=\sum_i\hat{h}(i)+\sum_{i\lt j}1/r_{ij}$ between two Slater determinants $\Phi$ and $\Phi'$ built from a set of orthonormal spin orbitals is determined by the number of spin orbitals in which $\Phi'$ differs from $\Phi$ (when the columns are arranged so that the differing spin orbitals fall in the same column, and the sign is adjusted accordingly).

  1. The same determinant: $\mel{\Phi}{\hat{H}}{\Phi}=\sum_ah_{aa}+\tfrac12\sum_{a,b}(U_{ab}-J_{ab})$ (Theorem 35.5; the sums run over the occupied spin orbitals of $\Phi$).
  2. Differ by one spin orbital ($\Phi_a^r$, obtained by replacing $\chi_a$ of $\Phi$ with an empty $\chi_r$): \begin{equation} \mel{\Phi}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a^r}=h_{ar}+\sum_{b}\Bigl(\mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{v}{\chi_r\chi_b}-\mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{v}{\chi_b\chi_r}\Bigr) \label{eq:35-SC1} \end{equation} ($b$ runs over all the occupied spin orbitals of $\Phi$; the term with $b=a$ is $0$).
  3. Differ by two spin orbitals ($\chi_a,\chi_b\to\chi_r,\chi_s$): \begin{equation} \mel{\Phi}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_{ab}^{rs}}=\mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{v}{\chi_r\chi_s}-\mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{v}{\chi_s\chi_r} \label{eq:35-SC2} \end{equation}
  4. Differ by three or more spin orbitals: $\mel{\Phi}{\hat{H}}{\Phi'}=0$.

The right-hand sides of Rules 1 and 2 are written using only one-electron integrals (two spin orbitals) and two-electron integrals (at most four spin orbitals). With these rules, the energy matrix between an enormous number of determinants (the matrix of the configuration interaction discussed later) can be assembled from a small number of one-electron and two-electron integrals. Let us check the rules against the results we have so far computed one term at a time.

Example 35.10: Rederiving the results of Section 35.5 with the Slater–Condon rules

(a) Use Rule 1 to find $\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a}$, and compare it with Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Hga}. (b) Use Rule 2 to find $\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_b}$, and compare it with Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Hab}.

Solution. (a) $\Phi_{\mathrm g}=\lvert\chi_1\bar{\chi}_1\rvert$ and $\Phi_a=\lvert\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2\rvert$ differ only in the spin orbital of the second column ($\bar{\chi}_1\to\bar{\chi}_2$). In Rule 1, $a=\bar{\chi}_1$ and $r=\bar{\chi}_2$, and the only common occupied spin orbital is $b=\chi_1$. We have $h_{ar}=h_{\bar{1}\bar{2}}=h_{12}$. For $\mel{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_1}{v}{\bar{\chi}_2\chi_1}$, the spin factor is $\braket{\beta}{\beta}\braket{\alpha}{\alpha}=1$, and the orbital integral is $\mel{\psi_1\psi_1}{v}{\psi_2\psi_1}=X$. For $\mel{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_1}{v}{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2}$, the spin factor of electron 1 is $\braket{\beta}{\alpha}=0$, so it is 0. The total is $h_{12}+X-0=h_{12}+X$, which agrees with Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Hga}.

(b) Interchange the columns of $\Phi_b=\lvert\bar{\chi}_1\chi_2\rvert$ to get $\lvert\chi_2\bar{\chi}_1\rvert=-\Phi_b$. This differs from $\Phi_a=\lvert\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2\rvert$ in two spin orbitals: $\chi_1\to\chi_2$ in the first column and $\bar{\chi}_2\to\bar{\chi}_1$ in the second column. Taking $a=\chi_1$, $b=\bar{\chi}_2$, $r=\chi_2$, $s=\bar{\chi}_1$ in Rule 2, we get $\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{H}}{\lvert\chi_2\bar{\chi}_1\rvert}=\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2}{v}{\chi_2\bar{\chi}_1}-\mel{\chi_1\bar{\chi}_2}{v}{\bar{\chi}_1\chi_2}=J_{12}-0$ (the second term vanishes because the spin factor of electron 1 is $\braket{\alpha}{\beta}=0$). Since $\Phi_b=-\lvert\chi_2\bar{\chi}_1\rvert$, we have $\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_b}=-J_{12}$, which agrees with Eq. \eqref{eq:35-Hab}.

These rules have an important corollary. If we regard the right-hand side of Rule 1 as the “matrix element of the Fock operator” $f_{ar}=h_{ar}+\sum_b(\mel{ab}{v}{rb}-\mel{ab}{v}{br})$ from an occupied orbital $a$ to an empty orbital $r$, then the Hartree–Fock equation (the condition that makes the orbitals the best possible) is $f_{ar}=0$. Therefore Brillouin's theorem (for general $N$ electrons) holds: the matrix element between the Hartree–Fock ground state $\Phi_0$ and a singly excited state $\Phi_a^r$ is $\mel{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a^r}=f_{ar}=0$. On the other hand, the matrix element (Rule 2) with a state $\Phi_{ab}^{rs}$ in which two electrons are excited simultaneously is in general nonzero. For this reason, the determinants of two-electron excitations are the main players that mix into the Hartree–Fock ground state and lower the energy (that is, bring in electron correlation).

35.7.3 The electron correlation energy

Let us state the limitation of the Hartree–Fock approximation in terms of energy. The difference between the value $E_{\mathrm{HF}}$ obtained by minimizing the energy of a single Slater determinant (the Hartree–Fock limit) and the energy $E_{\mathrm{exact}}$ of the exact (nonrelativistic) ground state is called the electron correlation energy:

\begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{corr}}\equiv E_{\mathrm{exact}}-E_{\mathrm{HF}}\ (\lt0) \label{eq:35-Ecorr} \end{equation}

The energy \eqref{eq:35-EN} in the Hartree–Fock approximation contains the Coulomb term $U_{ab}$ (the average repulsion between charge clouds) and the exchange term $J_{ab}$ (the effect of electrons with parallel spins avoiding one another). That is, the exchange energy ($-\tfrac12\sum J$) is included in the Hartree–Fock approximation and is not electron correlation. What is mainly left out is the effect (correlation) by which electrons with antiparallel spins avoid one another because of the Coulomb repulsion. The $U_{11}$ of the ground state $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ is the repulsion when the positions of the two electrons are distributed independently according to $\lvert\psi_1\rvert^2$; it does not include the effect of the electrons moving so as not to come too close to each other.

For the helium atom, $E_{\mathrm{HF}}=-2.8617\,E_{\mathrm h}$ and $E_{\mathrm{exact}}=-2.9037\,E_{\mathrm h}$, so that $E_{\mathrm{corr}}=-0.0420\,E_{\mathrm h}=-1.14\ \mathrm{eV}$. This is only about $1.4\ \%$ of the total energy ($79\ \mathrm{eV}$), but it is comparable in magnitude to the energies of chemical bonds and excitations (a few eV), so it cannot be neglected in quantitative discussions. There are various ways of incorporating electron correlation, such as methods that mix in determinants of two-electron excitations (configuration interaction) and the exchange–correlation functionals of density functional theory (AD 07).

Example 35.11: Electron correlation energy of the hydrogen molecule — mixing two determinants

If the ground state of the hydrogen molecule (STO-3G, $R=1.4\,a_0$) is approximated by $\Phi_0=\lvert\chi_{\mathrm g}\bar{\chi}_{\mathrm g}\rvert$ ($\sigma_{\mathrm g}^2$) alone, the total energy (including the internuclear repulsion $1/R=0.7143\,E_{\mathrm h}$) is $E_{\mathrm{HF}}=-1.1167\,E_{\mathrm h}$. Mix in the doubly excited determinant $\Phi_{\mathrm{D}}=\lvert\chi_{\mathrm u}\bar{\chi}_{\mathrm u}\rvert$ ($\sigma_{\mathrm u}^2$) and find by how much the energy is lowered. The required values are $h_{\mathrm{gg}}=-1.2528$, $h_{\mathrm{uu}}=-0.4756$, $U_{\mathrm{gg}}=0.6746$, $U_{\mathrm{uu}}=0.6975$, and $J_{\mathrm{gu}}=0.1813\,E_{\mathrm h}$ (from the same calculation as in Example 35.3).

Solution. The diagonal elements are (Eqs. \eqref{eq:35-Eg}, \eqref{eq:35-Eex}) $E_0=2h_{\mathrm{gg}}+U_{\mathrm{gg}}=-1.8310$ and $E_{\mathrm D}=2h_{\mathrm{uu}}+U_{\mathrm{uu}}=-0.2537\,E_{\mathrm h}$ (excluding the internuclear repulsion). For the off-diagonal element, $\Phi_0$ and $\Phi_{\mathrm D}$ differ in two spin orbitals, so Rule 2 (with $a=\chi_{\mathrm g}$, $b=\bar{\chi}_{\mathrm g}$, $r=\chi_{\mathrm u}$, $s=\bar{\chi}_{\mathrm u}$) gives $\mel{\chi_{\mathrm g}\bar{\chi}_{\mathrm g}}{v}{\chi_{\mathrm u}\bar{\chi}_{\mathrm u}}-\mel{\chi_{\mathrm g}\bar{\chi}_{\mathrm g}}{v}{\bar{\chi}_{\mathrm u}\chi_{\mathrm u}}=\mel{\sigma_{\mathrm g}\sigma_{\mathrm g}}{v}{\sigma_{\mathrm u}\sigma_{\mathrm u}}-0=J_{\mathrm{gu}}=0.1813$ (the second term vanishes because the spin factor of electron 1 is $\braket{\alpha}{\beta}=0$; for real orbitals, $\mel{\sigma_{\mathrm g}\sigma_{\mathrm g}}{v}{\sigma_{\mathrm u}\sigma_{\mathrm u}}$ equals the exchange integral $J_{\mathrm{gu}}$). The eigenvalues of the $2\times2$ matrix

$$ \begin{pmatrix}-1.8310&0.1813\\0.1813&-0.2537\end{pmatrix} $$

are $\lambda=\dfrac{E_0+E_{\mathrm D}}{2}\pm\sqrt{\Bigl(\dfrac{E_{\mathrm D}-E_0}{2}\Bigr)^2+J_{\mathrm{gu}}^2}=-1.04235\pm\sqrt{0.78865^2+0.1813^2}=-1.04235\pm0.80922$, and the lower one is $-1.85157\,E_{\mathrm h}$. Adding the internuclear repulsion $0.7143$ gives $-1.1373\,E_{\mathrm h}$, so that $E_{\mathrm{corr}}=-1.1373-(-1.1167)=-0.0206\,E_{\mathrm h}=-0.56\ \mathrm{eV}$. In this minimal basis, there are only two determinants of a two-electron singlet that have the same symmetry as $\sigma_{\mathrm g}^2$ ($\Sigma_{\mathrm g}^+$), namely $\Phi_0$ and $\Phi_{\mathrm D}$, so this is the exact value within the minimal basis. The mixing ratio, from the ratio of the eigenvector components, is $c_{\mathrm D}/c_0=-J_{\mathrm{gu}}/(E_{\mathrm D}-\lambda)=-0.1813/1.5979=-0.11$, which is small. However, when the nuclei are pulled apart, $E_{\mathrm D}-E_0$ becomes small and the mixing becomes essential (Chapter 37).

35.8 Summary and Exercises

35.8.1 Summary

  • Using Slater determinants whose columns are the spin orbitals $\chi_a=\psi_a\alpha$ and $\bar{\chi}_a=\psi_a\beta$ (the bar denotes $\beta$ spin), we distinguished the six states of the two-electron system, $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$, $\Phi_{\mathrm{ex}}$, $\Phi_a$, $\Phi_b$, $\Phi_c$, $\Phi_{\bar{c}}$, by drawing them in level diagrams. Since the Hamiltonian contains no spin, whether each term obtained by expanding a determinant is zero can be decided in two stages: the orthogonality of the spin integrals $\int\alpha^*\beta=0$ and the orbital orthogonality.
  • The ground-state energy is $E_{\mathrm g}=2h_{11}+U_{11}$. No exchange term appears for two electrons with antiparallel spins.
  • The Coulomb integral $U_{pq}=\mel{\psi_p\psi_q}{1/r_{12}}{\psi_p\psi_q}$ is the electrostatic repulsion between charge clouds ($U\gt0$), and the exchange integral $J_{pq}=\mel{\psi_p\psi_q}{1/r_{12}}{\psi_q\psi_p}$ is the self-repulsion of the overlap density $\psi_p^*\psi_q$ ($J\ge0$, $U_{pq}\ge J_{pq}$). For spin orbitals, $J_{ab}=\delta_{s_as_b}J_{pq}$. The correspondence with the $J,K$ of Szabo–Ostlund and AD 07 is $U_{ab}\leftrightarrow J_{ab}$, $J_{ab}\leftrightarrow K_{ab}$.
  • Even if the columns are rearranged, the determinant only changes sign, and if the set of spin orbitals is the same, it is the same state ($\Phi_{\mathrm{g}'}=-\Phi_{\mathrm g}$). There is only one ground state.
  • Even though $\braket{\Phi_a}{\Phi_b}=0$, we have $\mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_b}=-J_{12}\ne0$. $J$ is determined not by the level spacing but by the overlap density. $\mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a}=h_{12}+X$ is zero for Hartree–Fock orbitals (Brillouin's theorem). $\Phi_{\mathrm g}$ and $\Phi_c$ have different eigenvalues of $\hat{S}_z$, so they never mix.
  • Diagonal elements: $E_{\mathrm{ex}}=2h_{22}+U_{22}$, $E_a=h_{11}+h_{22}+U_{12}$, $E_c=E_a-J_{12}$. The singlet and triplet energies are $E_{\mathrm S,T}=E_a\pm J_{12}$ (splitting $2J_{12}$; the triplet is lower, as Hund's rules state). For helium 1s2s, $2J=2.39\ \mathrm{eV}$ (experiment $0.80\ \mathrm{eV}$). The 1:3 ratio in OLEDs and the design of TADF are governed by this $2J$.
  • The energy of a general Slater determinant is $E=\sum_ah_{aa}+\tfrac12\sum_{a,b}(U_{ab}-J_{ab})$. The matrix element between different determinants is determined by whether the number of differing spin orbitals is 0, 1, 2, or 3 or more (Slater–Condon). The electron correlation energy is $E_{\mathrm{corr}}=E_{\mathrm{exact}}-E_{\mathrm{HF}}$, and the exchange energy is included in the Hartree–Fock approximation.
\begin{equation} E_{\mathrm g}=2h_{11}+U_{11},\qquad E_a=h_{11}+h_{22}+U_{12},\qquad E_c=E_a-J_{12} \label{eq:35-key1} \end{equation}
\begin{equation} \mel{\Phi_a}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_b}=-J_{12},\qquad E_{\mathrm S}=E_a+J_{12},\quad E_{\mathrm T}=E_a-J_{12},\quad E_{\mathrm S}-E_{\mathrm T}=2J_{12} \label{eq:35-key2} \end{equation}
\begin{equation} E=\sum_ah_{aa}+\frac12\sum_{a,b}\bigl(U_{ab}-J_{ab}\bigr),\qquad U_{ab}=\mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\chi_a\chi_b},\quad J_{ab}=\mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{\tfrac{1}{r_{12}}}{\chi_b\chi_a} \label{eq:35-key3} \end{equation}
\begin{equation} \mel{\Phi_{\mathrm g}}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_a}=h_{12}+X=\mel{\psi_1}{\hat{F}}{\psi_2}=0\quad(\text{Hartree–Fock orbitals; Brillouin's theorem}) \label{eq:35-key4} \end{equation}
