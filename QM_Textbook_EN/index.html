Quantum Mechanics— From the Old Quantum Theory to Relativistic Quantum Mechanics: Quantum Mechanics for Students of Materials Engineering —

This book is a teaching resource that presents the quantum mechanics taught in university physics departments, from the basics, in a single volume. It is based on the three volumes of Yasuhide Mochizuki's handwritten notes, “Physics Notes: Quantum Mechanics.” We have included the axioms, definitions, derivations, worked calculations, figures, questions, and discussions of the original notes without omitting a single one, and, so that students of materials engineering who want to understand quantum mechanics thoroughly from the basics (including precocious high school students) can read it on their own, we have added the pronunciation of new symbols, the steps between the lines of the algebra, and concrete numerical examples and figures. Content that is not in the original notes but is indispensable for a textbook (the exact solution of the Morse potential, degenerate perturbation theory, the Roothaan–Hall equations, group theory and Tanabe–Sugano diagrams, time-dependent perturbation theory and linear response, field operators, the Dirac equation, and so on) has been added in chapters of its own.

The organization of the chapters follows the history of physics. Starting from the “dawn of quantum theory” (blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect, the Bohr model, and matter waves), we carefully solve potential wells, the harmonic oscillator, the hydrogen atom, and the Morse potential with the Schrödinger equation, pass through angular momentum and spin, perturbation theory, and the variational principle, and proceed to the Hartree–Fock approximation for many-electron systems, chemical bonds and bands, and ligand field theory, which gives rise to $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$, familiar from inorganic materials. Finally, we arrive at time-dependent quantum mechanics, which treats excited states and light, at second quantization, which describes a world in which the number of particles changes, and at relativistic quantum mechanics. Each section contains worked examples in which no intermediate steps are omitted, and the end of each chapter contains exercises. The examples draw heavily on topics from materials science, such as quantum wells and quantum dots in semiconductors, the STM, the colors of complexes and gemstones, the infrared absorption of molecules, and the electron microscope.

The mathematics you need can be supplemented through links to the relevant sections of the companion volume “University Mathematics (AD 02),” and classical physics through links to the relevant sections of “Introductory University Physics (AD 03) (in Japanese).” This book also serves as a foundation for going on to “Computational Materials Science (AD 05),” “Solid-State Physics (AD 06),” “Density Functional Theory (AD 07),” “Linear Response Theory (AD 08),” and “Elementary Particle Physics (AD 09).”

How to read this book
  • Part I (Before Entering the World of Quantum Mechanics) portrays the strangeness of quantum mechanics with almost no equations. It is a good idea to read this part first to get an overview of the whole.
  • Part II (The Dawn of Quantum Theory) through Part IV (Orbital and Spin Angular Momentum) form the backbone of quantum mechanics. We recommend reading them in order.
  • Part V (Perturbation Theory) and Part VI (The Variational Principle) are methods of approximation and form the foundation for Part VII (Many-Electron Systems) and Part VIII (Ligand Field Theory). Those who go on to the electronic states of materials and computational science would do well to read Part V through Part VIII consecutively.
  • Part IX (Time-Dependent Quantum Mechanics) and Part X (Second Quantization) are introductions to the interaction of light and matter, linear response, and the many-body problem, and Part XI (Relativistic Quantum Mechanics) is an introduction to elementary particle physics.
  • Physical constants, units, and symbols can be looked up in Appendix B. The solutions to the exercises are in Appendix A.
