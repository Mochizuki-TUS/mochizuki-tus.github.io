Chapter 7Electrons Passing through Slits — Wave Nature and Probability

We pass a stream of electrons through a narrow slit (a long, thin gap) and examine, on a screen placed beyond it, the positions where the electrons arrive. If electrons were tiny particles, we would expect them to arrive only within a narrow band as wide as the slit. In reality, however, the narrower the slit, the more widely the electrons spread as they arrive, and within that spread there is a pattern of fringes in which places where many electrons arrive alternate with places where none arrive at all. This phenomenon closely resembles the diffraction of water waves or light passing through a narrow gap, and it is the most direct evidence that electrons have the properties of waves (de Broglie's hypothesis from the previous chapter). Moreover, although electrons are detected one at a time as points, collecting many of them produces fringes. The purpose of this chapter is to investigate this puzzle with equations.

In this chapter, we derive, without skipping a single line, the result that when electrons of momentum $p$ strike a slit of width $2a$, their distribution on the screen takes the form $\bigl(\sin u/u\bigr)^2$ (with $u\propto x$). The procedure has three steps: (1) add up the waves emitted from all points of the slit (Huygens' principle); (2) approximate the path length so that it can be integrated; (3) integrate to obtain the probability density $\abs{\psi}^2$. Because many approximations are involved, we check with concrete numbers under what conditions each approximation is valid, using a dimensionless number (the Fresnel number) determined by the wavelength, the slit width, and the distance to the screen. Next, we consider what happens when the same experiment is carried out with particles of different mass, and we understand, in the language of the $\delta$ function, why the diffraction spread becomes smaller for heavier particles and why everyday objects travel straight (a geometric shadow). Finally, we treat the double slit and see that the results are entirely different when we “add probabilities” and when we “add probability amplitudes,” and that this difference is determined by whether “it is possible in principle to tell which slit the particle passed through.”

The calculation is almost the same as that for the diffraction and interference of light that you learned in classical physics (AD 03, “Introductory University Physics”). The difference is that the intensity of the wave takes on the meaning of “the probability that a single electron arrives there.” The arrival point of an individual electron cannot be predicted; only the distribution of many electrons follows $\abs{\psi}^2$. This is the Born interpretation, which we state as an axiom in the next chapter.

7.1 The Experimental Setup — Directing an Electron Beam at a Slit of Width 2a

7.1.1 The problem

The arrangement of the experiment is shown in Figure 7.1. From the electron gun on the left, a stream of electrons with a uniform momentum $p$ (an electron beam) flies out toward the right and strikes, perpendicularly, a narrow slit cut in a vertically standing wall. On the far side of the slit, at a distance $L$, there is a screen (a detection plane; we call it plane A) that records the positions where the electrons arrive.

Problem: The probability distribution behind a single slit

An electron beam of momentum $p$ is directed perpendicularly at a single slit of width $2a$. Find the distribution (probability density) of the probability that an electron arrives on plane A (on the $x$ axis), which lies at a distance $L$ from the slit.

Caution: $y$ is the coordinate on the slit plane, $x$ is the coordinate on the screen plane

The electrons travel horizontally; we write $y$ for the vertical coordinate on the slit plane and $x$ for the vertical coordinate on the screen plane. Note that the electrons do not travel “in the $x$ direction.” We assigned different letters to coordinates pointing in the same direction in order to distinguish “from which point of the slit the electron emerged” ($y$) from “where on the screen it arrived” ($x$). The difference $x-y$ between the position $x$ on the screen and the position $y$ on the slit determines the length of the path.

Since no force acts between the two planes, the electron moves as a free particle, keeping its momentum $p$. We suppose that the electrons arrive one at a time, repeat the same experiment many times, and examine the distribution of the positions where they arrive. What we want to know is the probability density $P(x)$ that gives the probability $P(x)\dd x$ that an electron arrives near the position $x$.

7.1.2 Caveats about the model

A real slit has both a thickness and a finite length, and electrons may also fly at relativistic speeds. If we included everything from the start, the calculation would become hard to follow, so we make the following simplifications.

  1. Treat the problem as a two-dimensional problem. We regard the slit as a strip that is infinitely long in the direction perpendicular to the page. Then we need to consider the motion of the particle only within the plane of the page (the direction of propagation and the $y$ direction).
  2. Regard the wave as a one-component scalar wave. We ignore the orientation of the electron's spin (Part IV), assuming that it does not affect the result.
  3. Take the incident electron beam to be a plane wave of momentum $p$. Because the incidence is perpendicular, the phase of the wave is the same at every point of the slit.
  4. Drop the decay of the wave amplitude and the obliquity factor. The amplitude of a wave spreading from a single point of the slit (in two dimensions, a cylindrical wave: a wave spreading circularly in the plane from a single point, whose amplitude decreases in inverse proportion to the square root $\sqrt r$ of the distance $r$) decreases with distance, and a wave emitted obliquely is weaker. However, in the range $x\ll L$ the length of the path hardly changes, so we consider only the phase (for the numerical values of a thought experiment reaching $x/L\sim0.4$, we check the limitations of the approximation in Section 7.3.2 and Section 7.6.4).
  5. Since the speed of the electron is small compared with the speed of light, we take $p=mv$ (for electrons of tens of kV, replace $p$ and $\lambda$ by the relativistic expressions of Chapter 6, Section 6.3).
  6. The screen is sufficiently far from the slit, with $a\ll L$ and $\abs{x}\ll L$. We check this condition concretely in Section 7.2.

7.1.3 Adding up the waves emitted from every point of the slit

As we saw in Chapter 6, a wave of wavelength $\lambda=h/p$ accompanies an electron of momentum $p$, and that wave is represented by a plane wave with a phase like $e^{ikx}$. Let us check once more the relation between the wavenumber $k$ and the momentum $p$.

Math Tool: $k=p/\hbar$

Rewriting the de Broglie relation $p=h/\lambda$ using $\hbar=h/2\pi$ and the wavenumber $k=2\pi/\lambda$, we obtain $$ p=\frac{h}{\lambda}=\frac{h}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}=\hbar\,k\qquad\text{hence}\qquad k=\frac{p}{\hbar} $$ Therefore, the phase factor of a plane wave depending on the position $x$ can also be written as $$ e^{ikx}=e^{i\frac{\hbar k x}{\hbar}}=e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}x} $$ (the second equality merely multiplies the numerator and denominator by $\hbar$). The phase of the wave is given by $p/\hbar$ times the distance. From now on we use $k=p/\hbar$ and write the phase as $kr=pr/\hbar$. Note also that the unit of $p$ is $\mathrm{kg\,m/s}$ and the unit of $\hbar$ is $\mathrm{J\,s}=\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$, so the unit of $p/\hbar$ is $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, and multiplying by $r$ gives a dimensionless phase.

Next we consider where on the screen, and how much, the electron wave that has passed through the slit arrives. What we use here is the same idea as Huygens' principle, which explained the diffraction of light (AD 03, Chapter 11, Section 11.8). Each point of the wave front passing through the slit itself becomes a new wave source and emits a wave, and the wave on the screen is the superposition of the waves emitted from all points of the slit.

The quantity that represents the electron wave is the probability amplitude (or wave function), which generally takes complex values. The probability amplitude that an electron passing through the slit at the point at position $y$ reaches the position $x$ on the screen is proportional to the phase $e^{ikr}$. Here $r$ is the length of the path from that point to the point on the screen (Figure 7.1). Therefore, the probability amplitude that an electron passing through the whole slit reaches the position $x$ is the sum of this over the whole slit:

\begin{equation} \psi(x)=A_0\int_{-a}^{a}e^{i\frac{p\,r}{\hbar}}\,\dd y \label{eq:7-psi-int} \end{equation}

Here $A_0$ is a constant independent of $x$ that is determined by the intensity of the incident electron beam (it also includes the constant factor multiplying the overall phase of the probability amplitude). The $e^{ipr/\hbar}$ in the integrand depends on both $x$ and $y$ through $r$. Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-int} is the equation that serves as the starting point of this chapter. The probability density $\abs{\psi(x)}^2$ can be written as $\abs{\psi}^2=\psi^*\psi$ using the complex conjugate $\psi^*$ of $\psi$.

Intuition: Adding up the phases along the paths as rotating arrows

The $e^{ipr/\hbar}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-int} can be regarded as an arrow (a phasor) in the complex plane of magnitude 1 whose direction is rotated through the angle $kr=pr/\hbar$. The arrows arriving from the points of the slit point in slightly different directions, because the path length $r$ differs slightly from point to point. The arrow obtained by adding all of them tip to tail is $\psi(x)$. When $x$ is different, the way the path lengths differ changes and the degree to which the arrows' directions are aligned changes, so there are places where a long arrow is produced (places that electrons reach easily) and places where the arrows cancel one another (places that electrons do not reach). This is the origin of the fringes (we examine it in detail in Figure 7.9).

This idea of “attaching the phase $e^{iS/\hbar}$ to all possible paths and adding them up” is also the starting point of the formulation of quantum mechanics called Feynman's path integral. For a free particle of constant energy, the action along a path (the abbreviated action $\int p\,\dd s$) becomes the product $pr$ of the momentum $p$ and the path length $r$ (this is a different quantity from the action $\int L\,\dd t$, which includes time). In this chapter we consider only the straight-line paths through the slit and add up the paths with different positions $y$ on the slit (a full treatment of the path integral is beyond the scope of this book).

Why is the time factor not written?

The wave function of a stationary electron beam, including time, has the form $\Psi(x,t)=\psi(x)\,e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ (Part III). The time phase factor $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ is common to the waves from all points of the slit, so after the summation it merely multiplies the whole, and it does not appear in the probability density $\abs{\Psi}^2=\abs{\psi}^2$. Therefore, in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-int} we wrote only the spatial part.

Example 7.1: Wavenumber and phase

(1) Find the de Broglie wavelength $\lambda$ and the wavenumber $k=p/\hbar$ of an electron with speed $v=1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. (2) Find the wavenumber of an electron with energy $100\ \mathrm{eV}$. (3) For the electron in (1), find the phase difference $k\,\Delta r$ between two waves whose path lengths differ by $0.10\ \mathrm{mm}$. (4) Verify with the numbers in (1) that $k=p/\hbar$ and $p=h/\lambda$ say the same thing.

Solution. (1) We have $p=m_{\mathrm e}v=9.109\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}\times1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}=9.109\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$. The wavelength is $$ \lambda=\frac{h}{p}=\frac{6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}}{9.109\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}}=7.27\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m}=0.727\ \mathrm{mm} $$ The wavenumber is $k=2\pi/\lambda=2\pi/(7.27\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m})=8.64\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Computing it with $\hbar$ instead gives $k=p/\hbar=9.109\times10^{-31}/(1.0546\times10^{-34})=8.64\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, which agrees (this is the check asked for in (4)). A wavelength of $0.7\ \mathrm{mm}$ is a size visible to the naked eye. An electron with a speed of $1\ \mathrm{m/s}$ is, as a wave, a very slow and very long wave.

(2) The momentum of an electron with $100\ \mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm{J}$ is $p=\sqrt{2m_{\mathrm e}E}=\sqrt{2\times9.109\times10^{-31}\times1.602\times10^{-17}}=5.40\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$. Hence $k=p/\hbar=5.40\times10^{-24}/(1.0546\times10^{-34})=5.12\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$ (wavelength $\lambda=2\pi/k=0.1226\ \mathrm{nm}$).

(3) $k\,\Delta r=8.64\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\times1.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m}=0.864\ \mathrm{rad}$ (about $50^\circ$). Since the path difference is $0.10/0.727=0.137$ times the wavelength $0.727\ \mathrm{mm}$, the phase difference is $2\pi\times0.137=0.864\ \mathrm{rad}$, which agrees.

7.2 Deriving the Probability Distribution for a Single Slit

Let us actually evaluate Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-int}. The integrand contains the path length $r=\sqrt{L^2+(x-y)^2}$, and as it stands the integral cannot be carried out. We therefore approximate $r$ by a linear (and quadratic) expression in $y$. We use two approximations. Both merely “drop what is small,” but we proceed by checking with numbers what is small and what it is small compared with.

7.2.1 Expanding the path length $r$

From the right triangle in Figure 7.1 (base $L$, height $x-y$), the Pythagorean theorem gives $r=\sqrt{L^2+(x-y)^2}$. Factoring out $L$, we obtain

\begin{equation} r=\sqrt{L^2+(x-y)^2}=L\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{x-y}{L}\right)^2}\;\approx\;L\left\{1+\frac12\left(\frac{x-y}{L}\right)^2\right\} \label{eq:7-r-approx} \end{equation}

In the last approximation we used the following binomial approximation.

Math Tool: The binomial approximation $(1+\varepsilon)^s\approx1+s\varepsilon$

If $\abs{\varepsilon}\ll1$, then for any real number $s$ $$ (1+\varepsilon)^s\approx1+s\,\varepsilon $$ holds. Taking the Maclaurin expansion of $f(\varepsilon)=(1+\varepsilon)^s$ about $\varepsilon=0$, we find, since $f(0)=1$ and $f'(0)=s$, that $f(\varepsilon)=1+s\varepsilon+\dfrac{s(s-1)}{2}\varepsilon^2+\cdots$, and the approximation is this expansion with the terms of second order and higher dropped (AD 02, Chapter 4, Section 4.2). This approximation is often written as $(1+b)^a\approx1+ab$ ($\abs{b}\ll1$), but in this book we use $s$ and $\varepsilon$ so that the letters are not confused with the half-width $a$ of the slit and the opening width $b$. In Eq. \eqref{eq:7-r-approx}, $s=\tfrac12$ and $\varepsilon=\bigl((x-y)/L\bigr)^2$. The first term dropped is $\dfrac{s(s-1)}{2}\varepsilon^2=-\dfrac{\varepsilon^2}{8}$, which in terms of $r$ becomes $-\dfrac{(x-y)^4}{8L^3}$. Consider the part of this term that depends on the integration variable $y$ (the $4x^3y$ term in the expansion of $(x-y)^4$, of magnitude roughly $\dfrac{x^3y}{2L^3}$). The range in which its phase $\dfrac{p}{\hbar}\dfrac{x^3y}{2L^3}$ is small compared with 1 rad is called the range of validity of the paraxial approximation (the part independent of $y$ becomes a common phase factor that is taken outside the integral and does not affect the probability density). For example, for an electron with $x/L=0.36$, $k=8.64\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, and $\abs{y}\le1\ \mathrm{mm}$ (Section 7.3), the phase of the part independent of $y$ is large, about $19\ \mathrm{rad}$, but the part that depends on $y$ is about $0.2\ \mathrm{rad}$ (Section 7.6.4).

Expanding $(x-y)^2$ on the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-r-approx}, we obtain

\begin{equation} r\approx L+\frac{(x-y)^2}{2L}=L+\frac{x^2}{2L}-\frac{xy}{L}+\frac{y^2}{2L} \label{eq:7-r-expand} \end{equation}

(We divided $(x-y)^2=x^2-2xy+y^2$ by $2L$.) The four terms on the right-hand side are as follows: $L$ is a reference length independent of $x$ and $y$, $x^2/2L$ is the term independent of $y$, $-xy/L$ is the term containing the product of $x$ and $y$, and $y^2/2L$ is the term containing $y$ alone.

7.2.2 Simplifying the exponent of the phase

Multiplying Eq. \eqref{eq:7-r-expand} by $p/\hbar$ gives the phase.

\begin{equation} \frac{p\,r}{\hbar}=\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{x^2}{2L}\right)-\frac{p\,x}{\hbar L}\,y+\frac{p}{\hbar}\frac{y^2}{2L} \label{eq:7-phase} \end{equation}

The first term does not contain $y$, so it can be taken outside the integral. The second term can be written compactly by setting $-\dfrac{px}{\hbar L}\,y=-q\,y$, that is, by using $q\equiv\dfrac{p\,x}{\hbar L}$ (in units of $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$). The third term is the $y^2$ term. A sum in the exponent can be written as a product of exponentials, so Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-int} becomes

\begin{equation} \psi(x)=A_0\,e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{x^2}{2L}\right)}\int_{-a}^{a}e^{-i\frac{p\,x}{\hbar L}\,y}\;e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\frac{y^2}{2L}}\,\dd y \label{eq:7-psi-3} \end{equation}

The factor $e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}(L+\frac{x^2}{2L})}$ in front of the integral is the phase factor of the wave that reaches the point $x$ on the screen from the center of the slit ($y=0$); its absolute value is 1, so it does not affect the probability density.

Caution: The denominator and the coefficient in the exponent are easy to get wrong

The denominator of the phase factor in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-3} is $x^2/\mathbf{2L}$, not $x^2/2$, and the coefficient of the cross term is $\dfrac{p\,x}{\hbar L}$, not $\dfrac{2px}{\hbar L}$. When expanding $(x-y)^2/(2L)$, it is easy to drop the $L$ in the denominator, or to forget to cancel the 2 of $-2xy$ against the 2 in the denominator. A dimension check helps. Both $x^2/(2L)$ and $xy/L$, which appear in the exponent of the phase, have the dimension of length, and only after they are multiplied by $p/\hbar$ (in units of $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$) do they become a dimensionless phase. The expression $x^2/2$, with the $L$ in the denominator dropped, has the dimension $\mathrm{m^2}$, so multiplying it by $p/\hbar$ does not make it dimensionless, and it cannot serve as a phase. However, the error $x^2/2$ is caught by a dimension check, whereas the error in the coefficient 2 of the cross term (writing $2px/\hbar L$) is not caught by dimensions. This one is checked by the expansion itself: dividing $(x-y)^2=x^2-2xy+y^2$ by $2L$ gives $-\dfrac{2xy}{2L}=-\dfrac{xy}{L}$, so the 2 cancels.

7.2.3 The condition for dropping the $y^2$ term — the Fresnel number

In the integral of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-3}, if the last factor $e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\frac{y^2}{2L}}$ is close to 1, it can be dropped and the integral can be carried out. Being close to 1 means that the exponent is close to 0, that is, that the phase $\dfrac{p}{\hbar}\dfrac{y^2}{2L}$ is sufficiently small compared with 1 rad. Using $k=p/\hbar=2\pi/\lambda$, in the range of integration $\abs{y}\le a$ we find

\begin{equation} \frac{p}{\hbar}\frac{y^2}{2L}=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\frac{y^2}{2L}=\frac{\pi\,y^2}{\lambda L}\;\le\;\frac{\pi\,a^2}{\lambda L} \label{eq:7-fresnel-phase} \end{equation}

The dimensionless quantity that appears on the far right is the following one.

Definition 7.1: Fresnel number

For a slit of half-width $a$, wavelength $\lambda$, and distance $L$ from the slit to the screen, the quantity $$ N_{\mathrm F}\equiv\frac{a^2}{\lambda L} $$ is called the Fresnel number. The condition for dropping the $y^2$ term is $\pi N_{\mathrm F}\ll1$, that is, $N_{\mathrm F}\ll1$. Diffraction under this condition is called Fraunhofer diffraction (far-field diffraction).

Why isn't “$y/L$ is small, so $y^2/L$ is even smaller” enough?

The condition $y/L\ll1$ (more precisely, $\abs{x-y}/L\ll1$) is the condition for using the binomial approximation (Eq. \eqref{eq:7-r-approx}), that is, the paraxial approximation; it is not the condition for dropping the $y^2$ term. Since $y^2/L$ has the dimension of length, whether it is “close to 0” cannot be decided by itself. This is because what determines the phase of a wave is a length compared with the wavelength $\lambda$. As Eq. \eqref{eq:7-fresnel-phase} shows, what really has to be small is the phase $\pi y^2/(\lambda L)$, obtained by comparing $y^2/(2L)$ with $\lambda/(2\pi)$, and this is a stricter condition than $y/L\ll1$. For example, if $a=1\ \mathrm{mm}$ and $L=1\ \mathrm{m}$, then $y/L\le10^{-3}$ is small for any wavelength. However, for light of wavelength $\lambda=1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, we get $N_{\mathrm F}=\dfrac{(10^{-3})^2}{10^{-6}\times1}=1$, so the phase reaches $\pi\ \mathrm{rad}$ and the $y^2$ term cannot be dropped. On the other hand, for an electron with a speed of $1\ \mathrm{m/s}$ ($\lambda=0.727\ \mathrm{mm}$), $N_{\mathrm F}=1.4\times10^{-3}$ and the term may be dropped. Even for the same geometry, the condition is satisfied or violated depending on the wavelength.

For an electron with a speed of $1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ ($\lambda=0.727\ \mathrm{mm}$), a slit half-width $a=1.0\ \mathrm{mm}$, and $L=1.0\ \mathrm{m}$, we have $N_{\mathrm F}=\dfrac{(1.0\times10^{-3})^2}{7.27\times10^{-4}\times1.0}=1.4\times10^{-3}$, and even at its largest the phase is only $\pi N_{\mathrm F}=4.3\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad}$. Therefore we may put $$ e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\frac{y^2}{2L}}\approx1\qquad(\abs{y}\le a) $$ The magnitude of the error is at most $4.3\times10^{-3}$, because $\abs{e^{i\phi}-1}\le\abs{\phi}$ ($\phi$ is the phase).

7.2.4 Carrying out the integral

Dropping the $y^2$ term, Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-3} becomes

\begin{equation} \psi(x)\approx A_0\,e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{x^2}{2L}\right)}\int_{-a}^{a}e^{-iq\,y}\,\dd y,\qquad q=\frac{p\,x}{\hbar L} \label{eq:7-psi-4} \end{equation}

For fixed $x$, $q$ is a constant, and the integrand is just $e^{-iqy}$. Since $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd y}e^{-iqy}=-iq\,e^{-iqy}$, an antiderivative of $e^{-iqy}$ is $\dfrac{e^{-iqy}}{-iq}$. Therefore (for $q\ne0$) we have

\begin{align} \int_{-a}^{a}e^{-iqy}\dd y &=\left[\frac{e^{-iqy}}{-iq}\right]_{-a}^{a} =\frac{e^{-iqa}-e^{iqa}}{-iq} =\frac{e^{iqa}-e^{-iqa}}{iq} \notag\\ &=\frac{2i\sin(qa)}{iq} =\frac{2\sin(qa)}{q} \label{eq:7-int} \end{align}

In the third equality we multiplied the numerator and denominator by $-1$, and in the fourth equality we used $e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}=2i\sin\theta$, which follows from Euler's formula (AD 02, Chapter 21, Section 21.4), with $\theta=qa$. In the limit $q\to0$, $\sin(qa)/q\to a$, so the integral approaches $2a$. This is the width of the slit itself (the waves from all points add up in phase), and it indeed agrees with $\displaystyle\int_{-a}^{a}1\,\dd y=2a$.

Substituting $q=px/(\hbar L)$ and introducing the dimensionless quantity $u\equiv qa=\dfrac{p\,a\,x}{\hbar L}$, we obtain

\begin{equation} \int_{-a}^{a}e^{-iqy}\dd y=\frac{2\hbar L}{p}\,\frac{\sin\frac{pa}{\hbar L}x}{x}=2a\,\frac{\sin u}{u},\qquad u=\frac{p\,a\,x}{\hbar L} \label{eq:7-int-u} \end{equation}

(we used $\dfrac{2\hbar L}{px}=\dfrac{2a}{u}$). Note that the factor $2$ is easy to drop (a constant can be absorbed into $A_0$, so it does not affect the shape of the probability density). Thus

\begin{equation} \psi(x)=2a\,A_0\,e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{x^2}{2L}\right)}\,\frac{\sin u}{u} \label{eq:7-psi-single} \end{equation}

is obtained.

7.2.5 The probability density $\abs{\psi(x)}^2$

The probability density that the electron arrives near the position $x$ is the square of the absolute value of the probability amplitude, $\abs{\psi(x)}^2$. In Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-single}, the absolute value of the phase factor is $\bigl|e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}(L+x^2/2L)}\bigr|=1$, so, taking $A_0$ to be real, we first have

\begin{equation} \abs{\psi(x)}^2=A_0^{\,2}\,\Bigl|e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{x^2}{2L}\right)}\Bigr|^2\frac{4\hbar^2L^2}{p^2}\,\frac{\sin^2\frac{pa}{\hbar L}x}{x^2}\label{eq:7-I-single0} \end{equation}

(we squared the $2\hbar L\sin(\cdot)/(px)$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-int-u}). The absolute value of the phase factor is 1, and since we may write $\dfrac{4\hbar^2L^2}{p^2x^2}=\dfrac{4a^2}{\left(\frac{pa}{\hbar L}x\right)^2}$, we find that

\begin{equation} \abs{\psi(x)}^2=4a^2A_0^{\,2}\left(\frac{\sin u}{u}\right)^2=4a^2A_0^{\,2}\left(\frac{\sin\frac{pa}{\hbar L}x}{\frac{pa}{\hbar L}x}\right)^2 \label{eq:7-I-single} \end{equation}

holds.

Theorem 7.1: Fraunhofer diffraction by a single slit

When an electron beam of momentum $p$ is directed perpendicularly at a single slit of width $2a$, then, within the range where the paraxial approximation and the Fraunhofer approximation ($N_{\mathrm F}=a^2/(\lambda L)\ll1$) hold, the probability density on the screen satisfies $$ \abs{\psi(x)}^2\propto\left(\frac{\sin u}{u}\right)^2,\qquad u=\frac{p\,a\,x}{\hbar L}=\frac{2\pi a}{\lambda L}\,x $$ The probability density vanishes, $\abs{\psi}^2=0$, at $u=\pm n\pi$ ($n=1,2,\dots$), and it has its principal maximum at $u=0$. The positions of the zeros are $x_n=\dfrac{n\pi\hbar L}{pa}=n\,\dfrac{\lambda L}{2a}$.

Here, the two ways of writing $u$ differ only in that $p=h/\lambda=2\pi\hbar/\lambda$ has been used to rewrite $\dfrac{pa}{\hbar L}=\dfrac{2\pi a}{\lambda L}$. The zero condition $u=n\pi$ becomes $\dfrac{2\pi a x}{\lambda L}=n\pi$, which gives $x_n=n\,\dfrac{\lambda L}{2a}$.

Math Tool: Integrals of $\sin u/u$

We state in advance the two integrals that will be used later (the derivation is in Example 7.2). $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin u}{u}\dd u=\pi,\qquad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^2u}{u^2}\dd u=\pi $$ The first follows from the Fourier transform of a rectangular pulse (AD 02, Chapter 30, Section 30.2). How a square wave is built up from a sum of trigonometric functions can be seen in the Fourier series simulator. $\sin u/u$ is sometimes written as $\operatorname{sinc}u$.

7.2.6 Normalization — the meaning of “normalizing gives $1/\pi$”

Since the electron always arrives somewhere on the screen, the probability density integrates to 1 over the whole screen (normalization). Let us fix the constant $4a^2A_0^{\,2}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-I-single} by this condition. Let $\alpha\equiv\dfrac{pa}{\hbar L}$ (in units of $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$; this $\alpha$ and the $\beta\equiv\dfrac{pb}{2\hbar L}$ of Section 7.4 both have the same dimension as a wavenumber, are symbols used only in this chapter, and are unrelated to the symbols used for the spin functions (Part IV), the fine-structure constant, the resonance integral, and so on). Then $u=\alpha x$, and since $\dd x=\dd u/\alpha$, we have

\begin{equation} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\abs{\psi}^2\dd x=4a^2A_0^{\,2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^2(\alpha x)}{(\alpha x)^2}\dd x=\frac{4a^2A_0^{\,2}}{\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^2u}{u^2}\dd u=\frac{4a^2A_0^{\,2}}{\alpha}\,\pi=1 \label{eq:7-norm} \end{equation}

Hence $4a^2A_0^{\,2}=\alpha/\pi$, and the probability density becomes

\begin{equation} P(x)=\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\left(\frac{\sin\alpha x}{\alpha x}\right)^2=\frac{pa}{\pi\hbar L}\left(\frac{\sin\frac{pa}{\hbar L}x}{\frac{pa}{\hbar L}x}\right)^2 \label{eq:7-P-x} \end{equation}

This is the normalized probability density.

Caution: $1/\pi$ is the coefficient when normalized in the variable $u$

The statement “normalizing gives $1/\pi$” refers to the coefficient of the probability density $P_u(u)=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\left(\dfrac{\sin u}{u}\right)^2$ ($\int P_u\dd u=1$) taken with $u=\dfrac{pax}{\hbar L}$ as the variable. The probability density taken with the position $x$ as the variable has the coefficient $\dfrac{\alpha}{\pi}$, as in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-P-x}. When the variable is changed, the value of a probability density changes according to $P_u\dd u=P\dd x$, namely $P=P_u\,\dd u/\dd x=\alpha P_u$ (the units also differ: $P_u$ is dimensionless, whereas $P$ is in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$). Distinguish clearly which variable you have normalized with respect to. Since the shape (the $x$ dependence) is the same, you need not worry about the coefficient when discussing the shape of the graph.

Note that although the paraxial approximation is an approximation for the range $\abs{x}\ll L$, we carried the normalization integral out to $\pm\infty$. This is a good approximation when the probability contained in the tails of the distribution (which decay as $1/x^2$) is small, that is, when the principal maximum fits comfortably within the extent $L$ of the screen ($\alpha L\gg1$). For the electron of this chapter's thought experiment ($v=1\ \mathrm{m/s}$), $\alpha L=8.6$, and about $3.5\ \%$ (roughly, $1/(\pi\alpha L)=3.7\ \%$) remains in the tails with $\abs{x}\gt L$, so $\alpha/\pi$ is an approximate value. For heavy particles, for which $\alpha L\gg1$ holds, or for electrons accelerated through tens of kV, the approximation holds well.

7.2.7 How to read the graph

Figure 7.2 shows the shape of the probability density in single-slit diffraction. Let us organize what can be read from it.

  • Principal maximum: It takes its maximum value at $u=0$ (the center of the screen) and first becomes 0 at $u=\pm\pi$. The width of the principal maximum (full width) is $2x_1=\lambda L/a$, and it becomes wider the narrower the slit is (the smaller $a$ is).
  • Zeros: At $u=\pm\pi,\pm2\pi,\dots$, that is, at $x=\pm n\lambda L/(2a)$, no electron arrives at all. They are equally spaced.
  • Secondary maxima: Between one zero and the next there is a peak; the height of the first secondary maximum is $4.72\ \%$ of that of the principal maximum and that of the second is $1.65\ \%$, so the heights decrease rapidly (Example 7.4). Of the total, $90.3\ \%$ lies inside the principal maximum.
  • Trend of the spreading: When the slit is made narrower, the range reached by the electrons is not just the narrow range of the slit through which they pass; on the contrary, it spreads out. We have $x_1=\lambda L/(2a)\propto1/a$. The longer the wavelength (the smaller the momentum), the more the pattern spreads.

Intuition: The position on the screen represents the transverse component of the momentum just after passing through the slit

The wave that reaches the point $x$ on the screen is a wave that has traveled in a direction at an angle $\theta\approx x/L$ to the axis of the slit. The $y$ component of the momentum of an electron traveling in this direction is $p_y=p\sin\theta\approx px/L$, so $q=\dfrac{px}{\hbar L}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-4} corresponds to $q=p_y/\hbar$. Then the integral in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-4}, $$ \int_{-a}^{a}e^{-ip_yy/\hbar}\dd y $$ is the Fourier transform of the shape of the slit (a rectangular function equal to 1 for $\abs{y}\le a$ and 0 otherwise). (The Fourier transform to the momentum representation was described in Chapter 2, Section 2.3. It is connected to the Fourier transform in AD 02, Chapter 30, through the correspondence $k=p_y/\hbar$. The relation between the width of a wave packet and the Fourier transform was also treated in AD 03, Chapter 17, Section 17.6.) That is, the distribution on the screen reflects “the distribution of the $y$-direction momentum of the electrons just after they pass through the slit.” When the slit restricts the position of the electron to a width $2a$, a spread in momentum $\Delta p_y$ arises. The position of the first zero, $u=\pi$, corresponds to $p_y=\pm\pi\hbar/a=\pm h/(2a)$, so $$ (\text{width in position }2a)\times(\text{spread in momentum }p_{y,1}=h/2a)\;\sim\;h $$ which is a concrete instance of the relation linking the uncertainties of position and momentum (the uncertainty principle of the next chapter). The narrower the slit, the smaller the uncertainty in position becomes, but in exchange the momentum spreads more widely.

Example 7.2: The integral of $\sin u/u$ and the normalization constant

(1) Show that $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin u}{u}\dd u=\pi$ by using the Fourier transform of a rectangular pulse. (2) Using integration by parts, derive $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^2u}{u^2}\dd u=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin2u}{u}\dd u$, and show that its value is $\pi$. (3) Verify that the normalized probability density is $\dfrac{1}{\pi}\left(\dfrac{\sin u}{u}\right)^2$ when normalized in $u$ and $\dfrac{\alpha}{\pi}\left(\dfrac{\sin\alpha x}{\alpha x}\right)^2$ when normalized in $x$, and explain the relation between the two.

Solution. (1) The Fourier transform of the rectangular pulse $f(t)=1$ ($\abs{t}\lt1$), $f(t)=0$ ($\abs{t}\gt1$) is $$ F(\omega)=\int_{-1}^{1}e^{-i\omega t}\dd t=\left[\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{-i\omega}\right]_{-1}^{1}=\frac{e^{i\omega}-e^{-i\omega}}{i\omega}=\frac{2\sin\omega}{\omega} $$ (the same calculation as in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-int}). Using the inverse transform $f(t)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(\omega)\,e^{i\omega t}\dd\omega$ at the point $t=0$ where $f$ is continuous ($f(0)=1$), we obtain $$ 1=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{2\sin\omega}{\omega}\dd\omega=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin\omega}{\omega}\dd\omega $$ and hence $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin\omega}{\omega}\dd\omega=\pi$.

(2) We integrate by parts, taking $\dfrac{1}{u^2}$ as the factor that is already a derivative and $\sin^2u$ as the factor to be differentiated. Since $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd u}\left(-\dfrac1u\right)=\dfrac{1}{u^2}$, $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^2u}{u^2}\dd u=\left[-\frac{\sin^2u}{u}\right]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{u}\,\bigl(2\sin u\cos u\bigr)\dd u $$ The boundary term is 0 because $\abs{\sin^2u/u}\le1/\abs{u}\to0$ ($u\to\pm\infty$) (it is continuous at $u=0$ as well, since $\sin^2u/u\to0$). Since $2\sin u\cos u=\sin2u$, the remainder is $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin2u}{u}\dd u$. With the change of variables $w=2u$ we have $\dfrac{\sin2u}{u}\dd u=\dfrac{\sin w}{w/2}\cdot\dfrac{\dd w}{2}=\dfrac{\sin w}{w}\dd w$, and the integral equals the value $\pi$ found in (1).

(3) The integral of the density normalized in $x$, $\dfrac{\alpha}{\pi}\left(\dfrac{\sin\alpha x}{\alpha x}\right)^2$, becomes, with the change of variables $u=\alpha x$, $\displaystyle\int\dfrac{\alpha}{\pi}\dfrac{\sin^2u}{u^2}\dfrac{\dd u}{\alpha}=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\cdot\pi=1$, so it is indeed normalized to 1. On the other hand, the integral over $u$ of the density $\dfrac{1}{\pi}\left(\dfrac{\sin u}{u}\right)^2$ taken with $u$ as the variable is also 1. These are two ways of writing the same probability, $P(x)\dd x=P_u(u)\dd u$, and they are related by $P(x)=\alpha P_u(u)$. If one integrated $\dfrac{1}{\pi}\left(\dfrac{\sin\alpha x}{\alpha x}\right)^2$ over $x$, the result would be $1/\alpha$ ($0.116\ \mathrm{m}$ for $\alpha=8.638\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$), not 1.

Example 7.3: Numbers for a single slit with the electron beam of an electron microscope

An electron beam with an accelerating voltage of $50\ \mathrm{kV}$ (relativistic wavelength $\lambda=5.355\ \mathrm{pm}$) is directed perpendicularly onto a single slit of width $2a=1.0\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ and observed on a screen at a distance $L=1.5\ \mathrm{m}$. (1) Find the position $x_1$ of the first zero and the full width of the principal maximum. (2) Find the Fresnel number and check whether the Fraunhofer approximation holds. (3) Compare the width of the geometric shadow of the slit with the full width of the principal maximum.

Solution. (1) By Theorem 7.1, $x_1=\dfrac{\lambda L}{2a}=\dfrac{5.355\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m}\times1.5\ \mathrm{m}}{1.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m}}=8.03\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m}=8.03\ \mathrm{\mu m}$. The principal maximum extends from $-x_1$ to $x_1$, so the full width is $2x_1=16.1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$. (In terms of angle, $\theta_1\approx\lambda/2a=5.4\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{rad}$.)

(2) The half-width is $a=0.50\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, so $N_{\mathrm F}=\dfrac{a^2}{\lambda L}=\dfrac{(0.50\times10^{-6})^2}{5.355\times10^{-12}\times1.5}=\dfrac{2.5\times10^{-13}}{8.03\times10^{-12}}=0.031$. The condition $N_{\mathrm F}\ll1$ holds, and the maximum value of the phase is as small as $\pi N_{\mathrm F}=0.098\ \mathrm{rad}$ (about $5.6^\circ$), so the Fraunhofer approximation is good.

(3) The geometric shadow (the range reached if the electrons traveled straight like bullets) has the same width as the slit, $1.0\ \mathrm{\mu m}$. The full width of the principal maximum, $16.1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, is about 16 times this, and the numbers also show that the electrons arrive spread out widely, to the extent that the slit has been made narrow. Note that $x_1=8.03\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ in this arrangement happens to coincide numerically with the fringe spacing of the double slit (spacing $d=1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$) in Example 2.1 of Chapter 2, but the meaning is different (that one is the interference fringe spacing $\lambda L/d$, whereas this one is the half-width $\lambda L/2a$ of the principal diffraction maximum).

Example 7.4: The secondary maxima of $(\sin u/u)^2$ and the total probability of the principal maximum

(1) Show that the positions of the maxima of $\bigl(\sin u/u\bigr)^2$ satisfy $\tan u=u$, and find the position and height of the first secondary maximum. (2) Find the probability contained in the principal maximum ($\abs{u}\le\pi$).

Solution. (1) The derivative of $f(u)=\sin^2u/u^2$ is $$ f'(u)=\frac{2\sin u\cos u\cdot u^2-\sin^2u\cdot2u}{u^4}=\frac{2\sin u\,(u\cos u-\sin u)}{u^3} $$ We have $f'=0$ when $\sin u=0$ (a zero; the minimum $f=0$) or when $u\cos u=\sin u$, that is, $\tan u=u$. As for the positive solutions of $\tan u=u$, in $0\lt u\lt\pi/2$ there is only $u=0$, and in the interval $\pi\lt u\lt3\pi/2$ the function $\tan u$ runs from 0 to $+\infty$ and crosses the line $y=u$, so the first solution is, numerically, $u_1=4.4934=1.430\pi$. Since $\sin u=u\cos u$ there, $\sin^2u=u^2\cos^2u$, and $f(u_1)=\cos^2u_1=\dfrac{1}{1+\tan^2u_1}=\dfrac{1}{1+u_1^{\,2}}=\dfrac{1}{1+20.19}=0.0472$. This is $4.72\ \%$ of the principal maximum (height 1).

(2) The probability of the principal maximum is $\dfrac{1}{\pi}\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^2u}{u^2}\dd u=\dfrac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^2u}{u^2}\dd u$. By integration by parts (the same as in Example 7.2 (2)), $\displaystyle\int_0^{U}\frac{\sin^2u}{u^2}\dd u=\mathrm{Si}(2U)-\frac{\sin^2U}{U}$ ($\mathrm{Si}(z)=\int_0^z\frac{\sin t}{t}\dd t$ is the sine integral), so with $\sin\pi=0$ at $U=\pi$, the value is $\dfrac{2}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Si}(2\pi)=\dfrac{2}{\pi}\times1.4182=0.903$. About $90.3\ \%$ of the total probability falls in the principal maximum, and the remaining $9.7\ \%$ is divided among the secondary maxima on the two sides.

7.3 What Happens When the Mass Is Changed? — Considering the Wave Nature of Particles

The probability density $\abs{\psi}^2=\dfrac{\alpha}{\pi}\left(\dfrac{\sin\alpha x}{\alpha x}\right)^2$, with $\alpha=\dfrac{pa}{\hbar L}$, obtained in the previous section depends on the mass $m$ of the particle through the momentum $p=mv$. How does $\abs{\psi}^2$ change when the same experiment is done with an electron and with a much heavier particle? This consideration gives the first answer to the question, “Why is it that, although in the world of quantum mechanics particles spread out as waves, the objects we see in everyday life travel straight without spreading?”

7.3.1 Experimental conditions

We consider the following conditions: the distance from the slit to the screen is $L=1.0\ \mathrm{m}$, the half-width of the slit is $a=1.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m}$ (width $2\ \mathrm{mm}$), and the speed of the particle is $v=1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Since the momentum is $p=mv$, we have

\begin{equation} \alpha=\frac{p\,a}{\hbar L}=\frac{m\,v\,a}{\hbar L}=\frac{2\pi\,m\,v\,a}{h\,L} \label{eq:7-alpha-def} \end{equation}

The quantity $\alpha$ is proportional to the mass $m$ (when $v$, $a$, and $L$ are held fixed). The larger $\alpha$ is, the narrower the width of the principal maximum, $2x_1=2\pi/\alpha$, becomes. Table 7.1 summarizes the numbers for various masses (each is computed individually in the examples up to the next section).

7.3.2 The case of the electron

Illustration 7.1: An electron with speed $1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$

When an electron is fired at $v=1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$, evaluating $\dfrac{pa}{\hbar L}$ in the rightmost form of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-alpha-def} gives $$ \alpha=\frac{2\pi\times9.109\times10^{-31}\times1.0\times10^{-3}}{6.626\times10^{-34}\times1.0}\doteqdot8.638\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} $$ (the numerator is $2\pi\,m_{\mathrm e}\,v\,a\,\mathrm{[kg\cdot m/s\cdot m]}$ and the denominator is $h\,L\,\mathrm{[J\,s\cdot m]}$, so the overall unit is $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$). Therefore the probability density normalized in terms of $u=\alpha x=8.638\,x$ can be written as $$ \abs{\psi}^2=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{\sin8.638\,x}{8.638\,x}\right)^2\qquad(x\text{ in meters}) $$ The probability density normalized in terms of the position $x$ has the coefficient $\alpha/\pi=8.638/\pi=2.750\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:7-P-x}). The zeros are given by $\alpha x=n\pi$, so $x_n=n\pi/8.638=0.364\,n\ \mathrm{m}$; that is, $x=\pm0.364,\ \pm0.727,\ \dots\ \mathrm{m}$.

As Figure 7.3 shows, this electron spreads over a range that is larger by orders of magnitude than the slit width of $2\ \mathrm{mm}$. A half-width of the principal maximum of $0.364\ \mathrm{m}$ means that, while traveling 1 m after leaving the slit, the electron spreads as much as 36 cm up and down. That is 180 times the slit width—“very widely spread.” This is because the wavelength of the electron, $0.727\ \mathrm{mm}$, is of the same order as the slit width of $2\ \mathrm{mm}$, so diffraction appears strongly (diffraction becomes pronounced when the wavelength is of the same order as the slit width or larger).

Caution: An electron with speed $1\ \mathrm{m/s}$ is a “thought experiment” value

Actual electron-beam experiments are done with electrons accelerated through a voltage of several tens of kV ($\lambda$ is on the order of picometers). An electron at $1\ \mathrm{m/s}$ would fall as much as $\dfrac12gt^2=4.9\ \mathrm{m}$ in one second under gravity alone and would be bent by even the slightest stray electromagnetic field, so it cannot actually be realized. This is a thought experiment that shows, with extremely simple numbers, a situation in which the wavelength is of the same order as the size of the slit. The line of reasoning ($\alpha\propto m$, the magnitude of $N_{\mathrm F}$) carries over unchanged to real experiments.

Example 7.5: The paraxial-approximation zero $0.364\ \mathrm{m}$ and the exact path-sum zero $0.390\ \mathrm{m}$

For an electron with $v=1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ ($\lambda=0.727\ \mathrm{mm}$, $a=1.0\ \mathrm{mm}$, $L=1.0\ \mathrm{m}$): (1) Find the first zero $x_1$ in the paraxial approximation and the Fresnel number. (2) If the sum of phases $\int e^{ikr}\dd y$ is computed without approximating $r=\sqrt{L^2+(x-y)^2}$, the first minimum appears at $x=0.390\ \mathrm{m}$. Explain this difference using the diffraction angle $\theta$ rather than the position $x$ on the screen.

Solution. (1) Since $\alpha=8.638\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, we have $x_1=\pi/\alpha=0.3637\ \mathrm{m}$, and the Fresnel number is $N_{\mathrm F}=a^2/(\lambda L)=\dfrac{10^{-6}}{7.27\times10^{-4}}=1.4\times10^{-3}$, so the condition of the Fraunhofer approximation is amply satisfied.

(2) The paraxial approximation was an approximation valid when $x/L$ is small. Here $x_1/L=0.364$ is no longer small. Let $R=\sqrt{L^2+x^2}$ be the distance from the center of the slit ($y=0$) to the point on the screen, and let the diffraction angle $\theta$ ($\tan\theta=x/L$, $\sin\theta=x/R$) be the angle that the direction from the center of the slit to the point on the screen makes with the axis of the slit (the horizontal line $y=0$). Then the length of the path through the position $y$ on the slit is $r=\sqrt{R^2-2xy+y^2}\approx R-y\sin\theta$ (we dropped the $y^2$ term for $y\ll R$ and used the binomial approximation). The phase difference is $-ky\sin\theta$, and the integral becomes that of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-int} with $q$ replaced by $k\sin\theta$. Thus the far-field (Fraunhofer) diffraction pattern can be written, using $\theta$, as $$ \abs{\psi}^2\propto\left(\frac{\sin(ka\sin\theta)}{ka\sin\theta}\right)^2 $$ The first zero is at $ka\sin\theta=\pi$, that is, $\sin\theta_1=\dfrac{\pi}{ka}=\dfrac{\lambda}{2a}=\dfrac{0.727}{2.0}=0.3637$ ($\theta_1=21.3^\circ$). The position on the screen is $x=L\tan\theta_1=L\,\dfrac{\sin\theta_1}{\sqrt{1-\sin^2\theta_1}}=\dfrac{0.3637}{\sqrt{1-0.1323}}\,\mathrm{m}=0.390\ \mathrm{m}$. The paraxial approximation regards $\tan\theta\approx\sin\theta\approx\theta$ and sets $x_1=L\sin\theta_1=0.364\ \mathrm{m}$, so for a large angle such as $x_1/L=0.36$, we have $\tan\theta_1/\sin\theta_1=1/\sqrt{1-\sin^2\theta_1}=1.074$, a deviation of about $7\ \%$ (the approximate formula $1+\tfrac12\sin^2\theta$ gives $1.066$; Table 7.5). In terms of the diffraction angle $\sin\theta=\lambda/(2a)$, the formula is correct.

7.3.3 The $\delta$ function — preparing the language of limits

Next, we consider the case of a large mass. As $\alpha\propto m$ is made larger, the principal maximum of the probability density in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-P-x} becomes taller and narrower. In the limit $m\to\infty$, the principal maximum becomes “an infinitely narrow, tall spike of area 1 located only at $x=0$.” The mathematical expression of such a limit is the following Dirac $\delta$ function.

Definition 7.2: Dirac $\delta$ function (delta function)

$\delta(x)$ is a “function” (more precisely, a distribution) with the following properties: for $x\ne0$ we have $\delta(x)=0$, at $x=0$ it equals $+\infty$, and the total integral is 1, $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(x)\dd x=1,\qquad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,\delta(x)\dd x=f(0)\quad(f\text{ is continuous at }x=0\text{}) $$ The $\delta$ function is expressed as the limit of a sequence of functions whose widths approach 0 while the area stays 1. A representative example is $$ \delta(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sin nx}{\pi x} $$ (This was also treated in AD 02, Chapter 30, Section 30.3.)

Let us check the expression $\dfrac{\sin nx}{\pi x}$ on the right-hand side. Putting $u=nx$ gives $\dfrac{\dd x}{x}=\dfrac{\dd u}{u}$, so for any $n$ $$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin nx}{\pi x}\dd x=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin u}{u}\dd u=\frac{1}{\pi}\cdot\pi=1 $$ holds (we used the value of $\pi$ from Example 7.2 (1); the area is always 1), the value at $x=0$ is $\lim_{x\to0}\dfrac{\sin nx}{\pi x}=\dfrac{n}{\pi}\to\infty$, and the width of the principal maximum is $\sim\pi/n\to0$. Therefore, as $n$ is made larger, a very narrow, tall spike of area 1 forms at the origin. Note that for $x\ne0$ this function does not converge to 0 but keeps oscillating (with amplitude $1/(\pi\abs{x})$); however, when it is multiplied by a smooth function $f$ and integrated, the oscillations cancel each other, and only $f(0)$ remains.

What appears in the slit diffraction pattern is the squared form (always positive, and suitable as a probability density).

\begin{equation} \delta(x)=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\left(\frac{\sin\alpha x}{\alpha x}\right)^2 \label{eq:7-dirac-seq} \end{equation}

As verified in Example 7.2 (3), this has area 1 independent of $\alpha$, a value at $x=0$ of $\alpha/\pi\to\infty$, and a principal-maximum width of $\pi/\alpha\to0$. It is exactly the single-slit probability density of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-P-x}. That is, the spread due to diffraction becomes the $\delta$ function in the limit of large mass ($\alpha\to\infty$). The graph of the $\delta$ function is drawn as an upward arrow standing at the origin (a single infinitely narrow, tall spike with area 1). The arrow at the origin in Figure 7.5 (a) is this, and the curves leading up to it are those for $\alpha=1,3,10$, which, in that order, become taller and narrower while keeping the same area 1.

7.3.4 The case of a heavy particle

Illustration 7.2: A particle of mass $6.626\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg}$

A particle of mass $6.626\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg}$ (about $6.6\ \mathrm{g}$) is fired at $v=1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. With the same slit conditions ($a=1.0\ \mathrm{mm}$, $L=1.0\ \mathrm{m}$), $$ \alpha=\frac{2\pi\times6.626\times10^{-3}\times1.0\times10^{-3}}{6.626\times10^{-34}\times1.0}=2\pi\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} $$ (we used $6.626\times10^{-3}\times10^{-3}/6.626\times10^{-34}=10^{28}$). The probability density normalized in terms of $u$ is $$ \abs{\psi}^2=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{\sin(2\pi\times10^{28}\,x)}{2\pi\times10^{28}\,x}\right)^2 $$ This takes the value $1/\pi$ only at $x=0$ and is almost 0 for $x\ne0$. The width of the principal maximum (first zero) is $x_1=\pi/\alpha=5.0\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm{m}$, which is as many as 14 orders of magnitude smaller than the size of a proton, $10^{-15}\ \mathrm{m}$. It is exactly the $\delta$ function of Eq. \eqref{eq:7-dirac-seq}: $\abs{\psi}^2\to\delta(x)$. The spread due to diffraction is too small to be detected, and the expression approaches the $\delta$ function. However, since the condition of the Fraunhofer approximation does not hold for this particle, its actual arrival position spreads, as the next discussion shows, over the whole shadow of the slit (width $2\ \mathrm{mm}$) (Figure 7.4 (b)).

Discussion: Understanding the $\delta$ function correctly — “the single point $x=0$” is a geometric shadow

The statement “the heavy particle reaches only $x=0$” has to be read with care. Computing the Fresnel number for the particle of Illustration 7.2, whose wavelength is $\lambda=h/(mv)=1.0\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{m}$, we obtain $N_{\mathrm F}=\dfrac{a^2}{\lambda L}=\dfrac{(1.0\times10^{-3})^2}{1.0\times10^{-31}\times1.0}=1.0\times10^{25}\gg1$. The condition $N_{\mathrm F}\ll1$ for the Fraunhofer approximation (Definition 7.1) is not satisfied here. Therefore the result $\delta(x)$ obtained by applying Eq. \eqref{eq:7-P-x} to this particle must not be read directly as the distribution of positions the particle reaches.

What does hold is the following statement. The “spreading due to diffraction” (the part that becomes a $\delta$ function in the limit $\alpha\to\infty$) disappears, and the wave emitted from each point of the slit travels straight ahead. A wave that leaves the slit at position $y$ and goes straight reaches $x=y$ on the screen. Adding up over all $y$ ($\abs{y}\le a$), the distribution on the screen is $$ P(x)=\int_{-a}^{a}\frac{1}{2a}\,\delta(x-y)\dd y=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2a}&(\abs{x}\lt a)\\[2mm]0&(\abs{x}\gt a)\end{cases} $$ that is, the geometric shadow of the slit (a uniform distribution of width $2a=2\ \mathrm{mm}$). This is the band in Figure 7.4 (b). The $\delta$ function in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-dirac-seq} is a mathematical expression for “the diffraction spreading has become 0”; it does not mean that the particle gathers at a single point.

This is the same as the fact that, in the limit where the wavelength approaches 0, wave optics goes over into geometric optics (light rays) (the limit $h\to0$ corresponds to classical mechanics). A heavy particle looks classical because its wavelength is too short for the diffraction fringes to be seen. In a wave-optics calculation (the Fresnel integral), all that remains is a blur of width $\sqrt{\lambda L}=3\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{m}$ at the edge of the shadow of width $2a$ (Section 7.6).

7.3.5 Mass and the spread of diffraction

Figure 7.5 (b) shows on a log–log plot that the first zero $x_1=\dfrac{\pi\hbar L}{mva}$ is inversely proportional to the mass $m$. Each time the mass increases by one order of magnitude, the spread narrows by one order of magnitude. On the other hand, $N_{\mathrm F}=\dfrac{a^2mv}{hL}$ also increases in proportion to the mass, and the mass at which $N_{\mathrm F}=1$ is $m=\dfrac{hL}{va^2}=6.6\times10^{-28}\ \mathrm{kg}$. For heavier masses, the Fraunhofer approximation itself can no longer be used, and the formula for $x_1$ is meaningless (the red region). Also, the mass at which the same $x_1$ becomes equal to $a$ (the half-width of the slit) is $m=\dfrac{hL}{2va^2}=3.3\times10^{-28}\ \mathrm{kg}$, and at almost the same mass $N_{\mathrm F}\sim1$. This is not an accident: the condition that the diffraction spread $\lambda L/2a$ equal the half-width $a$ of the shadow is the same as $N_{\mathrm F}=a^2/(\lambda L)=1/2$.

Example 7.6: Mass and the diffraction angle

The following particles are directed perpendicularly at a slit of width $2a=100\ \mathrm{nm}$. Find the direction of the first zero, $\theta_1\approx\lambda/(2a)$. (1) A thermal neutron (mass $1.675\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$, speed $2200\ \mathrm{m/s}$). (2) A helium atom (mass $4.003\ \mathrm{u}$, root-mean-square speed $\sqrt{3k_{\mathrm B}T/m}$ at $300\ \mathrm{K}$). (3) A $\mathrm{C_{60}}$ molecule (mass $720\ \mathrm{u}$, speed $200\ \mathrm{m/s}$). (4) A baseball (mass $0.145\ \mathrm{kg}$, speed $40\ \mathrm{m/s}$) passing through a doorway of width $1\ \mathrm{m}$. Here $1\ \mathrm{u}=1.6605\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$.

Solution. $\theta_1$ is given by $\sin\theta_1=\lambda/2a$, and if $\lambda\ll2a$, then $\theta_1\approx\lambda/2a$ (rad). (1) $\lambda=\dfrac{h}{mv}=\dfrac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{1.675\times10^{-27}\times2200}=1.80\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m}=0.180\ \mathrm{nm}$. $\theta_1=\dfrac{1.80\times10^{-10}}{1.0\times10^{-7}}=1.80\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad}=1.80\ \mathrm{mrad}$.

(2) The mass is $m=4.003\times1.6605\times10^{-27}=6.647\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$. $v=\sqrt{\dfrac{3\times1.381\times10^{-23}\times300}{6.647\times10^{-27}}}=1.37\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{m/s}$. $\lambda=\dfrac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{6.647\times10^{-27}\times1367}=7.29\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m}=0.0729\ \mathrm{nm}$. $\theta_1=7.29\times10^{-11}/10^{-7}=0.729\ \mathrm{mrad}$.

(3) $m=720\times1.6605\times10^{-27}=1.196\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{kg}$. $\lambda=\dfrac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{1.196\times10^{-24}\times200}=2.77\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m}=2.77\ \mathrm{pm}$. $\theta_1=2.77\times10^{-12}/10^{-7}=2.77\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad}=0.0277\ \mathrm{mrad}$.

(4) $\lambda=\dfrac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{0.145\times40}=1.14\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{m}$, and since $2a=1\ \mathrm{m}$, $\theta_1=1.1\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{rad}$.

The larger the mass (the larger the momentum), the shorter the wavelength and the smaller the diffraction angle. From the neutron to $\mathrm{C_{60}}$, the mass increases about 700-fold (the speeds also differ), and the diffraction angle becomes smaller by a factor of about 65. Even so, an angle of order $10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad}$ can be measured with a precision apparatus. For the ball the angle is $10^{-34}\ \mathrm{rad}$, and even after traveling the size of the universe (about $10^{26}\ \mathrm{m}$) the spread is only $10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m}$, so detection is impossible.

Example 7.7: Convergence to a $\delta$ sequence, and the geometric shadow

For $v=1\ \mathrm{m/s}$, $a=1\ \mathrm{mm}$, and $L=1\ \mathrm{m}$, find the probability that a particle of mass $m=10^{-30},\ 10^{-27},\ 10^{-24}\ \mathrm{kg}$ reaches $\abs{x}\lt1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, using the Fraunhofer formula \eqref{eq:7-P-x}. Also compute the Fresnel number and decide whether this result can be used.

Solution. We have $P(\abs{x}\lt x_0)=\displaystyle\int_{-x_0}^{x_0}\dfrac{\alpha}{\pi}\dfrac{\sin^2\alpha x}{(\alpha x)^2}\dd x=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\alpha x_0}^{\alpha x_0}\dfrac{\sin^2u}{u^2}\dd u=\dfrac{2}{\pi}\left[\mathrm{Si}(2U)-\dfrac{\sin^2U}{U}\right]$ (with $U=\alpha x_0$; this is the same expression as in Example 7.4 (2)).

  • $m=10^{-30}\ \mathrm{kg}$: $\alpha=\dfrac{mva}{\hbar L}=\dfrac{10^{-30}\times10^{-3}}{1.0546\times10^{-34}}=9.48\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $U=9.48\times10^{-6}\ll1$. In this case $\dfrac{\sin^2u}{u^2}\approx1$, so $P\approx\dfrac{2\alpha x_0}{\pi}=\dfrac{2\times9.48\times10^{-6}}{\pi}=6.0\times10^{-6}$. The Fresnel number is $N_{\mathrm F}=\dfrac{a^2mv}{hL}=\dfrac{10^{-6}\times10^{-30}}{6.626\times10^{-34}}=1.5\times10^{-3}\ll1$, so this result is valid.
  • $m=10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$: $\alpha=9.48\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $U=9.48\times10^{-3}$. $P\approx\dfrac{2\times9.48\times10^{3}\times10^{-6}}{\pi}=6.0\times10^{-3}$. $N_{\mathrm F}=1.5$, which lies right at the limit of the approximation.
  • $m=10^{-24}\ \mathrm{kg}$: $\alpha=9.48\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $U=9.48$. $P=\dfrac{2}{\pi}\left[\mathrm{Si}(18.96)-\dfrac{\sin^2(9.48)}{9.48}\right]=0.966$. However, $N_{\mathrm F}=1.5\times10^{3}\gg1$, so the Fraunhofer formula cannot be used.

From the property of a $\delta$ sequence, whose area stays equal to 1 while its width narrows, increasing $m$ concentrates the probability near $x=0$, but only as long as the Fraunhofer approximation holds. For the third mass the formula says “96.6% lies within $1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$,” which at first sight seems to approach a $\delta$ function, but in reality the width of the slit's shadow is $2\ \mathrm{mm}$, and the probability of falling within $\abs{x}\lt1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ is about the width ratio $1\ \mathrm{\mu m}/1\ \mathrm{mm}=10^{-3}$ (Figure 7.5 (c)). Even for a particle of mass $6.626\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg}$, we have $N_{\mathrm F}=1.0\times10^{25}$, and similarly the distribution on the screen coincides with a geometric shadow of width $2\ \mathrm{mm}$.

7.3.6 The spreading of a breath is not diffraction

The everyday experience that “when we blow a breath through a small, nearly closed mouth, the $\mathrm{O_2}$ and $\mathrm{N_2}$ molecules disperse” may seem to be the same phenomenon as electrons spreading at a slit. However, for the following reasons, this is not diffraction.

Caution: The spreading of a breath is not diffraction

The thermal de Broglie wavelength (Chapter 2, Section 2.5) at $300\ \mathrm{K}$ of the nitrogen molecule $\mathrm{N_2}$ (mass $28.0\ \mathrm{u}$), the main constituent of air, is $\lambda_T=\dfrac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi mk_{\mathrm B}T}}=0.019\ \mathrm{nm}$. If we take the size of the mouth to be $1\ \mathrm{cm}$, the wavelength is only $2\times10^{-9}$ times the size of the mouth, and the rough measure of the diffraction spread, $\theta\approx\lambda/(2a)$, is about $10^{-9}\ \mathrm{rad}$, which is not visible at all. The fan-like spreading of a breath is due not to diffraction but to the following reasons. (1) In air at normal pressure, the mean free path of a molecule is about $0.07\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, much shorter than the size of the mouth, so the molecules keep colliding with one another even after leaving the mouth. They move not as a propagating wave but as a fluid jet. (2) The jet entrains the surrounding air and, through turbulence, spreads conically downstream. (3) The molecules also fly in random directions because of their thermal motion (about $480\ \mathrm{m/s}$ on average at $300\ \mathrm{K}$). Quantum-mechanical diffraction can be seen in high-vacuum molecular beams, in which the molecules do not collide with one another (such as the $\mathrm{C_{60}}$ experiments, Section 7.5).

7.4 The Double Slit — The Sum of Probabilities and the Sum of Probability Amplitudes

Next, we use two slits. As we saw in Chapter 2, Section 2.1, when electrons are sent through two slits, bright and dark fringes form on the screen. In this section we derive the distribution of those fringes by the same method as in the previous section. At the same time, we show that when it is possible to tell “which slit the electron passed through,” no interference fringes appear, and we obtain only two single-slit diffraction patterns side by side. The difference between these two answers is the heart of quantum mechanics.

7.4.1 The double-slit arrangement

We express the setup of Figure 7.6 in equations. On the slit plane (the $y$ axis) there are two openings of width $b$, occupying the ranges $$ a\le y\le a+b\qquad\text{and}\qquad-a-b\le y\le-a $$ respectively. The central shielding plate occupies $-a\le y\le a$ (width $2a$). The centers of the two openings are at $y=\pm(a+b/2)=\pm d/2$, and $d=2a+b$ is called the center-to-center spacing. An electron beam of momentum $p$ is incident perpendicularly, directed at $y=0$.

Caution: $a$ and $b$ in this section have different meanings from those in the single-slit case

In the previous section we took $a$ to be the half-width of the slit, but in this section $b$ is the width of an opening and $a$ is the half-width of the central shielding plate. The width of the opening, the quantity corresponding to $2a$ for the single slit, is replaced by $b$ (when the single-slit result \eqref{eq:7-P-x} is used in this section, read $a\to b/2$).

Problem: Find the probability distribution on plane A at distance $L$ from the slits when an electron beam of momentum $p$ is directed perpendicularly at a double slit whose openings have width $b$, and compare it with the experimental result.

7.4.2 First, the sum of probabilities — when we can tell which slit the electron passed through

First, let us consider the case in which we can distinguish which slit the electron passed through. Suppose, for example, that there is a device near each slit that records the passage of an electron, and that the record is kept. In this case the electrons reaching the screen can be divided into two groups: “the group that passed through the upper slit” and “the group that passed through the lower slit.” Each group follows the distribution obtained when only a single slit is open. By symmetry, the probability that an electron passes through the upper slit and the probability that it passes through the lower slit are both $1/2$. Therefore, the probability distribution on the screen is the sum of probabilities of the distributions of the individual slits (each with weight $1/2$):

\begin{equation} P(x)=\tfrac12P_1(x)+\tfrac12P_2(x) \label{eq:7-P-sum} \end{equation}

Here $P_1$ and $P_2$ are the probability densities when only the respective slit is open.

Let us find $P_j$. When slit $j$ has its center at $y=y_j$ ($y_1=+d/2$, $y_2=-d/2$) and width $b$, we write a position inside the opening as $y=y_j+s$ ($-b/2\le s\le b/2$). After shifting the origin to $y_j$, the expansion of the path length, Eq. \eqref{eq:7-r-expand}, becomes $$ r\approx L+\frac{(x-y_j)^2}{2L}-\frac{(x-y_j)\,s}{L}+\frac{s^2}{2L} $$ (we squared $x-y=x-y_j-s$ and divided by $2L$). As in the previous section, the $s^2$ term can be dropped when the phase satisfies $\pi b^2/(4\lambda L)\ll1$. Then exactly the same calculation as in Section 7.2 can be carried out with the replacements $x\to x-y_j$, $a\to b/2$, and we obtain $$ \psi_j(x)=A_0\,e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{(x-y_j)^2}{2L}\right)}\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}e^{-i\frac{p(x-y_j)}{\hbar L}s}\dd s=b\,A_0\,e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{(x-y_j)^2}{2L}\right)}\,\frac{\sin v_j}{v_j},\qquad v_j=\beta\,(x-y_j) $$ (we let $\beta\equiv\dfrac{p\,b}{2\hbar L}$. Like $\alpha$ in Section 7.2.6, $\beta$ is a symbol used only in this chapter; under the replacement $a\to b/2$, $\alpha$ becomes $\beta$). Hence $\abs{\psi_j}^2=A_0^{\,2}b^2\left(\dfrac{\sin v_j}{v_j}\right)^2$, and normalizing gives, as for the single slit, $P_j(x)=\dfrac{\beta}{\pi}\left(\dfrac{\sin\beta(x-y_j)}{\beta(x-y_j)}\right)^2$. Substituting into Eq. \eqref{eq:7-P-sum}, we obtain

\begin{equation} P(x)=\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\left\{\left(\frac{\sin\beta\left(x-\frac{d}{2}\right)}{\beta\left(x-\frac{d}{2}\right)}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\sin\beta\left(x+\frac{d}{2}\right)}{\beta\left(x+\frac{d}{2}\right)}\right)^2\right\},\qquad\beta=\frac{p\,b}{2\hbar L},\quad d=2a+b \label{eq:7-P-sum2} \end{equation}

The coefficient $\dfrac{\beta}{2\pi}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-P-sum2} shows that each term is half of a “probability density of $1/\pi$ (of $\beta/\pi$ in the variable $x$).” If we take the variable $v=\beta(x\mp d/2)$, the coefficient is $\dfrac{1}{2\pi}$.

“There is no interference. But there is diffraction.”

Eq. \eqref{eq:7-P-sum2} has the form of single-slit diffraction patterns lined up, one at each of the two slit positions $x=\pm d/2$, without overlapping (Figure 7.7 (a)). The fine fringes (interference fringes) between the two peaks do not appear. “There is no interference, but there is diffraction.” The diffraction spread of each slit (the half-width $\lambda L/b$ of each peak) is larger the narrower the slit. For the two peaks to be seen clearly separated, the center-to-center spacing $d$ must be larger than this spread; that is, $d/2\gtrsim\lambda L/b$ is required. For example, for electrons with $a=b=1\ \mathrm{mm}$ and $v=1\ \mathrm{m/s}$, $d/2=1.5\ \mathrm{mm}$ and $\lambda L/b=0.727\ \mathrm{m}$, so the two peaks overlap completely and look like a single peak (the dashed line in Figure 7.7 (b)). This is a manifestation of the fact that the “sum of probabilities” contains only diffraction and no interference.

7.4.3 The sum of probability amplitudes — when we cannot tell which slit the electron passed through

Next, we consider the case in which the information about which slit the electron passed through is not kept anywhere. In this case the electron adds together the probability amplitudes of both of “the two possible paths” (Chapter 2, Section 2.1.4). The sum over the paths through the two openings is the same integral as in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-int}, added over the two ranges.

Derivation: The probability amplitude $\psi(x)$ for the double slit

[1] Starting point. The probability amplitude for an electron passing through the two openings $a\le y\le a+b$ and $-a-b\le y\le-a$ to arrive at the point $x$ on plane A is \begin{equation} \psi(x)=A_0\int_{a}^{a+b}e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}r}\dd y+A_0\int_{-a-b}^{-a}e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}r}\dd y \label{eq:7-psi-double} \end{equation} (the $A_0$ in front of the integrals is the same constant in both integrals).

[2] Path length. From Eq. \eqref{eq:7-r-expand}, $r\approx L+\dfrac{x^2}{2L}-\dfrac{xy}{L}+\dfrac{y^2}{2L}$. The last term $\dfrac{y^2}{2L}$ was “set to 0 because it is very small.” Its phase is at most $\dfrac{p}{\hbar}\dfrac{(a+b)^2}{2L}=\dfrac{\pi(a+b)^2}{\lambda L}$. For $a=b=1.0\ \mathrm{mm}$, $L=1.0\ \mathrm{m}$, and $\lambda=0.727\ \mathrm{mm}$ (an electron with $v=1\ \mathrm{m/s}$), $\dfrac{\pi(2\times10^{-3})^2}{7.27\times10^{-4}\times1}=0.017\ \mathrm{rad}$ is small. Hence we put $r\approx L+\dfrac{x^2}{2L}-\dfrac{xy}{L}$.

[3] Writing it in integral form. Taking the phase that does not depend on $y$ outside the integral and letting $q=\dfrac{px}{\hbar L}$, we obtain $$ \psi(x)\approx A_0\,e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{x^2}{2L}\right)}\left\{\int_{a}^{a+b}e^{-iqy}\dd y+\int_{-a-b}^{-a}e^{-iqy}\dd y\right\} $$

[4] Antiderivative. The antiderivative of $e^{-iqy}$ is $\dfrac{e^{-iqy}}{-iq}=-\dfrac{1}{iq}e^{-iqy}$, and since $\dfrac{1}{iq}=\dfrac{\hbar L}{ipx}$, we have \begin{equation} \psi(x)\approx A_0\,e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{x^2}{2L}\right)}\left\{\left[-\frac{\hbar L}{ipx}\,e^{-i\frac{p}{\hbar}\frac{x}{L}y}\right]_{a}^{a+b}+\left[-\frac{\hbar L}{ipx}\,e^{-i\frac{p}{\hbar}\frac{x}{L}y}\right]_{-a-b}^{-a}\right\} \label{eq:7-psi-d2} \end{equation}

[5] Substituting the upper and lower limits. The first bracket is $-\dfrac{\hbar L}{ipx}\bigl(e^{-iq(a+b)}-e^{-iqa}\bigr)$ and the second bracket is $-\dfrac{\hbar L}{ipx}\bigl(e^{iqa}-e^{iq(a+b)}\bigr)$ (note that the value at $y=-a$ is $e^{-iq(-a)}=e^{iqa}$ and the value at $y=-a-b$ is $e^{iq(a+b)}$). Adding the two, we get $$ -\frac{\hbar L}{ipx}\Bigl\{e^{-iq(a+b)}-e^{-iqa}+e^{iqa}-e^{iq(a+b)}\Bigr\}=\frac{\hbar L}{ipx}\Bigl\{\bigl(e^{iq(a+b)}-e^{-iq(a+b)}\bigr)-\bigl(e^{iqa}-e^{-iqa}\bigr)\Bigr\} $$ (we reversed all the signs inside the curly braces and changed the sign of the preceding coefficient from $-\to+$). Restoring $q=\dfrac{px}{\hbar L}$, we obtain \begin{equation} \psi(x)=A_0\,e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{x^2}{2L}\right)}\cdot\frac{\hbar L}{ipx}\left\{\left(e^{i\frac{px}{\hbar L}(a+b)}-e^{-i\frac{px}{\hbar L}(a+b)}\right)-\left(e^{i\frac{px}{\hbar L}a}-e^{-i\frac{px}{\hbar L}a}\right)\right\} \label{eq:7-psi-d3} \end{equation}

[6] Euler's formula. Using $e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}=2i\sin\theta$, each parenthesis becomes $2i\sin$, and since $\dfrac{\hbar L}{ipx}\cdot2i=\dfrac{2\hbar L}{px}$, we obtain \begin{equation} \psi(x)=A_0\,e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{x^2}{2L}\right)}\,\frac{2\hbar L}{px}\left(\sin\frac{p(a+b)}{\hbar L}x-\sin\frac{pa}{\hbar L}x\right) \label{eq:7-psi-d4} \end{equation}

[7] Probability density. The absolute value of the phase factor is 1. The amplitude $\psi$ is not squared, but when we form $\abs{\psi}^2$ the parenthesis is squared, and we obtain \begin{equation} \abs{\psi(x)}^2=A_0^{\,2}\,\frac{4\hbar^2L^2}{p^2x^2}\left(\sin\frac{p(a+b)}{\hbar L}x-\sin\frac{pa}{\hbar L}x\right)^2=4a^2A_0^{\,2}\left(\frac{\sin\frac{p(a+b)}{\hbar L}x-\sin\frac{pa}{\hbar L}x}{\frac{pa}{\hbar L}x}\right)^2 \label{eq:7-I-double} \end{equation} (in the last equality we used the rewriting $\dfrac{4\hbar^2L^2}{p^2x^2}=\dfrac{4a^2}{\left(\frac{pa}{\hbar L}x\right)^2}$).

∎

Let us check Eq. \eqref{eq:7-I-double}. At the center of the screen, $x\to0$, we have $\sin\dfrac{p(a+b)}{\hbar L}x-\sin\dfrac{pa}{\hbar L}x\to\dfrac{pb}{\hbar L}x$, so $\abs{\psi(0)}^2=4a^2A_0^{\,2}\left(\dfrac{b}{a}\right)^2=4b^2A_0^{\,2}$. This agrees with the intensity $(2b\,A_0)^2$ obtained when all the waves emerging from the two openings (each of width $b$) add in phase.

7.4.4 Decomposition into fringes × envelope

Let us transform $\sin B-\sin A$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-d4} with the sum-to-product formula for trigonometric functions (High School Mathematics, Chapter 5, Section 5.6): $$ \sin B-\sin A=2\cos\frac{A+B}{2}\,\sin\frac{B-A}{2} $$ Letting $A=qa$ and $B=q(a+b)$ ($q=\dfrac{px}{\hbar L}$), we have $\dfrac{A+B}{2}=\dfrac{q(2a+b)}{2}=\dfrac{qd}{2}$ and $\dfrac{B-A}{2}=\dfrac{qb}{2}$, and therefore

\begin{equation} \psi(x)=A_0\,e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{x^2}{2L}\right)}\,\frac{2}{q}\cdot2\cos\frac{qd}{2}\,\sin\frac{qb}{2}=2b\,A_0\,e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{x^2}{2L}\right)}\cos\frac{qd}{2}\;\frac{\sin(qb/2)}{qb/2} \label{eq:7-psi-d5} \end{equation}

Here we used $\dfrac{2\hbar L}{px}=\dfrac{2}{q}$ and $\dfrac{4}{q}\sin\dfrac{qb}{2}=2b\,\dfrac{\sin(qb/2)}{qb/2}$. Squaring, we obtain the following form.

Theorem 7.2: Fraunhofer diffraction by a double slit — fringes × envelope

When an electron beam of momentum $p$ is directed perpendicularly onto a double slit of width $b$ and center-to-center spacing $d=2a+b$, then, within the range where the paraxial approximation and the Fraunhofer approximation hold, the probability density obtained by adding the probability amplitudes is given by \begin{equation} \abs{\psi(x)}^2=4b^2A_0^{\,2}\;\underbrace{\cos^2\frac{p\,d\,x}{2\hbar L}}_{\text{fringes (interference)}}\;\underbrace{\left(\frac{\sin\frac{p\,b\,x}{2\hbar L}}{\frac{p\,b\,x}{2\hbar L}}\right)^2}_{\text{envelope (diffraction)}} \label{eq:7-fringe-env} \end{equation}

Let us read off the meaning of each factor in this expression.

  • Fringes (the $\cos^2$ factor): This is the interference arising from the phase difference $q\,d=\dfrac{pdx}{\hbar L}$ between the paths through the centers of the two openings. The bright fringes (maxima) appear at $\dfrac{pdx}{2\hbar L}=m\pi$, that is, at $x_m=m\,\dfrac{\lambda L}{d}$ ($m=0,\pm1,\pm2,\dots$), and the dark fringes (zeros) appear at $x=(m+\tfrac12)\dfrac{\lambda L}{d}$. Therefore, the fringe spacing is $\lambda L/d$ (we used $p=h/\lambda=2\pi\hbar/\lambda$). This is exactly the formula for Young's experiment in AD 03, Chapter 16, Section 16.2.
  • Envelope (the $\left(\dfrac{\sin(qb/2)}{qb/2}\right)^2$ factor): This is the diffraction pattern of a single slit of width $b$ (the previous section with $a\to b/2$), and it forms the “outer frame” of the intensity of the fringes. Its first zero is at $x=\lambda L/b$.
  • Missing orders: When a fringe maximum $x_m=m\lambda L/d$ coincides with a zero $x=n\lambda L/b$ ($n=\pm1,\pm2,\dots$) of the envelope, the fringe at that position vanishes. The condition for coincidence is $\dfrac{m}{d}=\dfrac{n}{b}$, that is, $m\,b=n\,d$ ($m=n\,d/b$); when $d/b$ is an integer $N$, the fringes of orders $m=N,2N,\dots$ are missing (missing orders).

Viewing it as subtraction: subtract the central shielding plate from a single large slit

The parenthesis $\sin q(a+b)-\sin qa$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:7-psi-d4} shows that $\dfrac{2}{q}\sin q(a+b)$ is “the amplitude of a single large slit of half-width $a+b$ (width $2(a+b)$)” (Eq. \eqref{eq:7-int} with $a\to a+b$), and that $\dfrac{2}{q}\sin qa$ is “the amplitude of a single slit of half-width $a$ (the size of the central shielding plate).” The amplitude of the double slit is equal to “the amplitude of the single large slit minus the amplitude for the part of the central plate.” Since the shielding plate does not let the waves in that part through (it removes them), this amounts to subtracting “the wave that would have come from the part of the plate” from “the wave coming from the large slit.” This viewpoint leads to Babinet's principle.

7.4.6 Two ways of adding — what determines the difference?

Sections 7.4.2 and 7.4.3 both start from the same raw material (the waves from each slit, whose phases have rotated). The difference lies in whether we square the results for the two slits before adding them, or square after adding them.

Law 7.1: Adding probabilities and probability amplitudes

Suppose that a certain final state (arriving at the point $x$ on the screen) can be reached by two processes (passing through slit 1, passing through slit 2), and let their probability amplitudes be $\psi_1(x)$ and $\psi_2(x)$, respectively.

  1. When the information that distinguishes by which process the particle arrived is not kept anywhere, even in principle, add the probability amplitudes and then square: $$ P=\abs{\psi_1+\psi_2}^2=\abs{\psi_1}^2+\abs{\psi_2}^2+2\,\Real\bigl(\psi_1^*\psi_2\bigr)\qquad(\text{with interference term}) $$
  2. When the processes can be distinguished (when information can be recorded), add the probabilities: $$ P=\abs{\psi_1}^2+\abs{\psi_2}^2\qquad(\text{without interference term}) $$

Eq. \eqref{eq:7-P-sum2} of Section 7.4.2 is case 2, and Eq. \eqref{eq:7-fringe-env} of Section 7.4.3 is case 1. As far as the formulas go, the difference is merely whether there is an interference term $2\Real(\psi_1^*\psi_2)$ between $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ or not. Its physical meaning, however, is deep. “Whether the processes can be distinguished” is decided not by what the electron knows, but by whether a trace of the path remains somewhere in the universe (in a measuring apparatus, in particles of the environment, …). The mere possibility that a trace remains makes the fringes vanish (Section 7.5). In the next chapter, we state this law as part of the Born interpretation and the principle of superposition.

Definition 7.3: Fringe visibility

From the maximum $I_{\max}$ and the minimum $I_{\min}$ of the intensity of the interference fringes, $$ V\equiv\frac{I_{\max}-I_{\min}}{I_{\max}+I_{\min}} $$ is called the visibility of the fringes. It satisfies $0\le V\le1$; $V=1$ means that the dark fringes are completely $0$ (the sharpest fringes), and $V=0$ means a state with no fringes at all.

Example 7.8: Fringes of a double slit and the missing orders

For an electron with $a=b=1.0\ \mathrm{mm}$, $L=1.0\ \mathrm{m}$, $v=1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$, (1) find the fringe spacing and the position of the first dark fringe of the envelope, (2) find the positions of the dark fringes (the first three), and (3) state which orders are missing. (4) If we change to $a=2b$ while keeping the relation $d=2a+b$ for the center-to-center spacing, which order is missing?

Solution. (1) $d=2a+b=3.0\ \mathrm{mm}$. Since $\lambda=7.274\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m}$, the fringe spacing is $\dfrac{\lambda L}{d}=\dfrac{7.274\times10^{-4}}{3.0\times10^{-3}}=0.2425\ \mathrm{m}$, and the first dark fringe of the envelope is at $\dfrac{\lambda L}{b}=0.7274\ \mathrm{m}$.

(2) The dark fringes are at $\cos\dfrac{pdx}{2\hbar L}=0$, that is, $x=(m+\tfrac12)\dfrac{\lambda L}{d}=\tfrac12\times0.2425,\ \tfrac32\times0.2425,\ \tfrac52\times0.2425=0.121,\ 0.364,\ 0.606\ \mathrm{m}$.

(3) The maxima of the fringes, $x_m=0.2425\,m$, coincide with the zeros of the envelope, $0.7274\,n$, when $m=3n$, that is, $m=3,6,\dots$, because $d/b=3$. The third-order fringe ($x=0.727\ \mathrm{m}$) is missing.

(4) If $a=2b$, then $d=2a+b=5b$ and $d/b=5$, so the fifth-order fringe is missing (the fringe spacing becomes finer, $\lambda L/d=\lambda L/5b$, and nine fringes, $-4\le m\le4$, fit inside the principal maximum of the envelope).

Example 7.9: Young's formula for the fringe spacing of electrons and light

An electron beam with an accelerating voltage of $50\ \mathrm{kV}$ ($\lambda=5.355\ \mathrm{pm}$) is passed through a double slit with center-to-center spacing $d=2.0\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ and observed on a screen a distance $L=0.35\ \mathrm{m}$ away. Find the fringe spacing. If light of wavelength $532\ \mathrm{nm}$ were used in the same geometry, what would the fringe spacing be? (The geometry is an assumed value.)

Solution. The fringe spacing is $\dfrac{\lambda L}{d}$. For the electrons it is $\dfrac{5.355\times10^{-12}\times0.35}{2.0\times10^{-6}}=9.4\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m}=0.94\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, which is finer than the wavelength of visible light, so a detector with sufficient resolution is needed. For light it is $\dfrac{532\times10^{-9}\times0.35}{2.0\times10^{-6}}=9.3\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m}=93\ \mathrm{mm}$. The wavelength ratio $532\ \mathrm{nm}/5.355\ \mathrm{pm}=9.9\times10^{4}$ is directly the ratio of the fringe spacings. With light, the fringes become too large, 9 cm, so actual optical experiments widen the slit spacing to bring the fringes to an easily visible size.

Example 7.10: Fringe visibility for a double slit with openings of different widths

The two openings have different widths. The amplitude reaching a point on the screen with only the upper slit open is $\abs{\psi_1}=1$, and with only the lower slit open it is $\abs{\psi_2}=0.5$ (this corresponds to a slit-width ratio of 2 : 1). Find the fringe visibility.

Solution. Let $\delta$ be the phase difference of the two amplitudes; then $\abs{\psi_1+\psi_2}^2=\abs{\psi_1}^2+\abs{\psi_2}^2+2\abs{\psi_1}\abs{\psi_2}\cos\delta$. Letting $I_1=\abs{\psi_1}^2=1$ and $I_2=\abs{\psi_2}^2=0.25$, for $\cos\delta=\pm1$ we have $I_{\max}=I_1+I_2+2\sqrt{I_1I_2}=(\sqrt{I_1}+\sqrt{I_2})^2=(1+0.5)^2=2.25$ and $I_{\min}=(\sqrt{I_1}-\sqrt{I_2})^2=(1-0.5)^2=0.25$. Therefore, the visibility is $$ V=\frac{I_{\max}-I_{\min}}{I_{\max}+I_{\min}}=\frac{2.25-0.25}{2.25+0.25}=0.8 $$ and, in general, $V=\dfrac{2\sqrt{I_1I_2}}{I_1+I_2}=\dfrac{2\times0.5}{1.25}=0.8$. Even at the dark fringes, $I_{\min}=0.25$, so the electron intensity does not fall completely to 0. Only when the intensities of the two paths are equal ($I_1=I_2$) is $V=1$.

7.5 What Are Electrons Doing When They Arrive One at a Time?

The calculations so far have looked only at the electron as a “wave.” But when we detect electrons on a screen, they are detected one at a time, each as a single point. Why is an electron, which should be spread out over the whole screen as a wave, found at a single point? And through which slit did a single electron pass? In this section we summarize what experiments show and how the idea of the probability amplitude answers these questions.

7.5.2 What does “passing through both slits” mean?

Through which of the two slits did a single electron pass? The answer is neither “it passed through one or the other” nor “it passed through both, half in each.” The experimental fact is that the electron is detected at a single point on the screen, undivided. However, the probability that the electron arrives is determined by the square of the absolute value of the sum of “the probability amplitude of arriving through the upper slit” and “the probability amplitude of arriving through the lower slit.”

Figure 7.9 applies the picture of “rotating arrows” from Section 7.1 to the double slit. (a) There are many (continuously many) paths that reach the point $x$ on the screen from each of the two openings. (b), (c) The probability amplitude $e^{ikr}$ of each path is represented as an arrow of the same length whose direction has rotated by the phase $kr$, and these arrows are chained together. The whole chain for opening 1 (blue) is $\psi_1$, the whole chain for opening 2 (red) is $\psi_2$, and the total arrow (black) is $\psi_1+\psi_2$. The phase difference between the paths through the centers of the two openings is $qd=\dfrac{pdx}{\hbar L}$; when this is an integer multiple of $2\pi$ (a bright fringe, with fringe order $m$), the two chains line up in the same direction, and when it is an odd multiple of $\pi$ (a dark fringe), they point in exactly opposite directions and cancel.

Intuition: The question “Which slit did it pass through?” has no answer

The rule of adding probability amplitudes does not force on us the picture that “the electron follows both paths.” All that the rule states is this: when two indistinguishable processes, “passing through slit 1 and arriving at the point $x$” and “passing through slit 2 and arriving at the point $x$,” lead to the same result (the electron is detected at the point $x$), the probability is obtained by adding the respective probability amplitudes and then squaring. In the standard interpretation (the Copenhagen interpretation), unless we examine the path by a measurement, “which slit it passed through” has no definite value. (There are other interpretations that admit trajectories of particles, such as Bohmian mechanics, but they predict the same distribution.) If we examine the path by a measurement (Section 7.5.3), one of the two slits becomes definite, but then the fringes disappear.

7.5.3 Checking which slit the electron passed through makes the fringes disappear (which-way detection)

As Feynman discussed in detail in “The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. III,” consider a thought experiment in which we shine light on the electron behind the slits to try to see which slit it passed through. If the light is scattered by the electron near a slit and is detected, we know which slit the electron passed through. In exchange, the fringes on the screen disappear. Restating this in the language of Section 7.4.2, since it has become possible to distinguish which slit the electron passed through, the result has changed to a sum of probabilities (without an interference term).

Example 7.11: Efficiency of which-way detection and fringe visibility

We monitor the electron with light that illuminates the two slits with equal intensity, and let $p$ be the probability that the electron scatters a photon. When a photon is scattered (probability $p$), which slit the electron passed through is recorded completely; when no photon is scattered (probability $1-p$), nothing is recorded. Suppose that the magnitudes of the amplitudes through the two slits are equal ($\abs{\psi_1}=\abs{\psi_2}$). Find the distribution on the screen and the fringe visibility $V$. Give the values for $p=0.3$ and $p=1$.

Solution. When a record remains, by item 2 of Law 7.1 the probability is the sum of probabilities $\abs{\psi_1}^2+\abs{\psi_2}^2$; when no record remains, by item 1 it is $\abs{\psi_1+\psi_2}^2$. Let $\abs{\psi_1}^2=\abs{\psi_2}^2=I_0$ and let $\delta$ be the phase difference; then $\abs{\psi_1+\psi_2}^2=2I_0(1+\cos\delta)$ and the sum of probabilities is $2I_0$. Since the overall distribution is the average of the two cases, $$ P(\delta)=(1-p)\cdot2I_0(1+\cos\delta)+p\cdot2I_0=2I_0\bigl[1+(1-p)\cos\delta\bigr] $$ holds. For $\cos\delta=\pm1$ we have $I_{\max}=2I_0(2-p)$ and $I_{\min}=2I_0\,p$, so the visibility is $$ V=\frac{I_{\max}-I_{\min}}{I_{\max}+I_{\min}}=\frac{2I_0\,(2-2p)}{2I_0\cdot2}=1-p $$ For $p=0.3$, $V=0.7$; for $p=1$ (completely recorded), $V=0$ (the fringes disappear completely). As the probability of recording increases, the fringes fade linearly.

There is also an argument that explains “leaving information about which path the electron took” by the scattered photon giving momentum to the electron and disturbing it (Heisenberg's thought experiment; the uncertainty principle of the next chapter). However, this is only one way of looking at it, and modern experiments show that the fringes disappear merely because information about the path remains, even without any disturbance of the momentum (for example, an experiment in an atom interferometer in which the information about the path is written into the internal state of the atom; Dürr, Nonn, and Rempe, Nature 395, 33, 1998). Moreover, if one performs an operation that erases the information (the “eraser”), the fringes return. Between the fringe visibility $V$ and the distinguishability of the paths $D$, the inequality $V^2+D^2\le1$ holds (Englert, 1996; Exercise 7.6).

7.5.4 The condition for interference — coherence

For the fringes to be visible, the phase relationship between the waves along the two paths must be kept constant throughout the experiment. This property is called coherence. In the case of electrons, two kinds of coherence become an issue because of the properties of the electron source.

  • Longitudinal coherence: If the electron energies are not all equal but are spread over a width $\Delta E$, the momentum varies over a width $\Delta p$, and the wavelength varies by $\Delta\lambda=\lambda\,\Delta p/p$. As the path difference grows, the fringe positions of waves with different wavelengths shift relative to one another, and the fringes disappear. A rough measure of the path difference up to which fringes are visible is the longitudinal coherence length $\xi=\dfrac{\lambda^2}{\Delta\lambda}=\dfrac{\lambda}{\Delta p/p}$.
  • Transverse coherence: If the electron source has a finite size, the electron waves emitted from different points of the source enter the two slits from different directions. The smaller the source, the more uniform the phase is over the spacing between the two slits (the width over which the fringes are viewed).

Example 7.12: Longitudinal coherence length of an electron beam

For an electron beam with an accelerating voltage of $200\ \mathrm{kV}$ (kinetic energy $E=200\ \mathrm{keV}$), find $\Delta p/p$ and the longitudinal coherence length $\xi$ when the energy width is (a) $\Delta E=0.7\ \mathrm{eV}$ for a field-emission electron source and (b) $\Delta E=2.5\ \mathrm{eV}$ for a thermionic electron source. The rest energy of the electron is $m_{\mathrm e}c^2=511.0\ \mathrm{keV}$.

Solution. Differentiating the relativistic relation $(pc)^2=E\,(E+2m_{\mathrm e}c^2)$ gives $2\,(pc)\,\dd(pc)=2\,(E+m_{\mathrm e}c^2)\,\dd E$, so we have $$ \frac{\Delta p}{p}=\frac{(E+m_{\mathrm e}c^2)\,\Delta E}{(pc)^2}=\frac{(E+m_{\mathrm e}c^2)\,\Delta E}{E\,(E+2m_{\mathrm e}c^2)} $$ For $E=200\ \mathrm{keV}$, we get $(pc)^2=200\times(200+1022.0)=2.444\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{keV^2}$, $pc=494.4\ \mathrm{keV}$, and $\lambda=\dfrac{hc}{pc}=\dfrac{1239.84\ \mathrm{eV\,nm}}{494.4\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{eV}}=2.508\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{nm}=2.508\ \mathrm{pm}$.

(a) $\dfrac{\Delta p}{p}=\dfrac{(200+511.0)\ \mathrm{keV}\times0.7\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{keV}}{2.444\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{keV^2}}=2.04\times10^{-6}$. $\xi=\dfrac{\lambda}{\Delta p/p}=\dfrac{2.508\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m}}{2.04\times10^{-6}}=1.23\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m}=1.23\ \mathrm{\mu m}$.

(b) $\dfrac{\Delta p}{p}=\dfrac{711.0\times2.5\times10^{-3}}{2.444\times10^{5}}=7.27\times10^{-6}$. $\xi=\dfrac{2.508\times10^{-12}}{7.27\times10^{-6}}=3.45\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m}=0.34\ \mathrm{\mu m}$.

Because the field-emission electron source has a smaller energy width, its coherence length is about 3.6 times longer. The coherence length is about $5\times10^{5}$ times the wavelength $2.5\ \mathrm{pm}$ (in case (a)), so interference fringes remain visible up to path differences of the order of $\mu\mathrm{m}$.

Application: Electron holography and the electron microscope

If we combine a highly coherent electron beam (from a field-emission electron source) with an electron biprism, we can extract, as an image, how large a phase shift the sample imparted to the electron wave, from the interference fringes between the electrons that passed through the sample and those that did not. This is electron holography, and it is used to visualize the electric potential distribution and the magnetic field distribution in materials (such as the magnetic flux lines inside magnetic materials and the magnetic flux in superconductors). The resolution of the transmission electron microscope (TEM) is also governed by the coherence of the electron beam. The fringe spacing $\lambda L/d$, the coherence length, and the detector efficiency, all discussed in the preceding sections, directly serve as indicators of the performance of the instrument.

7.5.5 Particle or wave? — sorting it out

Based on the content of this chapter, let us sort out the question, “Is the electron a particle or a wave?”

  • Propagation as a wave: From passing through the slits until reaching the screen, the electron behaves as a probability amplitude (a wave function). The amplitudes of the two paths are added (interference), the wavelength is $\lambda=h/p$, and how the electron spreads is determined by the formulas for diffraction (single slit) and interference (double slit).
  • Detection as a particle: When the electron is detected on the screen, it is found at a single point as a single electron with mass and charge. The arrival point of one electron cannot be predicted, and only the distribution of many collected electrons follows $\abs{\psi}^2$.
  • If we find out which path was taken, the wave property (the fringes) disappears. The wave nature (fringe visibility $V$) and the particle nature (the ease of distinguishing the path $D$) cannot both be seen completely in the same experiment ($V^2+D^2\le1$, complementarity; Example 7.11, Exercise 7.6).

In Chapter 2, Section 2.4, we stated that “the question is not wave or particle,” and in Chapter 1 we said that “the common sense of classical physics does not apply”; the reasons for these statements can now be understood concretely through the formulas of this chapter. In the next chapter, we organize as axioms the meaning of the probability density $\abs{\psi}^2$ (the Born interpretation), the commutation relation between position and momentum, and the uncertainty principle linking the slit width and the spread in momentum.

7.6 How Far Can the Approximations Be Used? — The Fraunhofer Approximation and the Fresnel Number (Advanced)

In this section, we summarize the conditions for these approximations and see what happens when the conditions are violated. The fact that a heavy particle makes a “geometric shadow” can also be understood from the results of this section.

7.6.2 The meaning of the Fresnel number

The Fresnel number $N_{\mathrm F}=a^2/(\lambda L)$ (Definition 7.1) has the following meaning. The path difference, to the central point $x=0$ on the screen, between the path through the center of the slit and the path through the edge $y=\pm a$ is, from Eq. \eqref{eq:7-r-expand}, $\dfrac{a^2}{2L}$. Using the wavelength $\lambda$, this can be written as $$ \frac{a^2}{2L}=\frac{N_{\mathrm F}}{2}\,\lambda $$ That is, $N_{\mathrm F}$ expresses how many times a half wavelength $\lambda/2$ the phase difference between the edge path and the center path amounts to (how many Fresnel zones, obtained by dividing the path difference into steps of $\lambda/2$, fit in half of the slit). If $N_{\mathrm F}\ll1$, the path difference is much smaller than a half wavelength, and the waves from the whole slit arrive almost in phase. The wave cannot make out the fine shape of the slit, and it spreads widely by diffraction (Fraunhofer diffraction). If $N_{\mathrm F}\gg1$, the path difference is much larger than the wavelength, the wave can be regarded as traveling straight from each point of the slit, and the geometric shadow of the slit appears. $N_{\mathrm F}\sim1$ is the intermediate case (Fresnel diffraction).

7.6.3 The distribution for different Fresnel numbers

If we use only the paraxial approximation and integrate without dropping the $y^2$ term, we are left with $\displaystyle\psi(x)=A_0\int_{-a}^{a}e^{i\frac{\pi}{\lambda L}(x-y)^2}\dd y$ (omitting a common phase factor). With the change of variables $t=(y-x)\sqrt{2/(\lambda L)}$, we get $\dfrac{\pi(x-y)^2}{\lambda L}=\dfrac{\pi t^2}{2}$, and this integral can be written in terms of the Fresnel integrals $$ \mathrm{C}(t)=\int_0^{t}\cos\frac{\pi s^2}{2}\dd s,\qquad\mathrm{S}(t)=\int_0^{t}\sin\frac{\pi s^2}{2}\dd s $$ Normalizing the incident intensity to 1, the intensity on the screen is \begin{equation} I(x)=\frac12\Bigl\{\bigl[\mathrm{C}(t_2)-\mathrm{C}(t_1)\bigr]^2+\bigl[\mathrm{S}(t_2)-\mathrm{S}(t_1)\bigr]^2\Bigr\},\qquad t_{1,2}=(\mp a-x)\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda L}} \label{eq:7-fresnel-I} \end{equation} ($t_1$ corresponds to $-a$ and $t_2$ to $+a$). Inside the shadow of the slit, when $t_1\to-\infty$ and $t_2\to+\infty$, from $\mathrm{C}(\pm\infty)=\pm\tfrac12$ and $\mathrm{S}(\pm\infty)=\pm\tfrac12$ we obtain $I\to\tfrac12(1^2+1^2)=1$, which is the same as the incident intensity. Figure 7.10 shows the distributions drawn with this formula for $N_{\mathrm F}=0.02,\ 1,\ 10,\ 100$.

As Figure 7.10 shows, the distribution for $N_{\mathrm F}=0.02$ is a smooth peak far wider than the shadow (Fraunhofer diffraction). As $N_{\mathrm F}$ grows, the distribution approaches the width of the shadow and takes the shape of a shadow with fringes at its edges. For $N_{\mathrm F}=100$, it is a nearly uniform shadow of width $2a$, and the width of the blur at the edge is only $a/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm F}}=0.1a$. The $6.6\ \mathrm{g}$ particle of Illustration 7.2 ($N_{\mathrm F}=10^{25}$) lies still farther beyond this right-hand end, and the width of its blur is only $a/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm F}}=3\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{m}$. The $\delta$ function for the heavy particle means “the spread due to diffraction is 0,” and the equations of wave optics support the conclusion of Section 7.3 that the shadow of the slit remains.

The limitation of the other approximation, the paraxial approximation, was also seen in Section 7.3. Eq. \eqref{eq:7-P-x} replaces the position $x$ on the screen by $L\tan\theta\approx L\sin\theta$. Table 7.5 shows the size of the error of this replacement.

Actual electron diffraction experiments are carried out at small angles of $\theta\sim10^{-3}$–$10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad}$, so the paraxial approximation is sufficient.

Example 7.13: The Fresnel number and the experimental distance

He–Ne laser light of wavelength $632.8\ \mathrm{nm}$ is incident on a single slit of width $2a=1.0\ \mathrm{mm}$. (1) Find the Fresnel number for a distance to the screen of $L=1.0\ \mathrm{m}$. (2) Find the distance $L$ for which the Fraunhofer approximation holds (take this to mean $N_{\mathrm F}\le0.01$). (3) Find the position of the first zero at that distance.

Solution. (1) Since $a=0.50\ \mathrm{mm}$ and $a^2=2.5\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, we have $\dfrac{a^2}{\lambda}=\dfrac{2.5\times10^{-7}}{6.328\times10^{-7}}=0.395\ \mathrm{m}$, and for $L=1.0\ \mathrm{m}$ the Fresnel number is $N_{\mathrm F}=\dfrac{a^2}{\lambda L}=0.395$. The condition $N_{\mathrm F}\ll1$ is not satisfied well enough, so the Fraunhofer approximation is inadequate.

(2) From $N_{\mathrm F}=\dfrac{a^2}{\lambda L}\le0.01$, we obtain $L\ge\dfrac{a^2}{0.01\,\lambda}=\dfrac{0.395\ \mathrm{m}}{0.01}=39.5\ \mathrm{m}$.

(3) $x_1=\dfrac{\lambda L}{2a}=\dfrac{6.328\times10^{-7}\times39.5}{1.0\times10^{-3}}=2.5\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m}=25\ \mathrm{mm}$. Although the slit width is $1\ \mathrm{mm}$, at a distance of $40\ \mathrm{m}$ the principal maximum has a width of $50\ \mathrm{mm}$. In an actual laboratory, a lens is placed immediately after the slit, and the diffraction pattern corresponding to infinity (the Fraunhofer pattern) is formed in its focal plane and observed there.

Application: The “camera length” and lenses in electron diffraction

In electron diffraction in a transmission electron microscope, the electron beam that has passed through the sample is focused onto the back focal plane of the objective lens to form a diffraction pattern. In this way, even inside an instrument in which $L$ is a few tens of centimeters, the Fraunhofer diffraction pattern for infinite distance is obtained in the focal plane. The spacing of the pattern is determined by the product of the length of the instrument (the camera length) and the wavelength. In diffraction by a crystal (the same idea as the diffraction grating in AD 03, Chapter 16, Section 16.3), instead of two slits there are many lattice sites (the arrangement of atoms), and the pattern becomes an array of points (diffraction spots). In solid-state physics (AD 06), this leads to the reciprocal lattice and the Laue condition.

7.7 Summary and Exercises

7.7.1 Summary

  • Problem setup: An electron beam of momentum $p$ is directed perpendicularly onto a single slit of width $2a$, and we find the probability distribution on a screen at distance $L$. We add up the waves coming from every point of the slit (probability amplitude $e^{ipr/\hbar}$, $k=p/\hbar$): $\psi(x)=A_0\displaystyle\int_{-a}^{a}e^{ipr/\hbar}\dd y$.
  • Approximations and conditions: $r=\sqrt{L^2+(x-y)^2}\approx L+\dfrac{x^2}{2L}-\dfrac{xy}{L}+\dfrac{y^2}{2L}$ (paraxial approximation; binomial approximation). The $y^2$ term can be dropped when its phase $\pi a^2/(\lambda L)=\pi N_{\mathrm F}$ is small (Fresnel number $N_{\mathrm F}\ll1$) (Fraunhofer approximation). The condition “$y/L$ is small” alone is not enough; the phase must be compared with the wavelength.
  • Single slit: $\psi=2aA_0\,e^{i\frac{p}{\hbar}\left(L+\frac{x^2}{2L}\right)}\dfrac{\sin u}{u}$, $u=\dfrac{pax}{\hbar L}$. The probability density is $\propto(\sin u/u)^2$, the zeros are at $x_n=n\lambda L/(2a)$, the principal maximum contains $90.3\ \%$ (of the total probability), and the first secondary maximum is $4.72\ \%$. Normalization: normalizing with respect to $u$ gives the coefficient $1/\pi$, and normalizing with respect to $x$ gives $\alpha/\pi$ ($\alpha=pa/\hbar L$). The narrower the slit, the wider the spread (a concrete example of the uncertainty in position and momentum).
  • Mass dependence and the $\delta$ function: $\alpha\propto m$. $\delta(x)=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\dfrac{\alpha}{\pi}\left(\dfrac{\sin\alpha x}{\alpha x}\right)^2$. An electron with a speed of $1\ \mathrm{m/s}$ spreads out to $0.36\ \mathrm{m}$. For a heavy particle the diffraction spread becomes 0, but then $N_{\mathrm F}\gg1$ and the distribution coincides with the geometric shadow of width $2a$. The spreading of a breath is not diffraction.
  • Double slit: The sum of probabilities $P=\tfrac12P_1+\tfrac12P_2$ (when we can distinguish which slit the electron passed through) is merely two single-slit peaks side by side. The sum of probability amplitudes gives $\abs{\psi}^2=4b^2A_0^{\,2}\cos^2\dfrac{pdx}{2\hbar L}\left(\dfrac{\sin\frac{pbx}{2\hbar L}}{\frac{pbx}{2\hbar L}}\right)^2$ ($d=2a+b$). The fringe spacing is $\lambda L/d$, the dark fringe of the envelope is at $\lambda L/b$, and if $d/b$ is an integer $N$, the fringe of order $N$ is missing.
  • One-at-a-time experiments and the probability interpretation: Electrons arrive one at a time at a single point (the arrival point cannot be predicted), and the distribution accumulated from many electrons is $\abs{\psi}^2$. If it can be known in principle which slit the electron passed through (a record remains), the fringes disappear (fringe visibility $V=1-p$, etc.). The visibility of the fringes is determined by coherence. Propagation is as a wave; detection is as a particle.
\begin{equation} \psi(x)=A_0\int e^{i\frac{p\,r}{\hbar}}\,\dd y,\qquad\abs{\psi}^2\propto\left(\frac{\sin u}{u}\right)^2,\quad u=\frac{p\,a\,x}{\hbar L},\quad x_n=n\,\frac{\lambda L}{2a},\quad N_{\mathrm F}=\frac{a^2}{\lambda L}\ll1 \label{eq:7-key1} \end{equation}
\begin{equation} \abs{\psi(x)}^2=4b^2A_0^{\,2}\,\cos^2\!\frac{p\,d\,x}{2\hbar L}\left(\frac{\sin\frac{p\,b\,x}{2\hbar L}}{\frac{p\,b\,x}{2\hbar L}}\right)^2,\qquad\Delta x_{\text{fringe}}=\frac{\lambda L}{d},\quad\delta(x)=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\left(\frac{\sin\alpha x}{\alpha x}\right)^2 \label{eq:7-key2} \end{equation}

Example 7.14: Summary check: a double slit for 50 kV electrons

An electron beam with an accelerating voltage of $50\ \mathrm{kV}$ ($\lambda=5.355\ \mathrm{pm}$) is directed perpendicularly onto a double slit with opening width $b=0.50\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ and center-to-center spacing $d=2.0\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, and is observed on a screen $L=1.5\ \mathrm{m}$ away. Find (1) the fringe spacing, (2) the position of the first dark fringe of the envelope, (3) the missing orders and the number of bright fringes inside the central peak of the envelope, and (4) the Fresnel number for a single opening, and decide whether the Fraunhofer approximation holds.

Solution. (1) The fringe spacing is $\dfrac{\lambda L}{d}=\dfrac{5.355\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m}\times1.5\ \mathrm{m}}{2.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m}}=4.02\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m}=4.0\ \mathrm{\mu m}$.

(2) The first dark fringe of the envelope is the first zero of a single slit of opening width $b$, at $\dfrac{\lambda L}{b}=\dfrac{5.355\times10^{-12}\times1.5}{0.50\times10^{-6}}=1.61\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m}=16.1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$.

(3) Since $d/b=2.0/0.50=4$ is an integer, $m\,b=n\,d$ gives $m=4n$; that is, the fringes of orders 4, 8, … are missing. Indeed, the position of the fourth-order fringe, $4\times4.02\ \mathrm{\mu m}=16.1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, coincides with the first dark fringe of the envelope. The bright fringes inside the central peak of the envelope ($\abs{x}\lt16.1\ \mathrm{\mu m}$) are those with $\abs{m}\lt4$, namely $m=0,\pm1,\pm2,\pm3$: seven in all. (The intensity of the first-order fringe is $\left(\dfrac{\sin(\pi/4)}{\pi/4}\right)^2=0.81$ times that of the central fringe.)

(4) The half-width of one opening is $b/2=0.25\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, so $N_{\mathrm F}=\dfrac{(b/2)^2}{\lambda L}=\dfrac{(0.25\times10^{-6})^2}{5.355\times10^{-12}\times1.5}=\dfrac{6.25\times10^{-14}}{8.03\times10^{-12}}=7.8\times10^{-3}$. Since $N_{\mathrm F}\ll1$ (the maximum phase is $\pi N_{\mathrm F}=0.024\ \mathrm{rad}$), the Fraunhofer approximation holds.
