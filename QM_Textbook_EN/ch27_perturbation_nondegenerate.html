Chapter 27Time-Independent Perturbation Theory — Nondegenerate First- and Second-Order Perturbation

The Schrödinger equation of the hydrogen atom can be solved so cleanly that both the energies and the wave functions can be written as formulas. But when one more electron is added to make the helium atom, the mere addition of the Coulomb repulsion between the electrons, $e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r_{12})$, means that the problem can no longer be solved analytically. An atom placed in an electric or magnetic field, a magnetic ion in a crystal, a slight anharmonicity of a molecular vibration—the Hamiltonian of a real system usually has the form of a sum of “a main part that can be solved exactly” and “an unsolvable but small correction.” Perturbation theory is a method that takes the solution of the main part as its starting point and systematically computes the effect of the correction as powers (first order, second order, …) of a small quantity; it is the most basic tool when quantum mechanics is used on problems involving materials.

This way of thinking exists in classical physics as well. The two-body problem of the Sun and the Earth alone can be solved exactly as a Kepler elliptical orbit (AD 03, Chapter 8, Section 8.2). In reality the Earth is also acted on by the attraction of the other planets, but since this is small compared with the attraction of the Sun, it suffices to correct for it by thinking that “the elliptical orbit shifts little by little.” The perturbation theory of quantum mechanics rests on the same idea. In quantum mechanics, however, one more phenomenon occurs that has no counterpart in classical physics: the correction does not merely move the energy, but other states mix in with the original state. If we follow this mixing in equations, the second-order correction to the energy (the “repulsion” of levels), the polarizability of atoms, the attraction between molecules (the van der Waals force), the energy shift of a quantum well in an electric field, and so on all come out of one and the same formula.

In this chapter we start from the form $\hat{H}=\hat{H}_0+\lambda\hat{V}$, in which the perturbation is adjusted by a small knob $\lambda$, separate the equations order by order in $\lambda$, and derive, line by line and using nothing but orthogonality, the first-order energy $E_n^{(1)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}$, the first-order wave function, and the second-order energy $E_n^{(2)}=-\sum_{i\ne n}\abs{V_{in}}^2/(E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)})$. We then compute a small step next to the wall of an infinite well (an application of first-order perturbation), a harmonic oscillator in an electric field, the anharmonic term of the Morse potential, the polarizability of the hydrogen atom, the quantum-confined Stark effect of a quantum well, and the van der Waals force between molecules (applications of second-order perturbation); finally, we check “how far the perturbation series can be trusted” with an exactly solvable two-level model.

27.1 What Is a Perturbation? — A Small Deviation from a Solvable Problem

27.1.1 Few problems can be solved exactly

Let us count the problems for which we have been able to solve the Schrödinger equation completely in the chapters so far. They are the infinite well (Chapter 10), the finite well (Chapter 11), the harmonic oscillator (Chapter 13), the hydrogen atom (Chapter 15), and the Morse potential (Chapter 20). Each of these is such an exceptionally clean problem that it appears in textbooks. When we deal with real materials, “extra terms” are always added (there are two or more electrons, which brings in electron–electron repulsion; an electric or magnetic field is applied from outside; the potential is not a perfect parabola), and the problem can no longer be solved exactly.

This is where approximation methods come in. In this book we study two standard methods. One is the variational method (Part VI), which minimizes the expectation value of the energy of a trial function. The other is the perturbation theory that we study in this part. Perturbation theory rests on the following idea.

Intuition: Examining the “neighborhood” of a solvable problem

Suppose the full Hamiltonian can be written as the sum of an exactly solvable part $\hat{H}_0$ and a part $\lambda\hat{V}$ that cannot be solved but is small. Since all the eigenstates (energies and wave functions) of $\hat{H}_0$ are known, we use them as “coordinate axes.” When the small $\lambda\hat{V}$ is added, the energy levels shift slightly, and the wave functions become ones “tilted slightly from the original axes.” The tilt is expressed by the components along the other axes (the admixture of other eigenstates). Perturbation theory is the procedure of finding the size of the shift and the tilt order by order in $\lambda$: first order, second order, and so on.

Let us compare this with perturbation in classical mechanics. The orbit of the Earth around the Sun is an ellipse if we consider the Sun alone (AD 03, Chapter 8, Section 8.2). The attraction of Jupiter and the other planets enters as a small correction, and the shape and orientation of the ellipse shift little by little. The point that “a small correction shifts the levels slightly” is the same in quantum mechanics, but quantum mechanics has in addition the picture that the correction causes other states to mix in. This means that not only do the levels (the energy) shift, but the distribution of the electron (the wave function) also changes.

27.1.3 Notation conventions

Notation: The $\hat{V}$ of this book, and the $\hat{H}'$ and $\hat{H}^{\mathrm P}$ of the related simulator and other sources

In this book we write the full Hamiltonian as

$$ \hat{H}=\hat{H}_0+\lambda\hat{V} $$

Here $\hat{H}_0$ is the exactly solvable Hamiltonian (the unperturbed Hamiltonian), $\hat{V}$ is the operator that expresses the “shape” of the perturbation, and $\lambda$ is a real number that adjusts the strength of the perturbation (the perturbation parameter). We write the eigenvalues of $\hat{H}_0$ as $E_n^{(0)}$ and the eigenfunctions as $\psi_n^{(0)}$. The superscript $(0)$ means “no perturbation,” and $(1)$, $(2)$, … denote the first-order, second-order, … corrections of the perturbation.

The related simulator “Perturbation Theory” (simulator) and Computational Materials Science (AD 05, Chapter 4) write the same quantities as $\hat{H}^{(0)}$ and $\hat{H}'$ (AD 05 writes $\hat{H}$ as the calligraphic letter $\hat{\mathcal{H}}$), and write the whole perturbation term as $\hat{H}^{\mathrm P}=\lambda\hat{H}'$ ($\mathrm{P}$ is the initial letter of perturbation). The correspondence is as follows.

In the general theory, the states are numbered $n=0,1,2,\dots$ (the ground state is $n=0$). However, since the convention for the particle in a box (infinite well) and the hydrogen atom is to start from $n=1,2,3,\dots$, we use $n=1,2,\dots$ in the well examples of Section 27.4. “$\sum_{i\ne n}$” denotes the sum over all states other than the $n$th. Since this is a different symbol from the $\lambda$ of wavelength, do not confuse them (no wavelength appears in this chapter).

27.1.4 $\lambda$ as a knob, and what “small” means

The $\lambda$ in $\hat{H}=\hat{H}_0+\lambda\hat{V}$ is a knob in the theory. $\lambda=0$ is the solvable problem and $\lambda=1$ is the real problem; we turn the knob little by little from $0$ and follow how the energies and wave functions change. $\lambda$ is an auxiliary line drawn for the calculation, not a real physical quantity. At the end, setting $\lambda=1$ turns $\lambda\hat{V}$ into $\hat{V}$.

Now, what does “the perturbation is small” mean? Merely saying that “the value of $\hat{V}$ is small” is meaningless, because by choosing the units (the scale of the energy) one can make it anything. The coefficients $c_k$ of the wave function and the energy corrections of second order and higher all appear in a form that contains the dimensionless ratio $$ \frac{\abs{V_{mn}}}{\abs{E_m^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}}} $$ (the ratio of the matrix element of the perturbation to the spacing between levels). (The first-order energy is $V_{nn}$ itself.) Perturbation theory holds well when this ratio is sufficiently small compared with $1$. In Section 27.7 we show that this can be checked exactly in the two-level model.

27.1.5 Matrix elements and expansion in a complete set

In perturbation-theory calculations, integrals of the perturbation $\hat{V}$ sandwiched between the unperturbed eigenfunctions $\psi_m^{(0)}$ and $\psi_n^{(0)}$ appear again and again. These are called matrix elements.

Definition 27.1: Matrix element of the perturbation

For the unperturbed eigenfunctions $\psi_m^{(0)}$ and $\psi_n^{(0)}$, the quantity

\begin{equation} V_{mn}\equiv\mel{\psi_m^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}=\int\psi_m^{(0)*}(\bm{r})\,\hat{V}\,\psi_n^{(0)}(\bm{r})\dd\tau \label{eq:27-Vmn} \end{equation}

is called the matrix element of the perturbation $\hat{V}$ ($\dd\tau$ is the volume element; in one dimension it is $\dd x$). In this book we also write $\mel{\psi_m^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}$ as $\mel{m^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{n^{(0)}}$ or simply $\mel{m}{\hat{V}}{n}$ (for the ket notation, see Section 10.2).

If $\hat{V}$ is a Hermitian operator (corresponding to a real physical quantity), then $V_{nm}=V_{mn}^*$. In particular, the diagonal element $V_{nn}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}$ is real and is “the expectation value of $\hat{V}$ in the state $\psi_n^{(0)}$.” In the language of matrices, $V_{mn}$ is the $(m,n)$ component of the matrix that represents $\hat{V}$ when the unperturbed eigenfunctions are taken as the basis (we did the same for angular momentum in Chapter 24).

Next we review the expansion in a complete set, the other foundation of perturbation theory. Since $\hat{H}_0$ is a Hermitian operator, the set $\{\psi_i^{(0)}\}$ of its eigenfunctions has the following properties.

  • Orthonormality: $\braket{\psi_i^{(0)}}{\psi_j^{(0)}}=\delta_{ij}$ (Section 10.2).
  • Completeness: Any function $\varphi$ (satisfying the boundary conditions) can be expanded as $\varphi=\sum_ic_i\psi_i^{(0)}$. The coefficients are found, by multiplying both sides by $\bra{\psi_j^{(0)}}$ and using orthogonality, to be $c_j=\braket{\psi_j^{(0)}}{\varphi}$.

This has the same form as expanding a vector in an orthonormal basis $\bm{e}_i$ as $\bm{a}=\sum_i(\bm{e}_i\cdot\bm{a})\bm{e}_i$. In the infinite well, the expansion in $\psi_k^{(0)}=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(k\pi x/L)$ is precisely the Fourier sine series (University Mathematics, Chapter 28, Section 28.3). In perturbation theory, we write the first-order correction $\psi_n^{(1)}$, which we are about to find, by means of this expansion. A perturbed state can be viewed, as in Figure 27.1, as a slightly tilted vector in the space whose axes are the unperturbed states.

Caution: Systems with a continuous spectrum

In a system such as the hydrogen atom, which has bound states ($E\lt0$, discrete levels) and also ionized states ($E\gt0$, continuous energies), the complete set must include these continuum states as well, and the sum $\sum_i$ becomes “the sum over the bound states” + “the integral over the continuum states.” In the polarizability of the hydrogen atom in Section 27.6, we will see that this effect amounts to about 19% of the total.

Example 27.1: Can the electric field on a hydrogen atom be regarded as a “perturbation”?

A uniform electric field of intensity $F=1.0\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{V/m}$ ($=100\ \mathrm{kV/cm}$, a fairly strong electric field that can be produced in a laboratory) is applied in the $z$ direction to the ground state (1s) of the hydrogen atom. Since the electron has charge $-e_0$, the perturbation obtained from $-\hat{\bm{d}}\cdot\bm{E}$ ($\hat{\bm{d}}=-e_0\hat{\bm{r}}$) is $\hat{V}=e_0F\hat{z}$. Compare the magnitude of this perturbation with the level spacing, and judge whether it can be treated as a perturbation.

Solution. First we fix the units. $E_{\mathrm h}=e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0a_0)=27.211\ \mathrm{eV}$ is the unit of energy for the hydrogen atom (the Hartree energy), and the levels of the hydrogen atom can be written $E_n=-E_{\mathrm h}/(2n^2)$. Therefore $E_2-E_1=\tfrac38E_{\mathrm h}=10.20\ \mathrm{eV}$. Next, the states directly connected to 1s (those for which the matrix element of $\hat{z}$ is nonzero) are the states with $\Delta l=\pm1$ and the same $m$, that is, $n\mathrm{p}_0$ with $l=1$, $m=0$ (since $\hat{z}\propto\cos\theta\propto Y_1^0$, only $Y_1^0$ is connected to $Y_0^0$). Here we take $2\mathrm{p}_0$ as a representative. We compute its matrix element by separating it into a radial part and an angular part. The radial functions of the hydrogen atom (in units with $a_0=1$) are $R_{10}=2e^{-r}$ and $R_{21}=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt6}\,r\,e^{-r/2}$, so the radial integral is

$$ \int_0^\infty R_{21}R_{10}\,r^3\dd r=\frac{2}{2\sqrt6}\int_0^\infty r^4e^{-3r/2}\dd r=\frac{1}{\sqrt6}\cdot\frac{4!}{(3/2)^5}=\frac{1}{\sqrt6}\cdot\frac{24\cdot32}{243}=\frac{128\sqrt6}{243}\quad(\text{units }a_0) $$

The angular part is $\mel{Y_1^0}{\cos\theta}{Y_0^0}=1/\sqrt3$, so we obtain

$$ \mel{2\mathrm{p}_0}{\hat{z}}{1\mathrm{s}}=\frac{128\sqrt6}{243}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\,a_0=\frac{128\sqrt2}{243}\,a_0=0.7449\,a_0 $$

Therefore the matrix element of the perturbation is $V_{21}=0.7449\,e_0Fa_0=0.7449\times(1.0\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{V/m})\times(5.292\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m})\,e_0=3.94\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV}$. On the other hand, the level spacing is $E_2-E_1=10.20\ \mathrm{eV}$, so the ratio is

$$ \frac{\abs{V_{21}}}{E_2^{(0)}-E_1^{(0)}}=\frac{3.94\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV}}{10.20\ \mathrm{eV}}=3.9\times10^{-5} $$

Another way to look at it is to compare with the magnitude of the electric field that the electron feels from the nucleus inside the atom, $e_0/(4\pi\varepsilon_0a_0^2)=5.14\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{V/m}$. We find $F/(5.14\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{V/m})=1.9\times10^{-5}$, which is small to the same degree. In either view the ratio is of order $10^{-5}$, so a strong laboratory electric field is a very small perturbation for the hydrogen atom, and perturbation theory holds extremely well.

Example 27.2: Practice with expansion in a complete set — Why does the $k=0$ term not appear?

For the infinite well of width $L$ ($0\le x\le L$), the state obtained when a small perturbation is added to the ground state $\psi_1^{(0)}=\sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(\pi x/L)$ can be written as a superposition of the functions $\psi_k^{(0)}=\sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(k\pi x/L)$. Why does that sum start from $k=1$ and not include a $k=0$ term?

Solution. There are two reasons. First, if $k=0$, then $\sin(0\cdot\pi x/L)=0$, and the “function” is identically 0. This cannot be normalized ($\braket{\psi}{\psi}=0$), so it is not a state. Second, as a more essential reason, the wave functions allowed in the infinite well are the functions that satisfy the boundary condition $\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0$, and if the perturbation is of finite magnitude, the perturbed wave function also satisfies this condition. A complete set for the set of functions satisfying the boundary condition is $\{\sin(k\pi x/L)\}_{k=1,2,\dots}$. A constant function or $\cos(k\pi x/L)$ does not become $0$ at $x=0$, so it is not contained in this function space.

Indeed, for a square-integrable function $\varphi$ on $[0,L]$, the expansion with the coefficients $c_k=\sqrt{2/L}\int_0^L\varphi(x)\sin(k\pi x/L)\dd x$ ($k=1,2,\dots$) converges to $\varphi$ (in the mean-square sense) (the Fourier sine series of University Mathematics, Chapter 28, Section 28.3). When the formula $\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}$ of the general theory is used for the well, we read it as $k=1,2,\dots$ to match the numbering that starts from $n=1$ (the $k=0$ term, even if it were present, vanishes automatically because $\sin0=0$).

27.2 Expansion in $\lambda$ and the Equations Order by Order

27.2.1 Starting point: the unperturbed problem and the problem with the perturbation

The “solved problem” that serves as the starting point of perturbation theory is the time-independent Schrödinger equation

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_0\,\psi_n^{(0)}=E_n^{(0)}\,\psi_n^{(0)},\qquad \hat{H}_0=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+v(\bm{r}) \label{eq:27-H0} \end{equation}

Here $v(\bm{r})$ is the potential energy of the solved part (an infinite well, a harmonic oscillator, the Coulomb potential of the hydrogen atom, and so on), and $m$ is the mass of the particle. As noted in the previous section, the superscript $(0)$ indicates “no perturbation.” The energies $E_n^{(0)}$ and the orthonormal eigenfunctions $\psi_n^{(0)}$ are assumed to be known for all $n$.

We add the perturbation term $\lambda\hat{V}$ ($\lambda$ is a small real number) to this system and want to solve the eigenvalue problem of the total Hamiltonian

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=\hat{H}_0+\lambda\hat{V} \label{eq:27-H} \end{equation}

That is, we start from the following three equations.

\begin{align} \hat{H}\psi_n&=E_n\psi_n \label{eq:27-tise}\\ \psi_n&=\psi_n^{(0)}+\lambda\psi_n^{(1)}+\lambda^2\psi_n^{(2)}+\cdots \label{eq:27-psi-exp}\\ E_n&=E_n^{(0)}+\lambda E_n^{(1)}+\lambda^2E_n^{(2)}+\cdots \label{eq:27-E-exp} \end{align}

Eq. \eqref{eq:27-tise} is the equation we want to solve, and Eqs. \eqref{eq:27-psi-exp} and \eqref{eq:27-E-exp} are the assumption that the solution $\psi_n$ and the energy $E_n$ can be expanded in power series in $\lambda$ around the solutions $\psi_n^{(0)}$ and $E_n^{(0)}$ at $\lambda=0$. $\psi_n^{(k)}$ and $E_n^{(k)}$ are the $k$th-order corrections due to the perturbation. Treating these as unknown quantities that we do not yet know, we substitute them into Eq. \eqref{eq:27-tise} and determine them.

27.2.2 Organizing by order in $\lambda$

First, we substitute Eqs. \eqref{eq:27-H} and \eqref{eq:27-psi-exp} into the left-hand side $\hat{H}\psi_n$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:27-tise} and expand. Multiplying out term by term with the distributive law gives

\begin{align*} \hat{H}\psi_n&=(\hat{H}_0+\lambda\hat{V})\bigl(\psi_n^{(0)}+\lambda\psi_n^{(1)}+\lambda^2\psi_n^{(2)}+\cdots\bigr)\\ &=\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(0)}+\lambda\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(1)}+\lambda^2\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(2)}+\cdots +\lambda\hat{V}\psi_n^{(0)}+\lambda^2\hat{V}\psi_n^{(1)}+\lambda^3\hat{V}\psi_n^{(2)}+\cdots \end{align*}

Collecting the terms with the same power of $\lambda$ (the $\lambda^2$ term is the sum of $\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(2)}$ and $\hat{V}\psi_n^{(1)}$, the latter being $\lambda\hat{V}$ multiplied onto $\lambda\psi_n^{(1)}$), we obtain

\begin{equation} \hat{H}\psi_n=\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(0)}+\lambda\bigl(\hat{V}\psi_n^{(0)}+\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(1)}\bigr)+\lambda^2\bigl(\hat{V}\psi_n^{(1)}+\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(2)}\bigr)+\cdots \label{eq:27-lhs} \end{equation}

Similarly, we substitute Eqs. \eqref{eq:27-psi-exp} and \eqref{eq:27-E-exp} into the right-hand side $E_n\psi_n$ and expand.

\begin{align} E_n\psi_n&=\bigl(E_n^{(0)}+\lambda E_n^{(1)}+\lambda^2E_n^{(2)}+\cdots\bigr)\bigl(\psi_n^{(0)}+\lambda\psi_n^{(1)}+\lambda^2\psi_n^{(2)}+\cdots\bigr)\notag\\ &=E_n^{(0)}\psi_n^{(0)}+\lambda\bigl(E_n^{(0)}\psi_n^{(1)}+E_n^{(1)}\psi_n^{(0)}\bigr)+\lambda^2\bigl(E_n^{(0)}\psi_n^{(2)}+E_n^{(1)}\psi_n^{(1)}+E_n^{(2)}\psi_n^{(0)}\bigr)+\cdots \label{eq:27-rhs} \end{align}

Here the coefficient of $\lambda^2$ is obtained by picking out, from the product of the two series, the combinations that give $\lambda^2$, that is, the three products $(0,2)$, $(1,1)$, and $(2,0)$. In general, the coefficient of $\lambda^k$ is the sum of $E_n^{(j)}\psi_n^{(k-j)}$ ($j=0,1,\dots,k$).

Eq. \eqref{eq:27-tise} is an equation that holds regardless of the value of $\lambda$, wherever we set the knob $\lambda$ (an identity in $\lambda$). Therefore the two power series in Eqs. \eqref{eq:27-lhs} and \eqref{eq:27-rhs} must have equal coefficients for each power of $\lambda$.

Math Tool: Uniqueness of power series

If an identity containing $\lambda$, $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_k\lambda^k=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_k\lambda^k$, holds for all $\lambda$ near $\lambda=0$, then $a_k=b_k$ (for all $k$). Indeed, letting $\lambda\to0$ gives $a_0=b_0$; subtracting $a_0$ from both sides, dividing by $\lambda$, and again letting $\lambda\to0$ gives $a_1=b_1$, and so on; repeating this, we can show the equalities in turn. This says the same thing as the fact that the coefficients of a Taylor expansion are uniquely determined by the derivatives $f^{(k)}(0)/k!$ (University Mathematics, Chapter 4, Section 4.2). The only difference here is that the coefficients are wave functions (vectors) rather than numbers, but the same argument holds.

Comparing the coefficients of $\lambda^0$, $\lambda^1$, and $\lambda^2$, we therefore obtain the following three equations.

\begin{align} \hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(0)}&=E_n^{(0)}\psi_n^{(0)} \label{eq:27-order0}\\ \hat{V}\psi_n^{(0)}+\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(1)}&=E_n^{(1)}\psi_n^{(0)}+E_n^{(0)}\psi_n^{(1)} \label{eq:27-order1}\\ \hat{V}\psi_n^{(1)}+\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(2)}&=E_n^{(2)}\psi_n^{(0)}+E_n^{(1)}\psi_n^{(1)}+E_n^{(0)}\psi_n^{(2)} \label{eq:27-order2} \end{align}

Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order0} is the unperturbed term ($\lambda^0$), which is just the Schrödinger equation of the “solved problem” given at the start. Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order1} is the equation that determines the first-order perturbation term ($\lambda^1$), and Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order2} is the equation that determines the second-order perturbation term ($\lambda^2$). The unknowns are $E_n^{(1)}$ and $\psi_n^{(1)}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order1}, and $E_n^{(2)}$ and $\psi_n^{(2)}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order2}; the structure is staircase-like, in that the equation of order $2$ can be solved once the result of order $1$ is used. In the next section, Section 27.3, we solve Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order1}, and in Section 27.5 we solve Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order2}.

27.2.3 The general $k$th-order equation and intermediate normalization

Carrying out the same operation on the coefficients of $\lambda^k$ gives the equation of any order. The coefficient of $\lambda^k$ is $\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(k)}+\hat{V}\psi_n^{(k-1)}$ on the left-hand side and $\sum_{j=0}^{k}E_n^{(j)}\psi_n^{(k-j)}$ on the right-hand side. Moving the $j=0$ term $E_n^{(0)}\psi_n^{(k)}$ of the right-hand side to the left-hand side gives the following formula.

Formula 27.1: The $k$th-order perturbation equation ($k\ge1$)

\begin{equation} \bigl(\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)\psi_n^{(k)}=-\hat{V}\psi_n^{(k-1)}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}E_n^{(j)}\psi_n^{(k-j)} \label{eq:27-orderk} \end{equation}

For $k=1$ this coincides with Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order1}, and for $k=2$ with Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order2}. The operator $\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)}$ on the left-hand side gives 0 when it is applied to $\psi_n^{(0)}$.

There is one point to note here. Since the left-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:27-orderk} has the form $(\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)})\psi_n^{(k)}$, adding a constant multiple of $\psi_n^{(0)}$ to $\psi_n^{(k)}$ does not change the left-hand side (because $(\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)})\psi_n^{(0)}=0$). That is, the equations alone do not determine the $\psi_n^{(0)}$ component of $\psi_n^{(k)}$. This corresponds to the fact that the Schrödinger equation is linear homogeneous, so the magnitude (and phase) of the wave function is not determined by the equation alone (Section 10.2). We therefore fix this degree of freedom by the following convention.

Definition 27.2: Intermediate normalization

We agree that all corrections with $k\ge1$ have no $\psi_n^{(0)}$ component:

\begin{equation} \braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(k)}}=0\qquad(k=1,2,3,\dots) \label{eq:27-imnorm} \end{equation}

This is called intermediate normalization. In this case $\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_n}=1$.

Under intermediate normalization, the $n$th coefficient in the expansion $\psi_n^{(k)}=\sum_ic_i^{(k)}\psi_i^{(0)}$ is 0. That is, the correction $\psi_n^{(k)}$ is built only from a superposition of states other than $\psi_n^{(0)}$. Under this convention, $\psi_n$ does not necessarily satisfy $\braket{\psi_n}{\psi_n}=1$ (we estimate its magnitude as $1+O(\lambda^2)$ at the end of Section 27.3, and renormalize at the end if necessary).

Using intermediate normalization, we obtain a concise formula that gives the energy at each order. Multiplying both sides of Eq. \eqref{eq:27-orderk} from the left by $\bra{\psi_n^{(0)}}$, the left-hand side becomes $0$ because $\bra{\psi_n^{(0)}}(\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)})=0$ (Hermiticity of $\hat{H}_0$). The right-hand side is $-\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(k-1)}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}E_n^{(j)}\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(k-j)}}$, and by intermediate normalization $\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(k-j)}}$ survives only for $j=k$ (where $\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}=1$). Therefore

\begin{equation} E_n^{(k)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(k-1)}}\qquad(k\ge1) \label{eq:27-Ek} \end{equation}

For $k=1$, $E_n^{(1)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}$, which agrees with the formula we derive directly in Section 27.3. For $k=2$, $E_n^{(2)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(1)}}$, and for $k=3$, $E_n^{(3)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(2)}}$. Eq. \eqref{eq:27-Ek} will be used for the third-order energy in Section 27.5.

Caution: This expansion is formal

Whether the series in Eqs. \eqref{eq:27-psi-exp} and \eqref{eq:27-E-exp} converge is not guaranteed at all by the discussion so far. For finite $\lambda$, the series may fail to converge and may diverge. Even so, perturbation theory is useful because, if $\lambda$ is sufficiently small, the first few terms give the true value to good accuracy (an asymptotic series). We examine the question of convergence in Section 27.7, using an exactly solvable two-level model.

Example 27.3: Solving the order-by-order equations with a $2\times2$ matrix

A system with two states (we number the lower state 1 and the upper state 2) is represented by the matrices $\hat{H}_0=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&\Delta\end{pmatrix}$ and $\hat{V}=\begin{pmatrix}0&V\\V&0\end{pmatrix}$ ($\Delta=0.10\ \mathrm{eV}$, $V=0.02\ \mathrm{eV}$). The unperturbed states are $\psi_1^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ ($E_1^{(0)}=0$) and $\psi_2^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$ ($E_2^{(0)}=\Delta$). Under intermediate normalization, find $E_1^{(1)}$, $\psi_1^{(1)}$, $E_1^{(2)}$, and $\psi_1^{(2)}$ of the lower state from the order-by-order equations \eqref{eq:27-order1} and \eqref{eq:27-order2}. Also compare the results with the $\lambda$ expansion of the exact solution $E_-=\bigl(\Delta-\sqrt{\Delta^2+4\lambda^2V^2}\bigr)/2$.

Solution. First order. Let $\psi_1^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\end{pmatrix}$. Intermediate normalization $\braket{\psi_1^{(0)}}{\psi_1^{(1)}}=a_1=0$ gives $\psi_1^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\a_2\end{pmatrix}$. The terms of Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order1} are $\hat{V}\psi_1^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\V\end{pmatrix}$, $\hat{H}_0\psi_1^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\\Delta a_2\end{pmatrix}$, $E_1^{(1)}\psi_1^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}E_1^{(1)}\\0\end{pmatrix}$, and $E_1^{(0)}\psi_1^{(1)}=0$, so the first component gives $0=E_1^{(1)}$ and the second component gives $V+\Delta a_2=0$. Hence

$$ E_1^{(1)}=0,\qquad a_2=-\frac{V}{\Delta}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\psi_1^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\-V/\Delta\end{pmatrix} $$

These are the results.

Second order. Let $\psi_1^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\b_2\end{pmatrix}$ (the first component is 0 by intermediate normalization). The terms of Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order2} are $\hat{V}\psi_1^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}V\cdot(-V/\Delta)\\0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-V^2/\Delta\\0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\hat{H}_0\psi_1^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\\Delta b_2\end{pmatrix}$, and the right-hand side is $E_1^{(2)}\psi_1^{(0)}+E_1^{(1)}\psi_1^{(1)}+E_1^{(0)}\psi_1^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}E_1^{(2)}\\0\end{pmatrix}$ (we used $E_1^{(1)}=E_1^{(0)}=0$). The first component gives $-V^2/\Delta=E_1^{(2)}$, and the second component gives $\Delta b_2=0$. Hence

$$ E_1^{(2)}=-\frac{V^2}{\Delta}=-\frac{(0.02\ \mathrm{eV})^2}{0.10\ \mathrm{eV}}=-4.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}=-4.0\ \mathrm{meV},\qquad\psi_1^{(2)}=0 $$

These are the results.

Comparison with the exact solution. Using $\sqrt{1+x}=1+\dfrac{x}{2}-\dfrac{x^2}{8}+\cdots$ with $x=4\lambda^2V^2/\Delta^2$, we have $\sqrt{\Delta^2+4\lambda^2V^2}=\Delta\Bigl(1+\dfrac{2\lambda^2V^2}{\Delta^2}-\dfrac{2\lambda^4V^4}{\Delta^4}+\cdots\Bigr)$. Therefore

$$ E_-=\frac{\Delta}{2}\Bigl(-\frac{2\lambda^2V^2}{\Delta^2}+\frac{2\lambda^4V^4}{\Delta^4}-\cdots\Bigr)=-\frac{V^2}{\Delta}\lambda^2+\frac{V^4}{\Delta^3}\lambda^4-\cdots $$

In this expansion the first-order term in $\lambda$ does not appear ($E_1^{(1)}=0$), and the coefficient of $\lambda^2$ agrees with $-V^2/\Delta=E_1^{(2)}$. Note also that in this model the eigenvalues of $\hat{H}(\lambda)$ do not change under $\lambda\to-\lambda$ (the sign can be reversed by $\sigma_z$), so $E_-$ is an even function of $\lambda$ and all the odd-order terms vanish.

27.3 First-Order Perturbation — Energy and Wave Function

27.3.1 Expanding the first-order correction in a complete set

The equation that determines the first-order perturbation is Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order1}:

$$ \hat{V}\psi_n^{(0)}+\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(1)}=E_n^{(1)}\psi_n^{(0)}+E_n^{(0)}\psi_n^{(1)} $$

We expand the unknown $\psi_n^{(1)}$ in the unperturbed eigenfunctions $\{\psi_i^{(0)}\}$. Since $\{\psi_i^{(0)}\}$ is an orthonormal and complete set of functions (Section 27.1.5), any function can be written in this form:

\begin{equation} \psi_n^{(1)}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_i\,\psi_i^{(0)} \label{eq:27-psi1-exp} \end{equation}

The $c_i$ are the expansion coefficients to be determined. We substitute this into Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order1}. In the term $\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(1)}$ on the left-hand side, we can use $\hat{H}_0\psi_i^{(0)}=E_i^{(0)}\psi_i^{(0)}$ for each term, so

\begin{align*} (\text{LHS})&=\hat{V}\psi_n^{(0)}+\hat{H}_0\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_i\psi_i^{(0)}=\hat{V}\psi_n^{(0)}+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_iE_i^{(0)}\psi_i^{(0)}\\ (\text{RHS})&=E_n^{(1)}\psi_n^{(0)}+E_n^{(0)}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_i\psi_i^{(0)} \end{align*}

Moving the second term on the right-hand side to the left-hand side and combining the sums over $c_i$ into one, we obtain

\begin{equation} \hat{V}\psi_n^{(0)}+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_i\bigl(E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)\psi_i^{(0)}=E_n^{(1)}\psi_n^{(0)} \label{eq:27-first-order-basic} \end{equation}

This is the basic equation that determines both the first-order energy and the wave function. The unknowns are $E_n^{(1)}$ and $\{c_i\}$. Since the equation is an equation between functions, multiplying by a suitable function from the left and integrating (taking an inner product) extracts an equation between numbers.

27.3.2 First-order energy

Multiply both sides of Eq. \eqref{eq:27-first-order-basic} by $\bra{\psi_n^{(0)}}$ from the left (that is, multiply by $\psi_n^{(0)*}$ and integrate over all space).

$$ \mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_i\bigl(E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_i^{(0)}}=E_n^{(1)}\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(0)}} $$

Here we use the orthonormality $\braket{\psi_i^{(0)}}{\psi_j^{(0)}}=\delta_{ij}$. In the sum on the left-hand side only the $i=n$ term remains, and its factor is $E_n^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}=0$, so we have

$$ \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_i\bigl(E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_i^{(0)}}=c_n\bigl(E_n^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)\cdot1=0 $$

The sum thus vanishes completely. The right-hand side is $E_n^{(1)}\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}=E_n^{(1)}\cdot1$. Therefore, we obtain the following result.

Formula 27.2: First-order energy

\begin{equation} E_n^{(1)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}=V_{nn} \label{eq:27-E1} \end{equation}

The first-order energy correction is the expectation value of the perturbation $\hat{V}$ in the unperturbed state $\psi_n^{(0)}$. The correction $\psi_n^{(1)}$ to the wave function (the coefficients $c_i$) does not appear in this expression.

Therefore, the total energy obtained through first order is

\begin{equation} E_n\simeq E_n^{(0)}+\lambda E_n^{(1)}=E_n^{(0)}+\lambda\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}=E_n^{(0)}+\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\lambda\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}} \label{eq:27-Etot1} \end{equation}

The last form can be read as “add the expectation value of the perturbation term to the unperturbed energy.” That the first-order correction to the energy can be found without correcting the wave function (leaving it unperturbed) is a great advantage of perturbation theory.

Intuition: Leave the wave function “as is” and just measure the perturbation

Imagine that the perturbation $\lambda\hat{V}$ is switched on suddenly (without giving the state time to deform). The distribution of the electron $\abs{\psi_n^{(0)}}^2$ is then still the original one. The average of the perturbation energy under that distribution is $\lambda V_{nn}$. In reality the state also deforms a little and the energy is lowered further, but that effect enters the higher-order terms of order $\lambda^2$ and beyond.

Check by the Hellmann–Feynman theorem

Let $E_n(\lambda)$ be an eigenvalue of $\hat{H}(\lambda)=\hat{H}_0+\lambda\hat{V}$, and let $\psi_n(\lambda)$ be the normalized eigenfunction. Differentiating $E_n(\lambda)=\mel{\psi_n}{\hat{H}(\lambda)}{\psi_n}$ with respect to $\lambda$ by the product rule (with $\psi_n'\equiv\dd\psi_n/\dd\lambda$) gives

$$ \frac{\dd E_n}{\dd\lambda}=\mel{\psi_n'}{\hat{H}}{\psi_n}+\mel{\psi_n}{\hat{H}}{\psi_n'}+\mel{\psi_n}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n} =E_n\bigl(\braket{\psi_n'}{\psi_n}+\braket{\psi_n}{\psi_n'}\bigr)+\mel{\psi_n}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n} $$

(We used $\hat{H}\psi_n=E_n\psi_n$ and the Hermiticity of $\hat{H}$.) The expression in parentheses is $\dd\braket{\psi_n}{\psi_n}/\dd\lambda=\dd1/\dd\lambda=0$, so we obtain

$$ \frac{\dd E_n}{\dd\lambda}=\mel{\psi_n(\lambda)}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n(\lambda)} $$

Setting $\lambda=0$ gives $\dd E_n/\dd\lambda|_{\lambda=0}=E_n^{(1)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}$, which agrees with Eq. \eqref{eq:27-E1}. Expanding the right-hand side further in $\lambda$ gives $V_{nn}+2\lambda\,\mathrm{Re}\,\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(1)}}+\cdots$, while the left-hand side is $E_n^{(1)}+2\lambda E_n^{(2)}+\cdots$, so $E_n^{(2)}=\mathrm{Re}\,\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(1)}}$ also follows (the same result as Eq. \eqref{eq:27-Ek}).

∎

Application: Variational upper bound for the ground state

For $n=0$ (the ground state), $E_0^{(0)}+\lambda E_0^{(1)}=\mel{\psi_0^{(0)}}{\hat{H}}{\psi_0^{(0)}}$ is the expectation value of the full Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$ in the trial function $\psi_0^{(0)}$. By the variational principle (Chapter 30), this is greater than or equal to the true ground-state energy. That is, the energy obtained through first order gives an upper bound on the true value for the ground state. The relation between the value $-74.83\ \mathrm{eV}$ from the first-order perturbation treatment of the electron–electron repulsion in the helium atom (Chapter 29) and the nonrelativistic exact value $-2.9037\,E_{\mathrm h}=-79.01\ \mathrm{eV}$ (the experimental value is $-79.005\ \mathrm{eV}$, the sum of the two ionization energies $24.587\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $54.418\ \mathrm{eV}$) is indeed of this kind.

27.3.3 First-order wave function

Next, we find the coefficients $c_k$ of the correction to the wave function. Multiply both sides of Eq. \eqref{eq:27-first-order-basic} by $\bra{\psi_k^{(0)}}$ from the left ($k$ is an arbitrary index).

\begin{equation} \mel{\psi_k^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_i\bigl(E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)\braket{\psi_k^{(0)}}{\psi_i^{(0)}}=E_n^{(1)}\braket{\psi_k^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(0)}} \label{eq:27-project-k} \end{equation}

Here we consider the case $k\ne n$ and the case $k=n$ separately.

[i] For $k\ne n$. By orthonormality, only the $i=k$ term remains in the sum on the left-hand side, and using $\braket{\psi_k^{(0)}}{\psi_k^{(0)}}=1$ it becomes $c_k\bigl(E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)$. The right-hand side is $0$ because $\braket{\psi_k^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}=0$. Hence Eq. \eqref{eq:27-project-k} becomes

$$ V_{kn}+c_k\bigl(E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)=0\qquad\therefore\quad c_k=-\frac{V_{kn}}{E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}}=-\frac{\mel{\psi_k^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}}{E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}} $$

Here we used the fact that the denominator is not 0 ($E_k^{(0)}\ne E_n^{(0)}$). When the levels are degenerate, this division cannot be carried out. We treat the situation in which it breaks down in Chapter 28.

[ii] For $k=n$. The $i=n$ term of the sum in Eq. \eqref{eq:27-project-k} vanishes because its factor is $E_n^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}=0$, so we get $\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}=E_n^{(1)}$, which merely takes us back to Eq. \eqref{eq:27-E1} of the previous subsection. $c_n$ does not appear in any equation and is therefore undetermined. This is exactly the freedom discussed in Section 27.2.3, and we fix it by the intermediate normalization $\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(1)}}=c_n=0$. That is, we omit $i=n$ from the sum in the expansion.

Substituting the above into Eq. \eqref{eq:27-psi1-exp}, we obtain the result for the wave function through first order.

Formula 27.3: First-order wave function

\begin{equation} \psi_n\simeq\psi_n^{(0)}+\lambda\psi_n^{(1)}=\psi_n^{(0)}-\lambda\sum_{i\ne n}\frac{V_{in}}{E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}}\,\psi_i^{(0)}=\psi_n^{(0)}-\sum_{i\ne n}\frac{\mel{\psi_i^{(0)}}{\lambda\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}}{E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}}\,\psi_i^{(0)} \label{eq:27-psi-first} \end{equation}

(Here $\sum_{i\ne n}$ is the sum over all states other than $n$. Intermediate normalization is assumed.)

In Eq. \eqref{eq:27-psi-first} the denominator is arranged as $E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}$, and a minus sign $-$ is attached at the front. Some textbooks write the same equation as $\displaystyle\sum_{i\ne n}\frac{V_{in}}{E_n^{(0)}-E_i^{(0)}}\psi_i^{(0)}$, but this merely absorbs the sign by interchanging the order of the subtraction in the denominator, and it is exactly the same expression. Throughout this chapter, this book uses the form $E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}$ consistently.

27.3.4 How to read the first-order wave function

Formula 27.3 shows that the other states $\psi_i^{(0)}$ mix into the state $\psi_n$ with weight given by the coefficient $\lambda c_i=-\lambda V_{in}/(E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)})$. Let us organize what can be read from this expression.

  • The larger the matrix element, the more strongly the states mix: a state with a larger numerator $V_{in}$ mixes more strongly into $\psi_n$.
  • The closer the levels, the more strongly the states mix (denominator effect): a state with a smaller denominator $E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}$ has a larger mixing weight. States that are close in energy are “easily connected” by the perturbation. As the denominator approaches 0, $c_i\to\infty$ and perturbation theory becomes unusable (degeneracy or quasi-degeneracy; Section 27.7, Chapter 28).
  • Some states do not mix (selection rule): if $V_{in}=0$, that state does not mix in at first order. For example, when $\hat{V}$ is an odd function of the coordinate (such as the electric-field perturbation $e_0F\hat{z}$), if $\psi_i^{(0)}$ and $\psi_n^{(0)}$ have the same parity, the integrand is an odd function and the integral is 0. Therefore, only states of opposite parity mix.
  • Normalization: the square of the norm of $\psi_n=\psi_n^{(0)}+\lambda\psi_n^{(1)}$ is, by the intermediate normalization ($\psi_n^{(1)}$ is orthogonal to $\psi_n^{(0)}$), $\braket{\psi_n}{\psi_n}=1+\lambda^2\sum_{i\ne n}\abs{c_i}^2+O(\lambda^3)$. That is, the deviation from 1 is of order $\lambda^2$, and to first order the state may be regarded as normalized. To normalize it exactly, multiply the whole by $\bigl(1+\lambda^2\sum_{i\ne n}\abs{c_i}^2\bigr)^{-1/2}$. The weight (probability) of the unperturbed state $\psi_n^{(0)}$ then becomes $1-\lambda^2\sum_{i\ne n}\abs{c_i}^2$, and the remaining $\lambda^2\sum\abs{c_i}^2$ is distributed among the other states.

Example 27.4: First-order energy for a uniform perturbation and a $\delta$ function placed at a node

To an infinite well of width $L$ ($0\le x\le L$, eigenfunctions $\psi_n^{(0)}=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)$, $n=1,2,\dots$), add the following perturbations. (1) A uniform $\hat{V}=V_0$ (a constant) over the whole well. (2) $\hat{V}=\lambda_0\,\delta(x-L/2)$ placed at the center of the well ($\lambda_0$ is a constant with the dimension “energy × length”; it is distinct from the perturbation parameter $\lambda$). In each case, find the first-order energy $E_n^{(1)}$ and explain the meaning of the result.

Solution. (1) By Eq. \eqref{eq:27-E1}, $E_n^{(1)}=\int_0^L\psi_n^{(0)}V_0\psi_n^{(0)}\dd x=V_0\int_0^L\bigl(\psi_n^{(0)}\bigr)^2\dd x=V_0$ (normalization). Also, the matrix element for $k\ne n$ is $V_{kn}=V_0\braket{\psi_k^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}=0$ (orthogonality), so $c_k=0$ and the wave function does not change. A perturbation that is a uniform constant over the whole well merely lifts all levels by the same amount $V_0$ and does not change the wave function. This agrees with the fact that when a constant is added to the potential in the Schrödinger equation, only the energy is shifted, by that constant (the exact result for $\lambda=1$ is $E_n=E_n^{(0)}+V_0$, so first order is completely correct).

(2) The $\delta$ function (the Dirac delta function) is the limiting function whose width goes to $0$ and whose height goes to $\infty$ while its area is kept at $1$ (University Mathematics, Chapter 30, Section 30.3). $\lambda_0\,\delta(x-L/2)$ is an idealization of a thin, tall barrier placed at the center of the well. From the property $\int f(x)\delta(x-a)\dd x=f(a)$ of the $\delta$ function (when $a$ lies inside the range of integration), we obtain

$$ E_n^{(1)}=\lambda_0\,\bigl(\psi_n^{(0)}(L/2)\bigr)^2=\lambda_0\cdot\frac{2}{L}\sin^2\frac{n\pi}{2}=\begin{cases}2\lambda_0/L&(n=1,3,5,\dots)\\0&(n=2,4,6,\dots)\end{cases} $$

Here $\sin^2(n\pi/2)$ is $1$ when $n$ is odd and $0$ when it is even. The odd-numbered levels are lifted by the same amount $2\lambda_0/L$, independent of $n$. The even-numbered levels do not move. The even-numbered states have a node at the center of the well, $x=L/2$, where the probability density is $0$, so they do not feel the perturbation placed at the center at all. This property that “a perturbation located at a node has no effect” is a typical example of how the perturbation acts differently on different levels (the mixing of wave functions caused by the $\delta$-function perturbation is the subject of Exercise 27.2).

Example 27.5: First-order perturbation of an anharmonic oscillator — the $x^4$ term

Add $\hat{V}=\hbar\omega(\hat{x}/b)^4$ ($b=\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}$; the perturbation parameter is $\lambda=g$) to a harmonic oscillator (mass $m$, angular frequency $\omega$, $n=0,1,2,\dots$). Find the first-order energy $E_n^{(1)}$ using the ladder operators. For $g=0.01$, find the ground-state energy through first order and compare it with the value $0.50726\,\hbar\omega$ from numerical diagonalization.

Solution. $b=\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}$ is the length of the oscillator, which was written $x_0$ in Chapter 13. In this chapter $x_0$ is used for the shift of the center caused by an electric field (Section 27.6.2), so we write the length of the oscillator as $b$. The position operator can be written in terms of the ladder operators as $\hat{x}=\dfrac{b}{\sqrt2}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)$ (Section 13.8). Hence $(\hat{x}/b)^4=\dfrac14(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)^4$. We use $\hat{a}\ket{n}=\sqrt{n}\ket{n-1}$ and $\hat{a}^\dagger\ket{n}=\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1}$. Expanding $(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)^4$ produces $2^4=16$ products, but the only ones that contribute to the diagonal element $\mel{n}{\cdot}{n}$ are the products with two $\hat{a}$ and two $\hat{a}^\dagger$ (if the numbers do not match, the state index does not return to its original value). We evaluate these six products by acting with the operators in order from the right:

Table 27.3: The six products contributing to the diagonal elements of $(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)^4$
Product (acting from the right)$\mel{n}{\cdot}{n}$
$\hat{a}\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}^\dagger$$(n+1)(n+2)$
$\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger$$(n+1)^2$
$\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}$$n(n+1)$
$\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger$$n(n+1)$
$\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}$$n^2$
$\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}\hat{a}$$n(n-1)$

For example, the first row arises because, after $\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}^\dagger\ket{n}=\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}\ket{n+2}$, acting with $\hat{a}\hat{a}$ gives $\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}\cdot\sqrt{n+2}\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n}$. The sum is $(n^2+3n+2)+(n^2+2n+1)+(n^2+n)+(n^2+n)+n^2+(n^2-n)=6n^2+6n+3$. Therefore we obtain

$$ E_n^{(1)}=\hbar\omega\cdot\frac14\,(6n^2+6n+3)=\frac{3\,(2n^2+2n+1)}{4}\,\hbar\omega $$

(The contribution to the total energy is $\lambda E_n^{(1)}=gE_n^{(1)}$.) For the ground state ($n=0$), $E_0^{(1)}=\tfrac34\hbar\omega$. For $g=0.01$, through first order $E_0\simeq\bigl(\tfrac12+0.01\times\tfrac34\bigr)\hbar\omega=0.5075\,\hbar\omega$. The difference from the numerically diagonalized value $0.50726\,\hbar\omega$ is $2.4\times10^{-4}\hbar\omega$, which agrees with the magnitude of the second- and higher-order corrections (the coefficient of $g^2$ is $-21/8$, and $-\tfrac{21}{8}\times10^{-4}=-2.6\times10^{-4}$). The growth of $E_n^{(1)}$ in proportion to $n^2$ (the higher the level, the larger the effect of the perturbation) is due to the fact that the higher the level, the larger the spread of $x$ and the larger the expectation value of $x^4$. Perturbation theory can be used only as long as, roughly, $g\,n^2\ll1$.

27.4 Application of First-Order Perturbation — A Step Next to the Wall of a Well

27.4.1 Setting up the problem

Let us apply the first-order perturbation formulas (Formulas 27.2 and 27.3) to a problem with an infinite well, whose solution can be written in closed form. We consider a situation in which there is a small step of height $V_0$ and width $\delta$ next to the wall at the bottom of the well. This is a simplified model of a semiconductor quantum well in which the composition at the interface is slightly blurred, so that the potential next to the wall is raised a little.

Illustration 27.1: A step next to the wall of an infinite well

A particle of mass $m$ is placed in the following potential energy $V_{\mathrm w}(x)$.

$$ V_{\mathrm w}(x)=\begin{cases}V_0 & (0\le x\le\delta)\quad\text{provided that }\dfrac{\delta}{L}\ll1\\[1mm] 0 & (\delta\le x\le L)\\[1mm] \infty & (x\lt0,\ L\lt x)\end{cases} $$

Find the energy and the wave function of the particle, including a first-order perturbation calculation.

The shape of the potential is as shown in Figure 27.2. In Sections 27.4.2–27.4.5 below, we solve the problem without skipping any steps. The results are Eqs. \eqref{eq:27-Ewell} and \eqref{eq:27-psi-well}.

27.4.2 The unperturbed problem and the setup of the perturbation

As the solvable problem $\hat{H}_0$, we choose the infinite well without the step. The energies and eigenfunctions (Chapter 10) are, for $n=1,2,3,\dots$,

\begin{equation} E_n^{(0)}=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}\,n^2=\frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2},\qquad \psi_n^{(0)}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\,\sin\frac{n\pi}{L}x\qquad(0\le x\le L) \label{eq:27-well0} \end{equation}

(Outside the well, $\psi_n^{(0)}=0$.) The step is a perturbation $\hat{V}$ added to this potential. The walls ($V=\infty$) are included in $\hat{H}_0$ as they are, and $\hat{V}$ represents only the step inside the well:

$$ \hat{V}(x)=\begin{cases}V_0&(0\le x\le\delta)\\0&(\delta\lt x\le L)\end{cases} $$

In the convention of this chapter, the perturbation term is $\lambda\hat{V}$, that is, $\hat{V}$ multiplied by the perturbation parameter $\lambda$, but in this example we set $\lambda=1$ and omit $\lambda$. The smallness of the perturbation comes not from the magnitude of $V_0$ but from the thinness of the step ($\delta/L\ll1$). The condition for perturbation theory to be usable is that the dimensionless quantity $\varepsilon=\dfrac{2\pi^2}{3}\dfrac{V_0}{E_1}\Bigl(\dfrac{\delta}{L}\Bigr)^3$ (Section 27.4.4), which serves as a measure of smallness, be much smaller than $1$. By Formula 27.2, the first-order energy is $E_n^{(1)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}$, and using the fact that $\hat{V}$ is $0$ outside $0\le x\le\delta$, the range of integration is restricted to $[0,\delta]$:

\begin{equation} E_n^{(1)}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_n^{(0)}\,\hat{V}\,\psi_n^{(0)}\dd x=\int_0^{\delta}\frac{2}{L}\,V_0\,\sin^2\frac{n\pi}{L}x\dd x \label{eq:27-E1int} \end{equation}

27.4.3 Calculating the first-order energy

Derivation: $E_n^{(1)}$ (the integral in Eq. \eqref{eq:27-E1int})

We use the half-angle formula $\sin^2\theta=\dfrac{1-\cos2\theta}{2}$ (High School Mathematics, Chapter 5, Section 5.3) with $\theta=n\pi x/L$.

\begin{align} E_n^{(1)}&=\frac{2V_0}{L}\int_0^{\delta}\frac12\Bigl(1-\cos\frac{2n\pi}{L}x\Bigr)\dd x=\frac{V_0}{L}\Bigl[x-\frac{L}{2n\pi}\sin\frac{2n\pi}{L}x\Bigr]_0^{\delta}\nonumber\\ &=V_0\Bigl[\frac{\delta}{L}-\frac{\sin(2n\pi\delta/L)}{2n\pi}\Bigr] \label{eq:27-E1exact} \end{align}

(We used the fact that the integral of $\cos ax$ is $\sin ax/a$. At the lower limit $x=0$, $\sin0=0$.) This is the exact value of $E_n^{(1)}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:27-E1exact}). Next, we use $\delta/L\ll1$. Letting $\theta\equiv2n\pi\delta/L$, as long as $n\delta/L\ll1$ we have $\theta\ll1$, and $\sin\theta$ can be approximated by the first two terms of its Taylor expansion, $\sin\theta\simeq\theta-\dfrac{\theta^3}{3!}$ (University Mathematics, Chapter 4, Section 4.2).

\begin{align*} E_n^{(1)}&\simeq\frac{V_0}{L}\Bigl[\delta-\frac{L}{2n\pi}\Bigl(\frac{2n\pi}{L}\delta-\frac{1}{3!}\Bigl(\frac{2n\pi}{L}\Bigr)^3\delta^3\Bigr)\Bigr]\\ &=\frac{V_0}{L}\Bigl[\delta-\delta+\frac{1}{6}\Bigl(\frac{2n\pi}{L}\Bigr)^2\delta^3\Bigr]=\frac{V_0}{L}\cdot\frac16\cdot\frac{4n^2\pi^2}{L^2}\,\delta^3=\frac{2\pi^2V_0\,\delta^3}{3L^3}\,n^2 \end{align*}

From the first line to the second, we used $\dfrac{L}{2n\pi}\cdot\dfrac{2n\pi}{L}\delta=\delta$, which cancels the $\delta$ of the first term, and $\dfrac{L}{2n\pi}\Bigl(\dfrac{2n\pi}{L}\Bigr)^3=\Bigl(\dfrac{2n\pi}{L}\Bigr)^2$.

∎

Summarizing the above, the first-order energy and the total energy through first order are as follows.

\begin{equation} E_n^{(1)}=\frac{2\pi^2V_0\,\delta^3}{3L^3}\,n^2,\qquad E_n=E_n^{(0)}+E_n^{(1)}=\Bigl(\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}+\frac{2\pi^2V_0\,\delta^3}{3L^3}\Bigr)n^2 \label{eq:27-Ewell} \end{equation}

As for the dimension, $E_n^{(1)}=V_0\times(\delta/L)^3\times(\text{dimensionless number})$, so it is in J (energy), the same as $V_0$. Both the unperturbed energy $E_n^{(0)}$ and the first-order correction $E_n^{(1)}$ are proportional to $n^2$, so $n^2$ can be factored out of the whole expression. All the levels are lifted to the extent that the step raises the potential.

27.4.4 Why is it proportional to $\delta^3$?

$E_n^{(1)}\propto\delta^3$ can be understood as follows. Near the wall ($0\le x\le\delta$), the wave function becomes $0$ at the wall and can be written $\psi_n^{(0)}\simeq\psi_n^{(0)\prime}(0)\,x$ (proportional to $x$). Since the slope is $\psi_n^{(0)\prime}(0)=\sqrt{2/L}\,n\pi/L$, the probability density of finding the particle in the region of the step is $\abs{\psi_n^{(0)}}^2\simeq\dfrac{2}{L}\Bigl(\dfrac{n\pi}{L}\Bigr)^2x^2$ (proportional to $x^2$). Integrating over the region of the step, and using $\int_0^{\delta}x^2\dd x=\delta^3/3$, we obtain

$$ E_n^{(1)}\simeq V_0\cdot\frac{2}{L}\Bigl(\frac{n\pi}{L}\Bigr)^2\cdot\frac{\delta^3}{3}=\frac{2\pi^2V_0\delta^3n^2}{3L^3} $$

which agrees exactly with Eq. \eqref{eq:27-Ewell}. “The particle is rarely found right next to the wall (the probability density is small because it goes as $x^2$), so a step next to the wall has little effect”—this is the meaning of $\delta^3$, and the factor $n^2$ comes from the square of the slope of the wave function ($\propto n$).

Writing the ratio $E_n^{(1)}/E_n^{(0)}$ using the unperturbed energy for $n=1$, $E_1=\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$, we obtain

\begin{equation} \varepsilon\equiv\frac{E_n^{(1)}}{E_n^{(0)}}=\frac{4mV_0\delta^3}{3\hbar^2L}=\frac{2\pi^2}{3}\,\frac{V_0}{E_1}\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{L}\Bigr)^3 \label{eq:27-eps} \end{equation}

which is a small dimensionless quantity independent of $n$. This $\varepsilon$ serves as a measure of the “smallness” of the first-order perturbation (it also appears in the coefficients of the wave function in the next section). Checking the dimension, $[mV_0\delta^3/(\hbar^2L)]=\mathrm{kg}\cdot\mathrm{J}\cdot\mathrm{m}^3/(\mathrm{J}^2\mathrm{s}^2\cdot\mathrm{m})=\mathrm{kg\,m^2}/(\mathrm{J\,s^2})=1$ ($1\ \mathrm{J}=\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s^2}$), so it is dimensionless.

27.4.5 Calculating the first-order wave function

By Formula 27.3, the first-order correction to the wave function is $\psi_n^{(1)}=-\sum_{k\ne n}\dfrac{V_{kn}}{E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}}\psi_k^{(0)}$ (the states of the well are $k=1,2,3,\dots$; the term with $k=0$ vanishes because $\sin0=0$). What we need are the matrix elements $V_{kn}=\mel{\psi_k^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}$ ($k\ne n$).

Derivation: Matrix element $V_{kn}$ ($k\ne n$)

As in Eq. \eqref{eq:27-E1int}, the range of integration is $[0,\delta]$.

$$ V_{kn}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_k^{(0)}\,\hat{V}\,\psi_n^{(0)}\dd x=\int_0^{\delta}\frac{2V_0}{L}\sin\frac{k\pi}{L}x\,\sin\frac{n\pi}{L}x\dd x $$

We convert the product $\sin\sin$ into a sum with the product-to-sum formula $\sin\alpha\sin\beta=\dfrac12\bigl[\cos(\alpha-\beta)-\cos(\alpha+\beta)\bigr]$ (High School Mathematics, Chapter 5, Section 5.5).

\begin{align} V_{kn}&=\frac{2V_0}{L}\int_0^{\delta}\frac12\Bigl[\cos\frac{(k-n)\pi}{L}x-\cos\frac{(k+n)\pi}{L}x\Bigr]\dd x\nonumber\\ &=\frac{V_0}{L}\Bigl[\frac{L}{(k-n)\pi}\sin\frac{(k-n)\pi}{L}x-\frac{L}{(k+n)\pi}\sin\frac{(k+n)\pi}{L}x\Bigr]_0^{\delta}\nonumber\\ &=V_0\Bigl[\frac{\sin\bigl((k-n)\pi\delta/L\bigr)}{(k-n)\pi}-\frac{\sin\bigl((k+n)\pi\delta/L\bigr)}{(k+n)\pi}\Bigr] \label{eq:27-Vkn-exact} \end{align}

(Since $k\ne n$, we have $k-n\ne0$ and $k+n\ne0$. At the lower limit $x=0$, $\sin0=0$.) This is the exact value of $V_{kn}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:27-Vkn-exact}). Next, assuming $(k\pm n)\pi\delta/L\ll1$, we use $\sin\theta\simeq\theta-\theta^3/6$. For $\theta=(k\pm n)\pi\delta/L$,

$$ \frac{\sin\theta}{(k\pm n)\pi}\simeq\frac{1}{(k\pm n)\pi}\Bigl[\frac{(k\pm n)\pi\delta}{L}-\frac16\Bigl(\frac{(k\pm n)\pi\delta}{L}\Bigr)^3\Bigr]=\frac{\delta}{L}-\frac16\,\frac{\pi^2(k\pm n)^2}{L^3}\,\delta^3 $$

holds. Substituting this into $V_{kn}$, the first terms ($\delta/L$) cancel each other, and the result is

\begin{align*} V_{kn}&\simeq V_0\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{L}-\frac{\pi^2(k-n)^2}{6L^3}\delta^3\Bigr)-\Bigl(\frac{\delta}{L}-\frac{\pi^2(k+n)^2}{6L^3}\delta^3\Bigr)\Bigr] =\frac{\pi^2V_0\delta^3}{6L^3}\bigl[(k+n)^2-(k-n)^2\bigr]\\ &=\frac{\pi^2V_0\delta^3}{6L^3}\cdot4nk=\frac{2\pi^2V_0\,\delta^3}{3L^3}\,nk \end{align*}

(here $(k+n)^2-(k-n)^2=4nk$).

∎

Setting $k=n$ gives $\dfrac{2\pi^2V_0\delta^3}{3L^3}n^2$, which agrees with the first-order energy in Eq. \eqref{eq:27-Ewell} (the diagonal element is $E_n^{(1)}$). Also, since $\dfrac{2\pi^2V_0\delta^3}{3L^3}=\varepsilon E_1$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:27-eps}), we can write

\begin{equation} V_{kn}\simeq\varepsilon E_1\,nk \label{eq:27-Vkn-approx} \end{equation}

The level spacing is $E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}=E_1(k^2-n^2)$, so the expansion coefficient in Formula 27.3 is

$$ c_k=-\frac{V_{kn}}{E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}}=-\frac{\varepsilon E_1\,nk}{E_1(k^2-n^2)}=\varepsilon\,\frac{nk}{n^2-k^2} $$

Rewriting this with $\varepsilon=\dfrac{4mV_0\delta^3}{3\hbar^2L}$, we obtain the following result.

Formula 27.4: Wave function of a well with a step next to the wall (first-order perturbation)

\begin{equation} \psi_n(x)\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\frac{n\pi}{L}x+\frac{4mV_0\delta^3}{3\hbar^2L}\sum_{k\ne n}^{\infty}\frac{nk}{n^2-k^2}\,\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\frac{k\pi}{L}x \label{eq:27-psi-well} \end{equation}

($k=1,2,3,\dots$ with $k\ne n$. The quantity $\varepsilon=4mV_0\delta^3/(3\hbar^2L)$ determines the magnitude of the expansion.)

Caution: The sign of the numerator, and the order of $n$ in the coefficient

The fraction in Eq. \eqref{eq:27-psi-well} is $\dfrac{nk}{n^2-k^2}$, whose denominator is in the reverse order of $E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\propto k^2-n^2$ in Formula 27.3. This is the result of absorbing the leading sign $-$ of Formula 27.3 into the denominator; $c_k\lt0$ for $k\gt n$ and $c_k\gt0$ for $k\lt n$. Since this is a place where it is easy to get the sign wrong along the way, it is a good idea to check that $V_{kn}\gt0$ (the step is a perturbation that raises the potential, which is consistent with the diagonal element at $k=n$ being positive).

From this result we can read off the effect of the perturbation. All the states with $k\ne n$ are mixed in (the step breaks the symmetry of the well, so there is no parity selection rule), and the closer $k$ is to $n$, and also the larger $k$ is (the factor $nk$), the larger the absolute value of the coefficient. For large $k$, however, $c_k\simeq-\varepsilon n/k$ decays only slowly, as $1/k$, and this approximation holds only for $(k\pm n)\pi\delta/L\ll1$, that is, for $k\ll L/(\pi\delta)$. For larger $k$ we must use the exact matrix element (Eq. \eqref{eq:27-Vkn-exact}). The exact $V_{kn}$ decays in proportion to $1/k$ for $k\gg L/(\pi\delta)$, so $c_k\propto1/k^3$ and the sum converges quickly.

27.4.7 Limitations of the approximation — how far can the Taylor expansion be used?

The expression $E_n^{(1)}\simeq2\pi^2V_0\delta^3n^2/(3L^3)$ derived in Section 27.4.3 is an approximation that uses the first two terms of the expansion of $\sin\theta$. With $\theta=2n\pi\delta/L$, from the expression $\sin\theta=\theta-\theta^3/6+\theta^5/120-\cdots$, which keeps one more term, the exact Eq. \eqref{eq:27-E1exact} becomes

$$ E_n^{(1)}=\frac{V_0}{L}\cdot\frac{L}{2n\pi}\Bigl(\frac{\theta^3}{6}-\frac{\theta^5}{120}+\cdots\Bigr)=\frac{2\pi^2V_0\delta^3n^2}{3L^3}\Bigl(1-\frac{\theta^2}{20}+\cdots\Bigr) $$

That is, the relative error of the approximate formula is of the order of $\theta^2/20$. For $n=1$ and $\delta/L=0.05$ we have $\theta=0.314$ and the error is only $0.5$%, but as $n$ increases the error grows as $n^2$. Example 27.6: Interface layer in a GaAs/AlGaAs quantum well

An electron (effective mass $m^*=0.067\,m_{\mathrm e}$) is confined in a GaAs quantum well of width $L=10\ \mathrm{nm}$, approximated as an infinite well. At the left interface, a thin layer of barrier material of thickness $\delta=0.5\ \mathrm{nm}$ and height $V_0=0.30\ \mathrm{eV}$ has seeped in ($V_0$ and $\delta$ are assumed (illustrative) values for the explanation, not the values of a specific real material; the conduction-band step of GaAs/Al$_x$Ga$_{1-x}$As ($x\approx0.3$) is about $0.25\ \mathrm{eV}$, and for AlAs it is about $1\ \mathrm{eV}$). (1) Find $E_1$ of the well without the step. (2) Find the dimensionless step parameter $\varepsilon$ and the first-order energy $E_1^{(1)}$. (3) Show that $E_n^{(1)}/E_n^{(0)}$ is independent of $n$. (4) Find the admixture $c_2$ of $\psi_2^{(0)}$ in the wave function.

Solution. (1) $E_1=\dfrac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m^*L^2}$. With $\hbar=1.0546\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$, $m^*=0.067\times9.109\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}=6.103\times10^{-32}\ \mathrm{kg}$, and $L=10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m}$, we have $E_1=\dfrac{(9.870)(1.112\times10^{-68})}{2(6.103\times10^{-32})(10^{-16})}\ \mathrm{J}=8.99\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{J}=56.1\ \mathrm{meV}$ ($1\ \mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$).

(2) By Eq. \eqref{eq:27-eps}, $\dfrac{V_0}{E_1}=\dfrac{300\ \mathrm{meV}}{56.12\ \mathrm{meV}}=5.345$ and $\Bigl(\dfrac{\delta}{L}\Bigr)^3=(0.05)^3=1.25\times10^{-4}$, so

$$ \varepsilon=\frac{2\pi^2}{3}\times5.345\times1.25\times10^{-4}=6.580\times5.345\times1.25\times10^{-4}=4.40\times10^{-3} $$

The first-order energy is then $E_1^{(1)}=\varepsilon E_1=4.40\times10^{-3}\times56.12\ \mathrm{meV}=0.247\ \mathrm{meV}$.

(3) Both $E_n^{(0)}$ and $E_n^{(1)}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:27-Ewell} are proportional to $n^2$, so $E_n^{(1)}/E_n^{(0)}=\varepsilon=4.4\times10^{-3}$ is independent of $n$. Therefore $E_2^{(1)}=4\times0.247=0.99\ \mathrm{meV}$ and $E_3^{(1)}=9\times0.247=2.2\ \mathrm{meV}$. Note that with the exact integral that does not use the Taylor approximation (Eq. \eqref{eq:27-E1exact}), $E_n^{(1)}/E_n^{(0)}=4.375\times10^{-3}$ ($n=1$), $4.310\times10^{-3}$ ($n=2$), and $4.205\times10^{-3}$ ($n=3$); the deviation from the approximation is larger for higher levels, but even for $n=3$ it is within 5%.

(4) For $n=1$ and $k=2$, $\dfrac{nk}{n^2-k^2}=\dfrac{2}{1-4}=-\dfrac23$, so $c_2=-\dfrac23\varepsilon=-2.9\times10^{-3}$. The first excited state is mixed into the unperturbed ground state with an amplitude of $-2.9\times10^{-3}$ (a probability of $8.6\times10^{-6}$). This means that the wave function hardly changes and only the energy rises, by $0.25\ \mathrm{meV}$. Note also that when the matrix is diagonalized exactly, $E_1=1.00417\,E_1^{(0)}$ ($+0.234\ \mathrm{meV}$), and most of the difference of $-0.013\ \mathrm{meV}$ from the first-order value $+0.247\ \mathrm{meV}$ is the contribution of second-order perturbation ($-0.012\ \mathrm{meV}$, Exercise 27.8).

Application: Interface blurring and impurity layers in quantum wells

In real GaAs/AlGaAs quantum wells, it is unavoidable that the composition at an interface “blurs” over a few atomic layers and that impurity layers are incorporated during growth. These are perturbations of the same form as a small step next to the wall of the well, and since the shift of the levels is proportional to $\delta^3$ (the cube of the thickness of the step), it is as small as a few hundred $\mu\mathrm{eV}$ for a layer about $0.5\ \mathrm{nm}$ thick. Yet this magnitude cannot be ignored in interpreting the linewidth of the emission spectrum (a few meV) and the fine structure of optical transitions. The first-order perturbation formula is a powerful tool that lets us estimate such “effects of small imperfections on the levels” immediately, without numerical calculation. Note also that the wavelength of the emitted light is determined by $\hbar\omega=E_{\mathrm g}+E_1^{\mathrm e}+E_1^{\mathrm h}$ ($E_{\mathrm g}$ is the band gap), so a level shift of $0.25\ \mathrm{meV}$ is only about $10^{-4}$ of the photon energy of $1.5\ \mathrm{eV}$.

27.5 Second-Order Perturbation — Level Repulsion and the Lowering of the Ground State

27.5.1 Expanding the second-order wave function and setting up the equation

To solve the second-order perturbation equation, Eq. \eqref{eq:27-order2}

$$ \hat{V}\psi_n^{(1)}+\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(2)}=E_n^{(2)}\psi_n^{(0)}+E_n^{(1)}\psi_n^{(1)}+E_n^{(0)}\psi_n^{(2)} $$

we follow the same procedure by which we solved first-order perturbation in Section 27.3. This time we again expand the second-order correction $\psi_n^{(2)}$ in the complete set $\{\psi_i^{(0)}\}$:

\begin{equation} \psi_n^{(2)}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}d_i\,\psi_i^{(0)} \label{eq:27-psi2-exp} \end{equation}

Here $d_i$ is the second-order expansion coefficient. The first-order correction $\psi_n^{(1)}=\sum_ic_i\psi_i^{(0)}$ on the right-hand side was found in the previous section (by intermediate normalization $c_n=0$, and $c_i=-V_{in}/(E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)})$ for $i\ne n$). We also use the first-order energy $E_n^{(1)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}=V_{nn}$ found in the previous section. Writing out the left-hand and right-hand sides separately, we have

\begin{align*} (\text{LHS})&=\hat{V}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_i\psi_i^{(0)}+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}d_iE_i^{(0)}\psi_i^{(0)}\\ (\text{RHS})&=E_n^{(2)}\psi_n^{(0)}+V_{nn}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_i\psi_i^{(0)}+E_n^{(0)}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}d_i\psi_i^{(0)} \end{align*}

(on the left-hand side we used $\hat{H}_0\psi_i^{(0)}=E_i^{(0)}\psi_i^{(0)}$). Moving the second and third terms of the right-hand side to the left-hand side and collecting terms, we obtain the following equation.

\begin{equation} \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_i\bigl(\hat{V}-V_{nn}\bigr)\psi_i^{(0)}+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}d_i\bigl(E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)\psi_i^{(0)}=E_n^{(2)}\psi_n^{(0)} \label{eq:27-second-order-basic} \end{equation}

This is the basic second-order equation, corresponding to the first-order Eq. \eqref{eq:27-first-order-basic}. Here too, we multiply by a suitable function from the left to extract an equation among numbers.

27.5.2 The second-order energy

Multiply both sides of Eq. \eqref{eq:27-second-order-basic} from the left by $\bra{\psi_n^{(0)}}$.

$$ \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_i\Bigl(\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_i^{(0)}}-V_{nn}\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_i^{(0)}}\Bigr)+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}d_i\bigl(E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_i^{(0)}}=E_n^{(2)}\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(0)}} $$

Here we use the orthonormality $\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_i^{(0)}}=\delta_{ni}$. In the second sum on the left-hand side only the $i=n$ term survives, and it vanishes because of the factor $E_n^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}=0$. In the first sum, the $i=n$ term vanishes because $c_n(V_{nn}-V_{nn})=0$ (with intermediate normalization, $c_n=0$ as well), so only the terms with $i\ne n$ remain. The right-hand side is $E_n^{(2)}\cdot1$. Hence we obtain

$$ E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{i\ne n}c_i\,V_{ni} $$

Substituting $c_i=-V_{in}/(E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)})$ and using $V_{ni}=V_{in}^*$ (the Hermiticity of $\hat{V}$), we find $V_{ni}V_{in}=\abs{V_{in}}^2$. In this way we obtain the formula for the second-order energy.

Formula 27.5: Second-order energy

\begin{equation} E_n^{(2)}=-\sum_{i\ne n}\frac{\abs{V_{in}}^2}{E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}}=\sum_{i\ne n}\frac{\abs{\mel{\psi_i^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}}^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_i^{(0)}} \label{eq:27-E2} \end{equation}

The second-order energy correction is a sum over all states $i$ other than $n$; the numerator is the square of the absolute value of the matrix element ($\ge0$), and the denominator is the level spacing.

This result is derived in the same way as $E_n^{(2)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(1)}}=\sum_ic_iV_{ni}$, which is obtained by setting $k=2$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:27-Ek}.

27.5.3 The second-order wave function

Multiply both sides of Eq. \eqref{eq:27-second-order-basic} from the left by $\bra{\psi_k^{(0)}}$ ($k\ne n$). From $\braket{\psi_k^{(0)}}{\psi_i^{(0)}}=\delta_{ki}$ and $\braket{\psi_k^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}=0$, we get

$$ \sum_{i\ne n}c_i\bigl(V_{ki}-V_{nn}\delta_{ki}\bigr)+d_k\bigl(E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)=0 $$

In the sum in the first term, the $V_{nn}\delta_{ki}$ part keeps only $i=k$ and gives $V_{nn}c_k$, so

$$ d_k\bigl(E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)=-\sum_{i\ne n}V_{ki}c_i+V_{nn}c_k $$

Substituting $c_i=-V_{in}/(E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)})$ and $c_k=-V_{kn}/(E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)})$ and dividing both sides by $E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}$, we obtain the following formula ($k\ne n$; $d_n$ is $0$ by intermediate normalization).

Formula 27.6: Coefficients of the second-order wave function

\begin{equation} d_k=\sum_{i\ne n}\frac{V_{ki}V_{in}}{\bigl(E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)\bigl(E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)}-\frac{V_{nn}V_{kn}}{\bigl(E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)^2}\qquad(k\ne n) \label{eq:27-dk} \end{equation}

We have $\psi_n^{(2)}=\sum_{k\ne n}d_k\psi_k^{(0)}$.

The first term corresponds to a “two-step transition” $n\to i\to k$. First the perturbation connects the state $\psi_n^{(0)}$ to $\psi_i^{(0)}$ ($V_{in}$), and then the perturbation once more connects $\psi_i^{(0)}$ to $\psi_k^{(0)}$ ($V_{ki}$); each step brings a denominator. The second term is a correction that is needed because the first-order energy $V_{nn}$ shifts the levels uniformly. At third order and higher, the same structure continues: each additional application of the perturbation adds one matrix element and one denominator (see the next subsection).

27.5.4 The third-order energy and the $2n+1$ theorem

The third-order energy is found in the same way. Setting $k=3$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:27-Ek} gives $E_n^{(3)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(2)}}=\sum_{k\ne n}V_{nk}d_k$. Substituting $d_k$ from Formula 27.6 and rewriting the denominators in the form $E_n^{(0)}-E_k^{(0)}$ (using $(E_k-E_n)(E_i-E_n)=(E_n-E_k)(E_n-E_i)$ and $(E_k-E_n)^2=(E_n-E_k)^2$), we obtain the following formula.

Formula 27.7: Third-order energy

\begin{equation} E_n^{(3)}=\sum_{k\ne n}\sum_{i\ne n}\frac{V_{nk}V_{ki}V_{in}}{\bigl(E_n^{(0)}-E_k^{(0)}\bigr)\bigl(E_n^{(0)}-E_i^{(0)}\bigr)}-V_{nn}\sum_{k\ne n}\frac{\abs{V_{kn}}^2}{\bigl(E_n^{(0)}-E_k^{(0)}\bigr)^2} \label{eq:27-E3} \end{equation}

Incidentally, is the second-order wave function $\psi_n^{(2)}$ (that is, $d_k$) really needed to find the third-order energy? In fact, $E_n^{(3)}$ is determined by the first-order wave function $\psi_n^{(1)}$ alone.

Derivation: Writing $E_n^{(3)}$ with the first-order wave function alone

Write the first- and second-order equations with $\bigl(\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)$ collected on the left-hand side (Eq. \eqref{eq:27-orderk} with $k=1,2$):

\begin{align*} \bigl(\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)\psi_n^{(1)}&=\bigl(E_n^{(1)}-\hat{V}\bigr)\psi_n^{(0)}\\ \bigl(\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)\psi_n^{(2)}&=\bigl(E_n^{(1)}-\hat{V}\bigr)\psi_n^{(1)}+E_n^{(2)}\psi_n^{(0)} \end{align*}

Multiply the second equation from the left by $\bra{\psi_n^{(1)}}$. By the Hermiticity of $\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)}$, the left-hand side can be moved over as $\bra{\psi_n^{(1)}}\bigl(\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)}\bigr)\ket{\psi_n^{(2)}}=\braket{(\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)})\psi_n^{(1)}}{\psi_n^{(2)}}$, and using the first equation we obtain $\braket{(E_n^{(1)}-\hat{V})\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(2)}}=E_n^{(1)}\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(2)}}-\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(2)}}=0-E_n^{(3)}$ (we used the intermediate normalization $\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(2)}}=0$ and Eq. \eqref{eq:27-Ek} with $k=3$). The right-hand side is $\mel{\psi_n^{(1)}}{E_n^{(1)}-\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(1)}}+E_n^{(2)}\braket{\psi_n^{(1)}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}$, and the last term is $0$. Therefore

\begin{equation} E_n^{(3)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(1)}}{\hat{V}-E_n^{(1)}}{\psi_n^{(1)}}=\sum_{k,i\ne n}c_k^*\,V_{ki}\,c_i-V_{nn}\sum_{k\ne n}\abs{c_k}^2 \label{eq:27-E3-psi1} \end{equation}

Substituting $c_k=-V_{kn}/(E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)})$ reproduces Eq. \eqref{eq:27-E3}.

∎

Theorem 27.1: Wigner's $2n+1$ theorem

If the wave functions $\psi^{(0)},\psi^{(1)},\dots,\psi^{(p)}$ of the perturbation through order $p$ are known, the energy is determined through order $2p+1$. (The “$n$” in the name of the theorem denotes the order of the perturbation. Here, so that it is not confused with the state number $n$, we have written the order as $p$.)

The derivation above is the case $p=1$ of this theorem (the first-order wave function determines the energy through third order). The case $p=0$ is the fact that the unperturbed wave function alone determines the first-order energy (Formula 27.2). We outline the proof for general $p$. Multiply the $k$th-order equation (Eq. \eqref{eq:27-orderk}) from the left by $\bra{\psi_n^{(j)}}$ ($j\le p$), and repeat the operation of moving $\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)}$ from one side to the other by Hermiticity, just as in the derivation above ($p=1$). Then the high-order wave function $\psi_n^{(2p)}$ in the energy of order $2p+1$, $E_n^{(2p+1)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(2p)}}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:27-Ek}), is replaced by products of wave functions of order $p$ and lower. The result, under intermediate normalization, is

$$ E_n^{(2p+1)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(p)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(p)}}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{l=1}^{p}E_n^{(2p+1-j-l)}\braket{\psi_n^{(j)}}{\psi_n^{(l)}} $$

and only the wave functions up to $\psi_n^{(p)}$ and the energies of order $2p$ and lower appear on the right-hand side (for $p=1$ it agrees with Eq. \eqref{eq:27-E3-psi1}). This theorem has the practical advantage that the third-order energy can be obtained without computing the second-order wave function, and it is the basis for greatly reducing the computational cost of variational perturbation theory (the Hylleraas method) and of perturbation theory for many-electron systems (Møller–Plesset perturbation theory and others).

27.5.5 Level repulsion

Let us look at the sign in Formula 27.5.

$$ E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{i\ne n}\frac{\abs{V_{in}}^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_i^{(0)}} $$

The numerator $\abs{V_{in}}^2$ is always greater than or equal to $0$, so the sign of each term is determined by the sign of the denominator $E_n^{(0)}-E_i^{(0)}$.

  • When state $i$ lies above state $n$ ($E_i^{(0)}\gt E_n^{(0)}$), the term is negative. State $n$ is pushed down.
  • When state $i$ lies below state $n$ ($E_i^{(0)}\lt E_n^{(0)}$), the term is positive. State $n$ is pushed up.

In other words, two levels connected by the perturbation move away from each other. This is called level repulsion. The strength of the repulsion is proportional to the square of the matrix element and inversely proportional to the level spacing. The closer the levels, the more strongly they repel. Figure 27.5 shows schematically the case of two levels and the case of the middle one of three levels.

In a two-level system, we can check the result against the exact one. The eigenvalues of $\hat{H}=\begin{pmatrix}E_1&\lambda V\\\lambda V&E_2\end{pmatrix}$ (with $E_2-E_1=\Delta\gt0$) are $E_\mp=\dfrac{E_1+E_2}{2}\mp\sqrt{\dfrac{\Delta^2}{4}+\lambda^2V^2}$. Assuming that $\lambda$ is small and expanding $\sqrt{\Delta^2/4+\lambda^2V^2}=\dfrac{\Delta}{2}\Bigl(1+\dfrac{2\lambda^2V^2}{\Delta^2}+\cdots\Bigr)$, we obtain

$$ E_1(\lambda)=E_1-\frac{\lambda^2V^2}{\Delta}+\cdots,\qquad E_2(\lambda)=E_2+\frac{\lambda^2V^2}{\Delta}+\cdots $$

so the second-order energies $E_1^{(2)}=-V^2/\Delta$ and $E_2^{(2)}=+V^2/\Delta$ agree with Formula 27.5 ($E_1^{(2)}=\abs{V_{21}}^2/(E_1-E_2)=-V^2/\Delta$, $E_2^{(2)}=\abs{V_{12}}^2/(E_2-E_1)=+V^2/\Delta$). The spacing between the two levels widens from $\Delta$ to $\sqrt{\Delta^2+4\lambda^2V^2}$.

This exact expression remains valid even for small $\Delta$. If we move $E_2^{(0)}$ externally to make $\Delta$ smaller, the second-order term $\lambda^2V^2/\Delta$ of perturbation theory grows large and the formula becomes unusable (we must have $\lambda V\lesssim\Delta/2$). The two exact levels, however, do not cross even at $\Delta=0$; they move apart while keeping the minimum spacing $2\lambda\abs{V}$. This is called an avoided crossing. At the point where the levels would cross, the eigenstates are of the form $(\psi_1^{(0)}\pm\psi_2^{(0)})/\sqrt2$, in which the two states are mixed half and half. Here we use, instead of the nondegenerate formula, the method of diagonalizing the matrix within the degenerate levels (Chapter 28). Level repulsion leads to an understanding of chemical bonds and of the bands of solids. When orbitals of the same energy on two atoms combine, they split into a bonding orbital (the lower one) and an antibonding orbital (the higher one); this is precisely the repulsion described here (Chapter 39). In a crystal, the mutual repulsion of the levels just above and below a band edge affects the curvature of the band (the effective mass) (Chapter 41).

It is no accident that $E_1^{(2)}+E_2^{(2)}=0$ in this two-level case. In general, the second-order energies of all levels add up to zero:

$$ \sum_nE_n^{(2)}=\sum_n\sum_{i\ne n}\frac{\abs{V_{in}}^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_i^{(0)}}=0 $$

Indeed, in the sum the $(n,i)$ term and the $(i,n)$ term have $\abs{V_{in}}=\abs{V_{ni}}$ and denominators that differ only in sign, so they pair up and cancel each other. This is the manifestation, in the second-order term, of the fact that the sum of the eigenvalues equals the trace of the matrix and becomes the quantity $\sum_nE_n^{(0)}+\lambda\sum_nV_{nn}$, which is first order in $\lambda$. The other level moves in the opposite direction by exactly as much as one level is repelled.

Theorem 27.2: The second-order energy of the ground state is negative

For a nondegenerate ground state ($n=0$), the second-order energy correction satisfies $E_0^{(2)}\le0$.

Proof

The ground-state energy $E_0^{(0)}$ is lower than the energies of all the excited states, so $E_i^{(0)}-E_0^{(0)}\gt0$ for every $i$ satisfying $i\ne0$ (equality does not hold because there is no degeneracy). On the other hand, $\abs{V_{i0}}^2\ge0$, so each term $-\abs{V_{i0}}^2/(E_i^{(0)}-E_0^{(0)})$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:27-E2} is $0$ or less, and their sum is also $0$ or less. Equality holds only when $V_{i0}=0$ for all $i\ne0$ (that is, when $\hat{V}$ does not connect the ground state to any other state).

∎

The fact that the second-order correction to the ground state is necessarily negative (the ground state is lowered further at second order by the perturbation) is a special case of level repulsion. Since there is no level below the ground state, there are no terms pushing it up, and only the terms pushing it down remain. This fact can also be understood physically as follows.

Intuition: “Mixing” states a little lowers the energy

In a two-level system, consider the trial function $\psi=\psi_1^{(0)}+c\,\psi_2^{(0)}$ ($c$ is real and small), obtained by mixing the upper state into the lower state $\psi_1^{(0)}$ with a coefficient $c$. Keeping terms through second order in $c$, the expectation value of the energy is $\mel{\psi}{\hat{H}}{\psi}/\braket{\psi}{\psi}\simeq E_1+2c\lambda V+c^2\Delta$ (the normalization correction in the denominator is of higher order than $c^2$). Minimizing this as a function of $c$, we obtain $E=E_1-\lambda^2V^2/\Delta$ at $c=-\lambda V/\Delta$ (which is exactly $c_2$ of Formula 27.3). That is, by mixing in the upper state “in the direction in which the perturbation lowers the energy,” we lower the energy at second order. The essence of level repulsion is that the gain from the perturbation ($2c\lambda V$) can be made to outweigh the energy cost of mixing in the upper state ($c^2\Delta$).

One more remark. The energy through first order, $E_0^{(0)}+\lambda E_0^{(1)}$, was a variational upper bound for the ground state (Section 27.3), but $E_0^{(0)}+\lambda E_0^{(1)}+\lambda^2E_0^{(2)}$, with the second-order term added, is not necessarily an upper bound on the true ground-state energy (it can miss in either direction, above or below). In the two-level example, the exact lower eigenvalue satisfies $E_-\ge E_1-\lambda^2V^2/\Delta$ (by $\sqrt{1+x}\le1+x/2$), so the value through second order is lower than the true value.

Application: Destabilization of a structure caused by the second-order energy (second-order Jahn–Teller effect, ferroelectricity)

Suppose that an atom in a crystal is displaced by a small amount $Q$ from its equilibrium position. The effect of the displacement on the electron system can be written as a perturbation $\hat{V}=Q\,\partial\hat{H}/\partial Q$ proportional to $Q$. Let $v=\mel{e}{\partial\hat{H}/\partial Q}{g}$ be the matrix element connecting the electronic ground state $g$ and an excited state $e$ (separated by $\Delta$). When the first-order energy $V_{gg}Q$ is 0, the second-order energy is $E_g^{(2)}=-Q^2\abs{v}^2/\Delta$. On the other hand, the lattice spring (elastic energy) is $+\tfrac12KQ^2$, so the total is

$$ \Delta E(Q)=\tfrac12\Bigl(K-\frac{2\abs{v}^2}{\Delta}\Bigr)Q^2 $$

If $K\lt2\abs{v}^2/\Delta$, then $Q=0$ becomes unstable and the crystal distorts spontaneously (the second-order Jahn–Teller effect, or pseudo-Jahn–Teller effect). The smaller the gap $\Delta$ to the excited state (the closer the material is to quasi-degeneracy), the more readily the destabilization occurs, and this provides a tool for discussing the origin of the spontaneous polarization of ferroelectrics. This structure is also taken up in the related simulator (Section 7 of perturbation-derivation).

27.6 Applications of Second-Order Perturbation — Oscillators, Atoms, Quantum Wells, and Intermolecular Forces

27.6.1 How to add up infinitely many intermediate states

The second-order energy (Formula 27.5) is a sum over all states other than $n$. In an actual problem, how this sum is handled is the crux of the calculation. The examples in this section involve the following three ways of handling it.

  • Most of the terms vanish: with ladder operators, only a few neighboring intermediate states have nonzero matrix elements (harmonic oscillator, Sections 27.6.2–27.6.3).
  • The first few terms are enough: terms whose denominator $E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}$ is small, or whose matrix element is large, dominate (the $k=2$ term of the quantum well, Section 27.6.5).
  • Without carrying out the sum, solve for the first-order wave function directly from a differential equation (the Dalgarno–Lewis method): the sum, which includes not only the bound states but also the continuum states, is replaced by a single ordinary differential equation (polarizability of the hydrogen atom, Section 27.6.4).

27.6.2 A harmonic oscillator in an electric field — an example that agrees with the exact solution

Example 27.8: The harmonic oscillator of a charged particle in an electric field

A one-dimensional harmonic oscillator of charge $q$, mass $m$, and angular frequency $\omega$ ($n=0,1,2,\dots$) is placed in a uniform electric field $F$ (in the $x$ direction). The perturbation is $\hat{V}=-qF\hat{x}$. Using ladder operators, find (1) the first-order energy $E_n^{(1)}$ and (2) the second-order energy $E_n^{(2)}$. (3) Compare with the exact solution (completing the square) and confirm that all corrections of third order and higher vanish. (4) For an oscillator with the mass of a proton and $\hbar\omega=0.372\ \mathrm{eV}$ (corresponding to a wavenumber of $3000\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$), find the displacement and the energy shift when $F=1.0\times10^8\ \mathrm{V/m}$ is applied.

Solution. Let $b=\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}$ and use $\hat{x}=\dfrac{b}{\sqrt2}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)$. The matrix elements are $\mel{n+1}{\hat{x}}{n}=b\sqrt{\dfrac{n+1}{2}}$ and $\mel{n-1}{\hat{x}}{n}=b\sqrt{\dfrac{n}{2}}$, and all the others (including $\mel{n}{\hat{x}}{n}$) are 0.

(1) $E_n^{(1)}=-qF\mel{n}{\hat{x}}{n}=0$.

(2) The only intermediate states are $n\pm1$. From $E_{n\pm1}^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}=\pm\hbar\omega$ and $\abs{V_{n\pm1,n}}^2=(qF)^2b^2\dfrac{n+1}{2},\ (qF)^2b^2\dfrac{n}{2}$, Formula 27.5 gives

$$ E_n^{(2)}=-\frac{(qF)^2b^2(n+1)/2}{+\hbar\omega}-\frac{(qF)^2b^2\,n/2}{-\hbar\omega}=-\frac{(qF)^2b^2}{2\hbar\omega}\bigl[(n+1)-n\bigr]=-\frac{(qF)^2b^2}{2\hbar\omega}=-\frac{(qF)^2}{2m\omega^2} $$

(Here we used $b^2=\hbar/m\omega$.) The value is the same for every level and independent of $n$ (Figure 27.6(a)). The push down from the upper level (the $n+1$ term) is larger by 1 than the push up from the lower level (the $n$ term).

(3) Exact solution: we can complete the square, $\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2-qFx=\tfrac12m\omega^2\Bigl(x-\dfrac{qF}{m\omega^2}\Bigr)^2-\dfrac{(qF)^2}{2m\omega^2}$ (since the expansion of $\tfrac12m\omega^2\bigl(x-x_0\bigr)^2$ is $\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2-m\omega^2x_0x+\tfrac12m\omega^2x_0^2$, we have $x_0=qF/m\omega^2$). This is a harmonic oscillator whose center has moved to $x_0$, with the constant $-(qF)^2/2m\omega^2$ added, so the exact eigenvalues are

$$ E_n=\hbar\omega\Bigl(n+\frac12\Bigr)-\frac{(qF)^2}{2m\omega^2} $$

The first-order term is 0, the second-order term is $-(qF)^2/2m\omega^2$, and there are no other corrections. The perturbation-theory result agrees completely with the exact solution at second order, and all terms of third order and higher vanish. The eigenfunction is $\psi_n(x-x_0)$ (the original shape translated by $x_0$), and the first-order correction to the wave function, $\psi_n^{(1)}=-x_0\,\psi_n'(x)$, is expressed by the mixing of the $n\pm1$ states ($c_{n\pm1}$).

(4) $x_0=\dfrac{qF}{m\omega^2}$. Here $\omega=\dfrac{0.372\ \mathrm{eV}}{\hbar}=\dfrac{0.372\times1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}}{1.0546\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}}=5.65\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $m=m_{\mathrm p}=1.673\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$, and $q=e_0$, so $m\omega^2=1.673\times10^{-27}\times3.19\times10^{29}=534\ \mathrm{N/m}$. Therefore $x_0=\dfrac{1.602\times10^{-19}\times10^{8}}{534}\ \mathrm{m}=3.0\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{m}$ (about 30 times the size of an atomic nucleus, $10^{-15}\ \mathrm{m}$). The energy shift is $-\dfrac{(qF)^2}{2m\omega^2}=-\dfrac{(1.602\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N})^2}{2\times534\ \mathrm{N/m}}=-2.4\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{J}=-1.5\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{eV}$, which is only $4\times10^{-6}$ times the vibrational quantum $0.372\ \mathrm{eV}$.

Application: Infrared absorption and polarizability of ionic crystals

The result $E_n^{(2)}=-(qF)^2/(2m\omega^2)$ equals the energy of the induced dipole, $-\tfrac12\alpha F^2$, when the electric field displaces the oscillator by $x_0=qF/m\omega^2$ and creates the induced dipole $d=qx_0=\dfrac{q^2}{m\omega^2}F$. That is, the polarizability is $\alpha=q^2/(m\omega^2)$, the same as the result for a charged particle attached to a spring in classical mechanics (ionic polarization, AD 03, Chapter 41, Section 41.1 (in Japanese)). The smaller the frequency $\omega$ (the softer the spring), the larger the polarizability. The phenomenon in which the electric field of light couples to an optical phonon of an ionic crystal (a mode in which the cations and anions vibrate in opposite directions) and infrared light is absorbed is the time-dependent version of this coupling between an electric field and a harmonic oscillator (Chapter 52).

27.6.3 The anharmonic term and the Morse potential — an example where first- and second-order perturbations come together

The vibrational potential of a molecule is a parabola (a harmonic oscillator) near the equilibrium position, but a little farther away it deviates from the parabola. Let us treat that deviation as a small perturbation. The Morse potential $V(x)=D\bigl(1-e^{-\beta x}\bigr)^2$ (Chapter 20; $x$ is the displacement from the equilibrium position, $D$ is the dissociation energy, and $\beta$ is a constant that determines the shape) is exactly solvable, so it makes a good practice case. In Chapter 20 we wrote $V=D_{\mathrm e}\bigl(1-e^{-ay}\bigr)^2$ ($y=r-r_{\mathrm e}$), so $D_{\mathrm e}$, $a$, and $y$ of Chapter 20 correspond to $D$, $\beta$, and $x$ of this section.

Derivation: Power expansion of the Morse potential in $x$

Let $u=\beta x$. From $e^{-u}=1-u+\dfrac{u^2}{2}-\dfrac{u^3}{6}+\cdots$ (University Mathematics, Chapter 4, Section 4.2), we have $1-e^{-u}=u-\dfrac{u^2}{2}+\dfrac{u^3}{6}-\cdots$. Squaring this, the coefficient of $u^4$ is $\dfrac{1}{2^2}+2\cdot\dfrac{1}{6}=\dfrac14+\dfrac13=\dfrac{7}{12}$, so that

$$ V(x)=D\Bigl(u^2-u^3+\frac{7}{12}u^4-\cdots\Bigr)=D\beta^2x^2-D\beta^3x^3+\frac{7}{12}D\beta^4x^4-\cdots $$

is obtained.

∎

We take the first term $D\beta^2x^2=\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$ as the harmonic oscillator in $\hat{H}_0$. That is, $\omega=\beta\sqrt{2D/m}$ ($m$ is the reduced mass). The rest is treated as the perturbation: $\hat{V}=a\hat{x}^3+c\hat{x}^4$, with $a=-D\beta^3$ and $c=\dfrac{7}{12}D\beta^4$. Figure 27.6(b) shows how closely the potential approaches the Morse potential with each term of the expansion.

The size of the expansion is measured by the product $s=\beta b$ of the typical magnitude $b=\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}$ of $\hat{x}$ and $\beta$. From $m\omega^2=2D\beta^2$ we have $s^2=\beta^2\hbar/(m\omega)=\hbar\omega/(2D)$, and if the energy of a single quantum is much smaller than the dissociation energy, then $s\ll1$. The $x^3$ term is $s$ times the $x^2$ term of $\hat{H}_0$, and the $x^4$ term is $s^2$ times it, so both the second-order perturbation by $x^3$ ($s\times s$) and the first-order perturbation by $x^4$ ($s^2$) contribute to the correction to the energy of order $s^2$.

Derivation: Second-order perturbation by $x^3$ and first-order perturbation by $x^4$

From $\hat{x}=\dfrac{b}{\sqrt2}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)$ we have $\hat{x}^3=\dfrac{b^3}{2^{3/2}}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger)^3$. Of the action of $\hat{x}^3$ on $\ket{n}$, the matrix elements that take $n$ to $n\pm1$ and $n\pm3$ are as follows (count the orderings such as $\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger$).

$$ \mel{n+3}{\hat{x}^3}{n}=\frac{b^3}{2^{3/2}}\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)},\qquad \mel{n+1}{\hat{x}^3}{n}=\frac{b^3}{2^{3/2}}\,3(n+1)^{3/2} $$ $$ \mel{n-1}{\hat{x}^3}{n}=\frac{b^3}{2^{3/2}}\,3n^{3/2},\qquad \mel{n-3}{\hat{x}^3}{n}=\frac{b^3}{2^{3/2}}\sqrt{n(n-1)(n-2)} $$

Here, $3(n+1)^{3/2}$ collects the three ways of arranging two $\hat{a}^\dagger$ and one $\hat{a}$. For example, the three products that contribute to $\mel{n+1}{\cdot}{n}$ are $\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}$, $\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger$, and $\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}^\dagger$ (acting in order from the right); they give $n\sqrt{n+1}$, $(n+1)\sqrt{n+1}$, and $(n+2)\sqrt{n+1}$, respectively, and the sum is $\sqrt{n+1}\,\bigl(n+(n+1)+(n+2)\bigr)=3(n+1)^{3/2}$. The other three are counted in the same way. The transition to $n+3$ comes only from the product of three $\hat{a}^\dagger$, and $(\hat{a}^\dagger)^3\ket{n}=\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}\ket{n+3}$. The transition to $n-1$ comes from the three arrangements $\hat{a}\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger$, $\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}$, and $\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}\hat{a}$ of two $\hat{a}$ and one $\hat{a}^\dagger$; they give $(n+1)\sqrt{n}$, $n\sqrt{n}$, and $(n-1)\sqrt{n}$, respectively, and the sum is $\sqrt{n}\,\bigl((n+1)+n+(n-1)\bigr)=3n^{3/2}$. The transition to $n-3$ comes only from $\hat{a}\hat{a}\hat{a}\ket{n}=\sqrt{n(n-1)(n-2)}\ket{n-3}$ (we used $\hat{a}\ket{n}=\sqrt n\ket{n-1}$ and $\hat{a}^\dagger\ket{n}=\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1}$). Since the level spacings are $E_{n\pm3}^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}=\pm3\hbar\omega$ and $E_{n\pm1}^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}=\pm\hbar\omega$, the four terms of Formula 27.5 are as follows.

Example 27.9: The anharmonic-term corrections agree with the exact solution of the Morse potential

Show that adding Eqs. \eqref{eq:27-cubic} and \eqref{eq:27-quartic} gives the term $-(\hbar\omega)^2(n+\tfrac12)^2/(4D)$ that corresponds to $\omega_{\mathrm e}x_{\mathrm e}$, and that this agrees with the exact energy levels of the Morse potential. Also, explain how the anharmonic terms shift $\expval{x}$.

Solution. We use $a=-D\beta^3$, $b^2=\hbar/m\omega$, and $m\omega^2=2D\beta^2$. We have $a^2b^6/(\hbar\omega)=D^2\beta^6\hbar^3/(m^3\omega^3\cdot\hbar\omega)=D^2\beta^6\hbar^2/(m^3\omega^4)$, and since $m^3\omega^4=m(m\omega^2)^2=4mD^2\beta^4$,

$$ \frac{a^2b^6}{\hbar\omega}=\frac{\beta^2\hbar^2}{4m},\qquad c\,b^4=\frac{7}{12}D\beta^4\cdot\frac{\hbar^2}{m^2\omega^2}=\frac{7}{12}D\beta^4\cdot\frac{\hbar^2}{m\cdot2D\beta^2}=\frac{7}{24}\,\frac{\beta^2\hbar^2}{m} $$

Therefore, the sum of the two corrections is

\begin{align*} E_n^{(2)}[a\hat{x}^3]+E_n^{(1)}[c\hat{x}^4]&=\frac{\beta^2\hbar^2}{m}\Bigl[-\frac{30n^2+30n+11}{32}+\frac{7}{24}\cdot\frac{6n^2+6n+3}{4}\Bigr]\\ &=\frac{\beta^2\hbar^2}{m}\cdot\frac{-3(30n^2+30n+11)+7(6n^2+6n+3)}{96}=\frac{\beta^2\hbar^2}{m}\cdot\frac{-48n^2-48n-12}{96}\\ &=-\frac{\beta^2\hbar^2}{2m}\Bigl(n+\frac12\Bigr)^2 \end{align*}

(we used $-48n^2-48n-12=-48(n+\tfrac12)^2$). On the other hand, since $(\hbar\omega)^2=\hbar^2\beta^2\cdot2D/m$, we have $\dfrac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{4D}=\dfrac{\hbar^2\beta^2}{2m}$, so the result above agrees with $-\dfrac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{4D}\bigl(n+\tfrac12\bigr)^2$. That is, to order $s^2$,

$$ E_n=\hbar\omega\Bigl(n+\frac12\Bigr)-\frac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{4D}\Bigl(n+\frac12\Bigr)^2 $$

which agrees completely with the exact energy levels of the Morse potential (Chapter 20). (The spectroscopic anharmonicity constants are $x_{\mathrm e}=\hbar\omega/4D$ and $\omega_{\mathrm e}x_{\mathrm e}=(\hbar\omega)^2/4D$.) For example, for $D=4.5\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $\hbar\omega=0.36\ \mathrm{eV}$, we have $x_{\mathrm e}=0.36/(4\times4.5)=0.020$ and $\hbar\omega_{\mathrm e}x_{\mathrm e}=0.0072\ \mathrm{eV}$ (about $58\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ in wavenumber), and the spacing between levels narrows toward the higher levels.

Shift of the expectation value of $x$: The $x^4$ term is an even function and does not shift $\expval{x}$ (it does not affect $\expval{x}$ at first order). The $x^3$ term acts through the admixture of the $n\pm1$ states. Since $\expval{x}=2\sum_{k\ne n}\mel{n}{\hat{x}}{k}c_k$ and $\hat{x}$ connects only to $n\pm1$, only the two terms $k=n\pm1$ remain. Inserting $c_{n\pm1}=V_{n\pm1,n}/(E_n^{(0)}-E_{n\pm1}^{(0)})$ and the matrix elements, $\mel{n}{\hat{x}}{n\pm1}c_{n\pm1}$ becomes $-\dfrac{3ab^4(n+1)^2}{4\hbar\omega}$ (for $n+1$) and $+\dfrac{3ab^4n^2}{4\hbar\omega}$ (for $n-1$), and the total is

$$ \expval{x}=-\frac{3ab^4(2n+1)}{2\hbar\omega}=-\frac{3a}{m^2\omega^4}E_n^{(0)}=\frac{3}{4\beta}\,\frac{E_n^{(0)}}{D} $$

(we used $E_n^{(0)}=\hbar\omega(n+\tfrac12)$, $b^4=\hbar^2/m^2\omega^2$, $m^2\omega^4=4D^2\beta^4$, and $a=-D\beta^3$). $\expval{x}$ becomes larger in proportion to $E_n^{(0)}$ (in the positive direction, that is, the direction in which the bond becomes longer). This expresses the following: for the harmonic potential ($a=0$), $\expval{x}=0$ and the vibration is symmetric, whereas for an anharmonic potential the outer wall is gentler than the inner wall, so the higher the energy, the more the average position shifts outward. The same result holds classically, and $\expval{x}\propto E$ is the origin of thermal expansion (as the temperature rises, states with large $n$ become occupied, and the average bond length stretches).

27.6.4 The polarizability of the hydrogen atom — a sum including continuum states

When a hydrogen atom is placed in an electric field $F$, the electron cloud shifts in the direction opposite to the field, and the atom acquires a dipole moment $d=\alpha F$ along the direction of the field (an induced dipole). The constant of proportionality $\alpha$ is the polarizability. Let us calculate this phenomenon by second-order perturbation theory, with the electric field as the perturbation. Since the ground state (1s) is nondegenerate, the formulas of this chapter can be used.

The perturbation is $\hat{V}=e_0F\hat{z}$, the same as in Example 27.1. The first-order energy is $E^{(1)}=e_0F\mel{1\mathrm{s}}{\hat{z}}{1\mathrm{s}}=0$, because the parity of 1s is even and $\hat{z}$ is odd (the integrand is an odd function). That is, there is no energy shift proportional to the electric field. By Formula 27.5, the second-order energy is

\begin{equation} E^{(2)}=-\sum_{k}\frac{e_0^2F^2\abs{\mel{k}{\hat{z}}{1\mathrm{s}}}^2}{E_k-E_1}\equiv-\frac12\,\alpha\,F^2 \label{eq:27-alpha-def} \end{equation}

The last equality is the definition of the polarizability through the energy $-\int_0^Fd\,\dd F'=-\tfrac12\alpha F^2$ of an induced dipole $d=\alpha F$. That is, $\alpha=2e_0^2\sum_k\abs{\mel{k}{\hat{z}}{1\mathrm{s}}}^2/(E_k-E_1)$. The sum is taken over all intermediate states $k$ that are coupled to 1s (the p states with $m=0$, $l=1$). In addition to the bound states $n\mathrm{p}_0$ ($n=2,3,4,\dots$), it includes the ionized continuum states ($E\gt0$). The shift of the electron cloud and the contributions of the intermediate states are drawn in Figure 27.7.

Example 27.10: The polarizability of the hydrogen atom — an estimate with only 2p as the intermediate state, and the exact solution

(1) Estimate $\alpha$ by taking only $2\mathrm{p}_0$ as the intermediate state. (2) Without carrying out the sum, solve for the first-order wave function directly from a differential equation (the Dalgarno–Lewis method), and find the exact $E^{(2)}$ and $\alpha$. (3) Compare (1) and (2), and evaluate the contribution of the intermediate states other than $2\mathrm{p}$.

Solution. (1) From $\mel{2\mathrm{p}_0}{\hat{z}}{1\mathrm{s}}=0.7449\,a_0$, found in Example 27.1, and $E_2-E_1=\tfrac38E_{\mathrm h}$ ($E_{\mathrm h}=e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0a_0)$), we obtain

$$ \alpha\simeq\frac{2e_0^2(0.7449\,a_0)^2}{\tfrac38E_{\mathrm h}}=\frac{2\times0.5549}{0.375}\cdot\frac{e_0^2a_0^2}{E_{\mathrm h}}=2.96\,(4\pi\varepsilon_0)\,a_0^3 $$

(we used $e_0^2a_0^2/E_{\mathrm h}=4\pi\varepsilon_0a_0^3$).

(2) We work in atomic units ($\hbar=m_{\mathrm e}=e_0=4\pi\varepsilon_0=1$, with $a_0$ as the unit of length and $E_{\mathrm h}$ as the unit of energy). Let the perturbation be $\hat{V}=F\hat{z}$ and set $F=1$; the powers of $F$ are restored later. We have $\psi^{(0)}=e^{-r}/\sqrt\pi$ and $E^{(0)}=-\tfrac12$. Since $E^{(1)}=0$, the first-order equation (Eq. \eqref{eq:27-orderk} with $k=1$) is

$$ \bigl(\hat{H}_0-E^{(0)}\bigr)\psi^{(1)}=-\hat{V}\psi^{(0)},\qquad\hat{H}_0=-\tfrac12\nabla^2-\frac1r $$

The right-hand side is $-z\,\psi^{(0)}=-r\cos\theta\,\psi^{(0)}$, and since the angular dependence is only $\cos\theta$, we assume the form $\psi^{(1)}=f(r)\cos\theta\,\psi^{(0)}$. We compute $\nabla^2\bigl(f\cos\theta\,\psi^{(0)}\bigr)$ using the derivatives $\psi^{(0)\prime}=-\psi^{(0)}$ and $\psi^{(0)\prime\prime}=\psi^{(0)}$ of $\psi^{(0)}$. Letting $u(r)=f\psi^{(0)}$, we have $\nabla^2(u\cos\theta)=\bigl(u''+\tfrac{2}{r}u'-\tfrac{2}{r^2}u\bigr)\cos\theta$ (the eigenvalue of the angular part is $-l(l+1)=-2$), with $u'=(f'-f)\psi^{(0)}$ and $u''=(f''-2f'+f)\psi^{(0)}$. Substituting these into $\bigl(\hat{H}_0-E^{(0)}\bigr)\psi^{(1)}=-\tfrac12\nabla^2\psi^{(1)}-\dfrac{1}{r}\psi^{(1)}+\tfrac12\psi^{(1)}$ and taking $\cos\theta\,\psi^{(0)}$ as the common factor, we have $$ -\frac12\Bigl[f''-2f'+f+\frac{2}{r}(f'-f)-\frac{2f}{r^2}\Bigr]-\frac{f}{r}+\frac{f}{2}=-\frac12f''+\Bigl(1-\frac1r\Bigr)f'+\frac{f}{r^2} $$ (the terms $-\tfrac12f$ and $+\tfrac12f$, and $+f/r$ and $-f/r$, cancel each other). Comparing with $-r\cos\theta\,\psi^{(0)}$ on the right-hand side, we obtain the ordinary differential equation for $f(r)$: $$ -\frac12f''+\Bigl(1-\frac1r\Bigr)f'+\frac{f}{r^2}=-r $$ Substituting $f=Ar^2+Br$ into this, with $f'=2Ar+B$ and $f''=2A$, the left-hand side becomes $-A+\bigl(1-\tfrac1r\bigr)(2Ar+B)+A+\tfrac{B}{r}=2Ar+B-2A$. $$ \psi^{(1)}=-\Bigl(\frac{r^2}{2}+r\Bigr)\cos\theta\;\psi^{(0)} $$

$$ \alpha=\frac92\ \text{(atomic units)}=\frac92\,(4\pi\varepsilon_0)\,a_0^3=0.667\ \text{Å}^3\times(4\pi\varepsilon_0) $$

(here $a_0^3=0.1482\ \text{Å}^3$). For the electric field $F=1.0\times10^7\ \mathrm{V/m}$ of Example 27.1, since $F/F_{\mathrm{at}}=1.9\times10^{-5}$, we have $E^{(2)}=-\tfrac94(1.9\times10^{-5})^2E_{\mathrm h}=-2.3\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{eV}$. This is a tiny lowering that is not proportional to the electric field but acts through its square, and the value obtained with only 2p as the intermediate state, $V_{21}^2/(E_2-E_1)=(3.94\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV})^2/(10.20\ \mathrm{eV})=1.5\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{eV}$, is about 66% of this total.

(3) The 2p-only estimate $2.96$ is $2.96/4.5=66\ \%$ of the exact value $4.5$. The remaining approximately $34\ \%$ comes from the contributions of the bound states $3\mathrm{p},4\mathrm{p},\dots$ and the continuum states. Calculating the contributions of the $n\mathrm{p}_0$ individually, $3\mathrm{p}$ gives $0.40$ ($8.9\ \%$), $4\mathrm{p}$ gives $0.13$ ($2.9\ \%$), and the bound states $5\mathrm{p}$ and above together give about $0.17$ ($3.8\ \%$), so the sum over the bound states is about $3.66$ ($81\ \%$). The remaining approximately $0.84$ ($19\ \%$) is the contribution from the continuum states.

Intuition: Why the 2p state alone accounts for about two-thirds

Each term of the sum, $\abs{\mel{k}{\hat{z}}{1\mathrm{s}}}^2/(E_k-E_1)$, is larger for a state with a larger matrix element and a smaller denominator. In addition to having the smallest energy difference from 1s ($10.2\ \mathrm{eV}$), the 2p state has a spatial spread close to that of 1s, and its matrix element of $\hat{z}$ is the largest. The higher the state $n\mathrm{p}$, the larger the denominator ($E_n-E_1\to13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$) and the smaller the matrix element ($\propto n^{-3/2}$), so the contributions die out rapidly. Even so, the total of the innumerable states whose individual contributions are small, together with the continuum states, accounts for about one-third of the whole.

Application: Polarizability and refractive index of matter as a dielectric

The polarizability of an atom or molecule is the basic quantity that determines the permittivity and the refractive index of a material. For a dilute gas of number density $N$, the relative permittivity can be written as $\varepsilon_{\mathrm r}\simeq1+N\alpha/\varepsilon_0$ (from the polarization $P=N\alpha F$ in AD 03, Chapter 41, Section 41.1 (in Japanese)). The larger the polarizability of an atom or molecule (one whose electrons are loosely bound and whose excited states lie close by), the larger its refractive index and the stronger its van der Waals force (next subsection). The hydrogen atom's $\alpha=4.5$ (atomic units) is 4.5 times the polarizability of a conducting sphere of radius $a_0$ ($1$, atomic units), which means that the electron cloud “deforms much more readily than a conducting sphere.” The Dalgarno–Lewis method is a powerful method that replaces a problem involving continuum states, for which the sum cannot be carried out, with a single solution of a differential equation, and it is widely used in calculations of atomic and molecular polarizabilities and van der Waals coefficients, and in perturbation-theory calculations for many-electron systems (A. Dalgarno & J. T. Lewis, Proc. R. Soc. London A 233, 70 (1955)).

The potential energy of the electron is $+e_0Fx$, and, choosing the origin of energy at the center of the well, we take $\hat{V}=e_0F\bigl(\hat{x}-L/2\bigr)$. (1) Find the matrix element $\mel{\psi_k^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_1^{(0)}}$. (2) Find the second-order energy $E_1^{(2)}$ of the ground state. (3) Find the numerical value for $m=m^*=0.067\,m_{\mathrm e}$, $L=10\ \mathrm{nm}$, $F=1.0\times10^7\ \mathrm{V/m}$ ($100\ \mathrm{kV/cm}$), and the percentage of the total that is obtained from the single term $k=2$ alone. (4) Describe the change in the wave function ($c_2$ and the shift of the average position).

Solution. (1) The constant part $-e_0FL/2$ of $\hat{V}$ does not contribute to the matrix elements with $k\ne1$ ($\braket{\psi_k^{(0)}}{\psi_1^{(0)}}=0$). For $k\ne1$, $\mel{\psi_k^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_1^{(0)}}=e_0F\,X_{k1}$, where $X_{k1}=\int_0^L\psi_k^{(0)}x\,\psi_1^{(0)}\dd x$. Using the product-to-sum formula $\sin\alpha\sin\beta=\tfrac12[\cos(\alpha-\beta)-\cos(\alpha+\beta)]$, we have

$$ X_{k1}=\frac{2}{L}\int_0^Lx\sin\frac{k\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{\pi x}{L}\dd x=\frac1L\int_0^Lx\Bigl[\cos\frac{(k-1)\pi x}{L}-\cos\frac{(k+1)\pi x}{L}\Bigr]\dd x $$

For $q=j\pi/L$ ($j$ an integer), integration by parts gives $\int_0^Lx\cos qx\dd x=\Bigl[\dfrac{x\sin qx}{q}\Bigr]_0^L-\int_0^L\dfrac{\sin qx}{q}\dd x=0+\Bigl[\dfrac{\cos qx}{q^2}\Bigr]_0^L=\dfrac{(-1)^j-1}{q^2}=\dfrac{\bigl((-1)^j-1\bigr)L^2}{j^2\pi^2}$ ($\sin j\pi=0$). When $k$ is odd, $k\pm1$ is even and $(-1)^{k\pm1}-1=0$, so $X_{k1}=0$ ($k\ne1$). This agrees with the parity selection rule ($\hat{x}-L/2$ is an odd function about the center of the well, so only states of opposite parity, that is, states with even $k$, are connected). When $k$ is even, $k\pm1$ is odd and $(-1)^{k\pm1}-1=-2$, so we obtain

$$ X_{k1}=\frac1L\Bigl[-\frac{2L^2}{(k-1)^2\pi^2}+\frac{2L^2}{(k+1)^2\pi^2}\Bigr]=\frac{2L}{\pi^2}\cdot\frac{(k-1)^2-(k+1)^2}{(k^2-1)^2}=-\frac{8kL}{\pi^2(k^2-1)^2}\qquad(k=2,4,6,\dots) $$

For $k=2$, $X_{21}=-\dfrac{16L}{9\pi^2}=-0.180\,L$ (this agrees with Example 10.4 in Section 10.2).

(2) The intermediate states are $k=2,4,6,\dots$, and $E_k^{(0)}-E_1^{(0)}=(k^2-1)E_1$. Substituting into Formula 27.5, we obtain

$$ E_1^{(2)}=-\sum_{k=2,4,\dots}\frac{(e_0F)^2\,64k^2L^2/\bigl(\pi^4(k^2-1)^4\bigr)}{(k^2-1)\,\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)}=-\frac{128}{\pi^6}\,\frac{m\,e_0^2F^2L^4}{\hbar^2}\,S,\qquad S=\sum_{k=2,4,6,\dots}\frac{k^2}{(k^2-1)^5} $$

This sum $S$ can be found exactly as follows. Since $k^2-1=(k-1)(k+1)$, we decompose $k^2/(k^2-1)^5$ into partial fractions:

$$ \frac{k^2}{(k^2-1)^5}=\frac{5}{256}\Bigl[\frac{1}{k+1}-\frac{1}{k-1}\Bigr]+\frac{5}{256}\Bigl[\frac{1}{(k+1)^2}+\frac{1}{(k-1)^2}\Bigr]+\frac{1}{128}\Bigl[\frac{1}{(k+1)^3}-\frac{1}{(k-1)^3}\Bigr]-\frac{1}{64}\Bigl[\frac{1}{(k+1)^4}+\frac{1}{(k-1)^4}\Bigr]-\frac{1}{32}\Bigl[\frac{1}{(k+1)^5}-\frac{1}{(k-1)^5}\Bigr] $$

(For example, at $k=2$ the right-hand side is $\tfrac{5}{256}\bigl(\tfrac13-1\bigr)+\tfrac{5}{256}\bigl(\tfrac19+1\bigr)+\tfrac{1}{128}\bigl(\tfrac1{27}-1\bigr)-\tfrac{1}{64}\bigl(\tfrac1{81}+1\bigr)-\tfrac{1}{32}\bigl(\tfrac1{243}-1\bigr)=\tfrac{4}{243}$, which agrees with the left-hand side $4/3^5$.) Setting $k=2j$ ($j=1,2,\dots$), we have $k-1=2j-1$ and $k+1=2j+1$, and, using $\lambda_m\equiv\sum_{j=1}^\infty(2j-1)^{-m}$, we have $\sum_{j=1}^\infty(2j+1)^{-m}=\lambda_m-1$. Also $\lambda_2=\pi^2/8$ and $\lambda_4=\pi^4/96$ (the values $\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6$ and $\zeta(4)=\pi^4/90$ of the Basel problem multiplied by $1-2^{-m}$). The sums of the brackets are as follows.

  • $m=1$: $\sum_j\bigl[\tfrac{1}{2j+1}-\tfrac{1}{2j-1}\bigr]=-1$ (adjacent terms cancel, and only the first $-1/1$ remains)
  • $m=2$: $\lambda_2+(\lambda_2-1)=\pi^2/4-1$; $m=3$: $(\lambda_3-1)-\lambda_3=-1$ ($\lambda_3$ cancels)
  • $m=4$: $\lambda_4+(\lambda_4-1)=\pi^4/48-1$; $m=5$: $(\lambda_5-1)-\lambda_5=-1$

Therefore

$$ S=\frac{5}{256}(-1)+\frac{5}{256}\Bigl(\frac{\pi^2}{4}-1\Bigr)+\frac{1}{128}(-1)-\frac{1}{64}\Bigl(\frac{\pi^4}{48}-1\Bigr)-\frac{1}{32}(-1)=\frac{5\pi^2}{1024}-\frac{\pi^4}{3072}+\frac{-5-5-2+4+8}{256}=\frac{\pi^2(15-\pi^2)}{3072} $$

Here the constant term vanishes, since $-5-5-2+4+8=0$, and the numerical value is $S=0.016483$. Therefore

\begin{equation} E_1^{(2)}=-\frac{15-\pi^2}{24\pi^4}\;\frac{m\,e_0^2F^2L^4}{\hbar^2}=-0.002195\,\frac{m\,e_0^2F^2L^4}{\hbar^2} \label{eq:27-qcse} \end{equation}

The dimension of the energy checks out: $m\,e_0^2F^2L^4/\hbar^2=\dfrac{\mathrm{kg}\cdot(\mathrm{C\,V/m})^2\,\mathrm{m^4}}{\mathrm{J^2s^2}}=\dfrac{\mathrm{kg\,J^2\,m^2}}{\mathrm{J^2s^2}}=\dfrac{\mathrm{kg\,m^2}}{\mathrm{s^2}}=\mathrm{J}$ ($\mathrm{C\,V}=\mathrm{J}$). The shift is proportional to $F^2$ and $L^4$.

(3) $m^*e_0^2F^2L^4/\hbar^2=\dfrac{(6.103\times10^{-32})(1.602\times10^{-19})^2(10^7)^2(10^{-8})^4}{(1.0546\times10^{-34})^2}\ \mathrm{J}=1.408\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}=0.879\ \mathrm{eV}$, so $E_1^{(2)}=-0.002195\times0.879\ \mathrm{eV}=-1.93\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}=-1.93\ \mathrm{meV}$. Exact diagonalization gives $-1.92\ \mathrm{meV}$, which agrees with the second-order perturbation result to within $0.6\ \%$. The contribution of the single term $k=2$ alone is as follows: the first term of the $\sum$ is $\dfrac{4}{(4-1)^5}=\dfrac{4}{243}=0.016461$, which is $0.016461/0.016483=99.87\ \%$ of the total $S=0.016483$ ($-1.927\ \mathrm{meV}$). The terms with $k=4$ and above amount to only $0.13\ \%$, and the two levels consisting of the ground state and the first excited state alone give a sufficiently good approximation.

(4) The matrix element of $\hat{V}$ is $V_{21}=e_0FX_{21}=(1.0\times10^7\ \mathrm{V/m})(-0.180\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m})e_0=-18.0\ \mathrm{meV}$, and the level spacing is $E_2-E_1=3E_1=168.4\ \mathrm{meV}$, so by Formula 27.3, $c_2=-\dfrac{V_{21}}{E_2^{(0)}-E_1^{(0)}}=+\dfrac{18.0}{168.4}=0.107$. Thus $\psi_1\simeq\psi_1^{(0)}+0.107\,\psi_2^{(0)}$. Since $\psi_2^{(0)}$ is positive for $x\lt L/2$ and negative for $x\gt L/2$, this mixing draws the electron toward the left side, where the potential is lower. The shift of the average position is $\expval{x}-\tfrac{L}{2}\simeq2c_2X_{12}=2(0.107)(-0.180\,L)=-0.0385\,L=-0.385\ \mathrm{nm}$ (exact diagonalization gives $-0.381\ \mathrm{nm}$).

Application: Long-wavelength shift of emission and absorption in a quantum well due to an electric field (QCSE), and optical modulators

When the levels of the electron (and the hole) are lowered by an electric field, the transition energy between the band edges is also lowered, and the emission and absorption shift to the long-wavelength side (the low-energy side). As Eq. \eqref{eq:27-qcse} shows, the amount of the shift is proportional to $m^*L^4F^2$. A hole with a larger effective mass is lowered more, and the wider the well, the more steeply the shift grows ($L^4$). On the other hand, since the electric field pulls the electron and the hole apart toward opposite sides of the well, the transition probability (the overlap integral) decreases. In a quantum well the electron and the hole are confined, so the exciton is hard to break up, and a much larger electric field than in a bulk crystal can be applied while the exciton absorption peak is preserved (D. A. B. Miller et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 53, 2173 (1984)). This property is used in electroabsorption optical modulators for optical communication, which switch the absorption of light on and off with a voltage.

27.6.6 The van der Waals force — why neutral atoms attract each other

Neutral atoms with spherical symmetry have neither charge nor dipole, so they should not attract each other at all through electrostatic forces. Even so, noble gases liquefy at low temperatures, and molecules condense to form molecular crystals. We show the origin of this attraction (the van der Waals force, or dispersion force) by second-order perturbation theory.

Example 27.12: The van der Waals force between two hydrogen atoms

Consider the interaction between two hydrogen atoms (in the 1s state) separated by a distance $R$. Assuming that $R$ is sufficiently larger than the size of an atom, treat the dipole–dipole interaction as a perturbation. (1) Expand the interaction to the lowest order, $R^{-3}$. (2) Show that the first-order energy vanishes. (3) Taking as intermediate states only the states in which both atoms are excited to 2p, find $C_6$ in $E^{(2)}=-C_6/R^6$, and compare it with the exact value $6.499\,E_{\mathrm h}a_0^6$.

Solution. (1) Place the nucleus of atom A at the origin and the nucleus of atom B at $(0,0,R)$. Let $\bm{r}_1=(x_1,y_1,z_1)$ be the position of electron 1 measured from its nucleus and $\bm{r}_2=(x_2,y_2,z_2)$ the position of electron 2 measured from its nucleus. The sum of the Coulomb interactions (nucleus–nucleus, electron–electron, and nucleus–electron) can be written, using $f(\bm{a})\equiv1/\abs{R\bm{e}_z+\bm{a}}$, as

$$ \hat{V}=\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[\frac1R+f(\bm{r}_2-\bm{r}_1)-f(\bm{r}_2)-f(-\bm{r}_1)\Bigr] $$

(the first term is nucleus–nucleus, the second is electron–electron, and the third and fourth are each electron with the other nucleus). Taylor-expanding for $\abs{\bm{a}}\ll R$, we obtain $f(\bm{a})=\dfrac1R-\dfrac{a_z}{R^2}+\dfrac{3a_z^2-a^2}{2R^3}+\cdots$. The constant term vanishes, since $\tfrac1R+\tfrac1R-\tfrac1R-\tfrac1R=0$. The $1$st-order term is $-\dfrac{z_2-z_1}{R^2}+\dfrac{z_2}{R^2}-\dfrac{z_1}{R^2}=0$, so it vanishes as well. The second-order term is

$$ \frac{1}{2R^3}\Bigl[3(z_2-z_1)^2-\abs{\bm{r}_2-\bm{r}_1}^2-\bigl(3z_2^2-r_2^2\bigr)-\bigl(3z_1^2-r_1^2\bigr)\Bigr]=\frac{1}{2R^3}\bigl[-6z_1z_2+2\bm{r}_1\cdot\bm{r}_2\bigr]=\frac{x_1x_2+y_1y_2-2z_1z_2}{R^3} $$

Therefore, the lowest-order term is

\begin{equation} \hat{V}=\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3}\bigl(x_1x_2+y_1y_2-2z_1z_2\bigr) \label{eq:27-dd} \end{equation}

(the dipole–dipole interaction).

(2) The unperturbed state is the state in which the two atoms are independently in 1s, $\psi^{(0)}=\psi_{1\mathrm{s}}(1)\psi_{1\mathrm{s}}(2)$, with $E^{(0)}=2E_1$. Since each term of $\hat{V}$ is a product of “a coordinate of electron 1” and “a coordinate of electron 2,” the first-order energy follows from $\mel{\psi^{(0)}}{x_1x_2}{\psi^{(0)}}=\mel{1\mathrm{s}}{x}{1\mathrm{s}}\,\mel{1\mathrm{s}}{x}{1\mathrm{s}}=0$ (1s is even and $x$ is odd; the other two terms are similar). Hence $E^{(1)}=0$.

(3) By Formula 27.5, the second-order energy is $E^{(2)}=-\sum\dfrac{\abs{\mel{m_1m_2}{\hat{V}}{1\mathrm{s}\,1\mathrm{s}}}^2}{E_{m_1}+E_{m_2}-2E_1}$ (the intermediate states $m_1,m_2$ are p states of the respective atoms). We consider the states in which both atoms are excited to 2p. The value $\mu\equiv\mel{2\mathrm{p}_0}{\hat{z}}{1\mathrm{s}}=0.7449\,a_0$ is the same as the matrix element $\mel{2\mathrm{p}_x}{\hat{x}}{1\mathrm{s}}$ of $\hat{x}$ for $2\mathrm{p}_x$ and the matrix element of $\hat{y}$ for $2\mathrm{p}_y$. The three terms of $\hat{V}$ connect to mutually different, orthogonal intermediate states $\ket{2\mathrm{p}_x2\mathrm{p}_x}$, $\ket{2\mathrm{p}_y2\mathrm{p}_y}$, $\ket{2\mathrm{p}_z2\mathrm{p}_z}$, and the matrix elements are $\dfrac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3}\mu^2\times(1,\ 1,\ -2)$, respectively. The denominator is $2(E_2-E_1)=\tfrac34E_{\mathrm h}$. The sum of the squared matrix elements is $\bigl(\tfrac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3}\bigr)^2\mu^4(1+1+4)=6\mu^4(\cdots)^2$, so

$$ E^{(2)}\simeq-\Bigl(\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3}\Bigr)^2\frac{6\mu^4}{2(E_2-E_1)}\equiv-\frac{C_6}{R^6},\qquad C_6=\frac{6\mu^4}{2(E_2-E_1)}\Bigl(\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigr)^2 $$

In atomic units ($e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)=1$, $a_0=1$), $\mu^4=0.7449^4=0.3079$ and $2(E_2-E_1)=0.75$, so $C_6=\dfrac{6\times0.3079}{0.75}=2.46\ E_{\mathrm h}a_0^6$. This is about $38\ \%$ of the exact value (the value including all pairs of p states and the continuum states), $6.499\,E_{\mathrm h}a_0^6$, and is a reasonable magnitude for a crude two-level estimate.

This result, $E^{(2)}=-C_6/R^6\lt0$, means that the energy is lowered as the two atoms approach each other (attraction). This is because the second-order energy of the ground state is always negative (Theorem 27.2). The intuitive reason for the $R^{-6}$ dependence is as follows. Even in a spherically symmetric ground state, the electron's position is biased at each instant, and an instantaneous dipole $d\sim e_0a_0$ arises. This dipole creates an electric field $\propto d/R^3$ at a distance $R$, and induces a dipole $\alpha d/R^3$ in the neighboring atom. The induced dipole aligns in the direction in which it attracts the original dipole, so the energy of attraction is $-d\cdot(\alpha d/R^3)/R^3\propto-\alpha d^2/R^6$. What the second-order perturbation represents is this effect, in which “the fluctuations of the electron clouds induce fluctuations in each other, so that they oscillate in a correlated way.”

Application: Dispersion forces, layered materials, and molecular crystals

The van der Waals force is the origin of every kind of cohesion that is “not a chemical bond”: the liquefaction of noble gases, the cohesion of molecular crystals, physisorption on material surfaces, protein folding, and so on. In layered materials such as graphite and $\mathrm{MoS_2}$, the bonding within a layer is a strong covalent bond, but adjacent layers are held together only by the van der Waals force (dispersion force), which is why the layers can be peeled off to obtain a single sheet (graphene, monolayer $\mathrm{MoS_2}$). The magnitude of the interatomic dispersion force ($C_6$) is larger for atoms with larger polarizability (Example 27.10). The $-(\sigma/R)^6$ term of the Lennard-Jones potential $4\epsilon[(\sigma/R)^{12}-(\sigma/R)^6]$ is this $-C_6/R^6$. Note that the ordinary Hartree–Fock method for treating the electron correlation of many-electron systems, as well as many approximations of density functional theory, cannot reproduce this dispersion force, so a dispersion correction is needed (“Computational Materials Science,” AD 05, and “Density Functional Theory,” AD 07). It is also known that when the distance between the atoms is very large and the time for light to travel back and forth between the two atoms can no longer be neglected, the interaction changes to $R^{-7}$ (the Casimir–Polder force) (this is beyond the scope of this chapter).

27.7 Convergence of the Perturbation Series and the Limits of Its Applicability

As noted in Section 27.2, it was at first not guaranteed at all whether the perturbation expansion $E_n=E_n^{(0)}+\lambda E_n^{(1)}+\lambda^2E_n^{(2)}+\cdots$ converges. Here, using two exactly solvable examples, we look at the cases in which the series “converges,” “does not converge,” and “is useful even though it does not converge,” and sort out the conditions under which perturbation theory can be used.

27.7.1 The exactly solvable two-level model — radius of convergence $\lambda_{\mathrm c}=\Delta/(2V)$

The two-level model, in which two levels (with spacing $\Delta$) are coupled by a perturbation $\lambda V$,

\begin{equation} \hat{H}(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda V\\\lambda V&\Delta\end{pmatrix} \label{eq:27-2level} \end{equation}

has eigenvalues that can be found exactly from the secular equation $\det(\hat{H}-E)=E(E-\Delta)-\lambda^2V^2=0$.

\begin{equation} E_\mp(\lambda)=\frac{\Delta\mp\sqrt{\Delta^2+4\lambda^2V^2}}{2} \label{eq:27-2level-exact} \end{equation}

The $\lambda$ expansion of the lower level $E_-$ is obtained, as in Example 27.3, by using the binomial series of $\sqrt{1+x}$ (the Taylor expansion, University Mathematics, Chapter 4, Section 4.2) $\sqrt{1+x}=1+\tfrac12x-\tfrac18x^2+\tfrac1{16}x^3-\tfrac{5}{128}x^4+\cdots$ with $x=4\lambda^2V^2/\Delta^2$, which gives

\begin{equation} E_-(\lambda)=-\frac{V^2}{\Delta}\lambda^2+\frac{V^4}{\Delta^3}\lambda^4-\frac{2V^6}{\Delta^5}\lambda^6+\frac{5V^8}{\Delta^7}\lambda^8-\cdots=-\Delta\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k-1}C_{k-1}\Bigl(\frac{\lambda V}{\Delta}\Bigr)^{2k} \label{eq:27-2level-series} \end{equation}

Here the coefficients $C_j=\dfrac{(2j)!}{j!\,(j+1)!}=1,1,2,5,14,\dots$ are the Catalan numbers, which come from the binomial coefficients of $\sqrt{1+4t}$. Both the absence of a first-order term in $\lambda$ ($E^{(1)}=0$) and the agreement of the second-order coefficient $-V^2/\Delta$ with Formula 27.5 can be seen here.

Let us examine the range in which this series converges. The binomial series $\sqrt{1+x}=\sum_j\binom{1/2}{j}x^j$ converges for $\abs{x}\lt1$ and diverges for $\abs{x}\gt1$. In the present case $x=4\lambda^2V^2/\Delta^2$, so the condition for convergence is

\begin{equation} \Bigl(\frac{2\lambda V}{\Delta}\Bigr)^2\lt1\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\abs{\lambda}\lt\lambda_{\mathrm c}\equiv\frac{\Delta}{2V} \label{eq:27-lambda-c} \end{equation}

The quantity $\lambda_{\mathrm c}$ is called the radius of convergence. The fact that the ratio of the terms of the series satisfies $\abs{\text{next term}/\text{previous term}}\to4\lambda^2V^2/\Delta^2$ also shows that the terms decrease for $\lambda\lt\lambda_{\mathrm c}$ and increase for $\lambda\gt\lambda_{\mathrm c}$.

Math Tool: The radius of convergence is determined by singularities in the complex plane

For real $\lambda$, $E_-(\lambda)$ is perfectly smooth on either side of $\lambda=\lambda_{\mathrm c}$, and it looks as if nothing is happening. However, the quantity under the square root in Eq. \eqref{eq:27-2level-exact}, $\Delta^2+4\lambda^2V^2$, vanishes at the complex values $\lambda=\pm i\Delta/(2V)$, and these are the singular points (branch points) of $E_-(\lambda)$. The distance from the origin $\lambda=0$ to the nearest singular point is the radius of convergence of the power series. Here the singular points lie on the imaginary axis at $\pm i\lambda_{\mathrm c}$, so the series ceases to converge at $\lambda=\lambda_{\mathrm c}$ on the real axis. This is a typical example of “the power series of a smooth real function diverges for a reason that cannot be seen within the real numbers,” and it is one of the reasons for introducing complex numbers (University Mathematics, Chapter 23, Section 23.7).

The meaning of Eq. \eqref{eq:27-lambda-c} is clear. $2\lambda V$ is the strength with which the perturbation couples the two levels, and $\Delta$ is the spacing between the levels, so the condition for convergence is exactly the intuition of Section 27.1.4: “the coupling strength is smaller than the spacing.” For the series to converge at $\lambda=1$ (the actual problem), $V\lt\Delta/2$ is necessary.

Example 27.13: Radius of convergence of the two-level model — when the series converges and when it diverges

27.7.2 Asymptotic series — useful even when divergent

The two-level model was a “well-behaved” example that is certain to converge inside $\lambda_{\mathrm c}$. In many real problems (oscillators, atoms, molecules, field theory), however, the radius of convergence of the perturbation series is zero. Let us see why perturbation theory is nevertheless useful by looking at a representative example: the anharmonic oscillator obtained by adding an $x^4$ term to the harmonic oscillator,

$$ \hat{H}=\frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2\hat{x}^2+g\,\hbar\omega\Bigl(\frac{\hat{x}}{b}\Bigr)^4,\qquad b=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}} $$

whose ground-state energy has the perturbation series ($g$ is the perturbation parameter, and Example 27.5 gives the first-order coefficient)

\begin{equation} \frac{E_0}{\hbar\omega}=\frac12+\frac34g-\frac{21}{8}g^2+\frac{333}{16}g^3-\frac{30885}{128}g^4+\cdots \label{eq:27-anh-series} \end{equation}

The coefficients $0.5,\ 0.75,\ -2.63,\ 20.8,\ -241,\ 3581,\ -63983,\ \dots$ grow rapidly while alternating in sign. The magnitudes of the higher-order coefficients grow factorially, and the $k$th-order coefficient approaches $(-1)^{k+1}\sqrt{6/\pi^3}\,3^k\,\Gamma(k+\tfrac12)$ ($\Gamma$ is the gamma function, a generalization of the factorial, $\Gamma(j+1)=j!$) (C. M. Bender & T. T. Wu, Phys. Rev. 184, 1231 (1969)). The ratio of the terms is $\abs{a_{k+1}g/a_k}\simeq3g\,(k+\tfrac12)$, so however small $g$ is, the terms begin to grow once $k$ exceeds $1/(3g)$. Therefore the radius of convergence is 0, and the series diverges for every $g\ne0$.

Why doesn't the series converge? (Dyson's argument)

If the series converged around $g=0$, then $E_0(g)$ would be an analytic function of $g$ in a neighborhood of $g=0$, and it should take meaningful (finite and real) values for $g\lt0$ as well. However, for $g\lt0$ the term $-\abs{g}x^4$ makes the potential arbitrarily low as $\abs{x}\to\infty$ (it is not bounded below), so no ground state exists and the system keeps “falling” without end. Therefore $g=0$ is a singular point of $E_0(g)$, and the radius of convergence must be 0. The nonconvergence of the perturbation series of quantum electrodynamics is also explained by a similar argument (F. J. Dyson, 1952).

Even if the series diverges, it is useful as an asymptotic series. An asymptotic series is a series for which the error of the partial sum truncated after the first few terms becomes smaller, as $g\to0$, faster than the last term included. The terms decrease up to a certain order and increase beyond it. Truncating near the point where the terms are smallest, $k\simeq1/(3g)$, gives the best accuracy, and the error is then about the size of the smallest term. The smaller $g$ is, the more terms can be used and the higher the accuracy attained.

27.7.3 Conditions under which perturbation theory can be used, and cases in which it cannot

From the results so far, the conditions under which perturbation theory can be used can be summarized as follows.

Criterion: Condition under which perturbation theory is reliable

\begin{equation} \abs{\lambda c_k}=\frac{\abs{\lambda V_{kn}}}{\abs{E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}}}\ll1\qquad(\text{for all }k\ne n) \label{eq:27-criterion} \end{equation}

The matrix elements of the perturbation must be sufficiently small compared with the level spacings to the states they connect to. In the two-level model, the condition for convergence is $2\lambda V\lt\Delta$ ($\lambda\lt\lambda_{\mathrm c}$).

Typical situations in which this condition breaks down, and what to do in each case, are summarized in Table 27.8.

An example of the “phenomena that do not appear in the perturbation series” of the fourth row is the ionization of a hydrogen atom in an electric field. In an electric field of strength $F$, the electron can escape by tunneling through the barrier of the potential created by the field, so the 1s state is not completely stable but has a finite lifetime. The ionization rate is proportional to $\exp\bigl(-2/(3F)\bigr)$ in atomic units, and it cannot be expanded in powers of $F$ around $F=0$ (the coefficients of all powers are 0). Therefore the perturbation series (which gives a real energy at every order) can never represent this effect. Perturbation theory is a method for finding “the slightly shifted position of a stably bound level”; the lifetime of a level and ionization lie outside it.

The second-order perturbation sum $\sum_i\abs{V_{in}}^2/(E_n^{(0)}-E_i^{(0)})$ of this chapter leads on to time-dependent problems. A sum of the same form, made time-dependent by including the frequency $\omega$ of an external field, is the susceptibility (response function) of linear response theory (companion volume “Linear Response Theory (AD 08)”, Contents; in this book, Chapter 54). In the limit $\omega\to0$, the polarizability is obtained from the second-order energy of this chapter (Sections 27.6.2 and 27.6.4). In addition, the idea of expanding the interaction that electrons exert on one another as a “perturbation” appears with the same structure in density functional theory (AD 07, Contents (in Japanese)) and in many-body perturbation theory.

27.7.4 Perturbation theory and the variational method

Perturbation theory and the variational method (Part VI) are both approximations for “problems whose exact solutions cannot be obtained,” but their underlying ideas differ.

  • Perturbation theory systematically expands, in a small parameter $\lambda$, the neighborhood of an exactly solvable system. Raising the order in principle raises the accuracy, the coefficients are given by formulas, and the dependence on $\lambda$ and on the level spacings can be seen clearly. However, convergence is not guaranteed, and in general an upper bound on the error is not known either.
  • The variational method searches, within a family of trial functions, for the one that minimizes the expectation value of the energy. It always gives an upper bound on the ground-state energy, and it can be used even when the perturbation is not small. However, it depends on how the trial functions are chosen, and to raise the accuracy systematically one must enlarge the family of functions.

The two are closely linked. The perturbation energy through first order, $E_0^{(0)}+\lambda E_0^{(1)}$, is exactly “the variational energy with the unperturbed ground state $\psi_0^{(0)}$ as the trial function,” and it is a single unoptimized point of the variational method (the parameters within the family have not been varied). For the helium atom, the first-order perturbation, with $\psi_0^{(0)}$ taken as the product of hydrogen-like 1s functions with effective nuclear charge $Z'=Z$, gives $-74.83\ \mathrm{eV}$ (Chapter 29); the variational calculation optimized with $Z'$ as a variational parameter gives $-77.49\ \mathrm{eV}$ at $Z'=Z-5/16$ (Chapter 31); and the nonrelativistic exact value is $-2.9037\,E_{\mathrm h}=-79.01\ \mathrm{eV}$ (the experimental value is $-79.005\ \mathrm{eV}$). The variationally optimized value is closer to the true value than the first-order perturbation value, and carrying perturbation theory on to second order reaches about $-79.1\ \mathrm{eV}$. In this way, the two methods are used selectively and in combination according to the situation.

27.8.1 Summary

  • Setup: $\hat{H}=\hat{H}_0+\lambda\hat{V}$, $\hat{H}_0\psi_n^{(0)}=E_n^{(0)}\psi_n^{(0)}$ (an orthonormal complete set), $V_{mn}=\mel{\psi_m^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(0)}}$. We expand the solutions as $\psi_n=\psi_n^{(0)}+\lambda\psi_n^{(1)}+\cdots$ and $E_n=E_n^{(0)}+\lambda E_n^{(1)}+\cdots$. The $\hat{H}^{(0)}$, $\hat{H}'$, and $\hat{H}^{\mathrm P}=\lambda\hat{H}'$ of the related simulator and AD 05 correspond to $\hat{H}_0$, $\hat{V}$, and $\lambda\hat{V}$ of this book.
  • Order-by-order equations: Because the equation is an identity in $\lambda$, the coefficients of $\lambda^k$ are equal. The $k$th order gives $(\hat{H}_0-E_n^{(0)})\psi_n^{(k)}=-\hat{V}\psi_n^{(k-1)}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}E_n^{(j)}\psi_n^{(k-j)}$. Under the intermediate normalization $\braket{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\psi_n^{(k)}}=0$, $E_n^{(k)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(0)}}{\hat{V}}{\psi_n^{(k-1)}}$.
  • First order: $E_n^{(1)}=V_{nn}$ (the expectation value of $\hat{V}$). Other states mix into the wave function, with coefficients $c_k=-V_{kn}/(E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)})$. A state mixes in more strongly the larger its matrix element and the closer its level, and if $V_{kn}=0$ it does not mix in. For the ground state, $E^{(0)}+\lambda E^{(1)}$ is a variational upper bound.
  • Step next to the wall of the well: $E_n^{(1)}=2\pi^2V_0\delta^3n^2/(3L^3)$, $\psi_n=\psi_n^{(0)}+\varepsilon\sum_{k\ne n}\dfrac{nk}{n^2-k^2}\psi_k^{(0)}$ ($\varepsilon=4mV_0\delta^3/(3\hbar^2L)$). Because the probability density near the wall is proportional to $x^2$, the shift is proportional to $\delta^3$. The approximation is valid in the range $n\delta/L\ll1$ and $k\ll L/(\pi\delta)$.
  • Second order: $E_n^{(2)}=-\sum_{i\ne n}\abs{V_{in}}^2/(E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)})$. A level is pushed down by the levels above it and pushed up by the levels below it (level repulsion). The sum over all levels is 0. For the ground state, $E_0^{(2)}\le0$. The coefficients of the second-order wave function are $d_k$ (Formula 27.6), and the third-order energy is given by Formula 27.7 and $E_n^{(3)}=\mel{\psi_n^{(1)}}{\hat{V}-E_n^{(1)}}{\psi_n^{(1)}}$ (the $2n+1$ theorem).
  • Applications of second-order perturbation: A harmonic oscillator in an electric field has $E^{(2)}=-(qF)^2/(2m\omega^2)$ (which agrees with the exact solution; polarizability $\alpha=q^2/m\omega^2$). The second order in the $x^3$ term and the first order in the $x^4$ term of the Morse potential give $-(\hbar\omega)^2(n+\tfrac12)^2/(4D)$. The polarizability of the hydrogen atom is $\tfrac92(4\pi\varepsilon_0)a_0^3$ (2p alone gives 2.96; the continuum states are included). The quantum-confined Stark effect gives $E_1^{(2)}=-\dfrac{15-\pi^2}{24\pi^4}\dfrac{m e_0^2F^2L^4}{\hbar^2}$. The van der Waals force is $-C_6/R^6$.
  • Convergence: The radius of convergence of the two-level model is $\lambda_{\mathrm c}=\Delta/(2V)$ (the singularities $\pm i\Delta/2V$ in the complex plane). In general the series is asymptotic, and for the anharmonic oscillator the coefficients grow factorially. For small $g$, truncate after $k\simeq1/(3g)$ terms. The condition for applicability is $\abs{\lambda V_{kn}}\ll\abs{E_k^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}}$. For degeneracy (Chapter 28) or strong perturbations (the variational method, diagonalization), use other methods.
\begin{equation} E_n=E_n^{(0)}+\lambda V_{nn}-\lambda^2\sum_{i\ne n}\frac{\abs{V_{in}}^2}{E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}}+\cdots,\qquad \psi_n=\psi_n^{(0)}-\lambda\sum_{i\ne n}\frac{V_{in}}{E_i^{(0)}-E_n^{(0)}}\,\psi_i^{(0)}+\cdots \label{eq:27-key1} \end{equation}
\begin{equation} E_n^{(1)}=\frac{2\pi^2V_0\,\delta^3}{3L^3}\,n^2,\qquad E_\mp(\lambda)=\frac{\Delta\mp\sqrt{\Delta^2+4\lambda^2V^2}}{2},\qquad\lambda_{\mathrm c}=\frac{\Delta}{2V} \label{eq:27-key2} \end{equation}
