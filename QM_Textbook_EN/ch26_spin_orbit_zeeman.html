Chapter 26The Physics of Spin–Orbit Interaction and Atoms in a Magnetic Field

In a powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement, have you ever noticed a diffraction peak on the high-angle side that looked split in two at its base? This happens because the Cu Kα line emitted from a copper X-ray tube (the X-rays given off when a $2p$ electron in the L shell falls into a hole in the K shell) contains two very close lines, with wavelengths $1.5406\ \text{Å}$ and $1.5444\ \text{Å}$, which are diffracted by the same crystal planes at slightly different angles. The fact that the yellow light of the low-pressure sodium lamps used for street lighting consists of two lines (the D lines) with wavelengths $589.0\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $589.6\ \mathrm{nm}$ is a phenomenon of the same kind.

Both appear because the spin–orbit interaction, in which the orbital motion and the spin of an electron influence each other, splits an energy level into two. In the previous chapter (Chapter 25), we gave the form of this interaction, $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{SO}}=\xi(r)\,\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$, in advance and solved the eigenvalue problem of $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ with matrices. In this chapter we dig into the physics of that interaction. We examine in turn (1) why a magnetic interaction appears even though the electron is inside an atom in which there is only an electric field, (2) how its magnitude changes with the atomic number $Z$, and whether it can explain the size of the splitting of the Na D lines and the Cu Kα lines, and (3) what happens when a magnetic field is applied.

The connection to classical physics (Introductory University Physics, AD 03) is as follows. When a moving charge is seen by a moving observer, its electric field and magnetic field appear mixed: the electric field mixes into the magnetic field, and the magnetic field into the electric field (AD 03, Chapter 58, Section 58.7 (in Japanese)). From the point of view of an electron going around inside an atom, it is the nucleus, at rest, that goes around the electron, and this circular current produces a magnetic field at the electron's position. Meanwhile, the spin of the electron is a tiny magnet (a magnetic moment), so in this magnetic field it has an energy $-\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{B}$. This is the true nature of the spin–orbit interaction.

If, however, we calculate this classical estimate as it stands, it comes out twice the experimental value. What resolves the discrepancy is the Thomas factor $\tfrac12$ (1926), a relativistic effect arising from the fact that the electron's rest frame is undergoing accelerated motion. This factor emerges naturally, without any assumption, from the Dirac equation, which we study in Part XI. Here we limit ourselves to stating its physical meaning, and for the exact formula for the fine structure of hydrogen (Dirac's formula), too, we only preview the result.

In the second half of this chapter we treat atoms in a magnetic field. In a weak magnetic field, the levels of total angular momentum $j$ split according to the Landé $g_J$ factor (the anomalous Zeeman effect), and in a strong magnetic field the orbital motion and the spin are decoupled and respond to the field separately (the Paschen–Back effect). Simply adding a Zeeman term to the $2\times2$ blocks of $2p$ obtained in the previous chapter lets us follow exactly the change from a weak to a strong magnetic field.

Furthermore, we survey qualitatively the doublet of the Cu Kα line and the separation angle in XRD, the branching ratio of the L absorption edge in X-ray absorption, and the roles that the spin–orbit interaction plays in magnetic materials (rare-earth magnets), ESR, topological insulators, phosphorescent OLEDs, and so on. The content of this chapter is used repeatedly in Part V (perturbation theory), Part VII (many-electron atoms and term symbols), and Part VIII (ligand field theory).

26.1 The Magnetic Field in the Rest Frame and the Thomas Factor — The Origin of the Spin–Orbit Interaction

26.1.1 Why does a magnetic interaction appear in an atom that has only an electric field?

An electron in an atom feels only the force due to the electric field $\bm{E}$ produced by the atomic nucleus (charge $+Ze_0$) and the electrostatic forces between the electron and the other electrons. The nucleus does not move and produces no magnetic field (we do not consider the hyperfine structure due to the nuclear spin here). That is, in the rest frame of the nucleus (the laboratory frame) there is no magnetic field; there is only an electric field. The electron, however, is moving with velocity $\bm{v}$. To an observer moving together with the electron (the electron's rest frame), the electric and magnetic fields appear mixed together. This is the result we derived from the Lorentz transformation in AD 03 (Chapter 58, Section 58.7 (in Japanese)). When the speed $v$ is much smaller than the speed of light $c$ ($\gamma\simeq1$), the result takes the following form.

Formula 26.1: Electric field and magnetic flux density seen by a moving observer ($v\ll c$)

Let $\bm{E},\bm{B}$ be the electric field and the magnetic flux density in the laboratory frame, and let $\bm{E}',\bm{B}'$ be the electric field and the magnetic flux density seen by an observer moving with velocity $\bm{v}$ relative to the laboratory frame. Then, for $v\ll c$,

\begin{equation} \bm{E}'\simeq\bm{E}+\bm{v}\times\bm{B},\qquad \bm{B}'\simeq\bm{B}-\frac{\bm{v}\times\bm{E}}{c^2} \label{eq:26-EBtrans} \end{equation}

In particular, when there is no magnetic field in the laboratory frame ($\bm{B}=\bm{0}$), the magnetic field seen by the moving observer is

\begin{equation} \bm{B}'=-\frac{\bm{v}\times\bm{E}}{c^2} \label{eq:26-Bprime0} \end{equation}

In other words, the moving observer sees a magnetic field.

Let us check the dimensions. The unit of $vE/c^2$ is $\mathrm{(m/s)(V/m)/(m/s)^2}=\mathrm{V\,s/m^2}=\mathrm{T}$, which agrees with the unit of magnetic flux density. The meaning of Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Bprime0} is that “if you move across an electric field, a magnetic field appears for the one who is moving.” As we saw in Chapter 23, a revolving charge becomes a circular current and produces a magnetic field at the center of the rotation. From the electron's point of view, the nucleus is revolving around the electron. The magnetic field that this circular current produces at the electron's position is $\bm{B}'$ (Figure 26.1).

26.1.2 Electric field of a central force and orbital angular momentum — the magnetic field in the rest frame

Let $V(r)$ be the potential energy of the electron (for the Coulomb attraction to the nucleus, $V(r)=-Ze_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r)$; for a many-electron atom, we may regard $V(r)$ as the effective potential that includes the shielding by the other electrons). The force on the electron (charge $-e_0$) is $\bm{F}=-\nabla V=-\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\dfrac{\bm{r}}{r}$, and this is equal to the force $-e_0\bm{E}$ due to the electric field $\bm{E}$, so the electric field can be written as

\begin{equation} \bm{E}=\frac{1}{e_0}\frac{\dd V}{\dd r}\frac{\bm{r}}{r} \label{eq:26-Ecentral} \end{equation}

For the Coulomb potential, $\dd V/\dd r=Ze_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$ and $\bm{E}=\dfrac{Ze_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\dfrac{\bm{r}}{r}$, which is the familiar electric field spreading outward from a nucleus of positive charge $+Ze_0$.

Derivation: The magnetic field $\bm{B}'$ in the electron's rest frame

Substitute Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Ecentral} into Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Bprime0}. Since $\bm{E}$ is proportional to $\bm{r}$, we only need to compute $\bm{v}\times\bm{r}$ in the cross product $\bm{v}\times\bm{E}$.

$$ \bm{B}'=-\frac{1}{c^2}\,\bm{v}\times\bm{E}=-\frac{1}{e_0c^2}\frac{1}{r}\frac{\dd V}{\dd r}\,(\bm{v}\times\bm{r}). $$

Here we use the fact that the cross product changes sign when its order is interchanged ($\bm{v}\times\bm{r}=-\bm{r}\times\bm{v}$), and the fact that the orbital angular momentum of the electron (mass $m_{\mathrm{e}}$) is $\bm{L}=\bm{r}\times\bm{p}=m_{\mathrm{e}}\,\bm{r}\times\bm{v}$. Then $\bm{v}\times\bm{r}=-\bm{r}\times\bm{v}=-\bm{L}/m_{\mathrm{e}}$. Therefore we obtain

\begin{equation} \bm{B}'=\frac{1}{e_0m_{\mathrm{e}}c^2}\frac1r\frac{\dd V}{\dd r}\,\bm{L} \label{eq:26-Bprime} \end{equation}

For an attractive potential, $\dd V/\dd r\gt0$, so $\bm{B}'$ points in the same direction as the orbital angular momentum $\bm{L}$.

∎

Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Bprime} gives the most important conclusion of this chapter: “the magnitude of $\bm{B}'$ is proportional to the orbital angular momentum.” Here lies the basis for the fact that changing the direction of the spin relative to $\bm{L}$ changes the energy, that is, for an interaction of the form $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$.

Discussion: This magnetic field is the same as the magnetic field of the circular current in Chapter 23

Let us examine the magnitude of Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Bprime0} for an electron in the Bohr model ($n=1$, radius $a_0$, speed $v=\alpha c$). The electric field of the nucleus is $E=e_0/(4\pi\varepsilon_0a_0^2)=5.14\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{V/m}$, and since $\bm{v}\perp\bm{E}$, we get

$$ B'=\frac{vE}{c^2}=\frac{\alpha\,E}{c}=\frac{(7.297\times10^{-3})(5.142\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{V/m})}{2.998\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}}=12.5\ \mathrm{T} $$

This is the same value as the $12.5\ \mathrm{T}$ found in Example 23.1 of Chapter 23 as the magnetic flux density $B=\mu_0I/(2a_0)$ produced at the position of the nucleus by the circular current ($I=e_0v/2\pi a_0$) of an electron going around a circle of radius $a_0$. In fact, using $\mu_0=1/(\varepsilon_0c^2)$, we have $B'=\dfrac{v}{c^2}\dfrac{e_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a_0^2}=\dfrac{\mu_0e_0v}{4\pi a_0^2}$, which agrees with $B=\dfrac{\mu_0}{2a_0}\dfrac{e_0v}{2\pi a_0}=\dfrac{\mu_0e_0v}{4\pi a_0^2}$. Whether we look at the nucleus from the electron or at the electron from the nucleus, it remains true that this is “the magnetic field that a relatively revolving charge produces at the center of the rotation.” Inside the atom, a magnetic field far exceeding that of a laboratory electromagnet (a few T) is generated “on its own.”

26.1.3 Coupling of the spin's magnetic moment to the magnetic field $\bm{B}'$

The electron spin carries a magnetic moment $\hat{\bm{\mu}}_s=-g_s\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\hat{\bm{S}}/\hbar=-\dfrac{g_se_0}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}}\hat{\bm{S}}$ (Chapter 23, Section 23.3.6; $g_s\approx2.0023$. Because the electron's charge is negative, the magnetic moment points in the direction opposite to the spin). Substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Bprime} into the energy $-\hat{\bm{\mu}}_s\cdot\bm{B}'$ of the magnetic moment in the magnetic field $\bm{B}'$, we obtain

\begin{equation} -\hat{\bm{\mu}}_s\cdot\bm{B}'=\frac{g_se_0}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}}\,\hat{\bm{S}}\cdot\bm{B}' =\frac{g_s}{2}\,\frac{1}{m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\frac1r\frac{\dd V}{\dd r}\,\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}} \label{eq:26-Hnaive} \end{equation}

Here we have replaced $\bm{L}$ by the operator $\hat{\bm{L}}$. If the spin $\hat{\bm{S}}$ is parallel to the orbital angular momentum $\hat{\bm{L}}$, then $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}\gt0$, and for $\dd V/\dd r\gt0$ the energy is raised. This is because the magnetic moment points in the direction opposite to $\hat{\bm{S}}$, that is, also opposite to $\bm{B}'$, which is an unfavorable direction in a magnetic field.

26.1.4 The discrepancy and the Thomas factor $\tfrac12$

Putting $g_s=2$ into Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Hnaive}, the coefficient becomes $\dfrac{1}{m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}$. However, to explain the spacing of the doublets of alkali atoms (Section 26.3), this coefficient has to be halved. Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit, who proposed the electron spin in 1925, were troubled by this “factor-of-two discrepancy.” In 1926, L. H. Thomas resolved this discrepancy by pointing out that a relativistic effect of changing the rest frame had been overlooked.

Intuition: Thomas precession — the electron's rest frame is “rotating”

When we derived Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Bprime0}, we treated the electron's rest frame as a single inertial frame. However, an electron in an atom is in circular motion (accelerated motion), so the electron's rest frame is not an inertial frame. If we switch, one after another, to the inertial frames that match the electron's velocity at each instant, then two successive Lorentz transformations (boosts) in different directions cannot be expressed as a single boost but include a rotation (the Thomas–Wigner rotation of relativity). As a result, the coordinate axes of the electron's rest frame rotate, as seen from the laboratory frame, with the angular velocity

\begin{equation} \bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{T}}=\frac{\gamma^2}{\gamma+1}\,\frac{\bm{a}\times\bm{v}}{c^2}\ \simeq\ \frac{\bm{a}\times\bm{v}}{2c^2}\qquad(v\ll c) \label{eq:26-omegaT} \end{equation}

Here $\bm{a}$ is the acceleration of the electron. This rotation is called the Thomas precession. For a central force, $\bm{a}=-\dfrac{1}{m_{\mathrm{e}}}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\bm{r}$, so that $\bm{a}\times\bm{v}=-\dfrac{1}{m_{\mathrm{e}}}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}(\bm{r}\times\bm{v})=-\dfrac{1}{m_{\mathrm{e}}^2}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\bm{L}$, and we obtain

$$ \bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{T}}=-\frac{1}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\frac1r\frac{\dd V}{\dd r}\,\bm{L} $$

On the other hand, in a magnetic field $\bm{B}'$ the spin precesses with the angular velocity $\bm{\Omega}_{B}=\dfrac{g_se_0}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}}\bm{B}'=\dfrac{g_s}{2}\dfrac{1}{m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\bm{L}$ (the coefficient of $\hat{\bm{S}}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Hnaive}). Since the direction of the spin is measured relative to the coordinate axes of the electron's rest frame, if the axes themselves rotate with $\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{T}}$ relative to the laboratory frame, the precession seen from the laboratory frame changes by that amount. In a circular orbit, $\bm{a}$ points inward and $\bm{v}$ is tangential, so $\bm{a}\times\bm{v}$ points in the direction opposite to $\bm{L}$, and the Thomas precession goes around in the direction opposite to the orbital motion (for this reason it weakens the precession due to the magnetic field). Therefore, the angular velocity of the spin precession seen from the laboratory frame is the sum of these two, $\bm{\Omega}_B+\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{T}}$, and its product with $\hat{\bm{S}}$, $(\bm{\Omega}_B+\bm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{T}})\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$, is the energy. For $g_s=2$ the coefficient is $\bigl(1-\tfrac12\bigr)\dfrac{1}{m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}$, that is, exactly half of that in Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Hnaive} (for a general $g_s$ the coefficient becomes $(g_s-1)/2$, and for $g_s=2.0023$ it is larger by about $0.2\ \%$. This is one of the reasons why the measured value of the hydrogen $2p$ splitting in Section 26.2 is about $0.2\ \%$ larger than the calculated value).

We omit the derivation of the Thomas precession formula, Eq. \eqref{eq:26-omegaT}, because it requires a rather involved calculation in special relativity (the composition of Lorentz transformations). If we expand the Dirac equation, which we study in Part XI, in the nonrelativistic limit, then even without considering the switching of rest frames and the like, the spin–orbit interaction containing $g_s=2$ and this factor $\tfrac12$ emerges automatically (Chapter 63). The Relativistic-QM explanation §4 of the related simulator also shows how $g=2$ and the spin–orbit term emerge when minimal coupling is introduced into the Dirac equation.

26.1.5 The Hamiltonian of the spin–orbit interaction

Law 26.1: Spin–orbit interaction

An electron (mass $m_{\mathrm{e}}$) in a central-force potential $V(r)$ is subject, as a relativistic effect of the motion and the electromagnetic field, to the following spin–orbit interaction.

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_{\mathrm{SO}}=\xi(r)\,\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}},\qquad \xi(r)=\frac{1}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\frac1r\frac{\dd V}{\dd r} \label{eq:26-HSO} \end{equation}

The factor $\dfrac12$ comes from the Thomas precession (for $g_s=2$, it is half of Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Hnaive}). This equation is what we wrote as $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{SO}}=\xi(r)\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ in the previous chapter. $\xi(r)$ has the dimension $[\mathrm{J}/(\mathrm{J\,s})^2]$, so that $\xi(r)\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ has the dimension of energy.

Let us check the dimension. The units of $\dfrac{1}{m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}$ are $\dfrac{1}{\mathrm{kg^2\,m^2s^{-2}}}\cdot\dfrac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{m^2}}=\dfrac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg^2\,m^4s^{-2}}}$, and multiplying by the units $\mathrm{(J\,s)^2}$ of $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ gives $\mathrm{J^3s^4/(kg^2m^4)}=\mathrm{J}\cdot(\mathrm{kg\,m^2s^{-2}})^2\mathrm{s^4}/(\mathrm{kg^2m^4})=\mathrm{J}$, which agrees with the dimension of energy.

Caution: The sign, and the spin–orbit interaction inside the atomic nucleus

For an electron in an atom, $\dd V/\dd r\gt0$ because of the attraction of the nucleus, and hence $\xi(r)\gt0$. The eigenvalue of $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ is positive for $j=l+\tfrac12$ ($\bm{L}$ and $\bm{S}$ roughly parallel) and negative for $j=l-\tfrac12$ (roughly antiparallel) (these are the eigenvalues found in the previous chapter), so the energy of the level with $j=l+\tfrac12$ is higher. It is good to remember this as: “in an attractive potential, the state in which the spin is parallel to the orbit is higher.” Note that the spin–orbit interaction of nucleons inside the atomic nucleus (the shell model of Mayer and Jensen, 1949) is due to the strong force; its sign is opposite, and the level with $j=l+\tfrac12$ is lower. Do not confuse it with the case of atoms.

Example 26.1: The magnetic field and energy that an $n=2$ electron in the Bohr model experiences through the spin–orbit interaction

In the Bohr model, find the magnetic flux density $B'$ that an electron in the $n=2$ circular orbit of the hydrogen atom (radius $r=4a_0$, speed $v=\alpha c/2$) receives in the electron's rest frame, and find $\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B'$ ($\mu_{\mathrm{B}}=5.788\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/T}$). Also find the energy change $g_s\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B'\times\tfrac12$ (including the Thomas factor) when the direction of the spin is flipped, and compare it with the actual splitting of the hydrogen $2p$ level, $4.53\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}$ (Section 26.2).

Solution. For $n=2$ we have $r=n^2a_0=4a_0$ and $v=\alpha c/n=\alpha c/2=1.094\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m/s}$. The magnitude of the electric field of the nucleus is $1/4^2$ of the value for $n=1$, $5.142\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{V/m}$, namely

$$ E=\frac{e_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(4a_0)^2}=\frac{5.142\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{V/m}}{16}=3.214\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{V/m}. $$

Since $\bm{v}\perp\bm{E}$, the magnitude given by Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Bprime0} is

$$ B'=\frac{vE}{c^2}=\frac{(1.094\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m/s})(3.214\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{V/m})}{(2.998\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s})^2}=0.391\ \mathrm{T} $$

This is $1/32$ of the $12.5\ \mathrm{T}$ for $n=1$: since $B'\propto vE\propto n^{-1}\cdot n^{-4}$, we get $1/2^5=1/32$. Therefore

$$ \mu_{\mathrm{B}}B'=(5.788\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV/T})(0.391\ \mathrm{T})=2.26\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}. $$

When the direction of the spin is flipped (the sign of $\hat{\bm{S}}\cdot\bm{B}'$ is changed), the energy of the magnetic moment changes by $2\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B'$ for $g_s=2$. Multiplying by the Thomas factor $\tfrac12$ gives

$$ \Delta E=\frac12\,g_s\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B'=\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B'=2.26\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV} $$

This is of the same order of magnitude as the actual splitting of hydrogen $2p$, $4.53\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}$, but differs from it by a factor of about 2. This difference is due to the difference between the Bohr model and the picture of quantum mechanics. The Bohr model assigns $L=2\hbar$ and $r^{-3}=1/(64a_0^3)$ to the $n=2$ circular orbit, whereas the quantum-mechanical $2p$ has $l=1$ ($\sqrt{l(l+1)}\,\hbar=1.41\hbar$), and because the electron is spread over the whole orbital, $\expval{r^{-3}}=1/(24a_0^3)$, which is $2.7$ times the value for the circular orbit (the contribution from the part close to the nucleus is large). Combining these gives a difference of a factor of $\bigl(\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac{1}{24}\bigr)\big/\bigl(2\cdot\tfrac{1}{64}\bigr)=2$. The purpose of this example is the qualitative confirmation that $B'$ is proportional to $L$, together with the confirmation of the order of magnitude. Moreover, $\Delta E$ is merely $7\times10^{-6}$ times the binding energy of $n=2$, $3.4\ \mathrm{eV}$. The spin–orbit interaction is an extremely small correction (the fine structure) compared with the positions of the levels, and may be treated as a perturbation.

26.2 Spin–Orbit Splitting in Hydrogen-Like Atoms and the $Z^4$ Law

26.2.1 $\xi(r)$ for the Coulomb potential

For a hydrogen-like atom (nuclear charge $+Ze_0$, one electron), $V(r)=-\dfrac{Ze_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}$. Differentiating this gives $\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}=\dfrac{Ze_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}$, and $\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}=\dfrac{Ze_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}$, so $\xi(r)$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:26-HSO} becomes

\begin{equation} \xi(r)=\frac{1}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\cdot\frac{Ze_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}=\frac{Ze_0^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\,\frac{1}{r^3} \label{eq:26-xi-coul} \end{equation}

The function $\xi(r)$ is proportional to $r^{-3}$ and grows rapidly as we approach the nucleus. The spin–orbit interaction is an interaction that acts mainly when the electron is near the nucleus. As $r\to0$ we have $\xi\to\infty$, but for an $s$ orbital ($l=0$) we have $\hat{\bm{L}}=\bm{0}$, so $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{SO}}=0$ and no problem arises. For orbitals with $l\ge1$, the radial function is small near the origin, $R_{nl}\sim r^l$, so the integral of $r^{-3}R_{nl}^2\,r^2\sim r^{2l-1}$ converges.

26.2.2 Radial average $\expval{r^{-3}}$

The energy of the spin–orbit interaction as a first-order perturbation is determined by the average of $\xi(r)$ over the radial function $R_{nl}(r)$ (previous chapter, Section 25.7.3: $\zeta_{nl}\equiv\hbar^2\int_0^\infty\xi(r)R_{nl}(r)^2r^2\,\dd r$). The average of $r^{-3}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:26-xi-coul} was obtained in Formula 17.7 of Chapter 17, Section 17.8:

\begin{equation} \expval{r^{-3}}_{nl}=\int_0^\infty R_{nl}(r)^2\,r^{-3}\,r^2\,\dd r=\frac{Z^3}{n^3\,l\bigl(l+\tfrac12\bigr)(l+1)\,a_0^3}\qquad(l\ge1) \label{eq:26-r3} \end{equation}

Here we check it directly for the $2p$ orbital. The normalized radial function is $R_{21}(r)=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt6}\,a_0^{-3/2}\dfrac{r}{a_0}\,e^{-r/2a_0}$ ($Z=1$), so $R_{21}^2=\dfrac{1}{24}a_0^{-3}\dfrac{r^2}{a_0^2}e^{-r/a_0}$. Therefore

$$ \expval{r^{-3}}_{2p}=\frac{1}{24\,a_0^{5}}\int_0^\infty r^{2}\,e^{-r/a_0}\,r^{-3}\,r^2\,\dd r=\frac{1}{24\,a_0^{5}}\int_0^\infty r\,e^{-r/a_0}\,\dd r=\frac{1}{24\,a_0^{5}}\cdot a_0^2=\frac{1}{24\,a_0^3} $$

(here $\int_0^\infty re^{-r/a_0}\dd r=a_0^2$; the normalization is also confirmed by $\int_0^\infty R_{21}^2r^2\dd r=\dfrac{1}{24a_0^5}\int_0^\infty r^4e^{-r/a_0}\dd r=\dfrac{24a_0^5}{24a_0^5}=1$). Setting $n=2$, $l=1$, $Z=1$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:26-r3} gives $\dfrac{1}{8\cdot1\cdot\frac32\cdot2\,a_0^3}=\dfrac{1}{24a_0^3}$, which agrees.

26.2.3 Formula for the coupling constant $\zeta_{nl}$

Theorem 26.1: Spin–orbit coupling constant of a hydrogen-like atom

For the state $(n,l)$ ($l\ge1$) of a hydrogen-like atom of nuclear charge $Z$, the spin–orbit coupling constant is

\begin{equation} \zeta_{nl}=\hbar^2\expval{\xi}=\frac{\alpha^2E_{\mathrm{h}}\,Z^4}{2\,n^3\,l\bigl(l+\tfrac12\bigr)(l+1)} \label{eq:26-zeta} \end{equation}

Here $\alpha$ is the fine-structure constant and $E_{\mathrm{h}}$ is the Hartree energy ($E_{\mathrm{h}}=27.211\,386\ \mathrm{eV}$).

Derivation

From Eq. \eqref{eq:26-xi-coul} and Eq. \eqref{eq:26-r3},

$$ \zeta_{nl}=\hbar^2\,\frac{Ze_0^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\,\expval{r^{-3}} =\frac{\hbar^2Ze_0^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\cdot\frac{Z^3}{a_0^3\,n^3l(l+\frac12)(l+1)} . $$

We rewrite the coefficient in terms of the fine-structure constant $\alpha=\dfrac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c}$, the Bohr radius $a_0=\dfrac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{m_{\mathrm{e}}e_0^2}=\dfrac{\hbar}{m_{\mathrm{e}}c\,\alpha}$, and the Hartree energy $E_{\mathrm{h}}=\dfrac{\hbar^2}{m_{\mathrm{e}}a_0^2}=\alpha^2m_{\mathrm{e}}c^2$. First, since $\dfrac{e_0^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}=\dfrac12\alpha\hbar c$,

$$ \frac{\hbar^2Ze_0^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2a_0^3}=\frac{Z\alpha\,\hbar^3}{2\,m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c\,a_0^3}. $$

Substituting $\dfrac{1}{a_0^3}=\dfrac{m_{\mathrm{e}}^3c^3\alpha^3}{\hbar^3}$ here, the $\hbar^3$ cancels and

$$ \frac{Z\alpha\,\hbar^3}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c}\cdot\frac{m_{\mathrm{e}}^3c^3\alpha^3}{\hbar^3}=\frac{Z\alpha^4\,m_{\mathrm{e}}c^2}{2}=\frac{Z\alpha^2}{2}\,E_{\mathrm{h}} $$

(in the last step $\alpha^2m_{\mathrm{e}}c^2=E_{\mathrm{h}}$). Multiplying by the remaining factor $\dfrac{Z^3}{n^3l(l+\frac12)(l+1)}$, we obtain Eq. \eqref{eq:26-zeta}.

∎

The reference quantity for numerical values is $\alpha^2E_{\mathrm{h}}=1.4490\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}=11.687\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (as a frequency, $350.4\ \mathrm{GHz}$). Since $\alpha^2E_{\mathrm{h}}=\alpha^4m_{\mathrm{e}}c^2$, the spin–orbit interaction is proportional to $\alpha^4$. This is why the fine structure is only about $\alpha^2\approx5\times10^{-5}$ times as large as the Bohr levels ($\propto\alpha^2m_{\mathrm{e}}c^2$). Dimension check: $\alpha$ is dimensionless, and $\zeta_{nl}$ has the dimension of the energy $E_{\mathrm{h}}$.

26.2.4 Splitting of the levels

From the formula in the previous chapter (Section 25.7.3), the shift of the levels due to the spin–orbit interaction is

\begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{SO}}(j)=\frac{\zeta_{nl}}{2}\Bigl[j(j+1)-l(l+1)-\tfrac34\Bigr]= \begin{cases} +\dfrac{l}{2}\,\zeta_{nl}&(j=l+\tfrac12)\\[3mm] -\dfrac{l+1}{2}\,\zeta_{nl}&(j=l-\tfrac12) \end{cases} \label{eq:26-ESO} \end{equation}

The spacing between the two levels is $\bigl(l+\tfrac12\bigr)\zeta_{nl}$. Using Eq. \eqref{eq:26-zeta}, this spacing takes a neat form.

\begin{equation} \Delta_{\mathrm{SO}}(nl)\equiv E(j=l+\tfrac12)-E(j=l-\tfrac12)=\Bigl(l+\tfrac12\Bigr)\zeta_{nl}=\frac{\alpha^2E_{\mathrm{h}}\,Z^4}{2\,n^3\,l\,(l+1)} \label{eq:26-FS} \end{equation}

Table 26.1 summarizes the values for hydrogen ($Z=1$). For the same $n$, the larger $l$ is, the smaller the splitting; for the same $l$, it decreases in proportion to $n^{-3}$. In both cases the reason is that the probability of finding the electron near the nucleus becomes smaller ($\expval{r^{-3}}$ becomes smaller).

26.2.5 The $Z^4$ law

Intuition: Why is it proportional to $Z^4$?

When the nuclear charge becomes $Z$ times larger, (i) the electron is attracted more strongly and its orbital shrinks to $1/Z$ of its size, so $\expval{r^{-3}}$ becomes $Z^3$ times larger (the scaling of Chapter 18). (ii) In addition, the electric field of the nucleus itself becomes $Z$ times larger, so $\xi(r)\propto Z$. Together this gives $Z\times Z^3=Z^4$, and the spin–orbit splitting grows steeply for the core electrons of heavier elements (Section 26.6). The ratio $\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}}/|E_n|$ to the binding energy $|E_n|\propto Z^2$ is proportional to $Z^2$, and is in fact of the order of $(Zv_1/c)^2=(Z\alpha)^2$ ($v_1=\alpha c$ is the speed in the $n=1$ state of the Bohr model). The spin–orbit interaction is a relativistic effect determined by the ratio $(v/c)^2$ of the electron's speed $v$ to the speed of light $c$. For the $2p$ state of hydrogen, $\Delta_{\mathrm{SO}}/|E_2|=\alpha^2/4=1.33\times10^{-5}$ is extremely small, but for a $2p$ electron at $Z=29$ (Cu), the hydrogen-like formula gives $(Z\alpha)^2/4=1.1\times10^{-2}$, which is about $8\times10^2$ times that of hydrogen ($Z^2=841$ times) and can hardly be called a small correction any longer (Section 26.6).

26.3 Fine Structure and the Doublets of Alkali Atoms

26.3.1 Classification of levels by $j$ — term symbols ${}^2S_{1/2}$, ${}^2P_{1/2}$, ${}^2P_{3/2}$

Because of the spin–orbit interaction, the state $nl$ of one electron splits into two levels, $j=l+\tfrac12$ and $j=l-\tfrac12$ (an $s$ orbital has only one, $j=\tfrac12$). In spectroscopy these levels are written with the term symbol ${}^{2S+1}L_J$, as in Table 25.1 of Chapter 25. For one electron the spin is $S=\tfrac12$, so the number at the upper left is $2S+1=2$ (a doublet); the capital letter $L=S,P,D,F,\ldots$ corresponds to $l=0,1,2,3,\ldots$, and the subscript at the lower right corresponds to the total angular momentum $j$ (here the $S$ in “${}^2S$” is the symbol for $l=0$ and is distinct from the spin $S$). For example,

  • $3s$: ${}^2S_{1/2}$ ($2$ states)
  • $3p$: ${}^2P_{1/2}$ ($2$ states) and ${}^2P_{3/2}$ ($4$ states)—the total of $6$ states equals the number of states of the $3p$ level before the splitting, $2(2l+1)=6$
  • $3d$: ${}^2D_{3/2}$ ($4$ states) and ${}^2D_{5/2}$ ($6$ states)

These are the levels that result. In general, the level $j$ is $(2j+1)$-fold degenerate (in the absence of a magnetic field).

26.3.2 The D lines of Na

The Na atom has one $3s$ electron outside the closed shell $1s^22s^22p^6$. In the picture that explains the spectrum with orbitals alone, without considering spin, the $3p$ level is a single level that is threefold degenerate with $m=-1,0,+1$, and the $3p\to3s$ transition should be a single line (as we saw in Chapter 23, this does not agree with experiment). The light emitted when the $3s$ electron is excited to $3p$ and returns to $3s$ is the D line of Na (it is the same line that Fraunhofer named D as a dark line in the spectrum of sunlight, and it is also the yellow light of the sodium lamp). The $3s\,{}^2S_{1/2}$ level does not split, but $3p$ is split into ${}^2P_{1/2}$ and ${}^2P_{3/2}$ (Figure 26.2). The selection rules for electric dipole transitions (Chapter 52) are $\Delta l=\pm1$, $\Delta j=0,\pm1$, and both of these two transitions are allowed.

  • ${}^2P_{3/2}\to{}^2S_{1/2}$: the D$_2$ line, wavelength $588.995\ \mathrm{nm}$
  • ${}^2P_{1/2}\to{}^2S_{1/2}$: the D$_1$ line, wavelength $589.592\ \mathrm{nm}$

(The wavelengths are values in air.) The photon energy is about $2.10\ \mathrm{eV}$, and the energy difference between the two lines equals the spacing between the levels $3p_{3/2}$ and $3p_{1/2}$, $17.2\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}=2.13\ \mathrm{meV}$. This is a difference of only $0.1\ \%$ of the photon energy, but a spectroscope separates them into two distinct lines.

Why is the D$_2$ line twice as intense as the D$_1$ line?

Inside a discharge lamp, Na atoms are excited to $3p$ by electron collisions. The energy differences among the six states of $3p$ (distinguished down to the magnetic quantum number) are extremely small, $2\ \mathrm{meV}$, compared with the thermal energy of the discharge $k_{\mathrm{B}}T$ (on the order of $\sim0.1\ \mathrm{eV}$), so the six states are excited in nearly equal proportions. Since ${}^2P_{3/2}$ contains four states and ${}^2P_{1/2}$ contains two, the ratio of the numbers of excited atoms is $4:2=2:1$. Atoms in either level emit light with about the same probability (the lifetime is about $16\ \mathrm{ns}$), so the ratio of the light intensities equals the ratio of the statistical weights $(2j+1)$ of the levels, $\mathbf{2:1}$. (This $2:1$ is the value for a thin vapor, in which the light is not reabsorbed by the atoms. In a lamp with a dense vapor, the strongly absorbed D$_2$ line is reabsorbed more, and the ratio becomes smaller than $2:1$.)

26.3.4 Why is $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ larger than Slater's $Z^*$? — an interaction determined near the nucleus

Compared with $Z^*\approx2.2$, which determines the spread and the energy of the Na $3p$ electron, $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}=3.55$ obtained from the spin–orbit splitting is more than 50% larger. The reason is that the spin–orbit interaction is determined by the average $\expval{r^{-3}}$, which weights the region near the nucleus strongly. Most of the orbital of a valence electron lies outside the inner shells, and the negative charge of the core electrons nearly cancels the nuclear charge ($Z^*\approx2$). However, the wave function of a $p$ orbital also penetrates slightly into the vicinity of the nucleus, inside the inner shells (penetration, Chapter 18, Section 18.2), where the shielding is incomplete and the electron feels a strong electric field close to that of the nuclear charge $Z$. Since $r^{-3}$ counts that region heavily, the $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ seen by the spin–orbit interaction becomes larger than the $Z^*$ seen by the energy. Even so, the shielding is still effective to about 70% ($Z_{\mathrm{eff}}/Z\approx0.3$), and $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is much smaller than $Z$. As in Table 26.2, $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ grows almost in proportion to $Z$ ($Z_{\mathrm{eff}}/Z$ decreases slightly from $0.32$ to $0.26$), and because the $n^{-3}$ in $\zeta\propto Z_{\mathrm{eff}}^4/n^3$ drops to $(3/6)^3=1/8$ from Na to Cs, the splitting is not as steep as $Z^4$; from Na to Cs it increases at a rate of about $Z^2$ (Figure 26.3).

The spin–orbit interaction is only one of the relativistic corrections to the electron. The following three contributions of the same magnitude enter the $\alpha^2$ correction to the energy of the hydrogen atom.

  • The relativistic correction to the kinetic energy (the mass–velocity term): $-\dfrac{\hat{\bm{p}}^4}{8m_{\mathrm{e}}^3c^2}$ (the $p^4$ term of the kinetic energy $\sqrt{p^2c^2+m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^4}-m_{\mathrm{e}}c^2$)
  • The Darwin term: $\dfrac{\hbar^2}{8m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\nabla^2V$ (the effect of the electron trembling over a range about as large as the Compton wavelength. Since $\nabla^2V\propto\delta^3(\bm{r})$, it acts only on $s$ orbitals)
  • The spin–orbit interaction: $\xi(r)\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ (this chapter)

When the three first-order energies are combined, the levels of a hydrogen-like atom are determined by $n$ and $j$ alone and are given by the Dirac fine-structure formula (an expansion of the exact solution of the Dirac equation)

\begin{equation} E_{nj}=-\frac{Z^2E_{\mathrm{h}}}{2n^2}\left[1+\frac{(Z\alpha)^2}{n^2}\left(\frac{n}{j+\frac12}-\frac34\right)+\cdots\right] \label{eq:26-dirac} \end{equation}

(derived in Part XI, Chapter 63). Writing out the values of the three terms for $n=2$ gives the results in Table 26.3. The total agrees with the value of Eq. \eqref{eq:26-dirac}, and $2s_{1/2}$ and $2p_{1/2}$ have the same energy (independent of $l$, determined by $n$ and $j$ alone). 26.4 Atoms in a Magnetic Field — The Landé $g$ Factor and the Anomalous Zeeman Effect

26.4.1 The Hamiltonian in a magnetic field and two energy scales

Apply a uniform magnetic flux density $\bm{B}=B\bm{e}_z$ to an atom. The orbital magnetic moment of the electron, $\hat{\bm{\mu}}_l=-\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\hat{\bm{L}}/\hbar$, and the spin magnetic moment, $\hat{\bm{\mu}}_s=-g_s\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\hat{\bm{S}}/\hbar$ (Chapter 23, Section 23.3.6), interact with the magnetic field, and the following term is added to the Hamiltonian:

\begin{equation} \hat{H}_{\mathrm{Z}}=-(\hat{\bm{\mu}}_l+\hat{\bm{\mu}}_s)\cdot\bm{B}=\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B}{\hbar}\bigl(\hat{L}_z+g_s\hat{S}_z\bigr)\simeq\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B}{\hbar}\bigl(\hat{L}_z+2\hat{S}_z\bigr) \label{eq:26-HZ} \end{equation}

(The deviation of $g_s=2.0023$ from $2$ is only about $0.1\ \%$, so from here on we set $g_s=2$. The diamagnetic term proportional to $B^2$ is ignored in this chapter.) This has the form of the orbital-only case (the normal Zeeman effect in Chapter 23, Section 23.2) with the spin term $2\hat{S}_z$ added. The full Hamiltonian is

$$ \hat{H}=\hat{H}_0+\xi(r)\,\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}+\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B}{\hbar}\bigl(\hat{L}_z+2\hat{S}_z\bigr) $$

A yardstick for the strength of the magnetic field is to compare $\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B$ with the spin–orbit coupling constant $\zeta$. Numerically,

$$ \mu_{\mathrm{B}}B=5.788\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}\times\frac{B}{1\ \mathrm{T}}=0.4669\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}\times\frac{B}{1\ \mathrm{T}}=13.996\ \mathrm{GHz}\times\frac{B}{1\ \mathrm{T}}\ (\text{converted to frequency}) $$

For the $2p$ level of hydrogen, $\zeta=3.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}$ (corresponding to a magnetic field of about $0.5\ \mathrm{T}$), and for the $3p$ level of Na, $\zeta=1.42\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}$ (about $25\ \mathrm{T}$). Therefore, for the magnetic fields that can be produced in the laboratory (a few T), $\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B\ll\zeta$ holds for Na.

  • Weak magnetic field ($\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B\ll\zeta$): The spin–orbit interaction plays the leading role, and the Zeeman term is a perturbation. The orbital and spin angular momenta act as a single unit, $\hat{\bm{J}}=\hat{\bm{L}}+\hat{\bm{S}}$, and $j$ and $m_j$ are good quantum numbers. This is the present section (26.4).
  • Strong magnetic field ($\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B\gg\zeta$): The Zeeman term plays the leading role, and the spin–orbit interaction is a perturbation. The orbital and spin angular momenta become uncoupled, and $m_l$ and $m_s$ are good quantum numbers. This is the next section, 26.5 (the Paschen–Back effect).

In a weak magnetic field, within the level $j$, which is $(2j+1)$-fold degenerate, we treat the Zeeman term as a first-order perturbation. Since $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Z}}$ commutes with $\hat{J}_z$ and does not change $m_j$, within the same $j$ there are no matrix elements between states with different $m_j$, and the energy is simply the diagonal element $\mel{j,m_j}{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Z}}}{j,m_j}$ itself (the general treatment of degenerate perturbation theory is studied in Chapter 28). The mixing between different $j$ appears as a second-order effect, $(\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B)^2/\zeta$, which is small in a weak field.

26.4.2 The Landé $g$ factor — the vector model and projection

We want the average of $\hat{L}_z+2\hat{S}_z=\hat{J}_z+\hat{S}_z$ within the level $j$. The average of $\hat{J}_z$ is $m_j\hbar$, but the average of $\hat{S}_z$ is not obvious. We reason as follows.

Intuition: The vector model — averaging by precession

The spin–orbit interaction has the form $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$, that is, it is an interaction between $\bm{L}$ and $\bm{S}$. In the classical vector model, $\bm{L}$ and $\bm{S}$ precess rapidly about their sum $\bm{J}$ (Figure 26.4). The magnetic moment $\hat{\bm{\mu}}=-\dfrac{\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}{\hbar}(\hat{\bm{L}}+2\hat{\bm{S}})$ also moves together with $\bm{L}$ and $\bm{S}$. If the change caused by the external magnetic field is much slower than this precession, the magnetic moment is averaged: the component perpendicular to $\bm{J}$ vanishes, and only the component parallel to $\bm{J}$ remains. The component of $(\bm{L}+2\bm{S})$ along $\bm{J}$ is $\dfrac{(\bm{L}+2\bm{S})\cdot\bm{J}}{\lvert\bm{J}\rvert^2}\bm{J}$. Therefore, the magnetic moment is proportional to $\bm{J}$ and can be written in the form $\hat{\bm{\mu}}=-g_J\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\hat{\bm{J}}/\hbar$. This constant of proportionality $g_J$ is the Landé $g$ factor.

Math Tool: Vector operators and the projection theorem

(i) A triple of operators $\hat{\bm{A}}=(\hat{A}_x,\hat{A}_y,\hat{A}_z)$ is a vector operator if its commutation relations with the total angular momentum $\hat{\bm{J}}$ are $\comm{\hat{J}_i}{\hat{A}_j}=i\hbar\sum_k\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat{A}_k$ (that is, it transforms like an ordinary vector when the coordinate axes are rotated). $\hat{\bm{L}}$, $\hat{\bm{S}}$, $\hat{\bm{J}}$ itself, the position $\hat{\bm{r}}$, the momentum $\hat{\bm{p}}$, and the magnetic moment $\hat{\bm{\mu}}$ are such operators.

(ii) Within the $(2j+1)$ states with fixed $l$, $s$, $j$ (the same level), the matrix elements of a vector operator $\hat{\bm{A}}$ are a constant multiple of the matrix elements of $\hat{\bm{J}}$: $\hat{\bm{A}}\to c\,\hat{\bm{J}}$. The reason is that $\hat{\bm{A}}$ and $\hat{\bm{J}}$ have the same form of commutation relations with $\hat{J}_z$ and $\hat{J}_\pm$, so within the $(2j+1)$-dimensional space, in which the selection rule that changes $m$ only by $0,\pm1$ and the manner of dependence on $m$ (the form $\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m\pm1)}$) are the same, the action of $\hat{\bm{A}}$ is fixed to be a constant multiple of the action of $\hat{\bm{J}}$.

(iii) The constant $c$ is determined by taking the scalar product of both sides of $\hat{\bm{A}}\to c\,\hat{\bm{J}}$ with $\hat{\bm{J}}$. From $\mel{j}{\hat{\bm{A}}\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}}{j}=c\,\mel{j}{\hat{\bm{J}}^2}{j}=c\,j(j+1)\hbar^2$ we obtain $c=\mel{j}{\hat{\bm{A}}\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}}{j}\big/\bigl[j(j+1)\hbar^2\bigr]$. This is Eq. \eqref{eq:26-proj} below (a special case of the Wigner–Eckart theorem). Classically, it corresponds to the vector model above: “of a rapidly precessing $\bm{A}$, only the component projected onto $\bm{J}$ remains after averaging.”

In quantum mechanics, this averaging holds exactly as the projection theorem. That is, within a level with fixed $l$, $s$, $j$, the matrix elements of any vector operator $\hat{\bm{A}}$ can be written as

\begin{equation} \mel{j,m'}{\hat{\bm{A}}}{j,m}=\frac{\mel{j}{\hat{\bm{A}}\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}}{j}}{j(j+1)\hbar^2}\,\mel{j,m'}{\hat{\bm{J}}}{j,m} \label{eq:26-proj} \end{equation}

Let us take $\hat{\bm{A}}=\hat{\bm{L}}+2\hat{\bm{S}}$ and compute the scalar product $(\hat{\bm{L}}+2\hat{\bm{S}})\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}$. From $\hat{\bm{L}}=\hat{\bm{J}}-\hat{\bm{S}}$ we get $\hat{\bm{L}}^2=\hat{\bm{J}}^2-2\hat{\bm{J}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}+\hat{\bm{S}}^2$, and therefore $\hat{\bm{J}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}=\tfrac12\bigl(\hat{\bm{J}}^2+\hat{\bm{S}}^2-\hat{\bm{L}}^2\bigr)$. Since $\hat{\bm{L}}+2\hat{\bm{S}}=\hat{\bm{J}}+\hat{\bm{S}}$, we have

\begin{align} (\hat{\bm{L}}+2\hat{\bm{S}})\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}&=(\hat{\bm{J}}+\hat{\bm{S}})\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}=\hat{\bm{J}}^2+\hat{\bm{S}}\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}\notag\\ &=\hat{\bm{J}}^2+\tfrac12\bigl(\hat{\bm{J}}^2+\hat{\bm{S}}^2-\hat{\bm{L}}^2\bigr)=\tfrac32\hat{\bm{J}}^2+\tfrac12\hat{\bm{S}}^2-\tfrac12\hat{\bm{L}}^2 \label{eq:26-LS2J} \end{align}

This is diagonal in terms of the eigenvalues $j(j+1)\hbar^2$, $s(s+1)\hbar^2$, $l(l+1)\hbar^2$ of $\hat{\bm{J}}^2$, $\hat{\bm{S}}^2$, $\hat{\bm{L}}^2$ (by the same method as in the previous chapter, we expressed $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ in terms of $\hat{\bm{J}}^2,\hat{\bm{L}}^2,\hat{\bm{S}}^2$).

Definition 26.1: Landé $g$ factor

When the magnetic moment of the level $(l,s,j)$ can be written as $\hat{\bm{\mu}}=-g_J\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\,\hat{\bm{J}}/\hbar$ (within the same level), the constant of proportionality $g_J$ is called the Landé $g$ factor. From $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Z}}=-\hat{\bm{\mu}}\cdot\bm{B}$, the energy in a weak magnetic field is $E=g_J\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\,m_j\,B$.

Theorem 26.2: Landé's formula for $g_J$

The $g$ factor of a level $(l,s,j)$ split by the spin–orbit interaction is, with $g_s=2$, given by

\begin{equation} g_J=1+\frac{j(j+1)+s(s+1)-l(l+1)}{2\,j(j+1)} \label{eq:26-gJ} \end{equation}

For a single electron ($s=\tfrac12$), if $j=l+\tfrac12$ then $g_J=\dfrac{2(l+1)}{2l+1}$, and if $j=l-\tfrac12$ then $g_J=\dfrac{2l}{2l+1}$.

Derivation

Substitute the eigenvalues of Eq. \eqref{eq:26-LS2J} into the coefficients of Eq. \eqref{eq:26-proj}.

$$ g_J=\frac{\tfrac32j(j+1)+\tfrac12s(s+1)-\tfrac12l(l+1)}{j(j+1)} =\frac32+\frac{s(s+1)-l(l+1)}{2j(j+1)} =1+\frac{j(j+1)+s(s+1)-l(l+1)}{2j(j+1)} $$

(The last rearrangement uses $\tfrac32=1+\tfrac12=1+\dfrac{j(j+1)}{2j(j+1)}$.) Substituting $s=\tfrac12$ and $j=l+\tfrac12$ gives $j(j+1)=(l+\tfrac12)(l+\tfrac32)=l^2+2l+\tfrac34$, and since $j(j+1)+\tfrac34-l(l+1)=l+\tfrac32=j+1$, we get $g_J=1+\dfrac{j+1}{2j(j+1)}=1+\dfrac{1}{2j}=1+\dfrac{1}{2l+1}=\dfrac{2(l+1)}{2l+1}$. For $j=l-\tfrac12$, $j(j+1)=l^2-\tfrac14$, and since $j(j+1)+\tfrac34-l(l+1)=\tfrac12-l=-j$, we get $g_J=1-\dfrac{j}{2j(j+1)}=1-\dfrac{1}{2(j+1)}=1-\dfrac{1}{2l+1}=\dfrac{2l}{2l+1}$.

∎

If $g_s$ is not set equal to $2$, then from $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}=\tfrac12\bigl(\hat{\bm{J}}^2+\hat{\bm{L}}^2-\hat{\bm{S}}^2\bigr)$ and $\hat{\bm{S}}\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}=\tfrac12\bigl(\hat{\bm{J}}^2+\hat{\bm{S}}^2-\hat{\bm{L}}^2\bigr)$ we can find the eigenvalue of $(\hat{\bm{L}}+g_s\hat{\bm{S}})\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}$, and the result is

$$ g_J=1+(g_s-1)\,\frac{j(j+1)+s(s+1)-l(l+1)}{2\,j(j+1)} $$

For $g_s=2$ this reduces to Eq. \eqref{eq:26-gJ}. For ${}^2S_{1/2}$ ($l=0$, $j=s=\tfrac12$), $g_J=g_s=2.0023$, which we use in the discussion of ESR in Section 26.7.

For the one-electron case we can also check the result directly, without using the projection theorem. In the previous chapter (Section 25.8.3) we found that the average of the spin in $\ket{j,m_j}$ is $\expval{\hat{S}_z}=\pm\dfrac{m_j\hbar}{2l+1}$ ($j=l\pm\tfrac12$). The average of $\hat{L}_z+2\hat{S}_z=\hat{J}_z+\hat{S}_z$ is

$$ \expval{\hat{L}_z+2\hat{S}_z}=m_j\hbar\pm\frac{m_j\hbar}{2l+1}=m_j\hbar\Bigl(1\pm\frac{1}{2l+1}\Bigr)=g_J\,m_j\hbar $$

so that, for each sign, $g_J=\dfrac{2l+2}{2l+1}$ ($j=l+\tfrac12$) and $\dfrac{2l}{2l+1}$ ($j=l-\tfrac12$), in agreement with Eq. \eqref{eq:26-gJ}. Also, the state with $m_j=j=l+\tfrac12$ is the single product state $R\,Y_l^l\ket{\uparrow}$, and we can check directly that $\expval{\hat{L}_z+2\hat{S}_z}=l\hbar+2\cdot\tfrac12\hbar=(l+1)\hbar$ and $g_Jj=\dfrac{2(l+1)}{2l+1}\Bigl(l+\tfrac12\Bigr)=l+1$.

We also show a concrete calculation using Clebsch–Gordan coefficients. Take $l=1$. From the eigenvectors of the $2\times2$ block in the previous chapter, the two states with $m_j=+\tfrac12$ are as follows (here we write $\alpha$ and $\beta$ of the previous chapter's notation as $\ket{\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\downarrow}$; they are spin functions, not the fine-structure constant): for $j=\tfrac32$, $\sqrt{\tfrac23}\,R\,Y_1^0\ket{\uparrow}+\sqrt{\tfrac13}\,R\,Y_1^1\ket{\downarrow}$, and for $j=\tfrac12$, $\sqrt{\tfrac13}\,R\,Y_1^0\ket{\uparrow}-\sqrt{\tfrac23}\,R\,Y_1^1\ket{\downarrow}$ (superpositions of $(m_l,m_s)=(0,\uparrow)$ and $(+1,\downarrow)$). The operator $\hat{L}_z+2\hat{S}_z$ is diagonal in the basis of product states, with eigenvalue $(m_l+2m_s)\hbar$, so taking the average weighted by the probability of each state gives

$$ j=\tfrac32:\ \tfrac23\,(0+1)+\tfrac13\,(1-1)=\tfrac23=\tfrac43\cdot\tfrac12,\qquad j=\tfrac12:\ \tfrac13\,(0+1)+\tfrac23\,(1-1)=\tfrac13=\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12 $$

These are in units of $\hbar$, and they agree with $g_Jm_j$ ($g_J=\tfrac43$ for $j=\tfrac32$ and $g_J=\tfrac23$ for $j=\tfrac12$).

Discussion: Limiting cases of $g_J$

Eq. \eqref{eq:26-gJ} places $g_J$ above or below $1$ according to the sign of the numerator $j(j+1)+s(s+1)-l(l+1)$. (i) If there is no spin ($s=0$, $j=l$), then $g_J=1$. Only the orbital magnetic moment contributes, and we return to the normal Zeeman effect of Chapter 23. (ii) If there is no orbital motion ($l=0$, $j=s$), then $g_J=2$, which equals the spin $g$ factor $g_s=2$. (iii) For $j=l+\tfrac12$, $g_J\gt1$, and for $j=l-\tfrac12$, $g_J\lt1$ (this can be understood as the magnetic moments reinforcing each other when the spin is parallel to the orbital motion and weakening each other when it is antiparallel).

Supplement: $g_J$ of many-electron atoms

For a term ${}^{2S+1}L_J$ of $LS$ coupling in a many-electron atom as well, the formula obtained from Eq. \eqref{eq:26-gJ} by replacing $j,l,s$ with the uppercase $J,L,S$ holds (because the projection theorem can be used): $g_J=1+\dfrac{J(J+1)+S(S+1)-L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)}$. For example, ${}^3P_2$ and ${}^3P_1$ have $g_J=\tfrac32$, and ${}^3P_0$ has $J=0$ and hence no Zeeman splitting (Exercise 26.3). The formula is used for the magnetic moments of rare-earth ions (Section 26.7).

Using Eq. \eqref{eq:26-LS2J} of the projection theorem, find $g_J$ for (1) ${}^2P_{3/2}$, (2) ${}^2P_{1/2}$, (3) ${}^2D_{3/2}$, and (4) ${}^2D_{5/2}$, and compare with the values from the formula \eqref{eq:26-gJ}.

Solution. We have $s=\tfrac12$ and $s(s+1)=\tfrac34$. The eigenvalue of $(\hat{\bm{L}}+2\hat{\bm{S}})\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}$ (in units of $\hbar^2$) is $\tfrac32j(j+1)+\tfrac38-\tfrac12l(l+1)$.

(1) $l=1$, $j=\tfrac32$: $j(j+1)=\tfrac{15}{4}$. $\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac{15}{4}+\tfrac38-1=\tfrac{45}{8}+\tfrac38-1=5$. $g_J=\dfrac{5}{15/4}=\dfrac43$.

(2) $l=1$, $j=\tfrac12$: $j(j+1)=\tfrac34$. $\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac34+\tfrac38-1=\tfrac98+\tfrac38-1=\tfrac12$. $g_J=\dfrac{1/2}{3/4}=\dfrac23$.

(3) $l=2$, $j=\tfrac32$: $\tfrac{45}{8}+\tfrac38-3=3$. $g_J=\dfrac{3}{15/4}=\dfrac45$.

(4) $l=2$, $j=\tfrac52$: $j(j+1)=\tfrac{35}{4}$. $\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac{35}{4}+\tfrac38-3=\tfrac{105}{8}+\tfrac38-3=\tfrac{21}{2}$. $g_J=\dfrac{21/2}{35/4}=\dfrac65$.

Note also that the sum of $g_J$ for the two levels with the same $l$ is $\tfrac43+\tfrac23=2$ for $l=1$ and $\tfrac65+\tfrac45=2$ for $l=2$, so that always $g_{l+1/2}+g_{l-1/2}=2$. As we will see in Section 26.5, this relation follows from the fact that the sum of the diagonal elements of $\hat{L}_z+2\hat{S}_z$ over the two states with the same $m_j$ does not change (conservation of the trace).

26.4.3 The anomalous Zeeman effect — the Na D lines

The photon energy of the transition from the upper level (up) to the lower level (low) is $h\nu_0$, the value in the absence of a magnetic field, plus the difference between the Zeeman energies of the two levels:

\begin{equation} h\nu=h\nu_0+\bigl(g_{\mathrm{up}}m_{\mathrm{up}}-g_{\mathrm{low}}m_{\mathrm{low}}\bigr)\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B \label{eq:26-Zshift} \end{equation}

Here $g_{\mathrm{up}},g_{\mathrm{low}}$ are the $g_J$ of the upper and lower levels, and $m_{\mathrm{up}},m_{\mathrm{low}}$ are the $m_j$ of the upper and lower levels. The selection rule for electric dipole transitions is $\Delta m_j=m_{\mathrm{up}}-m_{\mathrm{low}}=0,\pm1$ (Chapter 52). The lines with $\Delta m_j=0$ are called $\pi$ components (linearly polarized parallel to the magnetic field; they are not emitted along the direction of the magnetic field), and the lines with $\Delta m_j=\pm1$ are called $\sigma$ components (circularly polarized about the magnetic field; seen from a direction perpendicular to the magnetic field, they are linearly polarized perpendicular to the magnetic field).

When only the orbital motion is considered (no spin), the $g$ of the upper and lower levels is $1$ for both, so $h\nu-h\nu_0=(m_{\mathrm{up}}-m_{\mathrm{low}})\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B=0,\pm\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B$, and every transition gives three lines (the Lorentz triplet). This is Zeeman's discovery of 1896 (the Na D lines broaden in a magnetic field) together with Lorentz's classical explanation, the normal Zeeman effect (its treatment by degenerate perturbation theory is given in Chapter 28). However, when the Na D lines are resolved well, they split not into three lines but into four lines for D$_1$ and six lines for D$_2$. Because the electron has spin and $g_J$ differs between the upper and lower levels (${}^2P_{1/2}$ has $\tfrac23$, ${}^2P_{3/2}$ has $\tfrac43$, and ${}^2S_{1/2}$ has $2$), the number of lines and their spacings change. Since this is not normal, it is called the anomalous Zeeman effect.

D$_1$ line (${}^2P_{1/2}\to{}^2S_{1/2}$)

For the upper level, $g_{\mathrm{up}}=\tfrac23$ and $m_{\mathrm{up}}=\pm\tfrac12$; for the lower level, $g_{\mathrm{low}}=2$ and $m_{\mathrm{low}}=\pm\tfrac12$. All $2\times2=4$ combinations are allowed by $\Delta m_j=0,\pm1$. From Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Zshift},

$$ \begin{aligned} (m_{\mathrm{up}},m_{\mathrm{low}})=(+\tfrac12,+\tfrac12)&:\ \tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12-2\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac13-1=-\tfrac23,\qquad(-\tfrac12,-\tfrac12):\ +\tfrac23\quad(\pi),\\ (m_{\mathrm{up}},m_{\mathrm{low}})=(+\tfrac12,-\tfrac12)&:\ \tfrac13+1=+\tfrac43,\qquad(-\tfrac12,+\tfrac12):\ -\tfrac13-1=-\tfrac43\quad(\sigma) \end{aligned} $$

(in units of $\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B$).

D$_2$ line (${}^2P_{3/2}\to{}^2S_{1/2}$)

For the upper level, $g_{\mathrm{up}}=\tfrac43$ and $m_{\mathrm{up}}=\pm\tfrac32,\pm\tfrac12$; for the lower level, $g_{\mathrm{low}}=2$ and $m_{\mathrm{low}}=\pm\tfrac12$. Of the $2\times4=8$ combinations, the two with $\Delta m_j=\pm2$ ($\tfrac32\to-\tfrac12$ and $-\tfrac32\to+\tfrac12$) are forbidden, leaving six lines.

$$ \begin{aligned} &(\tfrac32\to\tfrac12):\ \tfrac43\cdot\tfrac32-2\cdot\tfrac12=2-1=+1,\quad(-\tfrac32\to-\tfrac12):\ -1\quad(\sigma),\\ &(\tfrac12\to\tfrac12):\ \tfrac43\cdot\tfrac12-1=-\tfrac13,\quad(-\tfrac12\to-\tfrac12):\ +\tfrac13\quad(\pi),\\ &(\tfrac12\to-\tfrac12):\ \tfrac23+1=+\tfrac53,\quad(-\tfrac12\to\tfrac12):\ -\tfrac53\quad(\sigma) \end{aligned} $$

Discussion: Before and after introducing spin — how many D lines do we see?

Comparing how many lines the $3p\to3s$ transition of Na gives in a magnetic field, in the picture without spin and in the picture with spin, we find the following.

  • Without spin (orbital only): with no magnetic field, $3p$ is threefold degenerate with $m=-1,0,+1$, and the D line is one line. When a magnetic field is applied, the normal Zeeman effect gives three lines (the Lorentz triplet).
  • With spin: even with no magnetic field, the spin–orbit interaction splits $3p$ into ${}^2P_{3/2}$ and ${}^2P_{1/2}$, and the D line becomes two lines (D$_2$ and D$_1$, in agreement with experiment). When a magnetic field is applied, D$_1$ gives four lines and D$_2$ gives six lines, 10 lines in total (the anomalous Zeeman effect, in agreement with experiment).

Only after spin is introduced can we explain both the doublet of two lines without a magnetic field and the patterns of four and six lines with a magnetic field. This became one piece of experimental support for the spin hypothesis of Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit.

26.5 From Weak to Strong Magnetic Fields — The Paschen–Back Effect

26.5.1 The $6\times6$ matrix for $2p$ — adding the Zeeman term to the matrix of the previous chapter

Note on notation. In this section we write the spin functions as $\ket{\uparrow}$ (the $\alpha$ of the previous chapter) and $\ket{\downarrow}$ (the $\beta$ of the previous chapter). This is unrelated to the fine-structure constant $\alpha=e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c)$ of Section 26.2.

In the previous chapter (Section 25.4), we built the $6\times6$ matrix of $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ on the six functions $R\,Y_1^{m_l}\ket{\uparrow}$, $R\,Y_1^{m_l}\ket{\downarrow}$ of the $p$ orbital, and saw that it splits into $1\times1$ and $2\times2$ blocks for each $m_j$. In a magnetic field the Hamiltonian is $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{SO}}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Z}}=\dfrac{\zeta}{\hbar^2}\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}+\dfrac{\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B}{\hbar}(\hat{L}_z+2\hat{S}_z)$ (we put $\zeta=\zeta_{np}$, assume that the radial function does not change, and consider only the six $p$ states with the same $n$). The Zeeman term is already diagonal on the basis $R\,Y_1^{m_l}\ket{\uparrow}$, $R\,Y_1^{m_l}\ket{\downarrow}$, and its eigenvalue is $\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B\,(m_l+2m_s)$ ($m_s=\pm\tfrac12$). We introduce the dimensionless magnetic field

\begin{equation} b\equiv\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B}{\zeta} \label{eq:26-b} \end{equation}

and order the basis as in the previous chapter, $(m_l,m_s)=(-1,\downarrow),(-1,\uparrow),(0,\downarrow),(0,\uparrow),(+1,\downarrow),(+1,\uparrow)$. Then the $6\times6$ matrix of $\hat{H}/\zeta$ is the matrix of $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}/\hbar^2$ of the previous chapter plus the diagonal elements of the Zeeman term, $b\,(m_l+2m_s)=b\,(-2,0,-1,+1,0,+2)$.

\begin{equation} \frac{\hat{H}}{\zeta}= \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac12-2b&0&0&0&0&0\\ 0&-\tfrac12&\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}&0&0&0\\ 0&\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}&-b&0&0&0\\ 0&0&0&b&\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}&0\\ 0&0&0&\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}&-\tfrac12&0\\ 0&0&0&0&0&\tfrac12+2b \end{bmatrix} \label{eq:26-6x6} \end{equation}

(To the diagonal elements $\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,0,0,-\tfrac12,\tfrac12$ of the matrix of $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ we added $-2b,0,-b,+b,0,+2b$. For example, an off-diagonal element is $\mel{-1,\uparrow}{\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}}{0,\downarrow}/\hbar^2=\tfrac12\mel{-1,\uparrow}{\hat{L}_-\hat{S}_+}{0,\downarrow}/\hbar^2=\tfrac12\sqrt{1\cdot2}\cdot1=\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ ($\hat{L}_-\ket{l{=}1,m{=}0}=\sqrt2\,\hbar\ket{1,-1}$, $\hat{S}_+\ket{\downarrow}=\hbar\ket{\uparrow}$), and a diagonal element is $\hat{L}_z\hat{S}_z/\hbar^2=m_lm_s$ ($-\tfrac12$ for $(-1,\uparrow)$).) Since $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{Z}}$ also commutes with $\hat{J}_z$ and does not change $m_j=m+m_s$, the matrix keeps the same block-diagonal form as in the previous chapter:

  • $m_j=-\tfrac32$: $1\times1$, $E/\zeta=\tfrac12-2b$ (state $R\,Y_1^{-1}\ket{\downarrow}$)
  • $m_j=-\tfrac12$: $2\times2$, basis $(R\,Y_1^{-1}\ket{\uparrow},\ R\,Y_1^0\ket{\downarrow})$, $\begin{bmatrix}-\tfrac12&\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\\ \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}&-b\end{bmatrix}$
  • $m_j=+\tfrac12$: $2\times2$, basis $(R\,Y_1^{0}\ket{\uparrow},\ R\,Y_1^{1}\ket{\downarrow})$, $\begin{bmatrix}b&\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\\ \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}&-\tfrac12\end{bmatrix}$
  • $m_j=+\tfrac32$: $1\times1$, $E/\zeta=\tfrac12+2b$ (state $R\,Y_1^{1}\ket{\uparrow}$)

It is enough to examine how the eigenvalues $\tfrac12$ and $-1$ of the $2\times2$ blocks of the previous chapter at zero field, $b=0$ ($j=\tfrac32$ and $j=\tfrac12$), change with the magnetic field.

26.5.2 Analytic solutions of the blocks

The cases $m_j=\pm\tfrac32$ consist of only one product state ($R\,Y_1^{+1}\ket{\uparrow}$ or $R\,Y_1^{-1}\ket{\downarrow}$), so there is no mixing, and the energy is exactly proportional to the magnetic field.

$$ E\bigl(m_j=\pm\tfrac32\bigr)=\zeta\Bigl(\tfrac12\pm2b\Bigr)=\frac{\zeta}{2}\pm2\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B . $$

The eigenvalues $\lambda$ of the block $\begin{bmatrix}b&\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\\ \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}&-\tfrac12\end{bmatrix}$ for $m_j=+\tfrac12$ follow from the characteristic equation $\lambda^2-(\text{sum of the diagonal elements})\lambda+(\text{determinant})=0$. The sum of the diagonal elements is $b-\tfrac12$ and the determinant is $b\cdot(-\tfrac12)-\tfrac12=-\dfrac{b+1}{2}$, so

$$ \lambda^2-\Bigl(b-\frac12\Bigr)\lambda-\frac{b+1}{2}=0 \ \Longrightarrow\ \lambda=\frac12\Bigl[\Bigl(b-\frac12\Bigr)\pm\sqrt{\Bigl(b-\frac12\Bigr)^2+2(b+1)}\Bigr]. $$

The expression under the square root can be rearranged as $\bigl(b-\tfrac12\bigr)^2+2b+2=b^2+b+\tfrac94=\bigl(b+\tfrac12\bigr)^2+2$. The block for $m_j=-\tfrac12$ is obtained by replacing $b\to-b$ (interchanging the two rows and the two columns gives the same form), so, taken together,

\begin{equation} \frac{E_\pm(m_j=\pm\tfrac12)}{\zeta}=\frac12\Bigl[\Bigl(\pm b-\frac12\Bigr)\pm'\sqrt{\Bigl(\pm b+\frac12\Bigr)^2+2}\Bigr] \label{eq:26-block} \end{equation}

(the outer $\pm$ refers to $m_j=\pm\tfrac12$, and the inner $\pm'$ to the upper and lower branches). For example, for $m_j=+\tfrac12$, $\dfrac{E}{\zeta}=\dfrac12\Bigl[b-\dfrac12\pm\sqrt{\bigl(b+\tfrac12\bigr)^2+2}\Bigr]$. At $b=0$ this gives $\tfrac12\bigl[-\tfrac12\pm\tfrac32\bigr]=\tfrac12,\ -1$, which returns to the eigenvalues of the previous chapter ($j=\tfrac32$ and $j=\tfrac12$). The six levels drawn with this formula are shown in Figure 26.6, and numerical values in Table 26.6.

26.5.3 The weak-field and strong-field limits

Weak-field limit ($b\ll1$). Differentiating Eq. \eqref{eq:26-block} with respect to $b$ and setting $b=0$, we obtain, for the upper branch of $m_j=+\tfrac12$, $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd b}\dfrac12\Bigl[b-\dfrac12+\sqrt{(b+\tfrac12)^2+2}\Bigr]_{b=0}=\dfrac12\Bigl[1+\dfrac{1/2}{3/2}\Bigr]=\dfrac23$, and for the lower branch $\dfrac12\Bigl[1-\dfrac13\Bigr]=\dfrac13$. Comparing with $g_Jm_j$ of the previous section, the upper branch is $j=\tfrac32$: $g_Jm_j=\tfrac43\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac23$, and the lower branch is $j=\tfrac12$: $g_Jm_j=\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac13$, in complete agreement. The slope $\pm2$ for $m_j=\pm\tfrac32$ also agrees with $\tfrac43\cdot\tfrac32=2$. In the form $E=E_{\mathrm{SO}}(j)+g_Jm_j\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B$, the weak-field result of Section 26.4 has thus been reproduced independently of the matrix diagonalization. Moreover, the sum of the diagonal elements (the trace) of the Zeeman term in each $m_j$ block is, for $m_j=+\tfrac12$, $b\cdot(1+0)$, that is, $2m_j\,b$, which equals the sum of the two slopes, $\tfrac23+\tfrac13=1=2m_j$. This is the reason for $g_{l+1/2}+g_{l-1/2}=2$ ($\tfrac43+\tfrac23=2$).

Strong-field limit ($b\gg1$). Since the Zeeman term dominates, $R\,Y_1^{m_l}\ket{\uparrow}$ and $R\,Y_1^{m_l}\ket{\downarrow}$ are approximate eigenstates, and the energy is $\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B\,(m_l+2m_s)$. To this we add the spin–orbit interaction $\zeta\,\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}/\hbar^2$ as a first-order perturbation. Of the expression in terms of ladder operators from the previous chapter, $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}=\tfrac12(\hat{L}_+\hat{S}_-+\hat{L}_-\hat{S}_+)+\hat{L}_z\hat{S}_z$, the first term changes $(m_l,m_s)$ to $(m_l\pm1,m_s\mp1)$, so it has no diagonal element in the same state. The only diagonal element comes from $\hat{L}_z\hat{S}_z$, and it is $\hbar^2m_lm_s$. Therefore

\begin{equation} E=\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B\,(m_l+2m_s)+\zeta\,m_lm_s\qquad(\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B\gg\zeta) \label{eq:26-strong} \end{equation}

(the two degenerate states $(+1,\downarrow)$ and $(-1,\uparrow)$ both have $m_l+2m_s=0$, but there is no matrix element of $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ between them, so the first-order perturbation may be left diagonal). In terms of $b$, these straight lines are $E/\zeta=b\,(m_l+2m_s)+m_lm_s$, with slopes $2,1,0,0,-1,-2$ and intercepts $\tfrac12,0,-\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12$. These are the gray dotted lines in Figure 26.6, and the values in Table 26.6 at $b=10$ ($20.5=10\cdot2+\tfrac12$, $10.0474\approx10\cdot1+0$, $-0.4477\approx0\cdot10-\tfrac12$, and so on) have already approached the asymptotes. Whereas the weak-field slopes are fractions ($\pm\tfrac23,\pm\tfrac13$), the strong-field slopes are the integers $(m_l+2m_s)$: the orbital motion and the spin respond to the magnetic field separately.

Intuition: From weak to strong fields — the “unraveling” of orbital motion and spin

In a weak field, the spin–orbit interaction ties the orbital motion and the spin together and keeps $\bm{J}$ conserved, so the magnetic field acts on $\bm{J}$ with $g_J$ times the strength. As the field grows stronger, $\bm{L}$ and $\bm{S}$ break free of the knot of the spin–orbit interaction and each precesses independently about the magnetic field $\bm{B}$. The region of most rapid change is $b\approx1$, where the strength of the knot ($\zeta$) and the magnetic field ($\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B$) are of the same magnitude. At this field, the precession of $\bm{L}$ under the action of the field (angular frequency $\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B/\hbar$), the precession of $\bm{S}$ (angular frequency $g_s\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B/\hbar\approx2\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B/\hbar$), and the precession of $\bm{L}$ and $\bm{S}$ about $\bm{J}$ due to the spin–orbit interaction (angular frequency of the order of $\zeta/\hbar$) all proceed at the same rate.

26.5.4 The boundary field — $\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B=\zeta$

The boundary between the weak and strong fields is $b=1$, that is,

\begin{equation} B_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{\zeta}{\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}=17.28\ \mathrm{T}\times\frac{\zeta}{1\ \mathrm{meV}} \label{eq:26-Bcross} \end{equation}

(with $\mu_{\mathrm{B}}=0.05788\ \mathrm{meV/T}$). For the hydrogen $2p$ level ($\zeta=0.0302\ \mathrm{meV}$) we get $B_{\mathrm{c}}=0.52\ \mathrm{T}$, and for the Li $2p$ level ($\zeta=0.0277\ \mathrm{meV}$) $0.48\ \mathrm{T}$, so the Paschen–Back regime is entered at the field of an ordinary electromagnet. For the Na $3p$ level ($\zeta=1.42\ \mathrm{meV}$) the field is $24.5\ \mathrm{T}$ (a large high-field facility is required), and for the Cs $6p$ level ($\zeta=45.8\ \mathrm{meV}$) it is about $790\ \mathrm{T}$, which cannot be realized as a static field. Thus, the lighter the atom, the smaller $\zeta$ is, and the easier it is to observe the Paschen–Back effect in the laboratory. The change of the Zeeman pattern in a strong field, discovered by F. Paschen and E. Back in 1912, is that the spectrum returns to the three-line pattern of the normal Zeeman effect (with fine structure).

The reason can be read from Eq. \eqref{eq:26-strong}. In a strong field, $m_s$ does not change in an optical transition ($\Delta m_s=0$), so the change in the photon energy for $3p\,(m_l,m_s)\to3s\,(0,m_s)$ is $\Delta E=\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B\,m_l+\zeta\,m_lm_s$. The first term gives the spacing of the Lorentz triplet ($m_l=0,\pm1$), and the second term further splits each outer line into a doublet whose two components are shifted by $\pm\zeta/2$ ($m_l=\pm1$, $m_s=\pm\tfrac12$). Altogether there are five lines (the two states with $m_l=0$ have the same energy). With increasing field, the four lines of D$_1$ and the six lines of D$_2$ “unravel” into a set of three lines.

Supplement: Levels do not cross

In Figure 26.6, the two levels with the same $m_j$ (the solid and dashed lines of the same color) do not cross. The two levels of $m_j=-\tfrac12$ come closest at $b=\tfrac12$, where the separation is $\sqrt2\,\zeta$ by Eq. \eqref{eq:26-block} (almost the same as the separation $\tfrac32\zeta$ without a field). This is called the non-crossing rule (levels with the same symmetry do not cross). On the other hand, levels with different $m_j$ may cross (for example, the point at $b=1$ where the levels $-\tfrac12$ (lower) and $-\tfrac32$ both equal $-1.5$). The conserved quantity $m_j$ decides whether level repulsion is allowed or not. In Part V, we learn the general reason why degenerate or nearly degenerate levels repel each other (Chapter 28).

26.6 X-Ray Spectra and the $2p$ Spin–Orbit Splitting in XAS and XPS

26.6.1 The doublet of characteristic X-rays — Cu Kα$_1$ and Kα$_2$

In elements of large atomic number, the core electrons move very close to the nucleus, in a strong electric field close to that of the nuclear charge $Z$. Because of the $Z^4$ law of Section 26.2, the spin–orbit interaction is therefore far larger than for valence electrons, with a magnitude ranging from $\mathrm{eV}$ to $\mathrm{keV}$. The most practical place where this shows up is X-ray spectroscopy with X-ray tubes and synchrotrons.

When an electron beam strikes a Cu plate, a $1s$ electron (K shell) is knocked out, and a hole is created in $1s$. A $2p$ electron then drops into it and emits an X-ray. This line is called the Kα line (it is the $n=2\to1$ transition, or, in Bohr's language, the transition from the L shell to the K shell. Moseley's law was treated in Chapter 5, in Chapter 15, Section 15.6.5, and in Chapter 18, Section 18.3.5). Since the $2p$ level is split by the spin–orbit interaction into $2p_{3/2}$ and $2p_{1/2}$, Kα consists of two lines.

  • Kα$_1$: $2p_{3/2}\to1s$, wavelength $1.5405929\ \text{Å}$
  • Kα$_2$: $2p_{1/2}\to1s$, wavelength $1.5444274\ \text{Å}$

The photon energy is $E=hc/\lambda$, and with $hc=12\,398.42\ \mathrm{eV\,\text{Å}}$ this gives $8047.8\ \mathrm{eV}$ for Kα$_1$ and $8027.8\ \mathrm{eV}$ for Kα$_2$, so the difference is

$$ \Delta E=E(\mathrm{K}\alpha_1)-E(\mathrm{K}\alpha_2)=19.98\ \mathrm{eV} $$

This is none other than the separation $\tfrac32\zeta_{2p}$ between $2p_{3/2}$ and $2p_{1/2}$ (the final state $1s$ is the same, so only the difference of the initial states matters). The order of the two levels (the electron energy is higher for $2p_{3/2}$) corresponds to the fact that Kα$_1$ has the larger photon energy (the shorter wavelength). Also, the transition from $2s$ to $1s$ has $\Delta l=0$ and is forbidden, so it does not appear in the Kα line (Figure 26.8(a)).

The intensity ratio 2 : 1 is due to the same reason as for the Na D lines in Section 26.3. $2p_{3/2}$ contains four states and $2p_{1/2}$ contains two states. The number of electrons that drop into the core hole is proportional to the number of states of the level, $4:2$, so the intensities are $I(\mathrm{K}\alpha_1):I(\mathrm{K}\alpha_2)\approx2:1$ (the measured ratio is about $2:1$).

26.6.2 Kα$_1$ and Kα$_2$ in powder XRD

The condition for X-rays of wavelength $\lambda$, incident at an angle $\theta$ on lattice planes of spacing $d$ in a crystal, to be diffracted is Bragg's equation $2d\sin\theta=\lambda$. Since Kα$_1$ ($\lambda_1$) and Kα$_2$ ($\lambda_2$) have slightly different wavelengths, they are diffracted from the same lattice planes at slightly different angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$:

\begin{equation} 2d\sin\theta_1=\lambda_1,\qquad 2d\sin\theta_2=\lambda_2\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \sin\theta_2=\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\sin\theta_1 \label{eq:26-bragg} \end{equation}

When the wavelength difference is small, differentiating both sides of $\lambda=2d\sin\theta$ with respect to $\theta$ gives $\Delta\lambda=2d\cos\theta\,\Delta\theta$, and dividing by $\lambda=2d\sin\theta$ gives $\Delta\lambda/\lambda=\Delta\theta/\tan\theta$. Therefore the separation of the peaks, seen in terms of the diffraction angle $2\theta$, is

\begin{equation} \Delta(2\theta)=2\tan\theta\,\frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} \label{eq:26-split2theta} \end{equation}

Since $\tan\theta$ grows steeply toward the high-angle side, the separation of Kα$_1$ and Kα$_2$ is small at low angles and clearly larger at high angles. The values for Cu ($\Delta\lambda/\lambda=2.489\times10^{-3}$) are given in Table 26.7.

26.6.3 Doublets in X-ray absorption (L edges) and in photoelectrons (XPS)

When a core electron is excited by X-rays into an empty state (X-ray absorption spectroscopy, XAS), then as the X-ray energy is swept, the absorption rises sharply at the point where that energy becomes equal to the separation between the core level and the empty level (the absorption edge). The absorption in which a $2p$ electron is excited into $3d$ splits into two: the one from $2p_{3/2}$ is the L$_3$ edge and the one from $2p_{1/2}$ is the L$_2$ edge (the absorption from $2s$ is the L$_1$ edge; $2s\to3d$ is forbidden as an electric dipole transition, and it becomes a weak absorption into $4p$ and the like). The L$_3$ edge is on the low-energy side, and the separation is $\tfrac32\zeta_{2p}$. In the 3d transition metals, the $2p$ splitting is about $6\ \mathrm{eV}$ for Ti, about $13\ \mathrm{eV}$ for Fe, and about $20\ \mathrm{eV}$ for Cu, increasing in line with the $Z^4$ law ($(26/22)^4\approx1.9$, $(29/22)^4\approx3.0$). The Cu value is the same quantity as the $19.98\ \mathrm{eV}$ of the Kα doublet (the splitting of the same $2p$ level), measured by a different spectroscopy.

The ratio of the absorption intensities (areas) at the L$_3$ edge and the L$_2$ edge is called the branching ratio. If electrons are excited from $2p_{3/2}$ (four electrons) and $2p_{1/2}$ (two electrons) to the same empty $3d$ states with the same transition probability, the intensities are in the ratio of the numbers of electrons, $4:2=2:1$ (the statistical value). In actual oxides of 3d transition metals, the electrostatic interaction between the excited $3d$ electron and the hole left in the $2p$ shell (multiplet effects; Part VII and Part VIII) mixes the $j$ of the $2p$ hole, so the branching ratio deviates from $2:1$. This deviation, and the difference between the absorption of right and left circularly polarized light in magnetic materials (X-ray magnetic circular dichroism, XMCD), are used to separate the spin and orbital components of the magnetic moment. For that analysis, the separation of the L$_3$ edge and the L$_2$ edge is essential.

In photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), X-rays knock core electrons out into vacuum. The spin–orbit splitting of a core level appears as a doublet in the spectrum of the photoelectron kinetic energy. The ratio of the areas of the doublet is determined by the ratio of the degeneracies $(2j+1)$ of the final state (the state in which a hole has been created).

$$ p:\ \frac{2p_{3/2}}{2p_{1/2}}=\frac{4}{2}=2:1,\qquad d:\ \frac{3d_{5/2}}{3d_{3/2}}=\frac{6}{4}=3:2,\qquad f:\ \frac{4f_{7/2}}{4f_{5/2}}=\frac{8}{6}=4:3 $$

These are the area ratios (Exercise 26.7). The size of the doublet splitting is about $20\ \mathrm{eV}$ for the $2p$ level of Cu ($2p_{3/2}$ at about $932.7\ \mathrm{eV}$, $2p_{1/2}$ at about $952.5\ \mathrm{eV}$) and about $6\ \mathrm{eV}$ for the $3d$ level of Ag, and it is also used as a marker for identifying elements. An $s$ orbital does not split (a single line).

26.7 Spin–Orbit Interaction in Materials

So far we have looked at atoms with a single electron (hydrogen-like atoms and alkali atoms) and at inner-shell X-rays. In this section we survey, qualitatively, the role that the spin–orbit interaction plays for electrons in materials—the $3d$ electrons of transition-metal ions, the $4f$ electrons of rare-earth ions, the $6p$ electrons of heavy elements, and so on. Detailed calculations are carried out in Part V (perturbation theory), Part VII (many-electron atoms), and Part VIII (ligand fields), and for the electronic states of solids the discussion leads on to Solid-State Physics (AD 06) (in Japanese) and Density Functional Theory (AD 07) (in Japanese).

26.7.2 Shifts of the $g$ value and ESR

The $g$ factor of a free electron spin is $g_s=2.0023$, but when unpaired electrons in a material (transition-metal ions such as Cu$^{2+}$ and Mn$^{2+}$, defects and color centers, organic radicals, and so on) are measured by electron spin resonance (ESR, Chapter 23, Section 23.9), the observed $g$ value deviates from $2$. In Cu$^{2+}$ complexes it appears in the range $g\approx2.0$–$2.4$. This is due to the spin–orbit interaction.

Intuition: Spin–orbit interaction mixes orbitals and “borrows” orbital angular momentum

An unpaired electron in a crystal field has had its orbital degeneracy lifted and is in a state in which the expectation value of the orbital angular momentum, $\expval{\hat{\bm{L}}}$, is almost $0$ (orbital quenching, Part VIII). For this reason ordinarily $g\approx g_s$. However, the spin–orbit interaction $\zeta\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ mixes into the ground state an excited orbital state that lies higher by $\Delta$ in the crystal field, in a proportion of about $\zeta/\Delta$ (second-order perturbation theory of Part V). Because the excited state has orbital angular momentum, the ground state “borrows” orbital angular momentum $\expval{\hat{\bm{L}}}\ne0$ by the amount that has been mixed in. As a result, the coupling between the magnetic field and the spin changes, and the shift of the $g$ factor is estimated as

$$ \Delta g\ \sim\ \frac{\zeta}{\Delta}\quad(\text{the coefficient is determined by the ion's}\ d\ \text{orbital configuration and symmetry}) $$

in order of magnitude. If $\zeta\approx0.1\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $\Delta\approx1.5\ \mathrm{eV}$ (the energy of the $d$–$d$ transition), then $\zeta/\Delta\approx0.07$, and multiplying by the coefficient (about 8 for the elongated octahedron of $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$) gives $\Delta g\approx8\zeta/\Delta\approx0.5$ if the free-ion value is used unchanged. When the $d$ orbitals spread onto the ligands through covalent bonding, the effective $\zeta$ is reduced to $0.5$–$0.8$ times the free-ion value, so that $\Delta g\approx0.3$ or so, which falls within the observed range $g\approx2.2$–$2.4$. There is also a tendency that $g\lt2$ when the $d$ shell is less than half full ($\mathrm{Ti^{3+}}$ and the like) and $g\gt2$ when it is more than half full ($\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ and the like). From the anisotropy of the $g$ value one can learn the symmetry of the orbital of the unpaired electron and the arrangement of the surrounding ligands, which is the reason ESR is used to analyze defects in materials and catalytically active species. Furthermore, from the resonance condition $h\nu=g\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B$, in the X band ($9.5\ \mathrm{GHz}$) the resonance field is $0.339\ \mathrm{T}$ for $g=2.0023$ and $0.309\ \mathrm{T}$ for $g=2.2$. The probabilities and selection rules of transitions between Zeeman levels (magnetic dipole transitions) are treated in the time-dependent perturbation theory of Part IX (Chapter 51, Chapter 52).

26.7.3 Magnetic moments — spin-only and $g_J\sqrt{J(J+1)}$

The magnetic moment of a magnetic ion is determined, from the temperature dependence of the paramagnetic susceptibility (Curie's law, AD 03, Chapter 51, Section 51.4 (in Japanese)), as the effective magnetic moment $\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (in units of $\mu_{\mathrm{B}}$). Depending on the state of the orbitals in the ion, two expressions are used.

  • When the orbital angular momentum is quenched ($3d$ transition-metal ions). The crystal field lifts the orbital degeneracy, so that $\expval{\hat{\bm{L}}}\approx0$, and the magnetic moment is carried by the spin alone (the spin-only value).
  • When $J$ is a good quantum number ($4f$ rare-earth ions). The $4f$ electrons are hidden behind the outer $5s$ and $5p$ shells and hardly feel the crystal field, and since the spin–orbit interaction is larger than the crystal field, $L$, $S$, and $J$ are all conserved. The ground term is determined by Hund's rules (Chapter 34).

In the respective cases, the effective magnetic moment is given by

\begin{equation} \mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=g_s\sqrt{S(S+1)}\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\approx2\sqrt{S(S+1)}\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\quad(\text{spin-only}),\qquad \mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=g_J\sqrt{J(J+1)}\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\quad(J\ \text{is a good quantum number}) \label{eq:26-mueff} \end{equation}

Here the $g_J$ in the latter is given by the formula of Section 26.4 (the form for an $LS$ term of a many-electron atom)

$$ g_J=1+\frac{J(J+1)+S(S+1)-L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)} $$

and $\sqrt{J(J+1)}\hbar$ is the magnitude of $\hat{\bm{J}}$.

Example 26.10: Effective magnetic moments of $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$, $\mathrm{Nd^{3+}}$, and $\mathrm{Dy^{3+}}$

Find (1) the spin-only $\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}$ of $\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}$ ($3d^5$, ground term ${}^6S_{5/2}$); (2) $g_J$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}$ of $\mathrm{Nd^{3+}}$ ($4f^3$, ground term ${}^4I_{9/2}$, $S=\tfrac32$, $L=6$, $J=\tfrac92$); and (3) $g_J$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}$ of $\mathrm{Dy^{3+}}$ ($4f^9$, ground term ${}^6H_{15/2}$, $S=\tfrac52$, $L=5$, $J=\tfrac{15}{2}$). (2) $J(J+1)=\tfrac92\cdot\tfrac{11}{2}=\tfrac{99}{4}$, $S(S+1)=\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac52=\tfrac{15}{4}$, $L(L+1)=42$.

$$ g_J=1+\frac{\tfrac{99}{4}+\tfrac{15}{4}-42}{2\cdot\tfrac{99}{4}}=1+\frac{\tfrac{114}{4}-42}{\tfrac{99}{2}}=1+\frac{-13.5}{49.5}=1-\frac{3}{11}=\frac{8}{11}. $$

This is because the orbital angular momentum of the $4f$ electrons contributes greatly to the magnetic moment. For rare-earth ions the spin-only formula cannot be used, and $g_J\sqrt{J(J+1)}$ agrees well with the measured values.

Application: Rare-earth permanent magnets and the spin–orbit interaction

Strong permanent magnets such as Nd$_2$Fe$_{14}$B and SmCo$_5$ contain $3d$ Fe and Co and $4f$ Nd and Sm. The division of roles is as follows. The spins of the $3d$ electrons of Fe and Co create a large magnetization and a high Curie temperature through the exchange interaction. The $4f$ electrons of Nd and Sm, on the other hand, have a large spin–orbit interaction ($\zeta\sim0.1\ \mathrm{eV}$), and their orbital angular momentum has not vanished. The charge distribution of a $4f$ electron is not spherical, and inside a crystal, under the electrostatic interaction with the surrounding ions (the crystal field), it tends to point in a particular direction relative to the crystal axes. Since the spin is tied to the orbital by the spin–orbit interaction, the orientation of the spin is constrained with respect to the crystal axes. This is the magnetic anisotropy: once the magnetization has been pointed in a direction, it becomes hard to move it away from that direction (the coercivity is large), and the material works as a permanent magnet. In $3d$ metals (Fe, Ni), the orbital angular momentum is quenched and the effect of the spin–orbit interaction is small, so the anisotropy energy is extremely small, on the order of $\mu\mathrm{eV}$ per atom. It can be said that it is the spin–orbit interaction that ties the spin and the crystal together through the “orbital” (for magnetism in solids, see AD 06, Chapter 20 (in Japanese)).

26.7.4 Topological insulators and Rashba splitting

Application: Spin–orbit interaction changes the states of electrons — Bi$_2$Se$_3$, Rashba splitting, and spintronics

Topological insulators. In Bi$_2$Se$_3$, which contains Bi ($Z=83$) and Se, the ordering of the bands is inverted (band inversion) because the spin–orbit interaction of the $p$ electrons of the heavy elements is large. The state that should originally be the bottom of the conduction band and the state that should be the top of the valence band are interchanged at the $\Gamma$ point. As a result, the interior of the crystal is an insulator with a gap (about $0.3\ \mathrm{eV}$), but metallic states (a Dirac cone) appear at the surface. In the surface states, the direction of the electron's spin is tied to the direction of its momentum (spin–momentum locking). Theoretical prediction and observation by ARPES were made around 2009 (ARPES is angle-resolved photoelectron spectroscopy, a method in which light knocks electrons out into vacuum and the bands $E(\bm{k})$ inside the crystal are measured from their kinetic energy and the angle at which they emerge. A Dirac cone is a dispersion in which the energy is proportional to the wavenumber $k$ and the bands meet in a conical shape).

Rashba splitting. At places where spatial inversion symmetry is broken, such as crystal surfaces and interfaces, there is an electric field perpendicular to the interface, $\bm{E}=E\bm{e}_z$. An electron moving in the interface with wavenumber $\bm{k}$ (velocity $\bm{v}=\hbar\bm{k}/m^*$) feels, in this electric field, a magnetic field in its rest frame, $\bm{B}'\propto-\bm{v}\times\bm{E}/c^2$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Bprime0}), and a Zeeman-type interaction $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{R}}=\alpha_{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\sigma}\times\bm{k})\cdot\bm{e}_z$ ($\bm{\sigma}$ denotes the Pauli matrices) acts on the spin. The energy $E_\pm(k)=\hbar^2k^2/2m^*\pm\alpha_{\mathrm{R}}k$ splits, in proportion to $k$, according to the direction of the spin. The $\alpha_{\mathrm{R}}$ in solids is much larger than the estimate obtained by putting the vacuum electric field into Eq. \eqref{eq:26-Bprime0}, and is determined near the atomic nuclei of the heavy elements. Using this splitting to control the spin electrically, without applying a magnetic field, is one of the challenges of spintronics.

Spin Hall effect. The spin–orbit interaction links the flow of charge to the flow of spin. When a current is passed through a nonmagnetic heavy metal (Pt, Ta, and so on), the electrons are bent in opposite directions depending on the direction of their spin, and a flow of spin (a spin current) arises in the transverse direction (the spin Hall effect). This spin current can reverse the direction of an adjacent magnet.

26.7.5 Phosphorescence and organic EL (OLED)

Application: Phosphorescence — spin–orbit interaction allows spin-forbidden transitions

The electronic states of molecules and crystals can be classified by the total spin $S$ (singlet $S=0$, triplet $S=1$). Since an electric dipole transition does not act on the electron spin, the spin does not change in the absorption or emission of light, and singlet ⇄ triplet transitions are spin-forbidden. However, the spin–orbit interaction links the orbital and the spin. The spin–orbit interaction of a many-electron system, $\sum_i\xi(r_i)\hat{\bm{l}}_i\cdot\hat{\bm{s}}_i$, turns the spin of each individual electron, so it does not commute with the total spin $\hat{S}^2$ and mixes singlet and triplet (states with different $S$). The degree of mixing becomes larger as $\zeta$ increases. For this reason, emission from the triplet to the singlet ground state (phosphorescence) is allowed, though weakly. The lifetime of phosphorescence is microseconds to milliseconds, far longer than that of spin-allowed fluorescence (nanoseconds). The red R line of ruby (the $^2E\to{}^4A_2$ transition of Cr$^{3+}$, Chapter 48) is also a transition in which the spin is reversed, and its lifetime is long, about $3\ \mathrm{ms}$.

In an organic EL device (OLED), the excitons generated by electrical excitation are formed, owing to spin statistics, in the ratio singlet : triplet $=1:3$ (the reason for this counting is given in Chapter 25, Section 25.8). Since only the singlet emits light in a fluorescent material, the internal quantum efficiency is limited to at most $25\ \%$. If an organometallic complex containing a heavy metal such as Ir or Pt (for example, Ir(ppy)$_3$) is used as the light-emitting material, the large spin–orbit interaction of the heavy atom ($\zeta\propto Z^4$) allows even the triplet excitons to emit light, and in theory an efficiency of about $100\ \%$ can be obtained. Since the 1998 report by Baldo, Forrest, and coworkers, this has been used for the red and green emission of the OLEDs of today's smartphones and televisions.

26.7.6 From $3d$ to $5d$ — from $Z^4$ to $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ after shielding

Example 26.11: Why is the spin–orbit interaction large in systems of $5d$ electrons (Ir oxides, etc.)?

Using the hydrogen-like form $\zeta_{nl}\propto Z_{\mathrm{eff}}^4/n^3$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:26-zeta}, explain qualitatively why $\zeta$ of $5d$ transition-metal ions (such as $\mathrm{Ir^{4+}}$) is about $0.4\ \mathrm{eV}$, nearly an order of magnitude larger than that of $3d$ transition-metal ions ($\zeta\approx0.05\ \mathrm{eV}$ for $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$, for example).

Solution. According to Eq. \eqref{eq:26-zeta}, $\zeta_{nl}\propto Z_{\mathrm{eff}}^4/(n^3\,l(l+\frac12)(l+1))$, and since $3d$ and $5d$ have the same $l=2$, the ratio is $\dfrac{\zeta_{5d}}{\zeta_{3d}}=\Bigl(\dfrac{Z_{\mathrm{eff},5d}}{Z_{\mathrm{eff},3d}}\Bigr)^4\Bigl(\dfrac{3}{5}\Bigr)^3$. (i) Effect of $n$: Since $5d$ extends farther out than $3d$, $n^{-3}$ reduces the ratio to $(3/5)^3=0.216$ times its value. This is an effect in the direction of reducing $\zeta$. (ii) Effect of the nuclear charge: The atomic number becomes about three times larger, from Fe (26) to Ir (77), and the electric field felt by a $d$ electron moving near the nucleus becomes stronger. If we use Eq. \eqref{eq:26-zeta} in reverse on the measured values of $\zeta$ to find $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$, then for $3d$ ($n=3$, $l=2$) we have $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}^4=810\,\zeta/(\alpha^2E_{\mathrm{h}})=810\times0.05/(1.449\times10^{-3})=2.8\times10^4$, which gives $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx13$ for $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$, and for $5d$ ($n=5$) we have $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}^4=3750\times0.4/(1.449\times10^{-3})=1.0\times10^6$, which gives $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx32$ for $\mathrm{Ir^{4+}}$. The ratio is about $2.5$ ($32/13$). Whether this is reasonable can be checked against an independent criterion. $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}/Z$ is $13/26=0.5$ for $\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$ and $32/77=0.4$ for $\mathrm{Ir^{4+}}$, which is comparable to the values for alkali atoms (around $0.3$, Table 26.2). Shielding relaxes the ratio of the atomic numbers, $77/26=3.0$, to a ratio of $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ of $2.5$, but the $Z^4$ effect, a factor of $2.5^4=39$, more than cancels the $n^{-3}$ effect of (i), a factor of $0.216$, and the ratio becomes $39\times0.216\approx8$. This agrees well with the actual ratio of $\zeta$ ($0.4/0.05=8$). What matters is that, because the spin–orbit interaction is determined near the nucleus, $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ still increases with $Z$ even after shielding, so that the steep increase of $Z^4$ beats the gentle decrease of $n^{-3}$.

As a result, in $5d$ transition-metal compounds the spin–orbit interaction becomes comparable in magnitude to the crystal field, the Coulomb repulsion, and the bandwidth. In Sr$_2$IrO$_4$, for example, the strong spin–orbit interaction splits the $t_{2g}$ orbitals into effective total angular momenta $j_{\mathrm{eff}}=\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac32$, and the $j_{\mathrm{eff}}=\tfrac12$ state, combined with the Coulomb repulsion, forms an insulator (a Mott insulator: an insulator that band theory predicts to be a metal, but in which the electrons cannot move because of the Coulomb repulsion between them). In calculations of the electronic states of materials containing heavy elements (first-principles calculations), the spin–orbit interaction must be included as a relativistic effect (the Dirac equation in Part XI, and the relativistic extension of density functional theory in AD 07).

Summary of this section: Spin–orbit interaction in materials

(i) Shifts of the $g$ value and ESR (orbital borrowing), (ii) the spin-only (orbital quenching) magnetic moment and the $g_J\sqrt{J(J+1)}$ magnetic moment ($4f$), (iii) magnetic anisotropy and permanent magnets (spin ⇄ orbital ⇄ crystal), (iv) band inversion in heavy elements (topological insulators) and Rashba splitting, (v) the breakdown of spin-forbiddenness (phosphorescence, OLED), (vi) the strong spin–orbit interaction in 5d systems. All of them originate in the theme of this chapter, namely that $\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$ ties the orbital angular momentum and the spin together. Since the strength of the coupling is proportional to $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}^4$, heavier elements bring about larger effects. In connection with this, we treat these topics once more from other viewpoints in AD 06, in Chapter 17 (in Japanese) (semiconductor bands and spin–orbit splitting) and Chapter 20 (in Japanese) (magnetism), and in AD 09, in Chapter 7 (in Japanese) (bound states and the spin–orbit interaction).

26.8.1 Summary

  • Origin: In the electron's rest frame, the electric field of the nucleus $\bm{E}=\dfrac{1}{e_0}\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\dfrac{\bm{r}}{r}$ appears as a magnetic field $\bm{B}'=-\bm{v}\times\bm{E}/c^2=\dfrac{1}{e_0m_{\mathrm{e}}c^2r}\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}\bm{L}$. The coupling $-\hat{\bm{\mu}}_s\cdot\bm{B}'$ to the magnetic moment of the spin ($g_s=2$) is halved by the factor $\tfrac12$ due to Thomas precession, giving $\hat{H}_{\mathrm{SO}}=\xi(r)\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}$, $\xi(r)=\dfrac{1}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\dfrac1r\dfrac{\dd V}{\dd r}$. It comes out automatically from the Dirac equation (Part XI).
  • Hydrogen-like atoms: From $\xi(r)=\dfrac{Ze_0^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2r^3}$ and $\expval{r^{-3}}=\dfrac{Z^3}{a_0^3n^3l(l+\frac12)(l+1)}$ we obtain $\zeta_{nl}=\dfrac{\alpha^2E_{\mathrm{h}}Z^4}{2n^3l(l+\frac12)(l+1)}$. The levels are $E_{\mathrm{SO}}(j)=\tfrac{\zeta}{2}[j(j+1)-l(l+1)-\tfrac34]$, and the spacing is $(l+\tfrac12)\zeta$. This is the $Z^4$ law. The spacing of H $2p$ is $\alpha^2E_{\mathrm{h}}/32=4.53\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}=10.95\ \mathrm{GHz}$.
  • Alkali atoms: The Na D lines ($588.995$ and $589.592\ \mathrm{nm}$, separation $17.2\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$) are ${}^2P_{3/2}\to{}^2S_{1/2}$ (D$_2$) and ${}^2P_{1/2}\to{}^2S_{1/2}$ (D$_1$), and their intensity ratio is the ratio of $(2j+1)$, $2:1$. For K, Rb, and Cs the separations are $57.7$, $237.6$, and $554.0\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. The $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ obtained from the hydrogen-like formula is smaller than $Z$ ($3.55$ for Na) but larger than Slater's $Z^*$ (an interaction determined near the nucleus). The fine structure of hydrogen is the sum of three terms (spin–orbit, mass–velocity, and Darwin) and is determined only by $n$ and $j$ (Dirac's formula, Part XI).
  • Weak magnetic field: $E=g_J\mu_{\mathrm{B}}m_jB$, with Landé's $g_J=1+\dfrac{j(j+1)+s(s+1)-l(l+1)}{2j(j+1)}$ (projection theorem, or Clebsch–Gordan coefficients). ${}^2S_{1/2}$: $2$, ${}^2P_{1/2}$: $\tfrac23$, ${}^2P_{3/2}$: $\tfrac43$. The Na D$_1$ line splits into four lines and the D$_2$ line into six lines (anomalous Zeeman effect).
  • Strong magnetic field: The $6\times6$ matrix for $2p$ is the $2\times2$ blocks of the previous chapter with $b\,(m+2m_s)$ added. The slope changes from the weak-field slope $g_Jm_j$ to the strong-field slope $m_l+2m_s$, and the energy goes over to $E=\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B(m_l+2m_s)+\zeta m_lm_s$ (Paschen–Back effect). The boundary is $\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B=\zeta$ (H $2p$: $0.52\ \mathrm{T}$, Na $3p$: $24.5\ \mathrm{T}$).
  • X-rays: The difference between Cu Kα$_1$ ($1.5405929\ \text{Å}$, $8047.8\ \mathrm{eV}$) and Kα$_2$ ($1.5444274\ \text{Å}$, $8027.8\ \mathrm{eV}$) is $19.98\ \mathrm{eV}=\tfrac32\zeta_{2p}$, and their intensity ratio is $2:1$. The separation of XRD peaks is $\Delta(2\theta)=2\tan\theta\,\Delta\lambda/\lambda$ ($0.29^\circ$ at $2\theta=90^\circ$ for Cu). The branching ratio of the L$_3$/L$_2$ edges and the area ratio of the XPS doublet are given by the ratio of $(2j+1)$ ($p$: $2:1$, $d$: $3:2$, $f$: $4:3$).
  • Materials: shifts of the $g$ value (orbital borrowing), spin-only and $g_J\sqrt{J(J+1)}$, magnetic anisotropy (rare-earth magnets), band inversion (Bi$_2$Se$_3$) and Rashba splitting, phosphorescence and OLED, the strong spin–orbit interaction in $5d$ systems.

26.8.2 Key equations

$$ \hat{H}_{\mathrm{SO}}=\xi(r)\,\hat{\bm{L}}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}},\qquad \xi(r)=\frac{1}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}^2c^2}\frac1r\frac{\dd V}{\dd r} \label{eq:26-key1} $$
$$ \zeta_{nl}=\frac{\alpha^2E_{\mathrm{h}}\,Z^4}{2\,n^3\,l\bigl(l+\tfrac12\bigr)(l+1)},\qquad E_{\mathrm{SO}}(j)=\frac{\zeta_{nl}}{2}\Bigl[j(j+1)-l(l+1)-\tfrac34\Bigr] \label{eq:26-key2} $$
$$ g_J=1+\frac{j(j+1)+s(s+1)-l(l+1)}{2\,j(j+1)},\qquad E=g_J\,\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\,m_j\,B \label{eq:26-key3} $$
$$ E=\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B\,(m_l+2m_s)+\zeta\,m_lm_s\quad(\mu_{\mathrm{B}}B\gg\zeta),\qquad \Delta(2\theta)=2\tan\theta\,\frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} \label{eq:26-key4} $$
