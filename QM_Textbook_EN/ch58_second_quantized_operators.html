Chapter 58One-Body and Two-Body Operators and Field Operators — Writing the Many-Body Hamiltonian

In Chapter 56 we solved the harmonic oscillator using only creation and annihilation operators, and in Chapter 57 we expressed the states of electrons (fermions) by their occupation numbers, so that we could write a Slater determinant as $\hat{c}_{i_1}^\dagger\cdots\hat{c}_{i_N}^\dagger\ket{0}$. But being able to write down states is not enough to make progress in physics. To investigate the electronic states of a material, we must write down, in the same language, the Hamiltonian that determines the total energy of $N$ electrons—the kinetic energy of the electrons, the forces from the atomic nuclei and external fields, and the Coulomb repulsion between the electrons. This chapter does exactly that.

In classical mechanics (AD 03), the total energy of $N$ particles splits into “the sum of the energies of the individual particles,” $\sum_a\bigl(\bm{p}_a^2/2m+U(\bm{r}_a)\bigr)$, and “the sum of the interactions over pairs of particles,” $\tfrac12\sum_{a\ne b}V(\bm{r}_a-\bm{r}_b)$. In quantum mechanics the structure of the Hamiltonian is the same. A sum of terms each acting on a single particle, like the former, is called a one-body operator; a sum of terms each involving two particles, like the latter, is called a two-body operator. The difference is that, because electrons cannot be distinguished from one another, the state becomes an antisymmetric sum of $N!$ terms (a Slater determinant). If we compute the matrix elements of these operators in the first-quantized formulation, we have to expand sums over permutations again and again, and, as we saw in Chapter 35, it is hard to see where we are going. In second quantization, a one-body operator is written with the two operators $\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}$ and a two-body operator with the four operators $\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}\hat{c}$, and the anticommutation relations of the operators take over the detailed expansion of the Slater determinant.

As a result, for example, the energy in the Hartree–Fock approximation (which took us many pages to obtain in Chapters 35 and 36) can be written in three lines in the language of second quantization. The total energy of the $\pi$ electrons of benzene, the splitting of the singlet and triplet of the 1s2s state of helium, and the fact that in a metal the Coulomb force is screened over a length as short as the interatomic distance—all of these come out of the forms of the two operators derived in this chapter. This chapter is the foundation for reading the “many-body Hamiltonian” in the next chapters, Chapter 59 (The Electron Gas and the Hubbard Model) and Chapter 60 (Photons and Phonons), and in the companion volumes “Linear Response Theory” (AD 08) and “Solid-State Physics” (AD 06).

58.1.1 Conventions used in this chapter (review of Chapter 57)

Review: conventions for spin orbitals and for $\hat{c}_i$, $\hat{c}_i^\dagger$ (Chapter 57, Section 57.5, Section 57.6)

  • Spin orbital $\chi_i(\bm{x})$: a one-electron state. Here $\bm{x}=(\bm{r},\sigma)$ is a coordinate that combines position and spin, and we write $\int\!\dd\bm{x}\equiv\sum_{\sigma}\int\dd^3r$ (Chapter 33. In Part X of this book, the coordinate $\bm{x}$ is set in boldface and the one-dimensional position $x$ in lightface, to distinguish them. The lightface $x$ appears only in the one-dimensional discretization of Section 58.5.1 and in Example 58.10). We assume that the spin orbitals satisfy the orthonormality $\int\chi_i^*(\bm{x})\chi_j(\bm{x})\dd\bm{x}=\delta_{ij}$ and the completeness $\sum_i\chi_i(\bm{x})\chi_i^*(\bm{x}')=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')$ ($\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')=\delta_{\sigma\sigma'}\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}')$). In Dirac notation, we write the ket corresponding to $\chi_i$ as $\ket{i}$, and $\mel{i}{\hat{t}}{j}=\int\chi_i^*(\bm{x})\,\hat{t}\chi_j(\bm{x})\dd\bm{x}$.
  • Fermion operators: $\hat{c}_i^\dagger$ creates one electron in the spin orbital $\chi_i$, and $\hat{c}_i$ annihilates one. The anticommutation relations are $$\acomm{\hat{c}_i}{\hat{c}_j^\dagger}=\delta_{ij},\qquad \acomm{\hat{c}_i}{\hat{c}_j}=\acomm{\hat{c}_i^\dagger}{\hat{c}_j^\dagger}=0$$ and in particular $(\hat{c}_i^\dagger)^2=0$ is the Pauli exclusion principle. The vacuum $\ket{0}$ represents “the state with no electrons at all” and satisfies $\hat{c}_i\ket{0}=0$ (in this book $\ket{0}$ denotes only the vacuum; the ground state of an interacting system will be written $\ket{\Psi_0}$ later).
  • Slater determinant: $\ket{k_1k_2\cdots k_N}\equiv\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k_2}^\dagger\cdots\hat{c}_{k_N}^\dagger\ket{0}$ corresponds to the wave function $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det\bigl[\chi_{k_b}(\bm{x}_a)\bigr]$ (the row index is the electron $a$, the column index is the orbital $k_b$). Interchanging the order of the $\hat{c}^\dagger$ changes the sign (antisymmetry). The particle-number operator is $\hat{n}_i=\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_i$ (eigenvalue $0$ or $1$), and $\hat{N}=\sum_i\hat{n}_i$.

58.1.2 One-body operators in first quantization

Among the operators that make up the total energy of $N$ electrons, the simplest is “the sum of the energies of the individual electrons.” We write $\hat{t}(a)$ for an operator that acts only on the coordinate $\bm{x}_a$ of electron $a$.

Definition 58.1: One-body operator

The operator obtained by applying an operator $\hat{t}$ that acts on a single particle to each of the $N$ particles and adding the results,

\begin{equation} \hat{T}=\sum_{a=1}^{N}\hat{t}(a) \label{eq:58-T1} \end{equation}

is called a one-body operator. $\hat{t}(a)$ acts only on the coordinate of particle $a$ and does nothing to the other particles.

Examples are as follows.

  • Kinetic energy: $\hat{t}=-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}}\nabla^2$.
  • The potential produced by an external field or by atomic nuclei: $\hat{t}=U(\bm{r})$. For example, the attraction from a nucleus of nuclear charge $Z$, $U=-Ze_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r)$.
  • The $z$ component of the spin: $\hat{t}=\hat{s}_z$ (the total spin is $\hat{S}_z=\sum_a\hat{s}_z(a)$).
  • The number of particles: $\hat{t}=1$ ($\hat{N}=\sum_a1=N$). The electric dipole $\hat{\bm{d}}=-e_0\sum_a\bm{r}_a$ ($\hat{t}=-e_0\bm{r}$) is also a one-body operator.

The correspondence with classical mechanics is clear. The classical kinetic energy of $N$ particles, $\sum_a\bm{p}_a^2/2m$, and the potential energy in an external field, $\sum_aU(\bm{r}_a)$, are both “sums over particles,” and if there are no forces between the electrons, each particle moves independently. In quantum mechanics too, if there is no interaction between electrons, $\hat{H}=\hat{T}$, which consists of a one-body operator alone, decomposes into the one-electron eigenvalue problem $\hat{t}\chi=\varepsilon\chi$. However, because electrons cannot be distinguished, the $N$-electron state is a Slater determinant, and when $\hat{T}$ acts on each term of the determinant, $N!$ terms appear. In second quantization, this nuisance is absorbed into the operator algebra. Showing this is the purpose of this section.

58.1.3 $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$ “reassigns a particle in orbital $j$ to orbital $i$”

The building block for the second-quantized form of a one-body operator is $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$ (annihilate one electron in orbital $j$ and create one electron in orbital $i$). What happens when it acts on a Slater determinant $\ket{k_1\cdots k_N}$? The following formula is the key.

Formula 58.1: $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$ causes a reassignment of orbitals

For any Slater determinant $\ket{K}=\ket{k_1k_2\cdots k_N}$, \begin{equation} \hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j\ket{k_1\cdots k_N}=\sum_{a=1}^{N}\delta_{j,k_a}\,\ket{k_1\cdots\underset{a\text{-th}}{i}\cdots k_N} \label{eq:58-replace} \end{equation} holds. The state $\ket{k_1\cdots i\cdots k_N}$ on the right-hand side is the state in which the $a$th orbital label $k_a$ has been rewritten as $i$ (replaced in the same position).

Derivation

First we find the commutator of $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$ and $\hat{c}_m^\dagger$. Using the anticommutation relation $\hat{c}_j\hat{c}_m^\dagger=\delta_{jm}-\hat{c}_m^\dagger\hat{c}_j$, we have $$\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j\hat{c}_m^\dagger=\delta_{jm}\hat{c}_i^\dagger-\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_m^\dagger\hat{c}_j=\delta_{jm}\hat{c}_i^\dagger+\hat{c}_m^\dagger\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$$ (in the second equality we used $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_m^\dagger=-\hat{c}_m^\dagger\hat{c}_i^\dagger$). Hence $$\comm{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j}{\hat{c}_m^\dagger}=\delta_{jm}\,\hat{c}_i^\dagger .$$ Next, we pass $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$ successively to the right through $\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\cdots\hat{c}_{k_N}^\dagger\ket{0}$. Each time it passes through one $\hat{c}_{k_a}^\dagger$, since $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j\hat{c}_{k_a}^\dagger=\hat{c}_{k_a}^\dagger\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j+\delta_{j,k_a}\hat{c}_i^\dagger$, two terms appear: a “term that passes through without doing anything” and a “term in which $\hat{c}_{k_a}^\dagger$ is replaced by $\delta_{j,k_a}\hat{c}_i^\dagger$.” The term that has passed through to the end vanishes because $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j\ket{0}=0$. Therefore $$\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j\,\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\cdots\hat{c}_{k_N}^\dagger\ket{0}=\sum_{a=1}^{N}\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\cdots\bigl(\delta_{j,k_a}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\bigr)\cdots\hat{c}_{k_N}^\dagger\ket{0}$$ and this is Eq. \eqref{eq:58-replace}.

∎

Intuition: One cannot move a particle into an occupied orbital

Eq. \eqref{eq:58-replace} automatically incorporates the Pauli exclusion principle. If the destination orbital $i$ is already the same as some other $k_b$ ($b\ne a$), the state on the right-hand side contains $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_i^\dagger=0$ and is therefore $0$. For example, for $N=2$, acting on the state $\ket{12}=\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_2^\dagger\ket{0}$, in which orbitals 1 and 2 are occupied, gives $$\hat{c}_3^\dagger\hat{c}_2\ket{12}=\ket{13},\qquad \hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_2\ket{12}=\ket{11}=0 .$$ The former is the state in which the second electron has been moved to the empty orbital 3; the latter becomes $0$ because we try to move it to orbital 1, which is already occupied. Also, $\hat{c}_2^\dagger\hat{c}_3\ket{12}=0$ means that there is no electron in orbital 3, so there is no electron to move.

58.1.4 Second-quantized form of a one-body operator

Now let us see, on the first-quantized side, how a one-body operator $\hat{T}$ acts on a Slater determinant. The result $\hat{t}\chi_k$ of letting a one-electron operator $\hat{t}$ act on an orbital $\chi_k$ can be expanded in the orbitals $\chi_i$ by using the completeness $\sum_i\chi_i(\bm{x})\chi_i^*(\bm{x}')=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')$: \begin{equation} \hat{t}\chi_k(\bm{x})=\sum_i\chi_i(\bm{x})\int\chi_i^*(\bm{x}')\,\hat{t}\chi_k(\bm{x}')\dd\bm{x}'=\sum_i\chi_i(\bm{x})\,t_{ik},\qquad t_{ik}\equiv\mel{i}{\hat{t}}{k} \label{eq:58-tk} \end{equation} The matrix of expansion coefficients $t_{ik}$ is none other than the representation matrix of $\hat{t}$ in the basis $\{\chi_i\}$ (AD 02, Chapter 11, Section 11.4).

Theorem 58.1: Second quantization of a one-body operator

A one-body operator $\hat{T}=\sum_{a=1}^N\hat{t}(a)$ corresponds in Fock space to the following operator. \begin{equation} \boxed{\ \hat{T}=\sum_{i,j}\mel{i}{\hat{t}}{j}\,\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j\ } \label{eq:58-T2} \end{equation} That is, it is obtained by multiplying the operation $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$ of “moving an electron in orbital $j$ to orbital $i$” by its amplitude (the transition matrix element) $t_{ij}=\mel{i}{\hat{t}}{j}$ and summing over all $(i,j)$. The right-hand side has a form that does not depend on the number of particles $N$.

Derivation

[i] $N=1$. The one-electron state $\ket{k}=\hat{c}_k^\dagger\ket{0}$ corresponds to the wave function $\chi_k$, and acting on it with $\hat{t}$ gives $\sum_it_{ik}\chi_i$ by Eq. \eqref{eq:58-tk}, that is, $\sum_it_{ik}\ket{i}$. On the other hand, Eq. \eqref{eq:58-T2} gives $\sum_{ij}t_{ij}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j\hat{c}_k^\dagger\ket{0}=\sum_{ij}t_{ij}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\bigl(\delta_{jk}-\hat{c}_k^\dagger\hat{c}_j\bigr)\ket{0}=\sum_it_{ik}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\ket{0}$, and the two coincide.

[ii] General $N$, the first-quantized side. We let $\hat{T}=\sum_a\hat{t}(a)$ act on the Slater determinant $\Phi_K=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\sum_{P}(\mathrm{sgn}\,P)\prod_{b=1}^N\chi_{k_{P(b)}}(\bm{x}_b)$. First consider a single term $\prod_b\chi_{k_{P(b)}}(\bm{x}_b)$. $\hat{t}(a)$ acts only on the $a$th factor, and by Eq. \eqref{eq:58-tk} $$\hat{t}(a)\,\chi_{k_{P(a)}}(\bm{x}_a)=\sum_it_{i,k_{P(a)}}\,\chi_i(\bm{x}_a)$$ it turns into a sum of terms in which that factor is replaced by an orbital $\chi_i$ (the coefficient of the replacement is $t_{i,k_{P(a)}}$). We sum this over $a=1,\dots,N$. For this permutation $P$, as $a$ runs over $1,\dots,N$, $c\equiv P(a)$ also runs over $1,\dots,N$ exactly once each, so if we change the summation variable to $c=P(a)$, the result is the sum over $c$ of the operation “rewrite the $c$th label $k_c$ in the sequence of orbital labels $(k_1,\dots,k_N)$ as $i$, and multiply by the coefficient $t_{i,k_c}$.” This rewriting occurs in the same form regardless of the choice of the permutation $P$, so if we attach the sign $\mathrm{sgn}\,P$ and sum over $P$, we obtain the Slater determinant for the rewritten sequence of labels: $$\hat{T}\,\Phi_{k_1\cdots k_N}=\sum_{c=1}^{N}\sum_{i}t_{i,k_c}\ \Phi_{k_1\cdots\underset{c\text{-th}}{i}\cdots k_N} .$$ [iii] General $N$, the second-quantized side. Letting Eq. \eqref{eq:58-T2} act on $\ket{k_1\cdots k_N}$ and using Formula 58.1, we obtain $$\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j\ket{k_1\cdots k_N}=\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}\sum_{c=1}^N\delta_{j,k_c}\ket{k_1\cdots i\cdots k_N}=\sum_{c=1}^{N}\sum_it_{i,k_c}\ket{k_1\cdots\underset{c\text{-th}}{i}\cdots k_N}$$ which is exactly the same expression as in [ii]. Since the Slater determinants $\ket{k_1\cdots k_N}$ form a basis of the antisymmetric states of $N$ electrons (Chapter 57), the two operators are the same.

∎

Intuition: The $\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}$ take over the expansion of the Slater determinant

When we computed the matrix elements of $\hat{T}$ in first quantization, we had to write out the sum over permutations $P$ ($N!$ terms) and drop many of the terms by orthogonality (the Slater–Condon rules of Chapter 35). In second quantization, this work is replaced by the mechanical operation of “using the anticommutation relation once each time $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$ is passed through.” Antisymmetry is built into the operators from the start as the sign that depends on the ordering of the $\hat{c}^\dagger$, so there is no need to write down $N!$ terms.

Example 58.1: Putting two electrons into two orbitals — agreement of first and second quantization

Suppose there are two spin orbitals $\chi_1,\chi_2$, and the matrix of the one-electron operator $\hat{t}$ is $t=\begin{pmatrix}t_{11}&t_{12}\\t_{21}&t_{22}\end{pmatrix}$. (1) Apply $\hat{T}=\sum_{ij}t_{ij}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$ to the state $\ket{12}=\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_2^\dagger\ket{0}$ in which two electrons have been put into these two orbitals. (2) Verify the result by first quantization. (3) For $t=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ (in arbitrary units), compare the value obtained with the sum of the eigenvalues of $t$.

Solution. (1) We use Formula 58.1 for $\ket{12}$ ($k_1=1,k_2=2$). $$\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_1\ket{12}=\ket{12},\quad \hat{c}_2^\dagger\hat{c}_2\ket{12}=\ket{12},\quad \hat{c}_2^\dagger\hat{c}_1\ket{12}=\ket{22}=0,\quad \hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_2\ket{12}=\ket{11}=0 .$$ (In the first two, the orbital to which the electron is moved is the orbital it was already in, which is the same as $\hat{n}_1\ket{12}=\ket{12}$ and so on. In the last two, the orbital to which the electron is moved is already occupied, so the result is $0$ by the Pauli exclusion principle.) Therefore $$\hat{T}\ket{12}=(t_{11}+t_{22})\ket{12}$$ so $\ket{12}$ is an eigenstate of $\hat{T}$, and its eigenvalue is the trace of $t$, $\mathrm{tr}\,t=t_{11}+t_{22}$.

(2) In first quantization, we apply $\hat{t}(1)+\hat{t}(2)$ to $\Phi_{12}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)-\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\bigr]$. Using the expansions $\hat{t}\chi_1=t_{11}\chi_1+t_{21}\chi_2$ and $\hat{t}\chi_2=t_{12}\chi_1+t_{22}\chi_2$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-tk}, we find, for example, $\hat{t}(1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)=\bigl[t_{11}\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)+t_{21}\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\bigr]\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)$. Applying $\hat{t}(1)+\hat{t}(2)$ to each of $\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)$ and $\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)$ gives $$\begin{aligned} \bigl[\hat{t}(1)+\hat{t}(2)\bigr]\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)&=(t_{11}+t_{22})\,\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)+t_{21}\,\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)+t_{12}\,\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2),\\ \bigl[\hat{t}(1)+\hat{t}(2)\bigr]\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)&=(t_{11}+t_{22})\,\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)+t_{12}\,\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)+t_{21}\,\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2) \end{aligned}$$ Since $\Phi_{12}$ is $1/\sqrt2$ times the difference (the former minus the latter), the coefficient of $\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)$ is $t_{11}+t_{22}$, the coefficient of $\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)$ is $-(t_{11}+t_{22})$, the coefficient of the product $\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)$, in which the same orbital appears twice, is $t_{12}-t_{12}=0$, and the coefficient of $\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)$ is $t_{21}-t_{21}=0$, so that $$\hat{T}\,\Phi_{12}=(t_{11}+t_{22})\,\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)-\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\bigr]=(t_{11}+t_{22})\,\Phi_{12}$$ This agrees with (1).

(3) The eigenvalues of $t=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ follow from $\det(t-\lambda)=\lambda^2-2\lambda-1=0$, which gives $\lambda=1\pm\sqrt2$. Their sum is $(1+\sqrt2)+(1-\sqrt2)=2$, which agrees with the trace $t_{11}+t_{22}=2+0=2$. When there are two orbitals and two electrons, there is only one state with $N=2$, and its energy equals the trace of $t$ regardless of the choice of orbitals (the basis). This is the simplest example of the “invariance under a change of basis” discussed in the next subsection.

58.1.5 How to read it and its properties

As Figure 58.1 shows, Eq. \eqref{eq:58-T2} is a sum over processes in which “one electron moves from orbital $j$ to orbital $i$.” The following properties hold.

  • Does not change the particle number. Since $\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}$ annihilates one particle and creates one, $\hat{T}\ket{K}$ is a state with particle number $N$. The next formula shows that $\comm{\hat{T}}{\hat{N}}=0$ (Exercise 58.1).
  • If $\hat{t}$ is Hermitian, then $\hat{T}$ is also Hermitian. Here $t_{ji}^*=t_{ij}$, and $\hat{T}^\dagger=\sum_{ij}t_{ij}^*\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_i=\hat{T}$.

Formula 58.2: Commutator of $\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b$

\begin{equation} \comm{\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b}{\hat{c}_c^\dagger\hat{c}_d}=\delta_{bc}\,\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_d-\delta_{ad}\,\hat{c}_c^\dagger\hat{c}_b \label{eq:58-gl} \end{equation} We use this relation in many places in this chapter (conservation of particle number, the density operator, and equations of motion).

Derivation

$\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b\hat{c}_c^\dagger\hat{c}_d=\hat{c}_a^\dagger(\delta_{bc}-\hat{c}_c^\dagger\hat{c}_b)\hat{c}_d=\delta_{bc}\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_d-\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_c^\dagger\hat{c}_b\hat{c}_d$. Similarly, $\hat{c}_c^\dagger\hat{c}_d\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b=\delta_{da}\hat{c}_c^\dagger\hat{c}_b-\hat{c}_c^\dagger\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_d\hat{c}_b$. For the terms that are products of four operators, $\hat{c}_c^\dagger\hat{c}_a^\dagger=-\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_c^\dagger$ and $\hat{c}_d\hat{c}_b=-\hat{c}_b\hat{c}_d$ give $\hat{c}_c^\dagger\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_d\hat{c}_b=\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_c^\dagger\hat{c}_b\hat{c}_d$, so they cancel when we take the difference. What remains is Eq. \eqref{eq:58-gl}.

∎

In particular, the commutator with $\hat{N}=\sum_m\hat{c}_m^\dagger\hat{c}_m$ follows by setting $(c,d)=(m,m)$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:58-gl}: $\comm{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j}{\hat{N}}=\sum_m\bigl(\delta_{jm}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_m-\delta_{im}\hat{c}_m^\dagger\hat{c}_j\bigr)=\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j-\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j=0$.

58.1.6 The form does not change under a change of basis

The right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-T2} appears to depend on the choice of orbitals $\{\chi_i\}$. Let us check that the same operator is obtained if we use a different orthonormal basis $\{\chi_\alpha'\}$. Using a unitary matrix $U$, we define the new orbitals by $\chi_\alpha'=\sum_i\chi_iU_{i\alpha}$.

Derivation: Invariance under a change of basis

The operator that creates an electron in a new orbital is $\hat{d}_\alpha^\dagger=\sum_iU_{i\alpha}\hat{c}_i^\dagger$ (the same transformation as for the coefficients of the orbitals), and the annihilation operator is its Hermitian conjugate, $\hat{d}_\alpha=\sum_iU_{i\alpha}^*\hat{c}_i$. The anticommutation relations are preserved: $$\acomm{\hat{d}_\alpha}{\hat{d}_\beta^\dagger}=\sum_{i,j}U_{i\alpha}^*U_{j\beta}\acomm{\hat{c}_i}{\hat{c}_j^\dagger}=\sum_iU_{i\alpha}^*U_{i\beta}=(U^\dagger U)_{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta},$$ and $\acomm{\hat{d}_\alpha}{\hat{d}_\beta}=0$ holds similarly. The representation matrix in the new basis is $t'_{\alpha\beta}=\mel{\chi_\alpha'}{\hat{t}}{\chi_\beta'}=\sum_{ij}U_{i\alpha}^*t_{ij}U_{j\beta}$ (that is, $t'=U^\dagger tU$). Using this to evaluate Eq. \eqref{eq:58-T2} in the new basis, we obtain $$\sum_{\alpha\beta}t'_{\alpha\beta}\,\hat{d}_\alpha^\dagger\hat{d}_\beta=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\sum_{ij}U_{i\alpha}^*t_{ij}U_{j\beta}\sum_kU_{k\alpha}\hat{c}_k^\dagger\sum_lU_{l\beta}^*\hat{c}_l=\sum_{ijkl}t_{ij}\underbrace{\Bigl(\sum_\alpha U_{k\alpha}U_{i\alpha}^*\Bigr)}_{(UU^\dagger)_{ki}=\delta_{ki}}\underbrace{\Bigl(\sum_\beta U_{j\beta}U_{l\beta}^*\Bigr)}_{(UU^\dagger)_{jl}=\delta_{jl}}\hat{c}_k^\dagger\hat{c}_l=\sum_{ij}t_{ij}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$$ so we return to the original form.

∎

In other words, $\hat{T}$ takes the same form $\sum t_{ij}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$ whichever basis it is written in; changing the basis merely changes, simultaneously, the matrix $t$ into $U^\dagger tU$ and the set of operators $(\hat{c}_i^\dagger,\hat{c}_i)$ into $(\hat{d}_\alpha^\dagger,\hat{d}_\alpha)$. If we choose as the basis the eigenfunctions of $\hat{t}$, $\hat{t}\phi_\alpha=\varepsilon_\alpha\phi_\alpha$, then $t'_{\alpha\beta}=\varepsilon_\alpha\delta_{\alpha\beta}$, and we can write \begin{equation} \hat{T}=\sum_\alpha\varepsilon_\alpha\,\hat{d}_\alpha^\dagger\hat{d}_\alpha=\sum_\alpha\varepsilon_\alpha\,\hat{n}_\alpha \label{eq:58-Tdiag} \end{equation} “Diagonalizing a one-body operator is the same as diagonalizing the matrix $t$,” and the energy of $N$ electrons is the sum of the energies $\varepsilon_\alpha$ of the occupied orbitals (the trace in Example 58.1 is a special case of this). Note that the case $t=\delta_{ij}$ (the identity matrix) is the particle-number operator $\hat{N}=\sum_i\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_i$, which does not depend on the basis. As for the Slater determinant itself, a unitary transformation that mixes only the $N$ occupied orbitals among themselves (an $N\times N$ unitary matrix $U$) changes only the phase $\det U$ (Chapter 57). A transformation that mixes occupied and empty orbitals turns it into a different Slater determinant (or a superposition of such determinants), so what is invariant under a change of the entire basis is not the state itself but the form of the operator $\hat{T}$.

58.2.2 The tight-binding (Hückel-type) model

The one-body operator most used in materials science is the tight-binding model, which describes the “hopping of electrons between orbitals” in crystals and molecules. Suppose that each site (atom) $j$ carries one orbital $\phi_j$ and that electrons can hop between neighboring sites. If we take these orbitals as the basis, the matrix elements of the one-electron Hamiltonian $\hat{h}$ are $h_{jj}=\alpha$ (the energy of the orbital by itself) and $h_{ij}=\beta$ (the resonance integral with the neighboring site), which is the same matrix as in the Hückel method of Chapter 40 (we neglect the overlap integral and take the orbitals to be orthonormal). By Theorem 58.1, the $N$-electron Hamiltonian is

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=\alpha\sum_{j,\sigma}\hat{n}_{j\sigma}+\beta\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}\bigl(\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{j\sigma}+\hat{c}_{j\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{i\sigma}\bigr) \label{eq:58-tb} \end{equation}

Here $\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}$ means summing over each pair of neighboring sites once, and $\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow$ is the spin. Since $\beta\lt0$, we follow the convention of the companion volumes (AD 07 and AD 08) and of many-body physics, use the positive hopping $t\equiv-\beta\gt0$, and choose the origin of energy at $\alpha=0$. Then the Hamiltonian can be written as

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}\bigl(\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{j\sigma}+\hat{c}_{j\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{i\sigma}\bigr) \label{eq:58-tb2} \end{equation}

Using the symbol $\mathrm{h.c.}$ (Hermitian conjugate), this is $-t\sum(\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{j\sigma}+\mathrm{h.c.})$. Each term represents “an electron hops from site $j$ to site $i$ ($\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{j\sigma}$)” or “the reverse hop,” and the amplitude of the hop is $-t$. Solving the secular equation of the Hückel method amounts to diagonalizing the one-electron matrix of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-tb2}, and the $N$-electron ground state is the Slater determinant in which the electrons fill the orbitals in order, starting from the lowest energy (Eq. \eqref{eq:58-Tdiag}).

Notation: Hückel's $\beta$ and the hopping $t$

In this book's Hückel method (Chapter 40), the resonance integral is $\beta\lt0$, and the levels were written in the form $E=\alpha+x\beta$. The hopping $t$ used in the companion volume “Density Functional Theory” (AD 07) and in many-body physics is $t=-\beta\gt0$, and the levels are written $E=-tx$. In this chapter we use $t$. Its value differs greatly depending on what it is fitted to. Fitting to the measured resonance energy of benzene (about 150 kJ/mol, about 1.6 eV) gives $t\approx0.8\ \mathrm{eV}$, whereas fitting to the ultraviolet absorption gives more than 2 eV (Chapter 40). In the Hückel method, $\alpha,\beta$ are parameters that include the electron–electron repulsion indirectly.

58.2.3 A cyclic molecule: the Fourier transform is a change of basis

Consider the case where $N_{\mathrm s}$ sites are arranged in a ring (periodic boundary conditions: $j=N_{\mathrm s}+1$ is the same as $j=1$). For $j=1,\dots,N_{\mathrm s}$, the one-electron matrix has the entry $-t$ only between neighbors, so the basis that diagonalizes it consists of the plane waves $e^{ikj}/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm s}}$ ($k=2\pi m/N_{\mathrm s}$, $m=0,\dots,N_{\mathrm s}-1$; the lattice spacing is taken to be 1). This is a unitary transformation, so it is a special case of the change of basis in Section 58.1.6.

Derivation: Diagonalizing the tight-binding Hamiltonian of a ring

We transform from the site operators to the wavenumber operators by $\hat{c}_{k\sigma}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm s}}}\displaystyle\sum_je^{-ikj}\hat{c}_{j\sigma}$ (conversely, $\hat{c}_{j\sigma}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm s}}}\displaystyle\sum_ke^{ikj}\hat{c}_{k\sigma}$). From $\sum_je^{i(k'-k)j}=N_{\mathrm s}\delta_{kk'}$ (when $k,k'$ are allowed values), we have $\acomm{\hat{c}_{k\sigma}}{\hat{c}_{k'\sigma}^\dagger}=\delta_{kk'}$. The sum of the hops between neighbors is $$\sum_{j}\hat{c}_{j\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{j+1,\sigma}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm s}}\sum_{k,k'}\Bigl(\sum_je^{-ikj}e^{ik'(j+1)}\Bigr)\hat{c}_{k\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k'\sigma}=\sum_ke^{ik}\,\hat{c}_{k\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k\sigma}$$ Adding its complex-conjugate term, $\sum_j\hat{c}_{j+1,\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{j\sigma}=\sum_ke^{-ik}\hat{n}_{k\sigma}$, and using $e^{ik}+e^{-ik}=2\cos k$, we obtain \begin{equation} \hat{H}=\sum_{k,\sigma}\varepsilon_k\,\hat{c}_{k\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k\sigma},\qquad \varepsilon_k=-2t\cos k\qquad\Bigl(k=\frac{2\pi m}{N_{\mathrm s}}\Bigr) \label{eq:58-ring} \end{equation}

∎

Eq. \eqref{eq:58-ring} is the same expression as Formula 40.4 for cyclic molecules in Chapter 40 and the band $E(k)=\alpha+2\beta\cos ka$ in Chapter 41 ($\beta=-t$). Increasing the number of sites without limit turns the levels into a band, and this is the doorway to solid-state physics.

Example 58.3: Total energy of the $\pi$ electrons of benzene and resonance stabilization

For benzene (a ring of six sites with six $\pi$ electrons), find $\varepsilon_k$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:58-ring} and express the total energy of the ground state in terms of $t$. Also, compare it with the total energy for the case of just three localized double bonds (the Kekulé structure) and find the resonance stabilization energy. What is a good choice for $t$ when comparing with the measured value?

Solution. Since $N_{\mathrm s}=6$, we have $k=2\pi m/6$ ($m=0,\dots,5$) and $\varepsilon_k=-2t\cos(2\pi m/6)=-2t\cos(60^\circ m)$: $$m=0:\ -2t,\quad m=1,5:\ -2t\cos60^\circ=-t,\quad m=2,4:\ -2t\cos120^\circ=+t,\quad m=3:\ -2t\cos180^\circ=+2t .$$ The levels are $-2t$ (one level), $-t$ (doubly degenerate, $m=1,5$), $+t$ (doubly degenerate), and $+2t$ (one level) (Figure 58.2). The six electrons enter the lower three orbitals (the one at $-2t$ and the two at $-t$) with opposite spins. The total energy is $$E_{\pi}=2\times(-2t)+4\times(-t)=-8t .$$ The three localized double bonds amount to three copies of the ground state of the two-site model (Example 58.2) placed side by side, so their energy is $3\times(-2t)=-6t$. The difference is $-6t-(-8t)=2t$: when the electrons delocalize by forming a ring, the system is stabilized by $2t$ (the resonance stabilization energy). To match the measured resonance energy of benzene (about 150 kJ/mol $=1.55\ \mathrm{eV}$, estimated from the heat of hydrogenation), we set $2t=1.55\ \mathrm{eV}$, that is, $t\approx0.78\ \mathrm{eV}$. (This is the same result as the delocalization energy $2\abs{\beta}$ of the Hückel method in Chapter 40.)

58.2.4 An introduction to the one-body density matrix — $\mel{\Phi}{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j}{\Phi}$

The expectation value of a one-body operator can be written as the trace of the product of the matrix $t$ and the “one-body density matrix.” Suppose that the Slater determinant $\ket{\Phi}$ is a state in which the eigenorbitals of $\hat{t}$ (operators $\hat{d}_\alpha$) are occupied with $n_\alpha=1$ or $0$. Then $\ket{\Phi}$ is an eigenstate of $\hat{n}_\alpha=\hat{d}_\alpha^\dagger\hat{d}_\alpha$ (eigenvalue $n_\alpha$), and $\hat{d}_\beta\ket{\Phi}$ is the state with an electron removed from orbital $\beta$ (it is $0$ if $\beta$ is empty). If $\alpha\ne\beta$, then $\hat{d}_\alpha\ket{\Phi}$ and $\hat{d}_\beta\ket{\Phi}$ have different occupation numbers, so they are orthogonal. Therefore the following holds:

\begin{equation} \mel{\Phi}{\hat{d}_\alpha^\dagger\hat{d}_\beta}{\Phi}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,n_\alpha \label{eq:58-dens1} \end{equation}

Returning to the original basis $\hat{c}_i=\sum_\alpha U_{i\alpha}\hat{d}_\alpha$, we find that the following holds:

\begin{equation} \mel{\Phi}{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j}{\Phi}=\sum_\alpha n_\alpha\,U_{i\alpha}^*U_{j\alpha}\equiv P_{ji},\qquad \mel{\Phi}{\hat{T}}{\Phi}=\sum_{ij}t_{ij}P_{ji}=\mathrm{tr}\,(tP) \label{eq:58-dens2} \end{equation}

The matrix $P=\sum_{\alpha\in\text{occ}}\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}$ is the projection matrix onto the occupied orbitals and is called the one-body density matrix. The diagonal element $P_{ii}=\mel{\Phi}{\hat{n}_i}{\Phi}$ is the number of electrons in orbital $i$ (the charge density at the site), and the off-diagonal element expresses how much the electrons move back and forth between orbitals $i$ and $j$ (the strength of the bond).

Example 58.4: Bond order of benzene — from the one-body density matrix

For the ground state of benzene, find $\mel{\Phi}{\hat{c}_{j\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{j+1,\sigma}}{\Phi}$ for neighboring sites with the spin $\sigma$ fixed, and reproduce the bond order $p=\sum_\sigma\mel{\Phi}{\hat{c}_{j\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{j+1,\sigma}}{\Phi}$ and the energy $-8t$.

Solution. Using the transformation $\hat{c}_{j\sigma}=N_{\mathrm s}^{-1/2}\sum_ke^{ikj}\hat{c}_{k\sigma}$ ($N_{\mathrm s}=6$) of Section 58.2.3, we have $$\mel{\Phi}{\hat{c}_{j\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{j'\sigma}}{\Phi}=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{k,k'}e^{-ikj}e^{ik'j'}\mel{\Phi}{\hat{c}_{k\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k'\sigma}}{\Phi}=\frac16\sum_{k\in\text{occ}}e^{ik(j'-j)}$$ (the terms with $k\ne k'$ vanish by Eq. \eqref{eq:58-dens1}). The occupied orbitals of spin $\sigma$ are the three with $k=0,\pm\pi/3$, so for $j'=j+1$ we obtain $$\frac16\bigl(1+e^{i\pi/3}+e^{-i\pi/3}\bigr)=\frac16\bigl(1+2\cos60^\circ\bigr)=\frac16\times2=\frac13 .$$ The bond order is the sum over the two spins, $p=2\times\tfrac13=\tfrac23$, which agrees with the value in Chapter 40. The value is the same for every pair of neighbors, so the six bonds are equivalent. (For $j'=j$ the value is $\tfrac16\times3=\tfrac12$, so the two spins together put $1$ electron on each site.) Taking the expectation value of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-tb2} and summing over the six bonds and the two spins, the energy is $$\mel{\Phi}{\hat{H}}{\Phi}=-t\sum_{\text{6 bonds}}\sum_\sigma\bigl(\tfrac13+\tfrac13\bigr)=-t\times6\times2\times\tfrac23=-8t$$ which agrees with Example 58.3 (the $+\tfrac13$ in each term is the contribution of $\hat{c}_{j}^\dagger\hat{c}_{j+1}$ and of its complex-conjugate term).

58.3 Two-Body Operators — Why Are the Indices in the Order $l,k$?

58.3.1 Two-body operators in first quantization: the Coulomb interaction

One-body operators alone cannot express the forces between electrons. This is because the Coulomb repulsion $v(\bm{x}_a,\bm{x}_b)$ between electron $a$ and electron $b$ depends simultaneously on the coordinates of two electrons. In classical mechanics, the Coulomb energy of $N$ point charges is the sum of the pairwise energies $\dfrac{q_aq_b}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\abs{\bm{r}_a-\bm{r}_b}}$, with each pair added exactly once (AD 03). In quantum mechanics it is exactly the same.

Definition 58.2: Two-body operator

For an operator $v(\bm{x}_a,\bm{x}_b)$ acting on the coordinates of two particles (unchanged when the two particles $a\ne b$ are interchanged), the sum over pairs of particles \begin{equation} \hat{V}=\sum_{a\lt b}v(\bm{x}_a,\bm{x}_b)=\frac12\sum_{a\ne b}v(\bm{x}_a,\bm{x}_b) \label{eq:58-V1} \end{equation} is called a two-body operator. The factor $\tfrac12$ corrects for the fact that, since $(a,b)$ and $(b,a)$ are the same pair, each pair is counted twice in the sum $\sum_{a\ne b}$. For the Coulomb repulsion between electrons, $v(\bm{x}_a,\bm{x}_b)=\dfrac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\abs{\bm{r}_a-\bm{r}_b}}$, which does not depend on spin.

It is important that the terms with $a=b$ are excluded from this sum. This is because an electron does not interact with itself through the Coulomb interaction ($v(\bm{x}_a,\bm{x}_a)=e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\cdot0)=\infty$). When we write the operator in second-quantized form, this fact shows up as the order of the indices.

58.3.2 Two-electron integrals

What determines the matrix elements of a two-body operator is the following integral.

Definition 58.3: Two-electron integral

For four spin orbitals $\chi_i,\chi_j,\chi_k,\chi_l$, \begin{equation} \mel{ij}{v}{kl}\equiv\iint\chi_i^*(\bm{x}_1)\,\chi_j^*(\bm{x}_2)\,v(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)\,\chi_k(\bm{x}_1)\,\chi_l(\bm{x}_2)\dd\bm{x}_1\dd\bm{x}_2 \label{eq:58-eri} \end{equation} is called the two-electron integral. The first orbitals of the bra and the ket ($i$ and $k$) correspond to the coordinates of electron 1, and the second orbitals ($j$ and $l$) to those of electron 2 (the same notation as in Chapter 35).

Since $v$ is real and $v(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)=v(\bm{x}_2,\bm{x}_1)$, there are the following two symmetries.

\begin{equation} \mel{ij}{v}{kl}=\mel{ji}{v}{lk},\qquad \mel{ij}{v}{kl}^*=\mel{kl}{v}{ij} \label{eq:58-sym} \end{equation}

The former is obtained merely by interchanging the names of the integration variables, $\bm{x}_1\leftrightarrow \bm{x}_2$ (a renaming of electrons 1 and 2). The latter is obtained by taking the complex conjugate of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-eri} and using the reality of $v$. The latter guarantees that $\hat{V}$ is Hermitian. When $v$ does not depend on spin, the spin integration gives $\mel{ij}{v}{kl}\propto\delta_{\sigma_i\sigma_k}\delta_{\sigma_j\sigma_l}$ (the spin of electron 1 is the same in $i$ and $k$, and that of electron 2 is the same in $j$ and $l$).

Correspondence of notation: Two-body operators in other books

This way of writing the indices, $\mel{ij}{v}{kl}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$, is the same, even down to how the indices of electrons 1 and 2 are arranged, as in the companion volumes “Density Functional Theory” (AD 07, Chapter 6, Section 6.6 (in Japanese); there the creation and annihilation operators are written without hats, as $a_\mu^\dagger,a_\mu$), “Linear Response Theory” (AD 08, Appendix B, Section B.6; the same $\hat{c}_i$ as in this book), and “Computational Materials Science” (AD 05, Chapter 9; $\mel{\chi_a\chi_b}{1/r_{12}}{\chi_c\chi_d}$). It is related to the chemists' notation $[ik\vert jl]$ by $\mel{ij}{v}{kl}=[ik\vert jl]$ (Chapter 35).

58.3.3 Second quantization of two-body operators

Theorem 58.2: Second quantization of two-body operators

The two-body operator $\hat{V}=\dfrac12\sum_{a\ne b}v(\bm{x}_a,\bm{x}_b)$ corresponds to the following operator in Fock space. \begin{equation} \boxed{\ \hat{V}=\frac12\sum_{i,j,k,l}\mel{ij}{v}{kl}\,\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k\ } \label{eq:58-V2} \end{equation} Note the order of the indices: in the matrix element $\mel{ij}{v}{kl}$ the order is $(i,j)$ and $(k,l)$, but the operators are arranged in the order $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger$ and $\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$ (for the annihilation operators, $l$ comes first and $k$ second).

The case of bosons

For bosons (Chapter 57) as well, simply replacing $\hat{c}\to\hat{a}$ and the anticommutator by the commutator $\comm{\hat{a}_i}{\hat{a}_j^\dagger}=\delta_{ij}$ gives the same forms, $\hat{T}=\sum_{ij}\mel{i}{\hat{t}}{j}\hat{a}_i^\dagger\hat{a}_j$ and $\hat{V}=\tfrac12\sum\mel{ij}{v}{kl}\hat{a}_i^\dagger\hat{a}_j^\dagger\hat{a}_l\hat{a}_k$ (this can be checked by acting on symmetrized states). The differences are (i) that any number of particles can occupy the same orbital, and (ii) that the sign of the exchange term $\mel{ij}{v}{ji}$ becomes $+$ (Exercise 58.7). The two-body operator for bosons is the foundation for the interactions of photons and phonons in Chapter 60.

Derivation [i]: Agreement for $N=2$

The two-electron state $\ket{kl}=\hat{c}_k^\dagger\hat{c}_l^\dagger\ket{0}$ corresponds to the wave function $\Phi_{kl}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\chi_k(\bm{x}_1)\chi_l(\bm{x}_2)-\chi_l(\bm{x}_1)\chi_k(\bm{x}_2)\bigr]$. For $N=2$ we have $\hat{V}=v(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)$, so the first-quantized matrix element is $$\mel{\Phi_{ij}}{v}{\Phi_{kl}}=\tfrac12\bigl[\mel{ij}{v}{kl}-\mel{ij}{v}{lk}-\mel{ji}{v}{kl}+\mel{ji}{v}{lk}\bigr]=\mel{ij}{v}{kl}-\mel{ij}{v}{lk}$$ (the second equality uses $\mel{ji}{v}{lk}=\mel{ij}{v}{kl}$ and $\mel{ji}{v}{kl}=\mel{ij}{v}{lk}$ from Eq. \eqref{eq:58-sym}). In second quantization, on the other hand, we rewrite the indices of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-V2} as $(p,q,r,s)$, so that $\hat{V}=\tfrac12\sum\mel{pq}{v}{rs}\hat{c}_p^\dagger\hat{c}_q^\dagger\hat{c}_s\hat{c}_r$, and first examine its action on a two-electron state. Using the anticommutation relations twice, we obtain $$\hat{c}_r\hat{c}_k^\dagger\hat{c}_l^\dagger\ket{0}=\delta_{rk}\hat{c}_l^\dagger\ket{0}-\hat{c}_k^\dagger\hat{c}_r\hat{c}_l^\dagger\ket{0}=\delta_{rk}\hat{c}_l^\dagger\ket{0}-\delta_{rl}\hat{c}_k^\dagger\ket{0},\qquad \hat{c}_s\hat{c}_r\hat{c}_k^\dagger\hat{c}_l^\dagger\ket{0}=\bigl(\delta_{rk}\delta_{sl}-\delta_{rl}\delta_{sk}\bigr)\ket{0}$$ The bra side has the same form: $\bra{0}\hat{c}_j\hat{c}_i\hat{c}_p^\dagger\hat{c}_q^\dagger\ket{0}=\delta_{ip}\delta_{jq}-\delta_{iq}\delta_{jp}$. Hence we obtain $$\bra{0}\hat{c}_j\hat{c}_i\,\hat{V}\,\hat{c}_k^\dagger\hat{c}_l^\dagger\ket{0}=\frac12\sum_{pqrs}\mel{pq}{v}{rs}\bigl(\delta_{ip}\delta_{jq}-\delta_{iq}\delta_{jp}\bigr)\bigl(\delta_{rk}\delta_{sl}-\delta_{rl}\delta_{sk}\bigr)=\frac12\bigl[\mel{ij}{v}{kl}-\mel{ij}{v}{lk}-\mel{ji}{v}{kl}+\mel{ji}{v}{lk}\bigr]$$ which agrees with the first-quantized value.

∎

Derivation [ii]: General $N$

We start from the following identity. Using the anticommutation relation $\hat{c}_k\hat{c}_j^\dagger=\delta_{jk}-\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_k$, we have $$\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_k\,\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l=\delta_{jk}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_l-\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_k\hat{c}_l=\delta_{jk}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_l+\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k,$$ that is, \begin{equation} \hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k=\bigl(\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_k\bigr)\bigl(\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\bigr)-\delta_{jk}\,\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_l \label{eq:58-normal} \end{equation} Here $\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l$ and $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_k$ on the right-hand side are the “reassignment” operations of Formula 58.1. We let the operators act on the Slater determinant $\ket{K}=\ket{k_1\cdots k_N}$. First, $\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\ket{K}=\sum_b\delta_{l,k_b}\ket{K_{b\to j}}$ (the state in which the $b$th orbital has been reassigned to $j$). Next we apply $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_k$. Since the label at the $a$th position ($a\ne b$) of the state $\ket{K_{b\to j}}$ is $k_a$ and the label at the $b$th position is $j$, $$\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_k\ket{K_{b\to j}}=\sum_{a\ne b}\delta_{k,k_a}\ket{K_{a\to i,\,b\to j}}+\delta_{k,j}\ket{K_{b\to i}}$$ The second term is the term in which “$\hat{c}_k$ annihilated the $j$ that had just been put into the $b$th position, and it was reassigned to $i$.” Therefore $$\bigl(\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_k\bigr)\bigl(\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\bigr)\ket{K}=\sum_{a\ne b}\delta_{k,k_a}\delta_{l,k_b}\ket{K_{a\to i,\,b\to j}}+\delta_{jk}\sum_b\delta_{l,k_b}\ket{K_{b\to i}}$$ and the last term is none other than $\delta_{jk}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_l\ket{K}$. The second term on the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-normal}, $-\delta_{jk}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_l$, cancels it exactly, so we obtain \begin{equation} \hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k\ket{K}=\sum_{a\ne b}\delta_{k,k_a}\delta_{l,k_b}\ket{K_{a\to i,\,b\to j}} \label{eq:58-2act} \end{equation} The right-hand side is a sum of operations that reassign only the labels at two different positions $a\ne b$, $(k_a,k_b)\to(i,j)$. Therefore $$\hat{V}\ket{K}=\frac12\sum_{a\ne b}\sum_{i,j}\mel{ij}{v}{k_ak_b}\ket{K_{a\to i,\,b\to j}} .$$ On the first-quantized side, we act with $\tfrac12\sum_{a\ne b}v(\bm{x}_a,\bm{x}_b)$ on $\Phi_K$. Expanding the function of two variables $v(\bm{x}_a,\bm{x}_b)\chi_{k}(\bm{x}_a)\chi_{l}(\bm{x}_b)$ in the products $\chi_i(\bm{x}_a)\chi_j(\bm{x}_b)$, the coefficient is $\iint\chi_i^*(\bm{x}_a)\chi_j^*(\bm{x}_b)v\,\chi_k(\bm{x}_a)\chi_l(\bm{x}_b)=\mel{ij}{v}{kl}$, which is the two-variable version of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-tk}: $$v(\bm{x}_a,\bm{x}_b)\,\chi_k(\bm{x}_a)\chi_l(\bm{x}_b)=\sum_{i,j}\mel{ij}{v}{kl}\,\chi_i(\bm{x}_a)\chi_j(\bm{x}_b) .$$ In one term $\prod_c\chi_{k_{P(c)}}(\bm{x}_c)$ of the Slater determinant, $v(\bm{x}_a,\bm{x}_b)$ acts only on the $a$th and $b$th factors $\chi_{k_{\alpha}}(\bm{x}_a)\chi_{k_{\beta}}(\bm{x}_b)$ ($\alpha=P(a)$, $\beta=P(b)$); by the equation above, these two factors are replaced by $\chi_i(\bm{x}_a)\chi_j(\bm{x}_b)$, with the coefficient $\mel{ij}{v}{k_\alpha k_\beta}$ attached. Since the permutation $P$ is a one-to-one correspondence, as we run over the distinct pairs $a\ne b$, $(\alpha,\beta)=(P(a),P(b))$ also runs over the distinct pairs $\alpha\ne\beta$ exactly once each ($a\ne b\Leftrightarrow\alpha\ne\beta$). So, as in the one-body case, we re-sum over $(\alpha,\beta)$, attach the sign $\mathrm{sgn}\,P$, and sum over $P$; this yields the Slater determinant for the rewritten sequence of labels, and we obtain $$\tfrac12\sum_{a\ne b}v(\bm{x}_a,\bm{x}_b)\,\Phi_K=\frac12\sum_{\alpha\ne\beta}\sum_{i,j}\mel{ij}{v}{k_\alpha k_\beta}\,\Phi_{K_{\alpha\to i,\,\beta\to j}}$$ This is the same expression as the second-quantization result above for $\hat{V}\ket{K}$ (with $a,b$ renamed $\alpha,\beta$). Since the Slater determinants form a complete set, the two agree.

∎

58.3.4 Why the order $\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$? — normal order and self-interaction

The rightmost factors in Eq. \eqref{eq:58-V2} are $\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$ (two annihilation operators), and the leftmost are $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger$ (two creation operators). The form in which all creation operators are placed on the left and all annihilation operators on the right is called normal order. Since a product of operators acts from the right, $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$ acts in the order “first annihilate two electrons with $\hat{c}_k$ and $\hat{c}_l$, and then create two with $\hat{c}_j^\dagger$ and $\hat{c}_i^\dagger$.” This order has the following two meanings.

  • Self-interaction is removed automatically. Since the annihilation comes first, the two electrons that are annihilated are two distinct electrons that were there from the start ($a\ne b$ in Derivation [ii]). The event “the electron just created is annihilated at once” cannot occur. If we change the order so that a creation operator comes between the annihilation operators, as in $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_k\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l$, then Eq. \eqref{eq:58-normal} gives \begin{equation} \frac12\sum_{ijkl}\mel{ij}{v}{kl}\,\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_k\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l=\hat{V}+\frac12\sum_{i,l}\Bigl(\sum_j\mel{ij}{v}{jl}\Bigr)\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_l=\hat{V}+\frac12\,v(0)\,\hat{N} \label{eq:58-selfint} \end{equation} (the last equality uses the completeness relation $\sum_j\chi_j^*(\bm{x}_2)\chi_j(\bm{x}_1)=\delta(\bm{x}_1-\bm{x}_2)$ of the complete set: $\sum_j\mel{ij}{v}{jl}=\int\chi_i^*(\bm{x})\,v(\bm{x},\bm{x})\,\chi_l(\bm{x})\dd\bm{x}=v(0)\delta_{il}$, where $v(0)$ is the value of $v$ at zero distance between the electrons). The extra term $\tfrac12v(0)\hat{N}$ is the energy of a single electron interacting with itself, and for the Coulomb interaction $v(0)=\infty$.
  • The normal-ordered form gives the correct answer for the vacuum and for a small number of particles. We have $\hat{V}\ket{0}=0$ and, for a one-electron state, $\hat{V}\hat{c}_k^\dagger\ket{0}=0$ (two annihilation operators in a row give $0$). This correctly expresses the fact that the interaction energy between electrons is 0 when there is only one electron (Example 58.5).

Caution: If you mistakenly write $\hat{c}_k\hat{c}_l$, the result changes sign

If we swap the order of the annihilation operators and write $\hat{V}'=\tfrac12\sum\mel{ij}{v}{kl}\,\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_k\hat{c}_l$, then since $\hat{c}_k\hat{c}_l=-\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$ we obtain $$\hat{V}'=-\hat{V}$$ In other words, the repulsion between electrons becomes an attraction, and both $U_{ab}$ and $J_{ab}$ change sign (for example, the triplet energy $h_{aa}+h_{bb}+(U_{ab}-J_{ab})$ becomes $h_{aa}+h_{bb}-(U_{ab}-J_{ab})$). Even if we keep the operators as $\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$ but mistakenly take the indices of the integral as $\mel{ij}{v}{lk}$, renaming the dummy indices $k\leftrightarrow l$ makes it identical to what we get by writing $\hat{c}_k\hat{c}_l$, so it is the same sign error. Unlike a one-body operator $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$, a two-body operator with its indices in the wrong order remains a plausible operator that is Hermitian and conserves the particle number, and only the answer is wrong, so the mistake is hard to notice. We check this numerically in Example 58.6.

Example 58.5: A single electron has no interaction energy — the effect of normal order

Consider a system with only one orbital, in the state $\ket{1}=\hat{c}_1^\dagger\ket{0}$ containing one electron. Apply to it (1) the normal-ordered $\hat{V}=\tfrac12u\,\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_1\hat{c}_1$ ($u=\mel{11}{v}{11}$) and (2) the reordered $\hat{V}''=\tfrac12u\,\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_1\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_1$, and compare the expectation values.

Solution. (1) We apply $\hat{c}_1\hat{c}_1$ to the one-electron state. We have $\hat{c}_1\ket{1}=\hat{c}_1\hat{c}_1^\dagger\ket{0}=(1-\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_1)\ket{0}=\ket{0}$, and further $\hat{c}_1\ket{0}=0$. Hence $\hat{V}\ket{1}=\tfrac12u\,\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_1^\dagger\cdot0=0$, so the interaction energy is $0$ (this also follows from $\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_1^\dagger=0$). (2) Since $\hat{V}''=\tfrac12u\,\hat{n}_1^2=\tfrac12u\,\hat{n}_1$ ($\hat{n}_1^2=\hat{n}_1$), we have $\hat{V}''\ket{1}=\tfrac12u\ket{1}$, and the expectation value is $\tfrac12u\gt0$. Even though there is only one electron, a repulsion energy $\tfrac12\mel{11}{v}{11}$ with itself appears. This is the one-orbital version of $\tfrac12\sum_j\mel{1j}{v}{j1}\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_1$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:58-selfint}. In a complete basis with the Coulomb interaction, this extra term becomes $\tfrac12v(0)\hat{N}=\infty$.

Example 58.6: Comparing the spectra of first and second quantization with random two-electron integrals

For a system with three spin orbitals and two electrons, compare the spectra of the operator $\hat{V}$ with random two-electron integrals $\mel{ij}{v}{kl}$ in three ways: (1) first quantization (in the $3\times3=9$-dimensional product space, the three-dimensional subspace that is antisymmetric under the exchange of electrons 1 and 2), (2) second quantization with the correct order $\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$, and (3) the wrong order $\hat{c}_k\hat{c}_l$.

Solution. We compute with the following program (Python). For $\mel{ij}{v}{kl}$ we use a real random tensor with the two symmetries of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-sym}. The second-quantized operators are represented by expressing $\hat{c}_i$ as a $2^3\times2^3$ matrix (Jordan–Wigner transformation: the $i$th operator is the Kronecker product of $i$ copies of $\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$, one annihilation matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, and identity matrices for the remaining factors).

# Compare the spectra of first and second quantization for 3 orbitals and 2 electrons
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(58)
M = 3                                              # number of spin orbitals (we put N = 2 electrons in them)

# --- random two-electron integrals v[i,j,k,l] = <ij|v|kl> (real; <ij|v|kl> = <ji|v|lk> = <kl|v|ij>)
a = rng.normal(size=(M, M, M, M))
a = a + a.transpose(1, 0, 3, 2)                    # swapping the names of electrons 1 and 2
v = a + a.transpose(2, 3, 0, 1)                    # Hermitian (symmetric, since it is real)

# --- first quantization: make v a matrix on the M×M-dimensional product space and pick out only the antisymmetric states
V1 = v.reshape(M*M, M*M)                           # row (i,j), column (k,l)
# P = (1 - SWAP)/2: projection matrix onto the antisymmetric part, using the swap of electrons 1 and 2 (the SWAP matrix)
# basis: √2 times column (i,j) of P = (|ij> - |ji>)/√2 (i<j). These M(M-1)/2 = 3 vectors form an orthonormal basis of the three-dimensional antisymmetric subspace
# B: the 9×3 matrix with them arranged as columns. B^T V1 B is the 3×3 matrix of the electron–electron interaction v(1,2) in this subspace
P = (np.eye(M*M) - np.eye(M*M).reshape(M, M, M, M).transpose(0, 1, 3, 2).reshape(M*M, M*M)) / 2
basis = [P[:, i*M + j] * np.sqrt(2) for i in range(M) for j in range(i+1, M)]
B = np.array(basis).T                              # antisymmetric states (|ij>-|ji>)/sqrt2, i<j
E1 = np.linalg.eigvalsh(B.T @ V1 @ B)              # first-quantization spectrum (0.5 sum_{a!=b} = v(1,2))

# --- second quantization: make c_i a 2^M × 2^M matrix by the Jordan–Wigner transformation
Z, I2, s = np.diag([1., -1.]), np.eye(2), np.array([[0., 1.], [0., 0.]])
def kron(*m):
    out = np.eye(1)
    for x in m: out = np.kron(out, x)
    return out
c = [kron(*([Z]*i + [s] + [I2]*(M-i-1))) for i in range(M)]
def V_hat(order):                                   # order='lk' (correct) or 'kl' (wrong)
    H = 0
    for i in range(M):
        for j in range(M):
            for k in range(M):
                for l in range(M):
                    cc = c[l] @ c[k] if order == 'lk' else c[k] @ c[l]
                    H = H + 0.5 * v[i, j, k, l] * c[i].T @ c[j].T @ cc
    return H
num = sum(ci.T @ ci for ci in c)
sel = np.isclose(np.diag(num), 2)                  # subspace with particle number 2
E2 = np.linalg.eigvalsh(V_hat('lk')[np.ix_(sel, sel)])
E3 = np.linalg.eigvalsh(V_hat('kl')[np.ix_(sel, sel)])
print("First quantization :", np.round(E1, 3))
print("Correct order      :", np.round(E2, 3))
print("Wrong order        :", np.round(E3, 3))

The results of the run are as follows.

MethodEigenvalues (three)
(1) First quantization$-6.170$, $1.720$, $12.007$
(2) Second quantization ($\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$, correct order)$-6.170$, $1.720$, $12.007$
(3) Second quantization ($\hat{c}_k\hat{c}_l$, wrong order)$-12.007$, $-1.720$, $6.170$

With the correct order, the three eigenvalues agree exactly with those of first quantization, and with the wrong order all the signs are reversed (since $\hat{V}'=-\hat{V}$, the eigenvalues are multiplied by $-1$). The same result is obtained with different random numbers. (For three or more electrons, the first-quantization side must be built in the antisymmetric space obtained by choosing $N$ of the $M$ orbitals, adding $v$ for each pair of electrons. The program above is only for two electrons.)

58.4 The Two-Electron Energy $U_{ab}-J_{ab}$ — Helium 1s2s and the Effect of Exchange

58.4.1 Expectation value in the Slater determinant $\ket{\Phi_{ab}}$

As a first application of Theorem 58.2, consider the state in which one electron is placed in each of two different spin orbitals $\chi_a,\chi_b$,

\begin{equation} \ket{\Phi_{ab}}=\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b^\dagger\ket{0}\qquad(a\ne b) \label{eq:58-Phiab} \end{equation}

and find the expectation value of the electron–electron repulsion $\hat{V}$ in it. This quantity is the basic unit of the Hartree–Fock approximation.

Formula 58.3: The expectation value of $\hat{V}$ in a two-electron Slater determinant

\begin{equation} \mel{\Phi_{ab}}{\hat{V}}{\Phi_{ab}}=U_{ab}-J_{ab},\qquad U_{ab}=\mel{ab}{v}{ab},\quad J_{ab}=\mel{ab}{v}{ba} \label{eq:58-UJ} \end{equation} $U_{ab}$ is called the Coulomb integral and $J_{ab}$ the exchange integral (Chapter 35).

Derivation

In the result $\mel{\Phi_{ij}}{\hat{V}}{\Phi_{kl}}=\mel{ij}{v}{kl}-\mel{ij}{v}{lk}$ obtained in Derivation [i] of Section 58.3.3, setting $(i,j)=(k,l)=(a,b)$ gives $\mel{ab}{v}{ab}-\mel{ab}{v}{ba}=U_{ab}-J_{ab}$.

It can also be read in the language of operators. Of the products of four operators in $\hat{V}$, only the four terms of the form “annihilate $a,b$ and create $a,b$” contribute to the expectation value in $\ket{\Phi_{ab}}$: $$\tfrac12\Bigl[\mel{ab}{v}{ab}\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_b\hat{c}_a+\mel{ba}{v}{ba}\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_a\hat{c}_b+\mel{ab}{v}{ba}\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_a\hat{c}_b+\mel{ba}{v}{ab}\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b\hat{c}_a\Bigr].$$ Here $\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_b\hat{c}_a=\hat{n}_a\hat{n}_b$, $\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_a\hat{c}_b=\hat{n}_a\hat{n}_b$, $\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_a\hat{c}_b=-\hat{n}_a\hat{n}_b$, and $\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b\hat{c}_a=-\hat{n}_a\hat{n}_b$ (because $\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_a\hat{c}_b=-\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_b\hat{c}_a$, and so on), and from Eq. \eqref{eq:58-sym} we have $\mel{ba}{v}{ba}=U_{ab}$ and $\mel{ba}{v}{ab}=J_{ab}$, so the expression above becomes $(U_{ab}-J_{ab})\hat{n}_a\hat{n}_b$. In $\ket{\Phi_{ab}}$ we have $\hat{n}_a\hat{n}_b=1$.

∎

Therefore, the total energy of the two electrons, including the one-body term, is, using the one-electron integral $h_{aa}=\mel{a}{\hat{h}}{a}$ (kinetic energy and nuclear attraction),

\begin{equation} E_{ab}=\mel{\Phi_{ab}}{\hat{T}+\hat{V}}{\Phi_{ab}}=h_{aa}+h_{bb}+U_{ab}-J_{ab} \label{eq:58-Eab} \end{equation}

This is the same as the expression obtained in Chapter 35 by expanding the two-electron Slater determinant, but in second quantization the four lines above are enough. Drawing the two terms $U_{ab}$ and $J_{ab}$ as vertex diagrams gives Figure 58.4.

Correspondence of notation: $U_{ab},J_{ab}$ and the $J_{ab},K_{ab}$ of Szabo–Ostlund and AD 07

The Coulomb integral $U_{ab}$ and exchange integral $J_{ab}$ of this book are the same as the symbols of “Computational Materials Science” (AD 05), using the same letters as the $U$ of the Hubbard model and the $J$ of the Hund coupling (which is convenient from Chapter 59 on), whereas the Szabo–Ostlund textbook and “Density Functional Theory” (AD 07) write the Coulomb integral as $J_{ab}$ and the exchange integral as $K_{ab}$: $U_{ab}\leftrightarrow J_{ab}^{\mathrm{S\text{-}O}}$, $J_{ab}\leftrightarrow K_{ab}^{\mathrm{S\text{-}O}}$. Since the same letter $J$ has the opposite meaning, take care when reading other books. For the energy, this book writes $E=\sum h_{aa}+\tfrac12\sum(U_{ab}-J_{ab})$, whereas S–O and AD 07 write $\sum h_{aa}+\tfrac12\sum(J_{ab}-K_{ab})$ (for a detailed correspondence table, see Table 35.2 in Chapter 35). Also, $J_{ab}$ is distinct from the total angular momentum $J$ of an atom (Chapter 25) and from the $\alpha$ of the Hückel method (which is also called the “Coulomb integral” but is a different quantity; Chapter 40).

58.4.2 Parallel or antiparallel spins

Let the orbitals $\chi_a,\chi_b$ be the products of spatial orbitals $\phi_a,\phi_b$ and spin functions, $\chi_a=\phi_a\xi_{\sigma_a}$, $\chi_b=\phi_b\xi_{\sigma_b}$. Since $v$ does not depend on spin, the spin integral of electron 1 and the spin integral of electron 2 appear separately.

Notation conventions: Integrals over spin orbitals and integrals over spatial orbitals

In Formula 58.3, $U_{ab},J_{ab}$ are integrals over the spin orbitals $\chi_a,\chi_b$. To distinguish the values, from here on we write the integrals over spin orbitals as $U^{\mathrm{so}}_{ab}=\mel{ab}{v}{ab}$ and $J^{\mathrm{so}}_{ab}=\mel{ab}{v}{ba}$. In contrast, from the end of this subsection through Section 58.4.3, Examples 58.7 and 58.8, and Exercise 58.3, $U_{ab},J_{ab}$ denote the integrals over the spatial orbitals $\phi_a,\phi_b$: $$U_{ab}=\mel{\phi_a\phi_b}{v}{\phi_a\phi_b},\qquad J_{ab}=\mel{\phi_a\phi_b}{v}{\phi_b\phi_a}$$ The relation between the two is given in the two bullet points below: $U^{\mathrm{so}}_{ab}=U_{ab}$ (independent of spin), and $J^{\mathrm{so}}_{ab}=J_{ab}$ (parallel spins) or $0$ (antiparallel spins). In the Hartree–Fock treatment of Section 58.7, we again use integrals over spin orbitals.

  • $U^{\mathrm{so}}_{ab}=\mel{ab}{v}{ab}$: the spin integrals of electron 1 ($\braket{\xi_{\sigma_a}}{\xi_{\sigma_a}}=1$) and of electron 2 ($\braket{\xi_{\sigma_b}}{\xi_{\sigma_b}}=1$) are both 1, so it does not depend on spin and equals the Coulomb integral of the spatial orbitals alone, $U_{ab}=\mel{\phi_a\phi_b}{v}{\phi_a\phi_b}$.
  • $J^{\mathrm{so}}_{ab}=\mel{ab}{v}{ba}$: the spin integral of electron 1 is $\braket{\xi_{\sigma_a}}{\xi_{\sigma_b}}$ and that of electron 2 is $\braket{\xi_{\sigma_b}}{\xi_{\sigma_a}}$, and their product is $\delta_{\sigma_a\sigma_b}$. If the spins are parallel ($\sigma_a=\sigma_b$), it equals the exchange integral of the spatial orbitals, $J_{ab}=\mel{\phi_a\phi_b}{v}{\phi_b\phi_a}$, and if they are antiparallel, it is $0$.

That is, for parallel spins ($\uparrow\uparrow$ or $\downarrow\downarrow$) we have $\mel{\Phi_{ab}}{\hat{V}}{\Phi_{ab}}=U_{ab}-J_{ab}$, and for antiparallel spins ($\uparrow\downarrow$) it is $U_{ab}$. The exchange integral $J_{ab}$ equals the Coulomb repulsion energy between the charges of the overlap density $\phi_a^*\phi_b$ of the two orbitals, and since the Coulomb repulsion is positive definite, $J_{ab}\ge0$ (Chapter 35). Therefore, even for the same combination of spatial orbitals, the electron–electron repulsion energy is lower when the spins are parallel. This is because electrons with parallel spins cannot approach each other, owing to the Pauli exclusion principle (the Pauli hole, Section 58.5.5), and so avoid the Coulomb repulsion. The standard explanation of Hund's rules (Chapter 34, Section 34.6) is this $-J_{ab}$ (the lowering of the Coulomb repulsion by the exchange hole). (For the ground terms of atoms, the origin of Hund's rules is discussed as involving not only the electron–electron repulsion but also a contribution from the nuclear-attraction energy through the contraction of the orbitals, so the matter is still under debate.)

58.4.3 Singlet and triplet — helium 1s2s

Consider the excited state 1s2s of the helium atom (one electron each in the 1s and 2s orbitals). The spatial orbitals $\phi_a=1\mathrm{s}$, $\phi_b=2\mathrm{s}$ are orthogonal. There are four spin combinations, which, written as Slater determinants, are $\ket{a\!\uparrow b\!\uparrow}=\hat{c}_{a\uparrow}^\dagger\hat{c}_{b\uparrow}^\dagger\ket{0}$, $\ket{a\!\downarrow b\!\downarrow}$, $\ket{a\!\uparrow b\!\downarrow}$, and $\ket{a\!\downarrow b\!\uparrow}$.

Derivation: Energies of the four 1s2s states

The matrix elements of $\hat{V}$ are $\mel{\Phi_{ij}}{\hat{V}}{\Phi_{kl}}=\mel{ij}{v}{kl}-\mel{ij}{v}{lk}$, and $\hat{T}$ gives $h_{aa},h_{bb}$ for the respective orbitals (since the orbitals are orthogonal, the off-diagonal elements of $\hat{T}$ are 0).

(i) Parallel spins: for $\ket{a\!\uparrow b\!\uparrow}$ and $\ket{a\!\downarrow b\!\downarrow}$, Formula 58.3 gives $E=h_{aa}+h_{bb}+U_{ab}-J_{ab}$ in each case. The matrix elements with the other states are $0$, because $S_z$ differs.

(ii) Antiparallel spins: $\ket{a\!\uparrow b\!\downarrow}$ and $\ket{a\!\downarrow b\!\uparrow}$ have $S_z=0$ and can mix with each other. The diagonal element is $E_0\equiv h_{aa}+h_{bb}+U_{ab}$ (the exchange term is $\mel{a\!\uparrow\,b\!\downarrow}{v}{b\!\downarrow\,a\!\uparrow}=0$). The off-diagonal element is $$\mel{a\!\uparrow b\!\downarrow}{\hat{V}}{a\!\downarrow b\!\uparrow}=\mel{a\!\uparrow\,b\!\downarrow}{v}{a\!\downarrow\,b\!\uparrow}-\mel{a\!\uparrow\,b\!\downarrow}{v}{b\!\uparrow\,a\!\downarrow}=0-J_{ab}$$ (The first term is $0$ because the spin states $\uparrow$ and $\downarrow$ of electron 1 are orthogonal. In the second term, electron 1 goes $a\!\uparrow\to b\!\uparrow$ and electron 2 goes $b\!\downarrow\to a\!\downarrow$, so the spins match and the spatial exchange integral $J_{ab}$ remains. By the convention of Section 58.4.2, $J_{ab}$ is the integral over the spatial orbitals, $\mel{\phi_a\phi_b}{v}{\phi_b\phi_a}$.) Hence the 2×2 matrix is $$\begin{pmatrix}E_0&-J_{ab}\\-J_{ab}&E_0\end{pmatrix}$$ and its eigenvalues are $E_0\mp J_{ab}$, with eigenvectors $$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(\ket{a\!\uparrow b\!\downarrow}+\ket{a\!\downarrow b\!\uparrow}\bigr)\ \ (E_0-J_{ab}),\qquad \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(\ket{a\!\uparrow b\!\downarrow}-\ket{a\!\downarrow b\!\uparrow}\bigr)\ \ (E_0+J_{ab}).$$ The former has the same energy as the two parallel-spin states and is the $S_z=0$ component of the $S=1$ triplet (the three components of the triplet are degenerate); the latter is the $S=0$ singlet.

∎

\begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{T}}=E_0-J_{ab}\ \ (\text{triplet, threefold degenerate}),\qquad E_{\mathrm{S}}=E_0+J_{ab}\ \ (\text{singlet}),\qquad E_{\mathrm{S}}-E_{\mathrm{T}}=2J_{ab} \label{eq:58-ST} \end{equation}

$E_0=h_{aa}+h_{bb}+U_{ab}$ is the energy of the configuration $\ket{a\!\uparrow b\!\downarrow}$ in which the spin directions are fixed (exchange neglected), and it lies midway between the singlet and the triplet. The triplet is lower than the singlet by $2J_{ab}$. The above is the result of the approximation in which the same spatial orbitals $\phi_a=1\mathrm{s}$, $\phi_b=2\mathrm{s}$ are used for both the singlet and the triplet, and regarding the measured splitting $0.80\ \mathrm{eV}$ as $2J_{ab}$ is valid only within this approximation (the actual 2s orbital differs slightly between the singlet and the triplet). The helium atom is an experimental system in which this splitting can be confirmed by observation. The excitation energies of the 1s2s states measured from the ground state ($1\mathrm{s}^2$, ${}^1\mathrm{S}$) are $19.82\ \mathrm{eV}$ for the triplet $2^3\mathrm{S}$ and $20.62\ \mathrm{eV}$ for the singlet $2^1\mathrm{S}$ (Figure 58.5).

Example 58.7: Two orbitals, two electrons: the energies of the four spin-combination states

Express the energies of the four states in which one electron occupies each of two different spatial orbitals $\phi_a,\phi_b$ in terms of $h_{aa},h_{bb},U_{ab},J_{ab}$, and summarize their degeneracies. Further, find the average energy of the three triplet states and the one singlet state (weighted by degeneracy).

Solution. From the derivation in Section 58.4.3, the three states consisting of the parallel-spin states $\ket{a\!\uparrow b\!\uparrow}$ and $\ket{a\!\downarrow b\!\downarrow}$ and the $S_z=0$ state $(\ket{a\!\uparrow b\!\downarrow}+\ket{a\!\downarrow b\!\uparrow})/\sqrt2$ have the energy $E_{\mathrm{T}}=h_{aa}+h_{bb}+U_{ab}-J_{ab}$ (triplet, degeneracy 3). The state $(\ket{a\!\uparrow b\!\downarrow}-\ket{a\!\downarrow b\!\uparrow})/\sqrt2$ has the energy $E_{\mathrm{S}}=h_{aa}+h_{bb}+U_{ab}+J_{ab}$ (singlet, degeneracy 1). The average weighted by degeneracy is $$\frac{3E_{\mathrm{T}}+1\cdot E_{\mathrm{S}}}{4}=h_{aa}+h_{bb}+U_{ab}+\frac{-3J_{ab}+J_{ab}}{4}=h_{aa}+h_{bb}+U_{ab}-\frac{J_{ab}}{2}$$ which is lower than $E_0$ by $J_{ab}/2$. This agrees with $\tfrac14$ of the trace of the matrix over the four states (the sum of the diagonal elements: the two parallel-spin states have $E_0-J_{ab}$, and the two antiparallel-spin states have $E_0$), that is, $\tfrac14\bigl[2(E_0-J_{ab})+2E_0\bigr]=E_0-J_{ab}/2$ (the trace is independent of the choice of basis).

Example 58.8: Helium 1s2s: reading off $J_{ab}$ and $E_0$ from experiment

From the measured energies $E(2^3\mathrm{S})=19.82\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $E(2^1\mathrm{S})=20.62\ \mathrm{eV}$ (measured from the ground state), find $J_{ab}$ and the difference of $E_0$ from the ground state (in the approximation that uses the same spatial orbitals for the triplet and the singlet). What do we learn by comparing with the $J_{ab}$ calculated with hydrogen-like orbitals?

Solution. From Eq. \eqref{eq:58-ST}, the splitting is $E_{\mathrm{S}}-E_{\mathrm{T}}=2J_{ab}=20.62-19.82=0.80\ \mathrm{eV}$, so $J_{ab}=0.40\ \mathrm{eV}$. Since $E_0$ is the average of $E_{\mathrm{T}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{S}}$, its difference from the ground-state energy $E_{\mathrm{g}}$ is $$E_0-E_{\mathrm{g}}=\frac{19.82+20.62}{2}=20.22\ \mathrm{eV}\qquad\bigl(=E_{\mathrm{T}}-E_{\mathrm{g}}+J_{ab}=19.82+0.40\bigr).$$ This $20.22\ \mathrm{eV}$ is the difference between $E_0=h_{aa}+h_{bb}+U_{ab}$ and the total energy of the ground state; it also contains the difference of the one-electron energies, and it is not the value of the Coulomb integral $U_{ab}$ alone (extracting $U_{ab}$ alone requires information about the shapes of the orbitals). In Chapter 35, the calculation with hydrogen-like 1s and 2s orbitals gave $J_{ab}=\tfrac{16}{729}Z\,E_{\mathrm{h}}=0.0439\,E_{\mathrm{h}}=1.19\ \mathrm{eV}$ ($2J=2.39\ \mathrm{eV}$). This is about three times the experimental value $0.40\ \mathrm{eV}$; the order, with the triplet lying lower, agrees, but the magnitude is overestimated. The reason is that the actual 2s electron is shielded by the 1s electron and feels the nuclear charge more weakly, so its orbital is more spread out than the hydrogen-like one, the overlap density $\phi_{1\mathrm{s}}^*\phi_{2\mathrm{s}}$ with the 1s orbital is small, and $J_{ab}$ becomes smaller (Chapter 35). That the energy differs by as much as $0.8\ \mathrm{eV}$ for the same electron configuration merely because of the orientation of the spins is the effect of the exchange term arising from the Pauli exclusion principle.

Question: What happens to the helium levels if we get the order of the indices wrong?

Using the wrong order $\hat{V}'=-\hat{V}$ (Section 58.3.4) gives $E_{\mathrm{T}}'=h_{aa}+h_{bb}-(U_{ab}-J_{ab})$ and $E_{\mathrm{S}}'=h_{aa}+h_{bb}-(U_{ab}+J_{ab})$. Not only does the force between the electrons become attractive, but the singlet lies lower than the triplet as well ($E_{\mathrm{S}}'-E_{\mathrm{T}}'=-2J_{ab}$), so the wrong order ends up predicting the order opposite to that of Hund's rules. We see that the order of the indices is not a mere notational convention: it determines the direction in which Hund's rules point.

58.5 The Field Operator $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$ — Turning the Wave Function into an Operator

58.5.1 Definition and anticommutation relations

So far we have used the operator $\hat{c}_i$ attached to the orbital index $i$. If we construct operators that create and annihilate an electron by the coordinate $\bm{x}=(\bm{r},\sigma)$ itself instead of by the orbital index, we can write the Hamiltonian in a form that does not depend on the basis (the choice of orbitals). The starting point is the expansion of a one-electron wave function, $\psi(\bm{x})=\sum_ic_i\chi_i(\bm{x})$. The expansion coefficient $c_i=\braket{\chi_i}{\psi}$ is a number, but we replace it by an operator $\hat{c}_i$. This is the field operator.

Before moving on to operators of a continuous coordinate, let us check the idea with a finite number of sites. (In this subsection $x$ is a one-dimensional position.) We divide a one-dimensional line into small intervals (sites) $j=1,\dots,M$ of width $\Delta$, and take as orbitals the functions $\chi_j(x)$ that have the constant value $\Delta^{-1/2}$ inside interval $j$ and the value $0$ outside (they satisfy $\int\chi_j^*\chi_{j'}\dd x=\delta_{jj'}$, and they become a complete set as $\Delta\to0$). $\hat{c}_j$ is the operator that “annihilates an electron at site $j$,” and $\acomm{\hat{c}_j}{\hat{c}_{j'}^\dagger}=\delta_{jj'}$. The sum $\sum_j\chi_j(x)\hat{c}_j$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:58-psi} below equals $\hat{c}_j/\sqrt{\Delta}$ when $x$ lies in interval $j$. So if we let $\hat{\psi}_j\equiv\hat{c}_j/\sqrt{\Delta}$, then $$\acomm{\hat{\psi}_j}{\hat{\psi}_{j'}^\dagger}=\frac{\delta_{jj'}}{\Delta}$$ holds. The right-hand side is a function that equals $1/\Delta$ when $x,x'$ lie in the same interval and $0$ when they lie in different intervals, and integrating it over $x'$ (multiplying by $\Delta$ and summing) gives $1$. That is, in the limit $\Delta\to0$ it becomes $\delta(x-x')$. The Kronecker $\delta$ divided by the width of the interval is the Dirac $\delta$ function, and from this we can also see why $\hat{\psi}$ is larger than $\hat{c}$ by the factor $\Delta^{-1/2}$ (in one dimension it has the dimension $[\text{length}]^{-1/2}$).

Definition 58.4: Field operator

For an orthonormal complete set $\{\chi_i(\bm{x})\}$, \begin{equation} \hat{\psi}(\bm{x})=\sum_i\chi_i(\bm{x})\,\hat{c}_i,\qquad \hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})=\sum_i\chi_i^*(\bm{x})\,\hat{c}_i^\dagger \label{eq:58-psi} \end{equation} are called the field operators. $\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})$ is the operator that “creates one electron at the coordinate $\bm{x}=(\bm{r},\sigma)$,” and $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$ is the operator that “annihilates one electron at the coordinate $\bm{x}$.” Because the wave function $\psi(\bm{x})$ (a number) is promoted to an operator $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$, the name “second quantization” arose (it means that, in contrast to the quantization of the wave function $=$ first quantization, that wave function is quantized once more).

Theorem 58.3: Anticommutation relations of the field operators

\begin{equation} \acomm{\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})}{\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')}=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}'),\qquad \acomm{\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})}{\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}')}=\acomm{\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})}{\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')}=0 \label{eq:58-psiac} \end{equation} Here $\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')=\delta_{\sigma\sigma'}\,\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}')$. For bosons the anticommutator becomes a commutator: $\comm{\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})}{\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')}=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')$.

Proof

From the definition and $\acomm{\hat{c}_i}{\hat{c}_j^\dagger}=\delta_{ij}$, $$\acomm{\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})}{\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')}=\sum_{i,j}\chi_i(\bm{x})\chi_j^*(\bm{x}')\acomm{\hat{c}_i}{\hat{c}_j^\dagger}=\sum_i\chi_i(\bm{x})\chi_i^*(\bm{x}')=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')$$ follows. The last equality is due to the fact that the orbitals $\{\chi_i\}$ form a complete set (the completeness relation $\sum_i\chi_i(\bm{x})\chi_i^*(\bm{x}')=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')$; in the language of the inner product of functions in AD 02, Section 27.2, this says that the $\delta$ function is “the kernel of the identity operator”). $\acomm{\hat{\psi}}{\hat{\psi}}=0$ follows immediately from $\acomm{\hat{c}_i}{\hat{c}_j}=0$.

∎

Intuition: $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$ does not depend on the basis, and it has $\delta$-function normalization

(i) Even if we construct $\hat{\psi}'(\bm{x})=\sum_\alpha\chi_\alpha'(\bm{x})\hat{d}_\alpha$ from another complete set $\{\chi_\alpha'\}$, the transformation of Section 58.1.6, $\chi_\alpha'=\sum_j\chi_jU_{j\alpha}$, $\hat{d}_\alpha=\sum_iU_{i\alpha}^*\hat{c}_i$, gives $\hat{\psi}'(\bm{x})=\sum_{ij}\chi_j(\bm{x})\bigl(\sum_\alpha U_{j\alpha}U_{i\alpha}^*\bigr)\hat{c}_i=\sum_i\chi_i(\bm{x})\hat{c}_i=\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$, so it is the same operator. What depends on the choice of orbitals is $\hat{c}_i$; $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$ is determined by the coordinate alone.
(ii) The right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-psiac} is not $\delta_{ij}$ (the Kronecker delta) but the Dirac $\delta$ function. This corresponds to the fact that the position eigenstate $\ket{\bm{x}}$ is normalized as $\braket{\bm{x}}{\bm{x}'}=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')$ (continuous spectrum). The dimension of $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$ is the reciprocal of the square root of the volume, $[\text{length}]^{-3/2}$.
(iii) In classical mechanics, quantization was carried out by replacing the Poisson bracket $\{q,p\}=1$ of position and momentum by the commutation relation $\comm{\hat{q}}{\hat{p}}=i\hbar$ of $\hat{q},\hat{p}$ (AD 03, Chapter 46, Section 46.3 (in Japanese)). The boson fields $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$ and $\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})$ correspond to a canonically conjugate pair of a classical field (for example, $\psi$ and $i\hbar\psi^*$ when the Schrödinger equation is regarded as a “field equation”), and $\comm{\hat{\psi}}{\hat{\psi}^\dagger}=\delta$ is the quantization of that pair. The anticommutation relations of fermions have no such classical counterpart (the Pauli exclusion principle does not exist in classical mechanics).

58.5.2 $\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\ket{0}$ is the “state with a particle at position $\bm{x}$”

Even if we change the orbital basis, $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$ does not change (Figure 58.6). What do we obtain when we apply this operator to the vacuum?

From Theorem 58.3, $\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\ket{0}$ plays the role of the position eigenstate $\ket{\bm{x}}$: $\bra{0}\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')\ket{0}=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')-\bra{0}\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})\ket{0}=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')$. Here we used $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})\ket{0}=0$. The one-electron state $\ket{\chi}=\sum_i\braket{\chi_i}{\chi}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\ket{0}$ can be written as

\begin{equation} \ket{\chi}=\int\chi(\bm{x})\,\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\dd\bm{x}\,\ket{0},\qquad \bra{0}\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})\ket{\chi}=\chi(\bm{x}) \label{eq:58-one} \end{equation}

(the former follows from $\int\chi(\bm{x})\chi_i^*(\bm{x})\dd\bm{x}=\braket{\chi_i}{\chi}$, and the latter from $\bra{0}\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')\ket{0}=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')$). That is, multiplying a one-electron state by $\bra{0}\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$ extracts the wave function. For $N$ electrons, we have the following.

Theorem 58.4: Wave function of a Slater determinant

For $\ket{\Phi}=\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k_2}^\dagger\cdots\hat{c}_{k_N}^\dagger\ket{0}$, \begin{equation} \bra{0}\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_N)\cdots\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_2)\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_1)\ket{\Phi}=\det\bigl[\chi_{k_b}(\bm{x}_a)\bigr]_{a,b=1,\dots,N} \label{eq:58-slater-field} \end{equation} holds (the rows label the electrons $a$ and the columns label the orbitals $k_b$). The probability amplitude for finding the $N$ electrons, that is, the normalized wave function, is $$\Phi(\bm{x}_1,\dots,\bm{x}_N)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\bra{0}\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_N)\cdots\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_1)\ket{\Phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det\bigl[\chi_{k_b}(\bm{x}_a)\bigr]$$ and it agrees with the Slater determinant of Chapter 33.

Proof

We have $\acomm{\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})}{\hat{c}_k^\dagger}=\chi_k(\bm{x})$ (from $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})=\sum_i\chi_i(\bm{x})\hat{c}_i$ and $\acomm{\hat{c}_i}{\hat{c}_k^\dagger}=\delta_{ik}$). We pass $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_1)$ to the right of $\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\cdots\hat{c}_{k_N}^\dagger\ket{0}$. Each time it passes through $\hat{c}_{k_a}^\dagger$ we have $\hat{\psi}\hat{c}_{k_a}^\dagger=\chi_{k_a}(\bm{x}_1)-\hat{c}_{k_a}^\dagger\hat{\psi}$, and since $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_1)\ket{0}=0$, $$\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_1)\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\cdots\hat{c}_{k_N}^\dagger\ket{0}=\sum_{a=1}^N(-1)^{a-1}\chi_{k_a}(\bm{x}_1)\,\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\cdots\widehat{\hat{c}_{k_a}^\dagger}\cdots\hat{c}_{k_N}^\dagger\ket{0}$$ ($\widehat{\ \ }$ means “omit that factor.” The sign $(-1)^{a-1}$ is the product of the factors $-1$ acquired in passing through $a-1$ operators $\hat{c}^\dagger$). This has the form of the cofactor expansion along the first row (electron 1) of the determinant $\det[\chi_{k_b}(\bm{x}_a)]$. Writing out just one step explicitly for $N=3$, $$\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_1)\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k_2}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k_3}^\dagger\ket{0}=\chi_{k_1}(\bm{x}_1)\hat{c}_{k_2}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k_3}^\dagger\ket{0}-\chi_{k_2}(\bm{x}_1)\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k_3}^\dagger\ket{0}+\chi_{k_3}(\bm{x}_1)\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k_2}^\dagger\ket{0}$$ where the signs of the coefficients, $+,-,+$, are exactly the signs $(-1)^{1+b}$ of the cofactor expansion along the first row, and the states of the two remaining $\hat{c}^\dagger$ in each term correspond to the $2\times2$ minor (cofactor) obtained by removing that element. If we now act on the remaining states with $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_2)$ and then with $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_3)$, the same operation is repeated in each term, which gives the expansion along the second and third rows. For example, the first term yields $\chi_{k_1}(\bm{x}_1)\bigl[\chi_{k_2}(\bm{x}_2)\chi_{k_3}(\bm{x}_3)-\chi_{k_3}(\bm{x}_2)\chi_{k_2}(\bm{x}_3)\bigr]$ (the $2\times2$ minor), and the three terms together make up the complete expansion of the $3\times3$ determinant. For general $N$ as well, repeating the procedure up to $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_N)$ completes the expansion of the determinant. For $N=2$, explicitly, $$\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_1)\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k_2}^\dagger\ket{0}=\chi_{k_1}(\bm{x}_1)\hat{c}_{k_2}^\dagger\ket{0}-\chi_{k_2}(\bm{x}_1)\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\ket{0},$$ $$\bra{0}\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_2)\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_1)\hat{c}_{k_1}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k_2}^\dagger\ket{0}=\chi_{k_1}(\bm{x}_1)\chi_{k_2}(\bm{x}_2)-\chi_{k_2}(\bm{x}_1)\chi_{k_1}(\bm{x}_2)=\det\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{k_1}(\bm{x}_1)&\chi_{k_2}(\bm{x}_1)\\\chi_{k_1}(\bm{x}_2)&\chi_{k_2}(\bm{x}_2)\end{pmatrix}$$ hold. As for normalization, if we normalize the position basis state of $N$ electrons as $\ket{\bm{x}_1\cdots \bm{x}_N}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}_1)\cdots\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}_N)\ket{0}$ (a correction for the fact that the $N!$ permutations give the same state), then $\Phi(\bm{x}_1,\dots,\bm{x}_N)=\braket{\bm{x}_1\cdots \bm{x}_N}{\Phi}$ acquires a factor of $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}$ and satisfies $\int\abs{\Phi}^2\dd\bm{x}_1\cdots\dd\bm{x}_N=\tfrac{1}{N!}\int\abs{\det}^2=1$.

∎

Caution: Where does the factor $1/\sqrt{N!}$ come from?

The left-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-slater-field}, $\bra{0}\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_N)\cdots\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_1)\ket{\Phi}$, is itself $\det[\chi_{k_b}(\bm{x}_a)]$ (with no coefficient), and squaring it and integrating over $\bm{x}_1,\dots,\bm{x}_N$ gives $N!$. This is because, if we attach name tags to the electrons and count “the $a$th electron is at $\bm{x}_a$,” there are $N!$ ways of attaching the tags, and the same physical state is counted $N!$ times. A normalized wave function carries the factor $1/\sqrt{N!}$ that corrects for this duplication (it equals $1$ for $N=1$ and $1/\sqrt2$ for $N=2$).

Example 58.9: Extracting a two-electron wave function with field operators

Find the wave function $\Phi(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)$ of the state $\ket{\Phi}=\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_2^\dagger\ket{0}$, in which electrons occupy two spin orbitals $\chi_1,\chi_2$, and check its normalization.

Solution. From the $N=2$ calculation in Theorem 58.4, $\bra{0}\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_2)\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_1)\ket{\Phi}=\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)-\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)$. Therefore $$\Phi(\bm{x}_1,\bm{x}_2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\chi_1(\bm{x}_1)\chi_2(\bm{x}_2)-\chi_2(\bm{x}_1)\chi_1(\bm{x}_2)\bigr].$$ Normalization: $\iint\abs{\Phi}^2=\tfrac12\bigl[\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_1}\braket{\chi_2}{\chi_2}+\braket{\chi_2}{\chi_2}\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_1}-\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_2}\braket{\chi_2}{\chi_1}-\braket{\chi_2}{\chi_1}\braket{\chi_1}{\chi_2}\bigr]=\tfrac12(1+1-0-0)=1$. We can see that the sign changes under $\bm{x}_1\leftrightarrow \bm{x}_2$ (antisymmetry), and that if $\chi_1=\chi_2$ then $\Phi\equiv0$ (the Pauli exclusion principle).

58.5.3 Density operator

The product of field operators $\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$ is the operator that counts the number of electrons at the coordinate $\bm{x}$.

\begin{equation} \hat{\rho}(\bm{x})=\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i,j}\chi_i^*(\bm{x})\chi_j(\bm{x})\,\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j,\qquad \hat{N}=\int\hat{\rho}(\bm{x})\dd\bm{x} \label{eq:58-rho} \end{equation}

The second equation reproduces $\hat{N}=\sum_i\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_i$ from the orthogonality of the orbitals, $\int\chi_i^*\chi_j\dd\bm{x}=\delta_{ij}$. $\hat{\rho}(\bm{x})$ is a one-body operator, and its matrix elements are $\chi_i^*(\bm{x})\chi_j(\bm{x})$. Its expectation value in a Slater determinant follows from Eq. \eqref{eq:58-dens1} ($\mel{\Phi}{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j}{\Phi}=\delta_{ij}n_i$ when $\ket{\Phi}$ occupies the orbitals $\{\chi_i\}$): $$\mel{\Phi}{\hat{\rho}(\bm{x})}{\Phi}=\sum_{i\in\text{occ}}\abs{\chi_i(\bm{x})}^2$$ which is the sum of the probability densities of the occupied orbitals. Integrating over all space gives the number of electrons $N$. The charge density is $-e_0\hat{\rho}(\bm{r})$, with $\hat{\rho}(\bm{r})=\sum_\sigma\hat{\rho}(\bm{r},\sigma)$.

58.5.4 Field-operator representation of the Hamiltonian

We rewrite the $\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}$ of Theorems 58.1 and 58.2 in terms of $\hat{\psi}$ using Eq. \eqref{eq:58-psi}. The one-body term is $\sum_{ij}\mel{i}{\hat{t}}{j}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j=\int\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\,\hat{t}\,\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})\dd\bm{x}$ (expanding the right-hand side gives $\sum_{ij}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j\int\chi_i^*\hat{t}\chi_j\dd\bm{x}$). For the two-body term, from $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})=\sum_{kl}\chi_l(\bm{x}')\chi_k(\bm{x})\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$ we obtain $\tfrac12\iint\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')v(\bm{x},\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})\dd\bm{x}\dd\bm{x}'=\tfrac12\sum_{ijkl}\mel{ij}{v}{kl}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$. Therefore the Hamiltonian of the electron system can be written as follows.

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=\int\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\Bigl[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}}\nabla^2+U(\bm{r})\Bigr]\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})\dd\bm{x}+\frac12\iint\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')\,v(\bm{r}-\bm{r}')\,\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})\dd\bm{x}\dd\bm{x}' \label{eq:58-Hfield} \end{equation}

Here $U(\bm{r})$ is the external potential due to the atomic nuclei (ions), and $v(\bm{r}-\bm{r}')=e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\abs{\bm{r}-\bm{r}'})$ is the Coulomb repulsion between electrons. The one-body term corresponds to the replacement $\psi\to\hat{\psi}$ in the energy of a one-electron wave function, $\int\psi^*\bigl(-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+U\bigr)\psi\dd\bm{x}$, and the two-body term corresponds to the Coulomb energy of pairs of particles, $\tfrac12\iint\rho(\bm{x})v\rho(\bm{x}')$, with the densities replaced by $\hat{\psi}^\dagger\hat{\psi}$. Note, however, that the ordering of the operators in the two-body term is $\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$ (corresponding to the order $\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$), and it is not the product of densities $\hat{\rho}(\bm{x})\hat{\rho}(\bm{x}')$ itself. Using $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')-\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$, the difference is

\begin{equation} \hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})=\hat{\rho}(\bm{x})\hat{\rho}(\bm{x}')-\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')\,\hat{\rho}(\bm{x}) \label{eq:58-rhorho} \end{equation}

(obtained by rearranging $\hat{\rho}(\bm{x})\hat{\rho}(\bm{x}')=\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\bigl[\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')-\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})\bigr]\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}')=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')\hat{\rho}(\bm{x})+\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$). The term $-\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')\hat{\rho}(\bm{x})$ on the right-hand side is the term that removes the self-interaction (the real-space version of $v(0)\hat{N}/2$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:58-selfint}).

Eq. \eqref{eq:58-Hfield} is written in terms of $\hat{\psi}$ alone, and $\hat{\psi}$ does not change under a change of basis (Section 58.5.1 (i)). Therefore the two-body operator $\hat{V}$, like the one-body operator $\hat{T}$, keeps its form under a change of basis ($\hat{c}\to\hat{d}$). The invariance of Section 58.1.6 holds for $\hat{V}$ as well, without modification.

58.5.5 Pair correlation and the exchange hole (Pauli hole)

With field operators we can construct an operator that measures the probability density of “finding one electron at the coordinate $\bm{x}$ and another electron at $\bm{x}'$ at the same time.”

Definition 58.5: Pair correlation function

For a state $\ket{\Phi}$, \begin{equation} \rho_2(\bm{x},\bm{x}')=\mel{\Phi}{\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})}{\Phi} \label{eq:58-g} \end{equation} is called the pair correlation function. By Eq. \eqref{eq:58-rhorho}, $\iint \rho_2\dd\bm{x}\dd\bm{x}'=\mel{\Phi}{\hat{N}^2-\hat{N}}{\Phi}=N(N-1)$, which is equal to the number of “pairs of two distinct electrons.”

To find the pair correlation of a Slater determinant, we compute the expectation value of four operators.

Formula 58.4: Expectation value of four operators in a Slater determinant

For $\ket{\Phi_0}=\prod_{i\in\text{occ}}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\ket{0}$ (the occupation number of orbital $p$ is $n_p=1$ or $0$), \begin{equation} \mel{\Phi_0}{\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_d\hat{c}_c}{\Phi_0}=n_an_b\bigl(\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd}-\delta_{ad}\delta_{bc}\bigr),\qquad \mel{\Phi_0}{\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_c}{\Phi_0}=\delta_{ac}\,n_a \label{eq:58-wick} \end{equation}

Proof

$\ket{\Phi_0}$ is an eigenstate of every $\hat{n}_p$ (with eigenvalue $n_p$). $\hat{c}_d\hat{c}_c\ket{\Phi_0}$ is $0$ unless $c,d$ are both occupied (and $c\ne d$), in which case it is $\pm1$ times the state with $c,d$ removed. Likewise, on the bra side, $\bra{\Phi_0}\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b^\dagger=\bigl(\hat{c}_b\hat{c}_a\ket{\Phi_0}\bigr)^\dagger$ is the state with $a,b$ removed. The inner product of the two states is nonzero only when the pair of removed orbitals is the same, $\{a,b\}=\{c,d\}$. If $(a,b)=(c,d)$, then $\hat{c}_b\hat{c}_a=\hat{c}_d\hat{c}_c$ and the inner product is $n_an_b$. If $(a,b)=(d,c)$, then $\hat{c}_b\hat{c}_a=\hat{c}_c\hat{c}_d=-\hat{c}_d\hat{c}_c$ and the inner product is $-n_an_b$. This gives the first half of the equation (if $a=b$, both sides are $0$). The second half is the same as Eq. \eqref{eq:58-dens1} of Section 58.2.4.

∎

The first half of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-wick} has the form “the expectation value of four operators decomposes into the product of the expectation values of two operators, $\mel{}{\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_c}{}\mel{}{\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_d}{}$, minus the swapped product” (the simplest case of Wick's theorem). We apply this to $\rho_2(\bm{x},\bm{x}')$. Since $\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x})\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}')\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})=\sum\chi_a^*(\bm{x})\chi_b^*(\bm{x}')\chi_d(\bm{x}')\chi_c(\bm{x})\,\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_d\hat{c}_c$, we have

\begin{equation} \rho_2(\bm{x},\bm{x}')=\sum_{a,b\in\text{occ}}\Bigl[\abs{\chi_a(\bm{x})}^2\abs{\chi_b(\bm{x}')}^2-\chi_a^*(\bm{x})\chi_b^*(\bm{x}')\chi_a(\bm{x}')\chi_b(\bm{x})\Bigr]=\rho(\bm{x})\rho(\bm{x}')-\abs{\gamma(\bm{x},\bm{x}')}^2 \label{eq:58-gSlater} \end{equation}

Here $\rho(\bm{x})=\sum_a\abs{\chi_a(\bm{x})}^2$ and $\gamma(\bm{x},\bm{x}')=\sum_{a\in\text{occ}}\chi_a(\bm{x})\chi_a^*(\bm{x}')$ (the position representation of the one-body density matrix).

Theorem 58.5: Exchange hole (Pauli hole)

The pair correlation function of a Slater determinant is the product $\rho(\bm{x})\rho(\bm{x}')$ for independent particles, minus the exchange term $\abs{\gamma(\bm{x},\bm{x}')}^2$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:58-gSlater}). At $\bm{x}'=\bm{x}$, since $\gamma(\bm{x},\bm{x})=\rho(\bm{x})$, $$\rho_2(\bm{x},\bm{x})=\rho(\bm{x})^2-\rho(\bm{x})^2=0 .$$ That is, two electrons with the same spin are not found at the same place. For a pair of electrons with opposite spins ($\sigma\ne\sigma'$), the exchange term vanishes because $\gamma(\bm{x},\bm{x}')\propto\delta_{\sigma\sigma'}$, and $\rho_2$ does not become 0 even at the same position $\bm{r}=\bm{r}'$ (the hole forms only between electrons of the same spin). The integral of the exchange term is $\iint\abs{\gamma}^2\dd\bm{x}\dd\bm{x}'=\sum_{a,b}\abs{\braket{\chi_a}{\chi_b}}^2=N$, and the electrons contained in the “hole” amount to one electron in total.

This hole is the “exchange hole” (also called the Pauli hole or the Fermi hole). It is the same as what we saw in Chapter 33, Section 33.6.7, as the property of the first-quantized wave function that $\abs{\Phi}^2$ vanishes at $\bm{x}_1=\bm{x}_2$ (the Fermi hole), and as the “exchange hole” of Chapter 57, Section 57.2. The mechanism by which the exchange integral $J_{ab}$ reduces the electron repulsion is that the Coulomb repulsion does not act inside the hole (Section 58.4.2).

Example 58.10: Exchange hole (Pauli hole) of two electrons in an infinite well: $8/L^2$ versus $9/L^2$ at $x=L/4$, $x'=3L/4$

In a one-dimensional infinite well of width $L$, consider the state in which electrons of the same spin ($\uparrow\uparrow$) occupy the orbitals $\phi_n(x)=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)$ ($n=1,2$), one electron in each. (1) Find $\rho_2^{\uparrow\uparrow}(x,x')$, and compare its value at $x=L/4$, $x'=3L/4$ with the product $\rho(x)\rho(x')$ for independent particles. (2) Find its value at $x=x'$. (3) Find the pair correlation at the same points for the case of opposite spins ($\uparrow$ in $\phi_1$, $\downarrow$ in $\phi_2$). (In this example $x$ denotes a one-dimensional position coordinate, and the spin is distinguished by the labels $\uparrow,\downarrow$.)

Solution. (1) In Eq. \eqref{eq:58-gSlater}, the occupied orbitals are $\phi_1,\phi_2$ (with a common spin): $$\rho_2^{\uparrow\uparrow}(x,x')=\abs{\phi_1(x)\phi_2(x')-\phi_1(x')\phi_2(x)}^2 .$$ (The same result also follows from $\rho\rho-\abs{\gamma}^2$.) At $x=L/4$, $\phi_1=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(\pi/4)=\sqrt{2/L}\,\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ and $\phi_2=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(\pi/2)=\sqrt{2/L}$. At $x'=3L/4$, $\phi_1=\sqrt{2/L}\,\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ and $\phi_2=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(3\pi/2)=-\sqrt{2/L}$. Hence $$\phi_1(x)\phi_2(x')-\phi_1(x')\phi_2(x)=\frac2L\Bigl[\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}(-1)-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}(1)\Bigr]=-\frac{2\sqrt2}{L},\qquad \rho_2^{\uparrow\uparrow}=\frac{8}{L^2}.$$ The product for independent particles follows from $\rho(x)=\phi_1^2+\phi_2^2=\tfrac2L\bigl(\tfrac12+1\bigr)=\tfrac3L$ (the same at $x=L/4$ and at $x'=3L/4$), which gives $\rho(x)\rho(x')=9/L^2$. The ratio is $8/9$. The difference $1/L^2$ is the exchange term $\abs{\gamma}^2$: $\gamma=\phi_1(x)\phi_1(x')+\phi_2(x)\phi_2(x')=\tfrac2L\bigl(\tfrac12-1\bigr)=-\tfrac1L$ and $\abs{\gamma}^2=1/L^2$, and indeed $9/L^2-1/L^2=8/L^2$.
(2) At $x=x'$, $\rho_2^{\uparrow\uparrow}(x,x)=\abs{\phi_1(x)\phi_2(x)-\phi_1(x)\phi_2(x)}^2=0$. It is 0 for every $x$, so the diagonal becomes a “hole” (Figure 58.7(a)).
(3) For $\phi_1\uparrow$, $\phi_2\downarrow$, the spins differ, so no exchange term appears, and adding the pair with $\uparrow$ at $x$ and $\downarrow$ at $x'$ and the reverse pair gives $\rho_2(x,x')=\phi_1(x)^2\phi_2(x')^2+\phi_2(x)^2\phi_1(x')^2$. For $x=L/4$, $x'=3L/4$: since $\phi_1(x)^2=\tfrac1L$, $\phi_2(x')^2=\tfrac2L$, $\phi_2(x)^2=\tfrac2L$, and $\phi_1(x')^2=\tfrac1L$, we get $\rho_2=\tfrac{1}{L}\cdot\tfrac{2}{L}+\tfrac{2}{L}\cdot\tfrac{1}{L}=4/L^2$. Also, at $x=x'$ we have $\rho_2=2\phi_1^2\phi_2^2\gt0$ (except at the zeros of $\sin$), so there is no hole. In both cases $\iint \rho_2\dd x\dd x'=2$ ($=N(N-1)$).

58.6 Plane-Wave Basis — The Hamiltonian in the Momentum Representation and the Coulomb Interaction

58.6.1 Box-normalized plane waves and field operators

In a system where space is uniform, such as the electrons in a crystal or the free electrons in a metal, taking the plane waves $e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}$ as the basis diagonalizes the kinetic energy. Imposing periodic boundary conditions (Chapter 10, Section 10.7) on a cubic box of side $L$ (volume $V=L^3$), the wavenumber takes the discrete values $\bm{k}=\dfrac{2\pi}{L}(n_x,n_y,n_z)$ ($n_x,n_y,n_z$ are integers). The normalized plane waves (box normalization) are

\begin{equation} \phi_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\,e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}},\qquad \int_V\phi_{\bm{k}}^*\phi_{\bm{k}'}\dd^3r=\delta_{\bm{k}\bm{k}'} \label{eq:58-box} \end{equation}

(here $\int_Ve^{i(\bm{k}'-\bm{k})\cdot\bm{r}}\dd^3r=V\delta_{\bm{k}\bm{k}'}$. In infinite space this becomes $\int e^{i(k'-k)x}\dd x=2\pi\delta(k-k')$ of AD 02, Chapter 30, Section 30.3, and with the continuum normalization $(2\pi\hbar)^{-3/2}e^{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{r}/\hbar}$ we have $\braket{\bm{p}}{\bm{p}'}=\delta(\bm{p}-\bm{p}')$. Box normalization and continuum normalization differ only in the coefficients $V^{-1/2}$ and $(2\pi\hbar)^{-3/2}$.) In the limit $V\to\infty$, a sum over wavenumbers can be replaced by an integral according to

\begin{equation} \sum_{\bm{k}}\ \longrightarrow\ V\int\frac{\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3} \label{eq:58-sumint} \end{equation}

(because in wavenumber space the volume occupied by a single $\bm{k}$ is $(2\pi/L)^3=(2\pi)^3/V$). If we use the operator $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}$ for the orbital $\chi_{\bm{k}\sigma}=\phi_{\bm{k}}(\bm{r})\xi_\sigma$ that includes the spin, the field operator is

\begin{equation} \hat{\psi}_\sigma(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\,\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma},\qquad \acomm{\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}}{\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}^\dagger}=\delta_{\bm{k}\bm{k}'}\delta_{\sigma\sigma'} \label{eq:58-psik} \end{equation}

The anticommutation relation $\acomm{\hat{\psi}_\sigma(\bm{r})}{\hat{\psi}_{\sigma'}^\dagger(\bm{r}')}=\delta_{\sigma\sigma'}\dfrac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{r}-\bm{r}')}=\delta_{\sigma\sigma'}\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}')$ follows from the completeness of the plane waves (Theorem 58.3).

As for the kinetic energy, the plane waves are eigenfunctions of $-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}}\nabla^2$ (with eigenvalue $\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}=\hbar^2k^2/2m_{\mathrm{e}}$), so the matrix of the one-body operator, $t_{\bm{k}\bm{k}'}=\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\delta_{\bm{k}\bm{k}'}$, is diagonal. From Theorem 58.1 we obtain

\begin{equation} \hat{T}=\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma}\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\,\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma},\qquad \varepsilon_{\bm{k}}=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}} \label{eq:58-Tk} \end{equation}

Here $\hat{n}_{\bm{k}\sigma}=\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}$ is “the number of electrons in the state with wavenumber $\bm{k}$ and spin $\sigma$,” and the total momentum can be written $\hat{\bm{P}}=\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma}\hbar\bm{k}\,\hat{n}_{\bm{k}\sigma}$. If there are no interactions, the ground state of $N$ electrons is a Fermi sea, in which the electrons are packed in order, starting from the wavenumbers of lowest energy (Chapter 59).

58.6.2 The Fourier transform of the Coulomb potential

To treat the electron–electron interaction $v(\bm{r})=\dfrac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}$ in the plane-wave basis, we need its Fourier components \begin{equation} v(\bm{q})=\int v(\bm{r})\,e^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\dd^3r,\qquad v(\bm{r})=\frac1V\sum_{\bm{q}}v(\bm{q})\,e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}} \label{eq:58-vq-def} \end{equation} (for $V\to\infty$, by Eq. \eqref{eq:58-sumint} the inverse transform becomes $\int\dd^3q/(2\pi)^3$). Since $1/r$ decays only slowly at large distances and its integral does not converge as it stands, we compute the Yukawa-type function $e^{-\kappa r}/r$, cut off by an exponential function, and then let $\kappa\to0$.

Derivation: Fourier transform of the Coulomb potential

In polar coordinates, taking $\bm{q}$ along the $z$ axis, $\int_0^\pi\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}e^{-iqr\cos\theta}\sin\theta\dd\theta\dd\varphi=4\pi\dfrac{\sin qr}{qr}$. Hence $$\int\frac{e^{-\kappa r}}{r}e^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\dd^3r=\int_0^\infty r^2\frac{e^{-\kappa r}}{r}\,4\pi\frac{\sin qr}{qr}\dd r=\frac{4\pi}{q}\int_0^\infty e^{-\kappa r}\sin qr\dd r=\frac{4\pi}{q}\cdot\frac{q}{\kappa^2+q^2}=\frac{4\pi}{q^2+\kappa^2}.$$ ($\int_0^\infty e^{-\kappa r}\sin qr\dd r=\mathrm{Im}\int_0^\infty e^{-(\kappa-iq)r}\dd r=\mathrm{Im}\,\dfrac{1}{\kappa-iq}=\dfrac{q}{\kappa^2+q^2}$.) Letting $\kappa\to0$ gives $\int\dfrac{1}{r}e^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\dd^3r=\dfrac{4\pi}{q^2}$. We therefore obtain \begin{equation} v(q)=\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{4\pi}{q^2}=\frac{e_0^2}{\varepsilon_0\,q^2} \label{eq:58-vq} \end{equation}

∎

As for dimensions, $e_0^2/\varepsilon_0=[\mathrm{J\,m}]$ and $q^{-2}=[\mathrm{m^2}]$, so $v(q)$ is $[\mathrm{J\,m^3}]$ (energy × volume). Numerically, from $\dfrac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}=14.40\ \mathrm{eV\,\text{Å}}$ we get $\dfrac{e_0^2}{\varepsilon_0}=4\pi\times14.40\ \mathrm{eV\,\text{Å}}=180.95\ \mathrm{eV\,\text{Å}}$, so if $q$ is measured in $\text{Å}^{-1}$, then $v(q)=180.95/q^2\ \mathrm{eV\,\text{Å}^3}$. $1/q^2$ diverges as $q\to0$ (long distances).

Bridge to AD 08: $4\pi e^2/q^2$ in Gaussian units and $e_0^2/(\varepsilon_0q^2)$ in SI

The companion volumes “Linear Response Theory” (AD 08), “Solid-State Physics” (AD 06), and “Density Functional Theory” (AD 07), as well as many-body physics textbooks such as Fetter–Walecka, write the interaction between electrons as $v(r)=e^2/r$ in Gaussian units (or atomic units) and take its Fourier component to be $v(q)=4\pi e^2/q^2$. This agrees with the SI result, Eq. \eqref{eq:58-vq}, $v(q)=e_0^2/(\varepsilon_0q^2)=4\pi\cdot\dfrac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\dfrac{1}{q^2}$, under the replacement $e^2\ \leftrightarrow\ \dfrac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}$ (the $e_0$ of this book is the same quantity as the elementary charge $e$ of AD 03 and AD 08). In atomic units ($e^2=4\pi\varepsilon_0=1$, $\hbar=m_{\mathrm{e}}=1$), $v(q)=4\pi/q^2$.

58.6.3 The electron–electron interaction in the momentum representation

Substitute Eq. \eqref{eq:58-psik} and Eq. \eqref{eq:58-vq-def} into the two-body term of the field-operator form, Eq. \eqref{eq:58-Hfield}. Writing the spin indices as $\sigma,\sigma'$, we have

\begin{equation} \hat{V}=\frac12\sum_{\sigma\sigma'}\iint\hat{\psi}_\sigma^\dagger(\bm{r})\hat{\psi}_{\sigma'}^\dagger(\bm{r}')\,v(\bm{r}-\bm{r}')\,\hat{\psi}_{\sigma'}(\bm{r}')\hat{\psi}_\sigma(\bm{r})\dd^3r\dd^3r' \label{eq:58-V-r} \end{equation}

for the two-body term.

Derivation: Momentum conservation and the form of $\hat{V}$

Substitute $\hat{\psi}_\sigma^\dagger(\bm{r})=V^{-1/2}\sum_{\bm{k}_1}e^{-i\bm{k}_1\cdot\bm{r}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_1\sigma}^\dagger$, $\hat{\psi}_{\sigma'}^\dagger(\bm{r}')=V^{-1/2}\sum_{\bm{k}_2}e^{-i\bm{k}_2\cdot\bm{r}'}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_2\sigma'}^\dagger$, $\hat{\psi}_{\sigma'}(\bm{r}')=V^{-1/2}\sum_{\bm{k}_3}e^{i\bm{k}_3\cdot\bm{r}'}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_3\sigma'}$, $\hat{\psi}_\sigma(\bm{r})=V^{-1/2}\sum_{\bm{k}_4}e^{i\bm{k}_4\cdot\bm{r}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}_4\sigma}$, and $v(\bm{r}-\bm{r}')=\dfrac1V\sum_{\bm{q}}v(q)e^{i\bm{q}\cdot(\bm{r}-\bm{r}')}$ into Eq. \eqref{eq:58-V-r}. The exponential function that depends on $\bm{r}$ is $e^{i(-\bm{k}_1+\bm{k}_4+\bm{q})\cdot\bm{r}}$, and the one that depends on $\bm{r}'$ is $e^{i(-\bm{k}_2+\bm{k}_3-\bm{q})\cdot\bm{r}'}$. Integrating each, we obtain $\int_Ve^{i(-\bm{k}_1+\bm{k}_4+\bm{q})\cdot\bm{r}}\dd^3r=V\delta_{\bm{k}_1,\bm{k}_4+\bm{q}}$ and $\int_Ve^{i(-\bm{k}_2+\bm{k}_3-\bm{q})\cdot\bm{r}'}\dd^3r'=V\delta_{\bm{k}_2,\bm{k}_3-\bm{q}}$. The coefficient is $\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac{1}{V^2}\cdot\dfrac1V\cdot V\cdot V=\dfrac{1}{2V}$. Writing $\bm{k}_4\to\bm{k}$ and $\bm{k}_3\to\bm{k}'$, we obtain \begin{equation} \hat{V}=\frac{1}{2V}\sum_{\bm{q}}\sum_{\bm{k},\bm{k}'}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma'}v(q)\,\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'-\bm{q},\sigma'}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma} \label{eq:58-Vk-all} \end{equation}

∎

Eq. \eqref{eq:58-Vk-all} represents a scattering in which “two electrons of wavenumbers $\bm{k},\bm{k}'$ are annihilated and two electrons of wavenumbers $\bm{k}+\bm{q},\bm{k}'-\bm{q}$ are created” (Figure 58.3(a)). The sum of the wavenumbers is conserved, $(\bm{k}+\bm{q})+(\bm{k}'-\bm{q})=\bm{k}+\bm{k}'$, and the two electrons exchange momentum $\hbar\bm{q}$ (conservation of momentum; because space is uniform, the total momentum is conserved). For each magnitude $q$ of the exchanged momentum, the Coulomb interaction strength $v(q)=e_0^2/(\varepsilon_0q^2)$ applies. The smaller $q$ is (the interaction of electrons far apart), the stronger it is.

The $\bm{q}=\bm{0}$ term is the interaction of electron pairs that do not exchange momentum, and $v(0)=\int v(\bm{r})\dd^3r=\infty$ (the integral of $1/r$ over all space diverges). This term corresponds to the electrostatic energy $\tfrac12\bar{n}^2V\cdot v(0)$ of the average charge density $\bar{n}=N/V$ of the electrons with itself. In a real solid, or in an electron system in a uniform positive background (the jellium model; Chapter 59), a positive charge (the ions or the background) that cancels the negative charge of the electrons is present in the same amount, and since the total charge, taken together with the electron–background attraction and the background–background repulsion, becomes zero, the $\bm{q}=\bm{0}$ terms cancel one another. Therefore, in the electron–electron interaction we exclude $\bm{q}=\bm{0}$ and write

\begin{equation} \hat{V}=\frac{1}{2V}\sum_{\bm{q}\ne\bm{0}}\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}'\sigma\sigma'}v(q)\,\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'-\bm{q},\sigma'}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma} \label{eq:58-Vk} \end{equation}

This is the many-body Hamiltonian $\hat{H}=\hat{T}+\hat{V}$ of the electron gas in a metal (Eqs. \eqref{eq:58-Tk} and \eqref{eq:58-Vk}).

58.6.4 Writing it with the density operator $\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}$

We define the Fourier component of the density operator $\hat{\rho}(\bm{r})=\sum_\sigma\hat{\psi}_\sigma^\dagger(\bm{r})\hat{\psi}_\sigma(\bm{r})$ as

\begin{equation} \hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}=\int\hat{\rho}(\bm{r})\,e^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}\dd^3r=\sum_{\bm{k},\sigma}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma} \label{eq:58-rhoq} \end{equation}

(the second equality follows from the fact that integrating $\hat{\rho}(\bm{r})=\dfrac1V\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}'\sigma}e^{i(\bm{k}'-\bm{k})\cdot\bm{r}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'\sigma}$ over $\bm{r}$ leaves only $\bm{k}'=\bm{k}+\bm{q}$). Here $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}$ is a one-body operator that “moves an electron of wavenumber $\bm{k}+\bm{q}$ to wavenumber $\bm{k}$,” and $\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}$ is a density wave that gives momentum $-\hbar\bm{q}$ to the electron system. It has the following properties.

  • $\hat{\rho}_{\bm{0}}=\hat{N}$.
  • $\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}^\dagger=\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}$ (because $\hat{\rho}(\bm{r})$ is Hermitian; Exercise 58.4). The inverse transform is $\hat{\rho}(\bm{r})=\dfrac1V\sum_{\bm{q}}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}$.

Formula 58.5: Product of density operators and the two-body operator

\begin{equation} \hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}=\hat{N}+\sum_{\bm{k},\bm{k}'}\sum_{\sigma\sigma'}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'-\bm{q},\sigma'}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma} \label{eq:58-rhorho-q} \end{equation} Therefore, the electron–electron interaction in Eq. \eqref{eq:58-Vk} can be written as \begin{equation} \boxed{\ \hat{V}=\frac{1}{2V}\sum_{\bm{q}\ne\bm{0}}v(q)\Bigl(\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}-\hat{N}\Bigr)\ } \label{eq:58-Vrho} \end{equation}

Eq. \eqref{eq:58-Vrho} has the same form as the classical electrostatic energy $\dfrac{1}{2V}\sum_{\bm{q}}v(q)\abs{\rho_{\bm{q}}}^2$ (the squared magnitude of the Fourier component of the charge density, multiplied by $v(q)$ and summed). The differences are (i) that $\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}$ is a product of operators, and (ii) that there is a $-\hat{N}$ term, which is a correction that subtracts the part in which an electron interacts with itself (self-interaction; Section 58.3.4). Indeed, the bracket in $\dfrac{1}{2V}\sum_{\bm{q}\ne\bm{0}}v(q)\,\hat{N}=\dfrac12\hat{N}\Bigl[\dfrac1V\sum_{\bm{q}\ne\bm{0}}v(q)\Bigr]$ is $v(\bm{r}=0)$ (the value at $\bm{r}=0$ of $\tfrac1V\sum_{\bm{q}}v(q)e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}$ with $\bm{q}=\bm{0}$ excluded), which is the same as $\tfrac12v(0)\hat{N}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:58-selfint}.

Example 58.11: Derivation of $\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}=\hat{N}+\sum\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}\hat{c}$

Derive Eq. \eqref{eq:58-rhorho-q} without skipping the lines that use the anticommutation relation.

Solution. By Eq. \eqref{eq:58-rhoq}, $\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}=\sum_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'-\bm{q},\sigma'}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}$ (with $\bm{k}$ rewritten as $\bm{k}'-\bm{q}$) and $\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}=\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}$ (with the replacement $\bm{k}\to\bm{k}+\bm{q}$). The product is $$\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}=\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma}\sum_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}^\dagger\,\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'-\bm{q},\sigma'}^\dagger\,\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'\sigma'} .$$ Applying the anticommutation relation $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'-\bm{q},\sigma'}^\dagger=\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{k}'-\bm{q}}\delta_{\sigma\sigma'}-\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'-\bm{q},\sigma'}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}$ to the middle factor $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'-\bm{q},\sigma'}^\dagger$, we obtain $$\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}=\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma\bm{k}'\sigma'}\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{k}'-\bm{q}}\delta_{\sigma\sigma'}\,\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}-\sum\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'-\bm{q},\sigma'}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}.$$ In the first term only $\bm{k}'=\bm{k}+\bm{q}$, $\sigma'=\sigma$ survive, so it gives $\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}=\hat{N}$. In the second term, interchanging the order of the annihilation operators as $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}=-\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}$ changes the sign to $+$ and gives $\sum\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'-\bm{q},\sigma'}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}$, which is exactly the summand of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-Vk}. This proves Eq. \eqref{eq:58-rhorho-q}. Multiplying it by $\dfrac{1}{2V}\sum_{\bm{q}\ne\bm{0}}v(q)$ and moving the $\hat{N}$ term to the left-hand side, we obtain Eq. \eqref{eq:58-Vrho}.

58.6.5 Application: Screened Coulomb interaction — Thomas–Fermi screening

In a metal, the Coulomb repulsion between electrons, $e_0^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r)$, is in fact weakened at large distances. This is because around a given electron the other electrons move away, and positive charge is left behind in relative terms. The simplest way to estimate this screening is the Thomas–Fermi approximation.

Derivation: Thomas–Fermi screening

Suppose that a slowly varying electrostatic potential $\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}(\bm{r})$ is applied to the electron gas of a metal (electron density $n$, Fermi energy $E_{\mathrm{F}}$). The potential energy of an electron (charge $-e_0$) is $U(\bm{r})=-e_0\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}(\bm{r})$. If the electron gas is locally in equilibrium and the chemical potential $\mu$ is independent of position (constant), then the sum of the local Fermi energy $E_{\mathrm{F}}(n(\bm{r}))=\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}}\bigl(3\pi^2n(\bm{r})\bigr)^{2/3}$ and the potential energy is constant: $E_{\mathrm{F}}(n(\bm{r}))+U(\bm{r})=\mu$. For the density deviation $\delta n=n(\bm{r})-n$, we have $$\frac{\dd E_{\mathrm{F}}}{\dd n}\,\delta n+U(\bm{r})=0,\qquad \frac{\dd E_{\mathrm{F}}}{\dd n}=\frac{2E_{\mathrm{F}}}{3n}\equiv\frac{1}{g(E_{\mathrm{F}})},\qquad g(E_{\mathrm{F}})=\frac{3n}{2E_{\mathrm{F}}}$$ ($g(E_{\mathrm{F}})$ is the density of states per unit volume and per unit energy at the Fermi level; it includes the twofold spin degeneracy; Chapter 10, Section 10.7). Hence $\delta n=-g(E_{\mathrm{F}})\,U=g(E_{\mathrm{F}})\,e_0\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$: electrons gather where the electric potential is high. The induced charge density is $-e_0\delta n=-e_0^2g(E_{\mathrm{F}})\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$. Rearranging the Poisson equation $-\varepsilon_0\nabla^2\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}=\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}-e_0^2g(E_{\mathrm{F}})\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}$ ($\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}$ is the charge placed from outside), we obtain \begin{equation} \bigl(\nabla^2-k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2\bigr)\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}=-\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\varepsilon_0},\qquad k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=\frac{e_0^2\,g(E_{\mathrm{F}})}{\varepsilon_0} \label{eq:58-TF} \end{equation} For a point charge $Q$, the solution is $\varphi_{\mathrm{el}}(r)=\dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}e^{-k_{\mathrm{TF}}r}$ (Yukawa-type: the bare Coulomb potential is multiplied by $e^{-k_{\mathrm{TF}}r}$), and its Fourier component is $\dfrac{Q}{\varepsilon_0(q^2+k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2)}$ (the Fourier transform of the Yukawa-type function in the previous subsection). Therefore, the screened electron–electron interaction is \begin{equation} v_{\mathrm{TF}}(\bm{r})=\frac{e_0^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\,e^{-k_{\mathrm{TF}}r},\qquad v_{\mathrm{TF}}(q)=\frac{e_0^2}{\varepsilon_0\,(q^2+k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2)} \label{eq:58-vTF} \end{equation} For free electrons, $g(E_{\mathrm{F}})=\dfrac{3n}{2E_{\mathrm{F}}}=\dfrac{m_{\mathrm{e}}k_{\mathrm{F}}}{\pi^2\hbar^2}$ ($n=k_{\mathrm{F}}^3/3\pi^2$, $E_{\mathrm{F}}=\hbar^2k_{\mathrm{F}}^2/2m_{\mathrm{e}}$), so, using $a_0=4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2/(m_{\mathrm{e}}e_0^2)$, we obtain \begin{equation} k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=\frac{e_0^2m_{\mathrm{e}}k_{\mathrm{F}}}{\pi^2\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}=\frac{4k_{\mathrm{F}}}{\pi a_0} \label{eq:58-kTF} \end{equation}

∎

Caution: Range of validity of Thomas–Fermi screening

This derivation assumes that the potential varies more slowly than $k_{\mathrm{F}}^{-1}$ ($q\ll k_{\mathrm{F}}$), and in a typical metal $k_{\mathrm{TF}}\gtrsim k_{\mathrm{F}}$, so it is a semiquantitative rather than a quantitative estimate (quantum effects such as Friedel oscillations, in which the screening charge oscillates between positive and negative with wavenumber $2k_{\mathrm{F}}$, are not included). Dynamical screening and screening that includes quantum effects are treated with the Lindhard function (the density response function of the free-electron gas) and the RPA (random-phase approximation, an approximation that sums the “bubbles” of electron–hole pairs to infinite order) (Chapter 59, Section 59.2 quotes only the resulting formulas; for the derivation and for screening and the RPA, see AD 08, Chapter 9 and Chapter 10).

Example 58.12: Thomas–Fermi screening length of copper

From the conduction-electron density of copper, $n=8.47\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, find $k_{\mathrm{F}}$, $E_{\mathrm{F}}$, $k_{\mathrm{TF}}$, and the screening length $1/k_{\mathrm{TF}}$. Also find the ratio of the screened $v_{\mathrm{TF}}(q)$ to the bare $v(q)$ at $q=k_{\mathrm{F}}$, and estimate the effect of screening at the nearest-neighbor interatomic distance ($0.256\ \mathrm{nm}$).

Solution. $k_{\mathrm{F}}=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}=(29.609\times8.47\times10^{28})^{1/3}=(2.508\times10^{30})^{1/3}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}=1.359\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}=1.36\ \text{Å}^{-1}$. $E_{\mathrm{F}}=\hbar^2k_{\mathrm{F}}^2/2m_{\mathrm{e}}=7.03\ \mathrm{eV}$ (the value in Chapter 10). From Eq. \eqref{eq:58-kTF}, using $a_0=5.2918\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m}$, $$k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=\frac{4k_{\mathrm{F}}}{\pi a_0}=\frac{4\times1.3586\times10^{10}}{\pi\times5.2918\times10^{-11}}=3.27\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}},\qquad k_{\mathrm{TF}}=1.81\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}=1.81\ \text{Å}^{-1},$$ and the screening length is $1/k_{\mathrm{TF}}=5.53\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m}=0.055\ \mathrm{nm}$. (Substituting $g(E_{\mathrm{F}})=3n/2E_{\mathrm{F}}=1.13\times10^{47}\ \mathrm{J^{-1}m^{-3}}$ and $e_0^2/\varepsilon_0=2.90\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{J\,m}$ into the other expression $k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=e_0^2g(E_{\mathrm{F}})/\varepsilon_0$ gives the same $3.27\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}}$.)

The ratio at $q=k_{\mathrm{F}}$ is $\dfrac{v(q)}{v_{\mathrm{TF}}(q)}=\dfrac{q^2+k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2}{q^2}=1+\dfrac{k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2}{k_{\mathrm{F}}^2}=1+\dfrac{3.27}{1.846}=2.77$. Against the bare value $e_0^2/(\varepsilon_0k_{\mathrm{F}}^2)=98.0\ \mathrm{eV\,\text{Å}^3}$, the screened value is $35.4\ \mathrm{eV\,\text{Å}^3}$.

In real space, at the nearest-neighbor interatomic distance $r=0.256\ \mathrm{nm}$ the screening factor is $e^{-k_{\mathrm{TF}}r}=e^{-0.256/0.0553}=e^{-4.63}=9.8\times10^{-3}$; that is, only about 1% remains. The bare Coulomb energy $1.44\ \mathrm{eV\,nm}/0.256\ \mathrm{nm}=5.6\ \mathrm{eV}$ becomes about $0.055\ \mathrm{eV}$ after screening. The screening length (the length over which the potential decreases by a factor of $e$) is $0.55\ \text{Å}$, and the nearest-neighbor interatomic distance $2.56\ \text{Å}$ is about 4.6 times that length, so the Coulomb repulsion between electrons is almost completely screened by the time it reaches the position of the neighboring atom. Inside a metal, the Coulomb force is almost completely screened over a short length comparable to the interatomic distance, and therefore, despite the strong interaction between electrons, the “free-electron”-like picture in which the electrons move almost independently of one another (Chapters 10 and 41) holds well. 58.7 The Second-Quantized Form of Hartree–Fock — Written in Three Lines

58.7.1 The Hartree–Fock energy

In Part VII, we took a single Slater determinant as the trial function, found the expectation value of the energy of $N$ electrons (Chapter 35), and derived the equation for the orbitals that minimize it (Chapter 36). In the language of second quantization, this takes only a few lines. We write the Hamiltonian of the electrons in a molecule or a solid as the sum of a one-body operator (the kinetic energy and the attraction from the atomic nuclei combined) and a two-body operator (the electron–electron repulsion).

\begin{equation} \hat{H}=\sum_{i,j}h_{ij}\,\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j+\frac12\sum_{i,j,k,l}\mel{ij}{v}{kl}\,\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k \label{eq:58-Hfull} \end{equation}

Here $h_{ij}=\mel{i}{\hat{h}}{j}$ and $\hat{h}=-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}}\nabla^2+U_{\mathrm{nuc}}(\bm{r})$, where $U_{\mathrm{nuc}}$ is the potential energy of the attraction by the nuclei (the repulsion between the atomic nuclei is a constant, so we drop it here). The indices $i,j,\dots$ in this section label spin orbitals, and $U_{ij},J_{ij}$ are integrals over spin orbitals ($U^{\mathrm{so}},J^{\mathrm{so}}$ of Section 58.4.2). For a pair with antiparallel spins, $J_{ij}=0$. The expectation value in the reference state $\ket{\Phi_0}=\prod_{i\in\text{occ}}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\ket{0}$ requires only one application of Formula 58.4 (Eq. \eqref{eq:58-wick}) to the one-body term and one to the two-body term.

Derivation: $E_{\mathrm{HF}}=\mel{\Phi_0}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_0}$ in three lines

(1) One-body term: since $\mel{\Phi_0}{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j}{\Phi_0}=\delta_{ij}n_i$, we get $\sum_{ij}h_{ij}\delta_{ij}n_i=\sum_ih_{ii}n_i$.
(2) Two-body term: substituting $\mel{\Phi_0}{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k}{\Phi_0}=n_in_j(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}-\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})$, we obtain $\dfrac12\sum_{ijkl}\mel{ij}{v}{kl}n_in_j(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}-\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})=\dfrac12\sum_{ij}n_in_j\bigl(\mel{ij}{v}{ij}-\mel{ij}{v}{ji}\bigr)$.
(3) Using the definitions $U_{ij}=\mel{ij}{v}{ij}$ and $J_{ij}=\mel{ij}{v}{ji}$, we obtain \begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{HF}}=\sum_{i\in\text{occ}}h_{ii}+\frac12\sum_{i,j\in\text{occ}}\bigl(U_{ij}-J_{ij}\bigr) \label{eq:58-EHF} \end{equation}

∎

Eq. \eqref{eq:58-EHF} is the same as Theorem 35.5 (the energy of a Slater determinant) in Chapter 35 and the expression for the total energy in AD 05, Chapter 9. In the terms with $i=j$, the two parts cancel each other because $U_{ii}=J_{ii}$ ($\mel{ii}{v}{ii}$ is the same integral), so the term in which an electron repels itself does not enter automatically. The terms with $i\ne j$ consist of (a) the direct term $U_{ij}$ (the electrostatic repulsion between the charge distributions $\abs{\chi_i}^2$ and $\abs{\chi_j}^2$, the Hartree term) and (b) the exchange term $-J_{ij}$ (which acts only between electrons of the same spin, the Fock term), and they correspond to the two vertices of Figure 58.4. For two electrons, $E=h_{aa}+h_{bb}+U_{ab}-J_{ab}$, which agrees with Eq. \eqref{eq:58-Eab}.

Example 58.13: $E_{\mathrm{HF}}$ of the helium atom in hydrogen-like 1s orbitals, and the Koopmans-type ionization energy

Approximate the ground state of the helium atom ($Z=2$) by the Slater determinant $\ket{\Phi_0}=\hat{c}_{1\mathrm{s}\uparrow}^\dagger\hat{c}_{1\mathrm{s}\downarrow}^\dagger\ket{0}$, in which one spin-up electron and one spin-down electron occupy the hydrogen-like 1s orbital. Find the total energy from Eq. \eqref{eq:58-EHF} and compare it with the experimental value $-79.005\ \mathrm{eV}$. Also find the first ionization energy of He as the difference from the energy of the 1s electron of He$^+$, and compare it with the experimental value $24.59\ \mathrm{eV}$.

Solution. The occupied spin orbitals are $a=1\mathrm{s}\!\uparrow$ and $b=1\mathrm{s}\!\downarrow$. The one-electron integral for the hydrogen-like 1s orbital is $h_{aa}=h_{bb}=-\dfrac{Z^2}{2}E_{\mathrm{h}}=-2\,E_{\mathrm{h}}=-54.42\ \mathrm{eV}$, the two-electron integral is $U_{ab}=\dfrac{5Z}{8}E_{\mathrm{h}}=1.25\,E_{\mathrm{h}}=34.01\ \mathrm{eV}$ (Chapter 29), and $J_{ab}=0$ (the spins are antiparallel). Since $U_{aa}-J_{aa}=0$ and $U_{bb}-J_{bb}=0$, Eq. \eqref{eq:58-EHF} becomes $$E_{\mathrm{HF}}=h_{aa}+h_{bb}+\tfrac12\bigl[(U_{ab}-J_{ab})+(U_{ba}-J_{ba})\bigr]=2h_{1\mathrm{s}}+U_{ab}=-4+1.25=-2.75\,E_{\mathrm{h}}=-74.83\ \mathrm{eV}.$$ This is $4.2\ \mathrm{eV}$ (5.3%) higher than the experimental value $-79.005\ \mathrm{eV}$. It is the same as the first-order perturbation value of Chapter 29.
The ground state of He$^+$ is a single hydrogen-like 1s electron, with $E(\mathrm{He}^+)=h_{1\mathrm{s}}=-2\,E_{\mathrm{h}}=-54.42\ \mathrm{eV}$. Therefore the ionization energy is $$E(\mathrm{He}^+)-E(\mathrm{He})=-2-(-2.75)=0.75\,E_{\mathrm{h}}=20.41\ \mathrm{eV}$$ which is $4.2\ \mathrm{eV}$ smaller than the experimental value $24.59\ \mathrm{eV}$. This $0.75\,E_{\mathrm{h}}$ is exactly equal to the orbital energy of the next subsection, $\varepsilon_{1\mathrm{s}}=h_{1\mathrm{s}}+U_{ab}=-0.75\,E_{\mathrm{h}}$, with its sign changed (Koopmans' theorem; since He$^+$ has only one electron, no orbital relaxation occurs).
Incidentally, in a Hartree–Fock calculation in which the orbital is optimized (the spread of the 1s orbital is determined variationally), $E_{\mathrm{HF}}\approx-2.862\,E_{\mathrm{h}}=-77.87\ \mathrm{eV}$, and the Koopmans ionization energy is $-\varepsilon_{1\mathrm{s}}\approx0.918\,E_{\mathrm{h}}\approx25.0\ \mathrm{eV}$. The difference from the experimental value ($-79.005-(-77.87)\approx-1.1\ \mathrm{eV}$) is the electron correlation energy, which lies beyond the Hartree–Fock level. The large discrepancy between 20.41 eV for the hydrogen-like orbital and the experimental 24.59 eV is mainly because the orbital spread (the shielding of the nuclear charge) has not been optimized, and it is improved by the variational method of Chapters 30–32.

58.7.2 The mean-field approximation and the Fock operator

The Hartree–Fock approximation replaces the two-body interaction between electrons by a one-body problem in which “one electron moves in the average field created by the other electrons.” In second quantization, this corresponds to replacing a product of four operators by a sum of terms of the form “product of two operators $\times$ its expectation value”:

\begin{equation} \hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k\ \approx\ \expval{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_k}\,\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l+\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_k\,\expval{\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l}-\expval{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_l}\,\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_k-\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_l\,\expval{\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_k} \label{eq:58-decouple} \end{equation}

(Constant terms are omitted. The expectation value $\expval{\cdot}$ is the expectation value in $\ket{\Phi_0}$.) Each term on the right-hand side corresponds to the decomposition of Formula 58.4, “subtract the swapped product from the product.” Using $\expval{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_k}=\delta_{ik}n_i$, the approximation of $\hat{V}$ is

\begin{equation} \hat{V}\ \approx\ \sum_{p,q}\Bigl[\sum_{j\in\text{occ}}\bigl(\mel{pj}{v}{qj}-\mel{pj}{v}{jq}\bigr)\Bigr]\hat{c}_p^\dagger\hat{c}_q\ \ (+\text{constant}) \label{eq:58-MF} \end{equation}

Indeed, the first and second terms of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-decouple} are equal to each other by the symmetry $\mel{ij}{v}{kl}=\mel{ji}{v}{lk}$, and with the coefficient $\tfrac12\times2=1$ they become $\sum_{jl}\bigl[\sum_in_i\mel{ij}{v}{il}\bigr]\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l$ (the Hartree term). The third and fourth terms likewise become $-\sum_{jk}\bigl[\sum_in_i\mel{ji}{v}{ik}\bigr]\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_k$ (the exchange term).

Definition 58.6: Fock operator

The operator obtained by adding the mean field of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-MF} to the one-body operator $\hat{h}$, \begin{equation} \hat{F}=\sum_{p,q}f_{pq}\,\hat{c}_p^\dagger\hat{c}_q,\qquad f_{pq}=h_{pq}+\sum_{j\in\text{occ}}\bigl(\mel{pj}{v}{qj}-\mel{pj}{v}{jq}\bigr) \label{eq:58-fock} \end{equation} is called the Fock operator, and the matrix $f_{pq}$ is called the Fock matrix. Here $\sum_j\mel{pj}{v}{qj}$ is the Coulomb (Hartree) term and $-\sum_j\mel{pj}{v}{jq}$ is the exchange (Fock) term (the same as $\hat{h}+\sum_b(\hat{U}_b-\hat{J}_b)$ of Chapter 36).

The Fock matrix connects the total energy with the orbital energies $\varepsilon_i=f_{ii}$. Setting $p=q=i$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:58-fock} and summing over the occupied orbitals $i$, we obtain $\sum_if_{ii}=\sum_ih_{ii}+\sum_{ij}(U_{ij}-J_{ij})$, and comparing this with Eq. \eqref{eq:58-EHF} gives

\begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{HF}}=\frac12\sum_{i\in\text{occ}}\bigl(h_{ii}+\varepsilon_i\bigr)\ \neq\ \sum_i\varepsilon_i \label{eq:58-Esum} \end{equation}

The sum of the orbital energies counts the electron–electron repulsion twice (the repulsion felt by electron $i$ includes $j$, and the repulsion felt by $j$ includes $i$), so it differs from the total energy (Chapter 36, Section 36.5).

In Chapter 36, we took the variation of $E_{\mathrm{HF}}$ with respect to the occupied orbitals, imposed the orthonormality of the orbitals by the method of Lagrange multipliers, and derived the eigenvalue equation of the Fock operator $\hat{h}+\sum_b(\hat{U}_b-\hat{J}_b)$. The $f_{pq}$ obtained here from the mean-field decomposition is equal to the matrix elements of that Fock operator. Furthermore, if we mix a small amount of empty orbitals into the occupied orbitals, $\chi_i\to\chi_i+\sum_a\delta_{ai}\chi_a$, the first-order change of $E_{\mathrm{HF}}$ is $2\,\mathrm{Re}\sum_{a,i}\delta_{ai}f_{ia}$ ($f_{ia}=f_{ai}^*$), and the condition that this vanish for arbitrary $\delta_{ai}$ is $f_{ai}=0$ (Brillouin's theorem in the next subsection). That is, the variational condition $\delta E_{\mathrm{HF}}=0$ and the condition $f_{ai}=0$ have the same content, so the algebra of Chapter 36 and the algebra of this section serve as checks on each other.

58.7.3 Brillouin's theorem

The singly excited state obtained from the reference state $\ket{\Phi_0}$ by moving an electron in the occupied orbital $i$ to the empty orbital $a$ is $\ket{\Phi_i^a}=\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_i\ket{\Phi_0}$ ($i$: occupied, $a$: unoccupied).

Theorem 58.6: Brillouin's theorem

\begin{equation} \mel{\Phi_i^a}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_0}=f_{ai} \label{eq:58-brill} \end{equation} Therefore, the Hartree–Fock condition (the energy is an extremum under changes of the orbitals) $\Leftrightarrow\ f_{ai}=0$ is the same as the condition that “$\hat{H}$ does not mix $\ket{\Phi_0}$ with the singly excited states.”

Proof

We have $\bra{\Phi_i^a}=\bra{\Phi_0}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_a$. Since $a$ is an empty orbital, $\hat{c}_a\ket{\Phi_0}=0$, and hence $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_a\ket{\Phi_0}=0$. Therefore $\mel{\Phi_0}{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_a\hat{H}}{\Phi_0}=\mel{\Phi_0}{\comm{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_a}{\hat{H}}}{\Phi_0}$. We use the commutator of Formula 58.2 on the one-body term and the two-body term. For the one-body term, $\comm{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_a}{\hat{c}_p^\dagger\hat{c}_q}=\delta_{ap}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_q-\delta_{iq}\hat{c}_p^\dagger\hat{c}_a$, and the second term vanishes in the expectation value because $\mel{\Phi_0}{\hat{c}_p^\dagger\hat{c}_a}{\Phi_0}=0$ ($a$ is empty), so we get $\sum_{pq}h_{pq}\delta_{ap}\delta_{iq}n_i=h_{ai}$. For the two-body term, consider $\comm{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_a}{\hat{c}_p^\dagger\hat{c}_q^\dagger\hat{c}_s\hat{c}_r}=\delta_{ap}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_q^\dagger\hat{c}_s\hat{c}_r+\delta_{aq}\hat{c}_p^\dagger\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_s\hat{c}_r-\delta_{is}\hat{c}_p^\dagger\hat{c}_q^\dagger\hat{c}_a\hat{c}_r-\delta_{ir}\hat{c}_p^\dagger\hat{c}_q^\dagger\hat{c}_s\hat{c}_a$. Of these, the last two terms, which contain $\hat{c}_a$, vanish because $\hat{c}_a$ is at the right end and acts on $\ket{\Phi_0}$ to give 0. Applying Formula 58.4 to the first two terms, we get $$\tfrac12\sum_{qrs}\mel{aq}{v}{rs}\,n_in_q\bigl(\delta_{ir}\delta_{qs}-\delta_{is}\delta_{qr}\bigr)+(\text{second term: same value})=\sum_{j\in\text{occ}}\bigl(\mel{aj}{v}{ij}-\mel{aj}{v}{ji}\bigr)$$ and together with $h_{ai}$ this gives $f_{ai}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:58-fock}).

∎

Moreover, when the Fock operator $\hat{F}$ acts on $\ket{\Phi_0}$, we obtain $\hat{F}\ket{\Phi_0}=\sum_if_{ii}\ket{\Phi_0}+\sum_{a,i}f_{ai}\ket{\Phi_i^a}$ (Exercise 58.6), so the coefficient of the singly excited component is $f_{ai}$, and the condition $f_{ai}=0$ of Brillouin's theorem is also the condition that $\ket{\Phi_0}$ be an eigenstate of $\hat{F}$. When this condition is satisfied, the Fock matrix splits into an occupied block and an unoccupied block, and it can be further diagonalized by a unitary transformation to $f_{pq}=\varepsilon_p\delta_{pq}$ (canonical orbitals; Chapter 36, Section 36.4.5).

58.7.4 Koopmans' theorem

The energy of the state $\ket{\Phi_0^{(i)}}=\hat{c}_i\ket{\Phi_0}$, from which one electron in orbital $i$ has been removed (the other orbitals are not changed; orbital relaxation is neglected), is given by Eq. \eqref{eq:58-EHF} for $N-1$ electrons. Since this is a sum with $i$ excluded, we need only drop the terms containing $i$: $$E_{N-1}^{(i)}=E_{\mathrm{HF}}-h_{ii}-\sum_{j\in\text{occ}}(U_{ij}-J_{ij})=E_{\mathrm{HF}}-f_{ii}$$ (the two-body term $\tfrac12\sum(U-J)$ containing $i$ has two pieces, $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$, so exactly $\sum_j(U_{ij}-J_{ij})$ is dropped). Therefore, the ionization energy is

\begin{equation} \mathrm{IP}_i=E_{N-1}^{(i)}-E_{\mathrm{HF}}=-f_{ii}=-\varepsilon_i \label{eq:58-koop} \end{equation}

This is the derivation of Koopmans' theorem (Chapter 36, Section 36.5.3) by second quantization, and the occupied orbital energy with its sign changed is the energy needed to remove the electron in that orbital. The approximation consists of two parts: (i) the orbitals do not change after the removal (neglect of relaxation; in reality, when an ion is formed the remaining electrons contract, and the IP becomes smaller than the Koopmans value), and (ii) neglect of electron correlation (the correlation energy is larger for the $N$-electron system). These two often partially cancel each other.

Example 58.14: Checking the sign of Formula 58.4 by hand: $E_{\mathrm{HF}}$ for two electrons

For $\ket{\Phi_0}=\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_2^\dagger\ket{0}$, calculate (1) $\mel{\Phi_0}{\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_2^\dagger\hat{c}_2\hat{c}_1}{\Phi_0}$ and (2) $\mel{\Phi_0}{\hat{c}_1^\dagger\hat{c}_2^\dagger\hat{c}_1\hat{c}_2}{\Phi_0}$ directly from the anticommutation relations, and compare them with Eq. \eqref{eq:58-wick}. Also express $E_{\mathrm{HF}}$ in terms of $h_{11},h_{22},U_{12},J_{12}$.

Solution. The second term vanishes because $\hat{c}_1\hat{c}_2^\dagger\ket{0}=-\hat{c}_2^\dagger\hat{c}_1\ket{0}=0$, and the first term is $(1-\hat{c}_2^\dagger\hat{c}_2)\ket{0}=\ket{0}$. In Eq. \eqref{eq:58-wick} with $(a,b,c,d)=(1,2,1,2)$ we get $n_1n_2(\delta_{11}\delta_{22}-0)=+1$. The two agree.
(2) Since $\hat{c}_1\hat{c}_2=-\hat{c}_2\hat{c}_1$, the sign of (1) is reversed, giving $-1$. In Eq. \eqref{eq:58-wick} with $(a,b,c,d)=(1,2,2,1)$ we have $\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd}=\delta_{12}\delta_{21}=0$ and $\delta_{ad}\delta_{bc}=\delta_{11}\delta_{22}=1$, so $n_1n_2(0-1)=-1$, and again the two agree.
$E_{\mathrm{HF}}$: Setting $i,j\in\{1,2\}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:58-EHF} gives $E_{\mathrm{HF}}=h_{11}+h_{22}+\tfrac12\bigl[(U_{12}-J_{12})+(U_{21}-J_{21})\bigr]=h_{11}+h_{22}+U_{12}-J_{12}$ (with $U_{11}-J_{11}=0$ and so on), which agrees with Formula 58.3. The $+1$ in (1) fixes the sign of $U_{12}$, and the $-1$ in (2) fixes the sign of $-J_{12}$.

58.7.5 The bridge to the Roothaan–Hall equations

If we write the Fock matrix $f_{pq}$ in terms of orbitals $\chi_p=\sum_\mu\phi_\mu C_{\mu p}$ expanded in a fixed set of basis functions (atomic orbitals) $\{\phi_\mu\}$, the condition $f_{ai}=0$ (or $\hat{F}\chi_p=\varepsilon_p\chi_p$) becomes the matrix equation $FC=SC\varepsilon$ ($S$ is the overlap matrix of the basis functions). These are the Roothaan–Hall equations, which we derive in Chapter 37. The calculation programs in “Computational Materials Science” (AD 05, Chapter 10) and “Density Functional Theory” (AD 07, Chapter 5 (in Japanese)) solve these equations self-consistently. Note that in the derivation of Koopmans' theorem in AD 05, Chapter 10, and in AD 07, the Coulomb integral is written $J$ and the exchange integral $K$, so convert between the notations with the correspondence table in Section 58.4.1.

58.8 The Equation of Motion and an Introduction to Green's Functions

58.8.1 Heisenberg's equation of motion: $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)=\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}e^{-i\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar}$

In this section $t$ denotes time (not the hopping $t$ of the tight-binding model). So far we have worked in the Schrödinger picture, in which the state (the ket) changes with time. In the Heisenberg picture of Chapter 50, the states are held fixed and the operators evolve in time: $\hat{A}_{\mathrm{H}}(t)=e^{i\hat{H}t/\hbar}\hat{A}\,e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}$, and the equation of motion is $i\hbar\dfrac{\dd\hat{A}_{\mathrm{H}}}{\dd t}=\comm{\hat{A}_{\mathrm{H}}}{\hat{H}}$. If we know how the operators that create and annihilate particles change with time, we know the time evolution of the many-body system.

We solve the case of noninteracting electrons ($\hat{H}_0=\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma}\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}$). From the commutator $\comm{\hat{c}_c}{\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b}=\delta_{ac}\hat{c}_b$ (by the anticommutation relations, it is the difference of $\hat{c}_c\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b=(\delta_{ac}-\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_c)\hat{c}_b=\delta_{ac}\hat{c}_b-\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_c\hat{c}_b$ and $\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b\hat{c}_c=-\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_c\hat{c}_b$), we obtain

\begin{equation} \comm{\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}}{\hat{H}_0}=\sum_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}\varepsilon_{\bm{k}'}\,\delta_{\bm{k}\bm{k}'}\delta_{\sigma\sigma'}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'\sigma'}=\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma} \label{eq:58-comm0} \end{equation}

Therefore the equation of motion is $i\hbar\dfrac{\dd\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}(t)}{\dd t}=\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}(t)$, and $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}(t)$ obeys an equation of the same form as the equation $i\hbar\dot{z}=\varepsilon z$ for a number. Solving it with the initial condition $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}(0)=\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}$, we obtain

\begin{equation} \hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}(t)=\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}\,e^{-i\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar},\qquad \hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}^\dagger(t)=\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}^\dagger\,e^{+i\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar} \label{eq:58-ct} \end{equation}

The annihilation operator rotates as $e^{-i\varepsilon t/\hbar}$ (the same as $e^{-i\omega t}$ of a classical wave), and the creation operator rotates in the opposite direction. The boson result $\hat{a}_{\bm{q}}(t)=\hat{a}_{\bm{q}}e^{-i\omega t}$ ($\hbar\omega$ is the energy of the quantum) previewed in Chapter 56 has now been given a general form. The particle number $\hat{n}_{\bm{k}\sigma}(t)=\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}$ is independent of time because the phases cancel.

When there is an interaction, a product of three operators appears in the equation of motion. The same calculation as for Eq. \eqref{eq:58-gl} ($\comm{\hat{c}_m}{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k}=\delta_{mi}\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k-\delta_{mj}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$) gives $\comm{\hat{c}_m}{\hat{V}}=\sum_{jkl}\mel{mj}{v}{kl}\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$. The right-hand side of the equation of motion for $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}$ contains $\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}\hat{c}$, and to examine it we need the equations for still more operators (a “chain” of equations). The method of cutting this chain off approximately is perturbation theory for Green's functions (Chapter 55, Chapter 59, and AD 08).

58.8.2 The equation of motion for the field operator — recovering the Schrödinger equation

We compute the equation of motion of the field operator, $i\hbar\dfrac{\partial\hat{\psi}(\bm{x},t)}{\partial t}=\comm{\hat{\psi}(\bm{x},t)}{\hat{H}}$, for $\hat{T}=\sum_{ij}t_{ij}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$, which has only a one-body term. Since $\comm{\hat{c}_i}{\hat{T}}=\sum_jt_{ij}\hat{c}_j$, we have

$$\comm{\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})}{\hat{T}}=\sum_{ij}\chi_i(\bm{x})\,t_{ij}\,\hat{c}_j=\sum_j\bigl(\hat{t}\chi_j\bigr)(\bm{x})\,\hat{c}_j=\hat{t}\,\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})$$

(we used the expansion $\sum_i\chi_i(\bm{x})t_{ij}=(\hat{t}\chi_j)(\bm{x})$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-tk}), and hence we obtain

\begin{equation} i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\psi}(\bm{x},t)=\Bigl[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_{\mathrm{e}}}\nabla^2+U(\bm{r})\Bigr]\hat{\psi}(\bm{x},t)+\int v(\bm{r}-\bm{r}')\,\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}',t)\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}',t)\,\hat{\psi}(\bm{x},t)\dd\bm{x}' \label{eq:58-eom} \end{equation}

Here the last term is the contribution from the two-body term $\hat{V}$; it is the equation above for $\comm{\hat{c}_m}{\hat{V}}$ written in terms of field operators. Without the interaction between electrons, only the first term on the right-hand side remains, and $\hat{\psi}$ obeys the same Schrödinger equation as the wave function $\psi$. The wave function $\psi(\bm{x},t)$, which is a number, has merely been replaced by the operator $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x},t)$; the form of the equation is the same. For example, the free-electron field operator $\hat{\psi}_\sigma(\bm{r},t)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar)}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}$ is obtained by turning the coefficients of the superposition of plane waves $e^{i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}-\omega t)}$ into operators. When there is an interaction, the second term on the right-hand side (the term in which the electrostatic potential created by the density $\hat{\rho}(\bm{r}')$ acts on $\hat{\psi}$) is added, and since the density remains an operator, the equation becomes the operator version of the nonlinear equations for the wave functions (the Hartree equations). If the density operator is replaced by its expectation value $\expval{\hat{\rho}}$, we return to the Hartree equations (Chapter 36).

58.8.3 An introduction to Green's functions — adding one electron to the Fermi sea and removing it

The basic tool for investigating the properties of many-body systems is the Green's function (for a single particle, see Chapter 55). Let us build the quantity that serves as its starting point in a simple way, using noninteracting electrons. The ground state of noninteracting electrons is the Fermi sea $\ket{\mathrm{FS}}=\prod_{\abs{\bm{k}}\lt k_{\mathrm{F}},\sigma}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}^\dagger\ket{0}$, in which all the states with wavenumber $\abs{\bm{k}}\lt k_{\mathrm{F}}$ are filled, and $\mel{\mathrm{FS}}{\hat{n}_{\bm{k}\sigma}}{\mathrm{FS}}=n_{\bm{k}}$ (equal to $1$ for $\abs{\bm{k}}\lt k_{\mathrm{F}}$ and to $0$ for $\abs{\bm{k}}\gt k_{\mathrm{F}}$) (Chapter 59). We consider the amplitude of adding one electron of wavenumber $\bm{k}$ at time $0$ ($\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger\ket{\mathrm{FS}}$), removing it at time $t$ ($\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)$), and returning to the Fermi sea (we omit the spin index).

Formula 58.6: The amplitude for adding one electron to the Fermi sea and removing it

\begin{equation} \mel{\mathrm{FS}}{\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)\,\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger}{\mathrm{FS}}=\bigl(1-n_{\bm{k}}\bigr)\,e^{-i\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar},\qquad \mel{\mathrm{FS}}{\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger\,\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)}{\mathrm{FS}}=n_{\bm{k}}\,e^{-i\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar} \label{eq:58-FSamp} \end{equation}

The first equation follows immediately if we write $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)=\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}e^{-i\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar}$ by Eq. \eqref{eq:58-ct} and use $\mel{\mathrm{FS}}{\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger}{\mathrm{FS}}=1-n_{\bm{k}}$ ($\hat{c}\hat{c}^\dagger=1-\hat{n}$). The second follows similarly from $\mel{\mathrm{FS}}{\hat{n}_{\bm{k}}}{\mathrm{FS}}=n_{\bm{k}}$. The meaning is as follows.

  • Wavenumber $\bm{k}$ outside the Fermi sea (vacant, $n_{\bm{k}}=0$): an electron can be added, and its amplitude keeps rotating with the phase $e^{-i\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar}$ (its magnitude is 1). Without an interaction, the added electron lives on (its lifetime is infinite).
  • Wavenumber $\bm{k}$ inside the Fermi sea (occupied, $n_{\bm{k}}=1$): into a state that already holds an electron, one more cannot be put (the Pauli exclusion principle, $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger=0$), so the amplitude is $0$. Instead, the process of removing an electron first (the propagation of a hole) is given by the second equation.

The natural reference for the energy of the operation of adding an electron is the chemical potential $\mu$ ($\mu=E_{\mathrm{F}}$ at absolute zero in a metal). This is because the energy needed to bring one electron from an external reservoir of electrons (chemical potential $\mu$) is $\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu$. Replacing $\hat{H}$ by $\hat{H}-\mu\hat{N}$ ($\hat{N}$ commutes with $\hat{H}_0$, so the form of the equation of motion does not change), and using $\xi_{\bm{k}}\equiv\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu$, we obtain $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)=\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}e^{-i\xi_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar}$, and the phase becomes $e^{-i\xi_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar}$ (in the equations below, read $\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}$ as $\xi_{\bm{k}}$). The time $\tau_{\mathrm{ph}}$ for the phase to make one full turn is $h/\abs{\xi_{\bm{k}}}$. For $\abs{\xi_{\bm{k}}}=1\ \mathrm{eV}$ we have

\begin{equation} \tau_{\mathrm{ph}}=\frac{h}{1\ \mathrm{eV}}=\frac{6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}}{1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}}=4.14\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{s}=4.14\ \mathrm{fs} \label{eq:58-period} \end{equation}

For an electron near the Fermi surface ($\xi_{\bm{k}}\approx0$), the phase hardly rotates.

Arranging all these amplitudes gives the time-ordered Green's function. For $t\gt0$ it is the amplitude of adding an electron and removing it, and for $t\lt0$ it is the amplitude of removing an electron first (creating a hole); since electrons are fermions, the latter carries a sign $-1$ (the time-ordered product $T$).

\begin{equation} \mel{\mathrm{FS}}{T\bigl[\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger(0)\bigr]}{\mathrm{FS}}=\theta(t)\bigl(1-n_{\bm{k}}\bigr)e^{-i\xi_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar}-\theta(-t)\,n_{\bm{k}}\,e^{-i\xi_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar} \label{eq:58-GT} \end{equation}

Here we set $\hbar=1$ and use the time Fourier transform (with kernel $e^{+i\omega t}$) and the adiabatic switching factor $\eta\to+0$. From $\int_0^\infty e^{i(\omega-\xi_{\bm{k}}+i\eta)t}\dd t=\dfrac{i}{\omega-\xi_{\bm{k}}+i\eta}$ and $\int_{-\infty}^0e^{i(\omega-\xi_{\bm{k}}-i\eta)t}\dd t=\dfrac{-i}{\omega-\xi_{\bm{k}}-i\eta}$, the quantity $G_0(\bm{k},\omega)$ obtained by dividing Eq. \eqref{eq:58-GT} by $i$ is

\begin{equation} G_0(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{\theta(\abs{\bm{k}}-k_{\mathrm{F}})}{\omega-\xi_{\bm{k}}+i\eta}+\frac{\theta(k_{\mathrm{F}}-\abs{\bm{k}})}{\omega-\xi_{\bm{k}}-i\eta} \label{eq:58-G0} \end{equation}

Here $\omega$ is also measured with $\mu$ as the origin (outside the Fermi surface $\xi_{\bm{k}}\gt0$, and inside it $\xi_{\bm{k}}\lt0$). Outside the Fermi surface the pole lies in the lower half-plane ($\omega=\xi_{\bm{k}}-i\eta$), and inside it, in the upper half-plane ($\omega=\xi_{\bm{k}}+i\eta$). This is the expression for the free-particle case of the Fetter–Walecka many-body Green's function, and it is the starting point of perturbation theory (Feynman diagrams).

Note on notation: $iG=\expval{T\hat{\psi}\hat{\psi}^\dagger}$ and $G=-i\expval{T\hat{\psi}\hat{\psi}^\dagger}$, and $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{\Psi_0}$

  • Same physics, only the placement of $i$ differs. Appendix C of the companion volume “Linear Response Theory” (AD 08) and the simulator “Green's Functions and Feynman Diagrams” (derivation) adopt the Fetter–Walecka convention $iG(\bm{x},\bm{x}')=\mel{\Psi_0}{T\bigl[\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')\bigr]}{\Psi_0}$. Many textbooks divide both sides by $i$ and write $G=-i\mel{\Psi_0}{T[\hat{\psi}\hat{\psi}^\dagger]}{\Psi_0}$. The right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-GT} is the former, $iG_0$, and Eq. \eqref{eq:58-G0} is the (Fourier-transformed) $G_0$ of the latter. The retarded Green's function of this book, $-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\theta(t)\expval{\acomm{\hat{c}(t)}{\hat{c}^\dagger}}$ (Chapter 55), has the same factor $-i$; for a free particle $\acomm{\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)}{\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger}=e^{-i\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar}$ (independent of $n_{\bm{k}}$), so $K^{\mathrm{R}}_{\bm{k}}(t)=-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\theta(t)e^{-i\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar}$ and $G^{\mathrm{R}}_{\bm{k}}(E)=(E-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}+i\eta)^{-1}$.   • $\ket{0}$ denotes only the vacuum. The companion volume “Linear Response Theory” (AD 08, Chapter 8) writes the ground state as $\ket{0}$, so be careful when reading the two side by side.

Example 58.15: $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)$ and adding an electron to the Fermi sea

(1) Derive $\hat{c}(t)=\hat{c}\,e^{-i\varepsilon t/\hbar}$ from the Heisenberg equation of motion for $\hat{H}_0=\varepsilon\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}$ (a single state), and verify it by directly computing $e^{i\hat{H}_0t/\hbar}\hat{c}\,e^{-i\hat{H}_0t/\hbar}$. (2) For wavenumbers at which the energy is $1\ \mathrm{eV}$ above the Fermi level of a metal ($\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu=+1\ \mathrm{eV}$, an empty state) and $1\ \mathrm{eV}$ below it (an occupied state), find the magnitude of $\mel{\mathrm{FS}}{\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger}{\mathrm{FS}}$ and the time $\tau_{\mathrm{ph}}$ in which its phase goes around once. (3) What is the period when $\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu=0.1\ \mathrm{eV}$?

Solution. (1) From $\comm{\hat{c}}{\varepsilon\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}}=\varepsilon\hat{c}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:58-comm0}) we obtain $i\hbar\dot{\hat{c}}=\varepsilon\hat{c}$, hence $\hat{c}(t)=\hat{c}\,e^{-i\varepsilon t/\hbar}$. In the direct calculation, since $\hat{c}$ decreases the occupation number by one, $\hat{c}\,f(\hat{n})=f(\hat{n}+1)\hat{c}$ holds. Using $e^{i\hat{H}_0t/\hbar}=e^{i\varepsilon\hat{n}t/\hbar}$, we find $e^{i\varepsilon\hat{n}t/\hbar}\hat{c}\,e^{-i\varepsilon\hat{n}t/\hbar}=e^{i\varepsilon\hat{n}t/\hbar}e^{-i\varepsilon(\hat{n}+1)t/\hbar}\hat{c}=e^{-i\varepsilon t/\hbar}\hat{c}$, which agrees.
(2) For the upper state, $n_{\bm{k}}=0$, so the amplitude in Eq. \eqref{eq:58-FSamp} is $1\cdot e^{-i(\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu)t/\hbar}$, with magnitude $1$ and period $\tau_{\mathrm{ph}}=h/(1\ \mathrm{eV})=4.14\ \mathrm{fs}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:58-period}). For the lower state, $n_{\bm{k}}=1$, so the amplitude for adding an electron is $(1-1)e^{\cdots}=0$ (the Pauli exclusion principle). This state shows up instead in the amplitude for removing an electron, $\mel{\mathrm{FS}}{\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)}{\mathrm{FS}}=e^{-i(\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu)t/\hbar}$ (magnitude 1; since $\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu=-1\ \mathrm{eV}$, the phase rotates in the opposite direction).
(3) $\tau_{\mathrm{ph}}=h/(0.1\ \mathrm{eV})=41.4\ \mathrm{fs}$. If the energy is one tenth, the period becomes ten times longer: the closer to the Fermi surface, the more slowly the phase rotates.

58.8.4 A bridge to linear response — time evolution of the density operator

The response of an electron system to an external field (Chapter 54) is determined by the time correlation of the commutator between the operator coupled to the external field and the operator being observed. An external potential $U_{\mathrm{ext}}(\bm{r},t)$ acting on the electron density couples through $\hat{H}_1=\int U_{\mathrm{ext}}(\bm{r},t)\hat{\rho}(\bm{r})\dd^3r$ (for an electric field $\bm{E}$ this is $-\hat{\bm{d}}\cdot\bm{E}$, with $\hat{\bm{d}}=-e_0\int\bm{r}\hat{\rho}\dd^3r$), and if $U_{\mathrm{ext}}=U_{\bm{q}}(t)e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}+\text{c.c.}$, then $\hat{H}_1=U_{\bm{q}}\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}+U_{\bm{q}}^*\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}$. Therefore the quantity observed is $\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}$ and the quantity coupled to the external field is $\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}$, and the response is determined by $\mel{\Psi_0}{\comm{\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}(t)}{\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}(0)}}{\Psi_0}$ (this is $\hat{H}_1=-F(t)\hat{B}$ of Chapter 54 with $\hat{B}=\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}$. AD 08 writes the external field as $+f\hat{B}$, so the sign is opposite. See the box in Chapter 54, Section 54.2).

Its time evolution can be found with Formula 58.2. We have $\comm{\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}}{\hat{T}}=\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma}\bigl(\varepsilon_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\bigr)\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}$ (from $\comm{\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}}{\hat{c}_{\bm{p}}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{p}}}=\delta_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\bm{p}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{p}}-\delta_{\bm{k}\bm{p}}\hat{c}_{\bm{p}}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}$). Moreover, $\hat{V}$ is a function of $\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}'}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:58-Vrho}), and the $\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}$ commute with one another (Exercise 58.4), so $\comm{\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}}{\hat{V}}=0$. Without interactions, the time evolution of each term follows from Eq. \eqref{eq:58-ct}: $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger(t)\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}(t)=\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}\,e^{-i\omega_{\bm{k}\bm{q}}t}$ with $\hbar\omega_{\bm{k}\bm{q}}=\varepsilon_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}$, and we obtain $\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}(t)=\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma}e^{-i\omega_{\bm{k}\bm{q}}t}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q},\sigma}$. By Formula 58.2, the expectation value of the commutator in the Fermi sea is given by

\begin{equation} \mel{\mathrm{FS}}{\comm{\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}(t)}{\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}(0)}}{\mathrm{FS}}=\sum_{\bm{k}\sigma}\bigl(n_{\bm{k}}-n_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}\bigr)\,e^{-i\omega_{\bm{k}\bm{q}}t} \label{eq:58-rhocomm} \end{equation}

Here $n_{\bm{k}}-n_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}$ is $+1$ when $\bm{k}$ is occupied and $\bm{k}+\bm{q}$ is empty, $-1$ in the opposite case, and $0$ when both are occupied or both are empty. This shows that only the excitation of an electron–hole pair, which adds momentum $\hbar\bm{q}$ and lifts an electron from $\bm{k}$ to $\bm{k}+\bm{q}$ (excitation energy $\hbar\omega_{\bm{k}\bm{q}}$), contributes to the response. Taking the Fourier transform of Eq. \eqref{eq:58-rhocomm} in time gives the density response function of the electron gas (the Lindhard function). The properties of electron–hole pair excitations are studied in Chapter 59, and the Lindhard function, screening, and plasmons in AD 08, Chapters 9 and 10.

58.9 Summary and Exercises

58.9.1 Summary

  • One-body operator: $\hat{T}=\sum_a\hat{t}(a)\ \Leftrightarrow\ \hat{T}=\sum_{ij}\mel{i}{\hat{t}}{j}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$. $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j$ reassigns an electron in orbital $j$ to orbital $i$ (Formula 58.1: $\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j\ket{k_1\cdots k_N}=\sum_a\delta_{j,k_a}\ket{\cdots i\cdots}$). It does not change the particle number ($\comm{\hat{T}}{\hat{N}}=0$), its form is the same when the basis is changed, and the matrix $t$ becomes $U^\dagger tU$. $\hat{N}=\sum\hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c}$, $\hat{S}_z=\tfrac\hbar2\sum(\hat{n}_{k\uparrow}-\hat{n}_{k\downarrow})$, $\hat{S}_+=\hbar\sum\hat{c}_{k\uparrow}^\dagger\hat{c}_{k\downarrow}$.
  • Tight-binding model: $\hat{H}=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle\sigma}(\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^\dagger\hat{c}_{j\sigma}+\mathrm{h.c.})$ ($t=-\beta\gt0$). For the ring, $\hat{H}=\sum\varepsilon_k\hat{n}_{k\sigma}$ with $\varepsilon_k=-2t\cos k$. Benzene (six sites, six electrons) has $E=-8t$, and its difference of $2t$ from three localized double bonds ($-6t$) is the resonance stabilization. One-body density matrix $P$: $\mel{\Phi}{\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j}{\Phi}=P_{ji}$, bond order $2/3$.
  • Two-body operator: $\hat{V}=\tfrac12\sum_{a\ne b}v(\bm{x}_a,\bm{x}_b)\ \Leftrightarrow\ \hat{V}=\tfrac12\sum\mel{ij}{v}{kl}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$, $\mel{ij}{v}{kl}=\iint\chi_i^*(1)\chi_j^*(2)v\chi_k(1)\chi_l(2)$. The order $\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k$ (normal order: first annihilate two, then create two) removes the self-interaction ($\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_k\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l=\hat{V}\text{ term}+\delta_{jk}\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_l$). If the order is wrong, $\hat{V}\to-\hat{V}$ (the sign changes).
  • $U$ and $J$: $\mel{\Phi_{ab}}{\hat{V}}{\Phi_{ab}}=U_{ab}-J_{ab}$. $J_{ab}\ne0$ only for parallel spins. For the 1s2s configuration of helium, $E_{\mathrm{T}}=E_0-J$ and $E_{\mathrm{S}}=E_0+J$, and from the measured values $19.82\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $20.62\ \mathrm{eV}$ we obtain $2J=0.80\ \mathrm{eV}$.
  • Field operator: $\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})=\sum_i\chi_i(\bm{x})\hat{c}_i$, $\acomm{\hat{\psi}(\bm{x})}{\hat{\psi}^\dagger(\bm{x}')}=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}')$ (from completeness). $\Phi(\bm{x}_1,\dots,\bm{x}_N)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\bra{0}\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_N)\cdots\hat{\psi}(\bm{x}_1)\ket{\Phi}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det[\chi_j(\bm{x}_i)]$. $\hat{\rho}(\bm{x})=\hat{\psi}^\dagger\hat{\psi}$. The pair correlation $\rho_2=\rho\rho-\abs{\gamma}^2$ is 0 at $\bm{x}=\bm{x}'$ (the Pauli hole). For the $\uparrow\uparrow$ case of the infinite well with $n=1,2$, at $\bm{x}=L/4$ and $\bm{x}'=3L/4$ the values are $8/L^2$ versus $9/L^2$.
  • Plane-wave basis: $\hat{\psi}_\sigma(\bm{r})=V^{-1/2}\sum e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\sigma}$, $\hat{T}=\sum\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\hat{n}_{\bm{k}\sigma}$, $v(q)=e_0^2/(\varepsilon_0q^2)$ (this corresponds to $4\pi e^2/q^2$ in Gaussian units under $e^2\leftrightarrow e_0^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0$), $\hat{V}=\dfrac{1}{2V}\sum_{\bm{q}\ne0}v(q)\hat{c}_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'-\bm{q}}^\dagger\hat{c}_{\bm{k}'}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}=\dfrac{1}{2V}\sum_{\bm{q}\ne0}v(q)(\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}-\hat{N})$. Thomas–Fermi screening: $v(q)=e_0^2/[\varepsilon_0(q^2+k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2)]$, $k_{\mathrm{TF}}^2=4k_{\mathrm{F}}/\pi a_0$. For Cu, $1.81\ \text{Å}^{-1}$, with a screening length of $0.055\ \mathrm{nm}$.
  • Hartree–Fock: From $\mel{\Phi_0}{\hat{c}_a^\dagger\hat{c}_b^\dagger\hat{c}_d\hat{c}_c}{\Phi_0}=n_an_b(\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd}-\delta_{ad}\delta_{bc})$ we obtain $E_{\mathrm{HF}}=\sum h_{ii}+\tfrac12\sum(U_{ij}-J_{ij})$. Fock operator: $f_{pq}=h_{pq}+\sum_j(\mel{pj}{v}{qj}-\mel{pj}{v}{jq})$; Brillouin's theorem: $\mel{\Phi_i^a}{\hat{H}}{\Phi_0}=f_{ai}$; Koopmans' theorem: $\mathrm{IP}_i=-f_{ii}$. For He (hydrogen-like 1s), $-74.83\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $\mathrm{IP}=20.41\ \mathrm{eV}$ (experiment: $24.59\ \mathrm{eV}$).
  • The equation of motion and Green's functions: $\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)=\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}e^{-i\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar}$. $\mel{\mathrm{FS}}{\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}^\dagger}{\mathrm{FS}}=(1-n_{\bm{k}})e^{-i\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar}$ (period $4.14\ \mathrm{fs}$ for $\varepsilon-\mu=1\ \mathrm{eV}$). $iG=\mel{}{T\hat{\psi}\hat{\psi}^\dagger}{}$ and $G=-i\mel{}{T\hat{\psi}\hat{\psi}^\dagger}{}$ describe the same physics. $\ket{0}$ is the vacuum, and the ground state is $\ket{\Psi_0}$. From $\comm{\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}}{\hat{T}}$ we obtain the excitation energy of an electron–hole pair, $\varepsilon_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}$.
\begin{equation} \hat{T}=\sum_{ij}\mel{i}{\hat{t}}{j}\,\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j,\qquad \hat{V}=\frac12\sum_{ijkl}\mel{ij}{v}{kl}\,\hat{c}_i^\dagger\hat{c}_j^\dagger\hat{c}_l\hat{c}_k,\qquad \mel{\Phi_{ab}}{\hat{V}}{\Phi_{ab}}=U_{ab}-J_{ab} \label{eq:58-key1} \end{equation}
\begin{equation} \hat{V}=\frac{1}{2V}\sum_{\bm{q}\ne\bm{0}}v(q)\bigl(\hat{\rho}_{-\bm{q}}\hat{\rho}_{\bm{q}}-\hat{N}\bigr),\ \ v(q)=\frac{e_0^2}{\varepsilon_0q^2},\qquad E_{\mathrm{HF}}=\sum_{i}h_{ii}+\frac12\sum_{ij}\bigl(U_{ij}-J_{ij}\bigr),\qquad \hat{c}_{\bm{k}}(t)=\hat{c}_{\bm{k}}e^{-i\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}t/\hbar} \label{eq:58-key2} \end{equation}

Related simulator: Read the amplitude of the Green's function for putting one electron into a sea of electrons and removing it, in diagram form, in Green's Functions and Feynman Diagrams (derivation); check how the energy differs with the way the spins are placed, through the “bookkeeping” of the Coulomb integral $U$ and the exchange integral $J$, in Many-Electron States and the Ligand Field (derivation).
