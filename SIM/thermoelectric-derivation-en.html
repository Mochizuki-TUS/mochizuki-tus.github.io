Theory of Thermoelectric Conversion — Deriving the Seebeck Coefficient, the Figure of Merit and the Optimal Carrier Concentration

This page is the companion explanation to thermoelectric-simulator-en.html. It derives, without skipping intermediate steps, where the results you saw in the simulator come from: “as carriers are added, |S| decreases while σ and κe increase,” “the ZT peak appears at 1019–1021 cm−3 (1019–1020 cm−3 for many materials),” “the height of the peak is determined by the quality factor B alone,” and “the efficiency is determined by the average ZT.” The main tools are the Fermi–Dirac distribution, the density of states, integration by parts and Taylor expansion. The Boltzmann equation and the relaxation-time approximation (§3), the gamma function (§5) and simple differential equations (§10) also appear (the values of the Fermi–Dirac integrals and the maximization of ZT are left to numerical computation). This page goes beyond Section 5-3, “Thermoelectric Conversion” (pp. 96–98), of the textbook and Lecture 12 of “Inorganic Materials Science,” and is written with second- and third-year undergraduates in mind. A gentle explanation of why a temperature difference produces a voltage is given in §1.1; how to read the screen of each simulator tab, what the controls mean, and the approximations used in the calculation are collected in Appendix A.

The backbone of this page The theory of thermoelectric conversion is built in the following three steps. Step 1. Phenomenology (Onsager): the three coefficients of Seebeck, Peltier and Thomson are not independent; they are linked by Π = ST and μTh = TdS/dT. Step 2. Microscopic theory (Boltzmann equation): S is “the average of E − EF carried by the carriers that take part in transport,” and the Lorenz number is its “variance.” Step 3. Optimization: in the single parabolic band model, ZT is determined by a single material parameter B, and the efficiency of a device is determined by the average ZT. Of these, “the maximum of ZT is determined by B alone” (§7) is the heart of this page.

1. Introduction — Thermoelectric Conversion and the Figure of Merit

When a temperature difference is applied across a material, a voltage appears (the Seebeck effect). Conversely, when a current is passed through the junction of two different materials, heat is absorbed or released at the junction (the Peltier effect). Using the former, electricity can be taken directly from heat (thermoelectric power generation); using the latter, a refrigerator with no moving parts (Peltier cooling) can be made. Thermoelectric cooling with semiconductors was demonstrated by Goldsmid and Douglas in 1954, and the subsequent course of materials development is summarized in the review by Snyder and Toberer. The performance of both is determined by the same set of material constants, and the dimensionless figure of merit combines them into one.

(1) ZT=S2σTκ =S2σTκe+κL

S is the Seebeck coefficient, σ the electrical conductivity, and κ the thermal conductivity, which is the sum of κe carried by the carriers and κL carried by lattice vibrations (phonons). The numerator S2σ is called the power factor. Looking at Eq. (1) alone, it seems that one should simply make S and σ large and κ small, but the three quantities are not independent. Increasing the carrier concentration raises σ, but it lowers |S| and raises κe. Solving this tug of war quantitatively is the purpose of this page.

Notation e > 0: elementary charge (the charge of an electron is −e). EF: Fermi level (at finite temperature, the chemical potential of the electrons). Ec: bottom of the conduction band. x = (E − Ec)/kBT: reduced energy. η = (EF − Ec)/kBT: reduced Fermi level. f0: Fermi–Dirac distribution. τ: relaxation time. m*: density-of-states effective mass (in equations it is the mass itself; in tables and in the simulator it is expressed in units of the electron mass me, as a number such as 1.2). μ: mobility; μ0: mobility in the nondegenerate limit. z: coordinate along the length of the sample. V: the potential measured by a voltmeter.
The text is written mainly for n-type (electrons). For p-type (holes) the signs of S and Π are reversed and the other equations are unchanged. Only in §10 and in Problem 5 of §13, η denotes the conversion efficiency rather than the reduced Fermi level (to match the notation of the simulator). Note also that the Onsager coefficients L11, L12 (§2) and the Lorenz numbers L, L0 (§3 onward) are different things, and that the leg length ℓ in §10 is a different symbol from the reduced Lorenz number l in §6.

1.1 Where does the thermoelectric voltage come from? — Diffusion of hot charged particles

Before going into what is inside S in Eq. (1), let us first grasp, with almost no equations, why a temperature difference produces a voltage. Suppose one end of a rod of n-type semiconductor is heated and the other end is cooled. In a nondegenerate electron gas the average kinetic energy of an electron is (3/2)kBT, so electrons on the hot side move around more vigorously than those on the cold side. The more vigorously particles move, the farther they spread, so the electrons as a whole tend to diffuse from the hot side to the cold side. As a result, electrons accumulate on the cold side, which becomes negatively charged, while the positive charge of the donor ions that released the electrons is left behind on the hot side. The electric field created by this charge imbalance acts to push the electrons heading toward the cold side back toward the hot side, and when it balances the drive of diffusion, the charge imbalance stops growing. In this way a potential difference arises between the two ends. This is the origin of the Seebeck voltage (the magnitude a voltmeter reads also includes a contribution from the temperature dependence of the Fermi level; see the box below and the explanation that follows). In a p-type material the diffusing particles are positively charged holes, so the cold side becomes positive and the hot side, left with the negative charge of the acceptor ions, becomes negative; the direction of the voltage is reversed. This is why measuring the sign of the voltage tells you the carrier type. You can watch this in the animation of simulator tab ① (Appendix A.2).

Let us fix the sign convention. As in Eq. (2) of §2, this page takes the temperature difference to be ΔT = Th − Tc > 0 and defines (potential of the cold side Vc) − (potential of the hot side Vh) = SΔT. The relation S = −ΔV/ΔT in the lecture slides is read as follows: if the potential difference ΔV is measured in the same direction as the temperature difference, as “hot side − cold side” (ΔV = Vh − Vc), then −ΔV/ΔT = (Vc − Vh)/ΔT, which is the same as the convention of this page. In an n-type material the cold side is negative (Vc < Vh), so S < 0; in a p-type material the cold side is positive, so S > 0.

How far is the picture of “diffusion due to the difference in kinetic energy” correct? This picture is meant for grasping the origin and sign of the thermoelectric voltage. To obtain even the magnitude of |S| correctly, the Boltzmann equation of §3–§5 is needed. If we rewrite the result of §5 (Eq. (25)) in the convention in which the relaxation time is written τ ∝ (E − Ec)r (r = λ − 1/2), then for a nondegenerate semiconductor |S| = (kB/e)(r + 5/2 − η) (the Pisarenko relation of §5). Below we confirm that this 5/2 − η splits into two parts: “the balance between diffusion and the electric field” and “the temperature dependence of the Fermi level.”

Consider nondegenerate electrons whose relaxation time does not depend on energy (r = 0). In an open circuit (zero current) the interior of the sample remains almost electrically neutral (the charge imbalance needed to create the electric field is tiny compared with the number of carriers), so the electron concentration n equals the donor concentration and is almost constant, independent of position.

(a) Electrostatic part: the difference in electrostatic potential (balance between diffusion and the electric field). The pressure of the electron gas, nkBT, varies from place to place with the temperature, and it pushes the electrons toward the cold side with a force per unit volume of −d(nkBT)/dz = −nkBdT/dz. Per electron this is −kBdT/dz, which, for dT/dz < 0 (with z taken from the hot side toward the cold side), points in the positive z direction, that is, toward the cold side. When τ is constant, multiplying both sides of Eq. (9) by the momentum pz and summing over all states turns the right-hand side into a frictional force −nm*⟨vz⟩/τ proportional to the mean velocity (that is, to the current), and the left-hand side into the sum of the pressure gradient d(nkBT)/dz and neℰ (neℰ is the electrostatic force per unit volume acting on the electrons, −neℰ, with its sign reversed; that is, the sum of the force from the pressure gradient, −d(nkBT)/dz, and the electrostatic force, −neℰ, balances the frictional force) (for a nondegenerate parabolic band, m*⟨vz2⟩ = kBT). At zero current the frictional force vanishes, so the forces acting on a single electron balance. Writing the electron charge as q = −e, we have

qℰ+(−kBdTdz)=0 ⟹ ℰ=−kBedTdz

(the second term is the push due to heat, and the electric force qℰ = kBdT/dz cancels it). In terms of the electrostatic potential φ (ℰ = −dφ/dz), dφ/dz = (kB/e)dT/dz, so integrating from the hot end to the cold end gives φc − φh = (kB/e)(Tc − Th) = −(kB/e)ΔT. The electrostatic potential on the cold side is lower by (kB/e)ΔT. What the animation of simulator tab ① shows as particle motion is this part (a).

(b) Chemical-potential part: the difference in chemical potential (the temperature dependence of the Fermi level). However, what a voltmeter measures is not the electrostatic potential itself but the difference in the electrochemical potential of the electrons (the Fermi level) (see the explanation of ℰ* in §2.1). The height of the Fermi level measured from the conduction band bottom at that position, EF − Ec = ηkBT, follows from n = Nceη in Eq. (26) as

EF−Ec=kBTln⁡nNc ,Nc∝T3/2 ⟹ d(EF−Ec)dT =kBln⁡nNc−kBT·32T =kB(η−32)

This shows that it varies with temperature even when n is constant (we used d ln Nc/dT = 3/(2T)). If the temperature difference is small (so that η is almost the same at both ends), the difference between the cold end and the hot end is (EF − Ec)c − (EF − Ec)h = kB(η − 3/2)(Tc − Th) = (3/2 − η)kBΔT. The voltmeter potential is V = φ − (EF − Ec)/e (this splits the electrochemical potential of the electrons, −eV, into the electrostatic energy −eφ and the chemical-potential part EF − Ec; differentiating gives ℰ* = ℰ + (1/e)dEF/dz of §2.1). Therefore

Vc−Vh =(φc−φh) −1e[(EF−Ec)c−(EF−Ec)h] =−kBeΔT −(32−η)kBeΔT =−(52−η)kBeΔT

we obtain S = −(kB/e)(5/2 − η). This coincides with Eq. (25) with τ constant (λ = 1/2, r = 0). Note that in the nondegenerate case η < 0, so the two parts add in the same direction, and that (b) is larger than (a). For example, at η = −2, (a) is 1 and (b) is 3.5 (both in units of (kB/e)ΔT), so |S| = 4.5 × 86.17 μV/K = 388 μV/K. The visible part, “hot particles are pushed out,” is only a portion of what the voltmeter reads. When τ depends on energy (r ≠ 0), splitting ℰ in the same way from ℰ* with J = 0 in Eq. (14), part (a) becomes (1 + r)(kB/e)ΔT (for r > 0, faster electrons are less likely to be scattered and travel farther, so the push toward the cold side becomes stronger), while part (b) is unchanged, so the total is r + 5/2 − η.

Why |S| is small in metals. In a metal the electrons are degenerate, and only the electrons within about kBT of EF take part in transport (Eq. (11) of §3.1). On the hot side, the number of electrons occupying states above EF increases, while the number of empty states below EF also increases. Electrons with energies above EF tend to flow from the hot side to the cold side, but at energies below EF there are fewer electrons on the hot side, so they tend to flow in the opposite direction, from the cold side to the hot side. As a result, the contributions above and below EF almost cancel, only the slight asymmetry of σ(E) around EF remains, and |S| stays at a few μV/K. The quantitative expression of this is Mott’s formula, Eq. (20) of §4.

2. Phenomenology — The Three Coefficients and the Kelvin Relation

2.1 Definitions of the three effects

Consider a sample whose temperature is distributed along its length as T(z). When no current flows (open circuit), the Seebeck coefficient is defined by the potential difference that appears between the two ends. As in the simulator, the sign is chosen so that (potential of the cold side) − (potential of the hot side) = SΔT. Written locally, this reads

(2) ℰ*≡−dVdz =SdTdz(J=0)

Here ℰ* is the gradient of the potential V measured by the voltmeter, with the sign reversed. Because the Fermi level also varies from place to place inside the sample, ℰ* is the electrostatic field ℰ plus the gradient of EF, that is, the electrochemical field ℰ* = ℰ + (1/e)dEF/dz (here EF means the height measured from the conduction band bottom Ec(z) at that position. The part by which Ec(z) itself moves up and down with the electrostatic potential corresponds to ℰ in the first term. The reference from which the energy E is measured in §3 is likewise Ec(z) at that position). In an n-type material, electrons on the hot side diffuse toward the cold side and the cold side becomes negatively charged, so S < 0. When the temperature is uniform, the ratio of the current density J to the heat flux density JQ that it carries is the Peltier coefficient.

(3) JQ=ΠJ(dTdz=0)

When a current is passed through the junction of two materials A and B, Π differs on the two sides, so the difference between the heat carried in and the heat carried out, (ΠA − ΠB)J, is absorbed or released at the junction. This is the Peltier heat. The third effect, the Thomson effect, is treated in §2.4.

2.2 Linear response and the Onsager reciprocity theorem

When both the temperature gradient and the electric field are small, the flows J and JQ can be written as linear expressions in the “forces.” If we choose the forces so that the entropy production rate is the sum of “flow × force,” Jℰ*/T + JQd(1/T)/dz, the relations take the form

(4) J=L11ℰ*T+L12ddz(1T) JQ=L21ℰ*T+L22ddz(1T)

Here Lij are coefficients determined by the material. From the fact that the microscopic equations of motion are symmetric under time reversal, Onsager showed that, with the forces chosen in this way, the off-diagonal coefficients are equal (the reciprocity theorem).

(5) L12=L21

2.3 Reading off the coefficients and deriving the Kelvin relation

Noting that d(1/T)/dz = −(1/T2)dT/dz, we read off the four coefficients from Eq. (4).

  • Electrical conductivity: if dT/dz = 0, then J = (L11/T)ℰ*. Hence σ = L11/T.
  • Seebeck coefficient: setting J = 0 gives 0 = L11ℰ*/T − (L12/T2)dT/dz. Solving for ℰ* gives ℰ* = [L12/(TL11)]dT/dz, and comparing with Eq. (2), S = L12/(TL11).
  • Peltier coefficient: for dT/dz = 0, taking the ratio of the two equations gives JQ/J = L21/L11. Hence Π = L21/L11.
  • Thermal conductivity: substituting the ℰ* obtained for J = 0 into the second equation gives JQ = [L21L12/(L11T2) − L22/T2]dT/dz. Hence κ = (L11L22 − L12L21)/(L11T2).

Substituting the reciprocity theorem, Eq. (5), into the expression for the Peltier coefficient gives

(6) Π=L21L11 =L12L11 =T·L12TL11 =ST
The second Kelvin relation Π = ST The Seebeck and Peltier coefficients are not separate material constants; one is converted into the other simply by multiplying by the temperature. “A temperature difference produces a voltage” and “a current carries heat” are the same single coupling seen from opposite directions. W. Thomson (later Lord Kelvin) obtained this relation from thermodynamic considerations, and the Onsager reciprocity theorem gave it a general foundation.

Let us rewrite Eq. (4) in a practical form. Solving the first equation for ℰ* and writing the result in terms of σ and S gives ℰ* = J/σ + SdT/dz. Substituting this into the second equation and using σT = L11 and S = L12/(TL11), we obtain

(7) JQ=L21L11J +(L21L12L11T2−L22T2)dTdz =ΠJ−κdTdz

The heat flux is the sum of “the Peltier heat carried by the current” and “heat conduction due to the temperature gradient.” The device calculation in §10 starts from this equation.

2.4 The Thomson effect and the first Kelvin relation

Suppose that, in the steady state, a uniform current J flows through a sample that has a temperature gradient. The energy flow is the sum of the heat flux JQ and VJ, carried by the charge at potential V, and this is constant, independent of position: d(JQ + VJ)/dz = 0. Substituting here dV/dz = −ℰ* = −J/σ − SdT/dz and JQ = STJ − κdT/dz (Eq. (7) with Π = ST put in), and using d(ST)/dz = SdT/dz + T(dS/dT)dT/dz, we obtain

(8) J[SdTdz+TdSdTdTdz] −ddz(κdTdz)−J2σ−SJdTdz=0 ⟹ddz(κdTdz)+J2σ −μThJdTdz=0 ,μTh=TdSdT

(the second line is obtained from the first after the SJdT/dz terms cancel, by multiplying the whole by −1). The first term is the heat flowing in by heat conduction, the second term is the Joule heat (positive regardless of the direction of the current), and the third term is the reversible Thomson heat, whose sign changes with the direction of the current. Its coefficient μTh = TdS/dT is called the first Kelvin relation (using Π = ST, it can also be written μTh = dΠ/dT − S). Although this is the reverse of the order of derivation, it is common to call this the first relation and Π = ST of §2.3 the second relation, and this page follows that convention. If S does not depend on temperature, the Thomson heat vanishes. The constant-property model of §10 uses this approximation.

3. Writing the Transport Coefficients in Terms of σ(E)

We calculate the coefficients Lij of §2 from the motion of the carriers. The tools are the Boltzmann equation and the relaxation-time approximation.

3.1 The deviation of the distribution

The electron distribution function f is slightly displaced from the equilibrium distribution f0 by the electric field and the temperature gradient, and scattering tries to bring it back to f0. In the steady state the two balance, and if the rate of return is written 1/τ (the relaxation-time approximation), we have

(9) vz∂f∂z −eℰ∂f∂pz =−f−f0τ

Here −eℰ is the force acting on an electron and pz is the momentum. Replacing f on the left-hand side by f0 (keeping terms to first order in the deviation) gives δf = f − f0 = −τ[vz∂f0/∂z − eℰ∂f0/∂pz]. Since f0 = 1/(ey + 1), with y = (E − EF)/kBT, depends on position through EF(z) and T(z), we use df0/dy = kBT∂f0/∂E and the chain rule to obtain

(10) ∂f0∂z =df0dy∂y∂z =∂f0∂EkBT [−1kBTdEFdz −E−EFkBT2dTdz] =∂f0∂E [−dEFdz−E−EFTdTdz] ∂f0∂pz =∂f0∂E∂E∂pz =∂f0∂Evz

Substituting these into δf and collecting terms with eℰ + dEF/dz = eℰ*, we obtain

(11) δf=−τvz(−∂f0∂E) [eℰ*+E−EFTdTdz]

Since δf is proportional to −∂f0/∂E, the only states whose distribution is displaced and that take part in transport are those at energies where −∂f0/∂E is non-negligible.

3.2 Current and heat flux

Let g(E) be the number of states per unit volume and per unit energy (the density of states, including spin). The current density is the sum over states of the product of the charge −e and the velocity vz of each state, weighted by δf; the heat flux density is the same with the heat E − EF carried in place of the charge. In an isotropic system, averaging over the direction of the velocity gives vz2 → v2/3, so inserting Eq. (11) we obtain

(12) J=−e∫gvzδfdE =e∫gv2τ3(−∂f0∂E) [eℰ*+E−EFTdTdz]dE JQ=∫(E−EF)gvzδfdE =−∫(E−EF)gv2τ3(−∂f0∂E) [eℰ*+E−EFTdTdz]dE

Here we define the energy-resolved conductivity σ(E) and its weighted integrals 𝒦k.

(13) σ(E)=e2g(E)v(E)2τ(E)3 , 𝒦k=∫σ(E)(E−EF)k(−∂f0∂E)dE (k=0,1,2)

Using g v2τ/3 = σ(E)/e2, Eq. (12) becomes more compact:

(14) J=𝒦0ℰ*+𝒦1eTdTdz JQ=−𝒦1eℰ*−𝒦2e2TdTdz

3.3 Reading off the coefficients

The procedure is the same as in §2.3. Setting J = 0 gives ℰ* = −[𝒦1/(eT𝒦0)]dT/dz. Comparing this with Eq. (2) gives S; substituting ℰ* into the second equation gives JQ = −[(𝒦2 − 𝒦12/𝒦0)/(e2T)]dT/dz, from which κe follows. Setting dT/dz = 0 gives J = 𝒦0ℰ* and JQ = −(𝒦1/e𝒦0)J, from which σ and Π follow.

(15) σ=𝒦0, S=−𝒦1eT𝒦0, Π=−𝒦1e𝒦0=ST, κe=1e2T (𝒦2−𝒦12𝒦0)

Π = ST holds automatically. In fact, rewriting Eq. (14) in the form of Eq. (4) gives L11 = T𝒦0 and L12 = L21 = −T𝒦1/e, so the microscopic calculation satisfies the reciprocity theorem.

3.4 Reading them as a mean and a variance

Regard σ(E)(−∂f0/∂E) as a weight that expresses “how much current is carried by carriers of which energy,” and write the average with this weight as ⟨A⟩ = ∫A σ(E)(−∂f0/∂E)dE/𝒦0. Since 𝒦1/𝒦0 = ⟨E − EF⟩ and 𝒦2/𝒦0 = ⟨(E − EF)2⟩, Eq. (15) becomes

(16) S=−⟨E−EF⟩eT , L≡κeσT =⟨(E−EF)2⟩−⟨E−EF⟩2(eT)2
S is the mean, the Lorenz number is the variance We call the weight σ(E)(−∂f0/∂E) the transport window. S is the average of the heat E − EF carried per carrier in the window, divided by eT. Since heat divided by temperature is entropy, S can also be described as “the entropy carried by one unit of charge.” And the Lorenz number L is the spread (variance) of E − EF within the window. If the window lies on only one side of EF, the average is large (large |S|); if it straddles EF, the positive and negative parts cancel and the average becomes small (small |S|).

Misconception 1: “the more carriers, the larger |S|” is not true The S in Eq. (16) is determined not by the number of carriers but by how far the window is offset with respect to EF. Metals have very many carriers but small |S| (§4), whereas semiconductors with few carriers have large |S| (§5). The fact that 𝒦0, the amount of current carried, and 𝒦1, the bias in the heat carried, are different integrals is the starting point for thinking about thermoelectric materials.

4. The Metallic Limit — Mott’s Formula

In a metal, EF lies deep inside the conduction band (EF − Ec ≫ kBT), and σ(E) varies smoothly near EF. So we expand, treating the window width kBT as small.

4.1 The Sommerfeld expansion

−∂f0/∂E is an even function of E − EF, and its total integral is ∫(−∂f0/∂E)dE = f0(−∞) − f0(∞) = 1. Expanding a smooth function H(E) in a Taylor series around EF and integrating, the first-order term vanishes because it is an odd function, and we obtain

(17) ∫H(E)(−∂f0∂E)dE =H(EF) +12H″(EF) ∫(E−EF)2(−∂f0∂E)dE+⋯ =H(EF)+π26(kBT)2H″(EF)+⋯

In the second line we used the value of the second moment. Putting y = (E − EF)/kBT and f(y) = 1/(ey + 1), we have (−∂f0/∂E)dE = (−df/dy)dy, and using the evenness and integration by parts we get

(18) ∫(E−EF)2(−∂f0∂E)dE =(kBT)2·2∫0∞y2(−dfdy)dy =(kBT)2·2{[−y2f]0∞+2∫0∞yfdy} =4(kBT)2∫0∞yey+1dy =4(kBT)2·π212 =π23(kBT)2

The last integral is evaluated by expanding 1/(ey + 1) = Σk≥1(−1)k+1e−ky and using ∫0∞ye−kydy = 1/k2, which gives Σ(−1)k+1/k2 = (1 − 2/22)Σ1/k2 = (1/2)(π2/6) = π2/12 (the even-numbered terms are first included and then subtracted twice).

4.2 Mott’s formula

We apply Eq. (17) to 𝒦0 and 𝒦1. For 𝒦0, H = σ(E), and to lowest order 𝒦0 ≈ σ(EF). For 𝒦1, H = σ(E)(E − EF); since H(EF) = 0 and H′ = σ′(E − EF) + σ,

(19) H″=σ″(E−EF)+2σ′ ⟹ 𝒦1≈π26(kBT)2·2σ′(EF) =π23(kBT)2σ′(EF)

Substituting this into S = −𝒦1/(eT𝒦0) of Eq. (15) gives Mott’s formula (this form, written with σ(E), is due to Cutler and Mott).

(20) S=−π23kB2Te σ′(EF)σ(EF) =−π23kBekBT [dln⁡σ(E)dE]E=EF
What to read from Mott’s formula (i) S ∝ T. The Seebeck coefficient of a metal is proportional to temperature.
(ii) S is determined by the logarithmic derivative of σ(E). The more asymmetric σ(E) is around EF, the larger |S| becomes. In ordinary metals d ln σ/dE ~ 1/EF, so |S| ~ (kB/e)(kBT/EF). At 300 K, kBT = 25.85 meV is only 0.52 % of EF = 5 eV, so |S| is a few μV/K, far smaller than kB/e = 86.17 μV/K (−2.2 μV/K in Problem 8).
(iii) Making σ(E) steeper raises |S|. The technique of raising |S| by creating a sharp feature (a resonant level) in the density of states (Heremans et al.) is based on this idea.

4.3 The Lorenz number of a metal

7.4 How to increase B

From Eq. (39), there are four ways to increase B.

  • Lower κL (§9).
  • Raise μ0: reduce scattering, or use a band with a small effective mass along the conduction direction.
  • Raise the density-of-states effective mass m*: however, simply making a single valley heavier is a loss. For acoustic-phonon scattering μ0 ∝ m*−5/2, so B ∝ Ncμ0 ∝ 1/m* actually decreases (if m* of the room-temperature preset is doubled and μ0 is reduced accordingly by a factor of 2−5/2 (300 → 53 cm2/Vs), B goes from 0.442 to 0.221 and the maximum of ZT from 1.10 to 0.65. The “Effective mass m*” option in simulator tab ③ does not change μ0, so it corresponds to the next case, “increase the number of valleys”). What pays off is raising only the density-of-states effective mass without making the conduction-direction effective mass heavier, that is, the next item, “increase the number of valleys,” or the use of band anisotropy.
  • Increase the number of valleys Nv: if there are Nv equivalent valleys, Nc becomes Nv times larger. If scattering between valleys is neglected, B can be made Nv times larger without lowering the mobility of a single valley. Band convergence (Pei et al.), which aligns the energies of the band edges of several bands, corresponds to this.
Methods outside the SPB model Resonant levels (Heremans et al.) and quantum wells and wires (Hicks and Dresselhaus) are methods that make σ(E) steeper by changing the shape of the density of states itself ((E − Ec)1/2 in Eq. (22)), and they cannot be evaluated by the B of the single parabolic band alone. One has to return to the “mean and variance of the window” of Eq. (16).

8. The Wiedemann–Franz Law and the Lorenz Number

Using the Lorenz number of Eq. (16), the thermal conductivity carried by the carriers can be written as

(40) κe=LσT

Here L varies with η, and its two limits are

(41) L→(2+λ)(kBe)2(η≪−1) , L→π23(kBe)2(η≫1)

The nondegenerate value is obtained by putting Fj → Γ(j + 1)eη in Eq. (31), which gives A2 → (2 + λ)Γ(2 + λ)/[(1 + λ)Γ(1 + λ)] = 2 + λ and A3 → (3 + λ)(2 + λ); then A3 − A22 = (2 + λ)(3 + λ) − (2 + λ)2 = 2 + λ gives the result. The degenerate value is Eq. (21). For λ = 0, the nondegenerate value is 2(kB/e)2 = 1.485×10−8 W Ω K−2 and the degenerate value is (π2/3)(kB/e)2 = 2.443×10−8 W Ω K−2, and their ratio is π2/6 = 1.645.

Why is L smaller in the nondegenerate case? (acoustic-phonon scattering) As in §3.4, L is the variance of E − EF within the window. In the degenerate case the window is −∂f0/∂E itself, and its variance is (π2/3)(kBT)2 of Eq. (18). In the nondegenerate case the window has the form x1+λe−x (a gamma distribution), and its variance is (2 + λ)(kBT)2. For acoustic-phonon scattering (λ = 0) this is 2(kBT)2, which is smaller than the degenerate value (π2/3)(kBT)2 ≈ 3.29(kBT)2. However, for 2 + λ > π2/3, that is, λ > π2/3 − 2 = 1.29, the order is reversed; for example, for ionized-impurity scattering (λ = 2) the nondegenerate L = 4(kB/e)2 exceeds L0 = 3.29(kB/e)2. L is a measure of “how broad a range of heat energies the electricity-carrying carriers carry.”

In experiments S can be measured but η cannot be known directly, so Kim et al. proposed an expression that approximates L of the SPB (acoustic-phonon scattering) from |S| alone.

(42) L≈[1.5+exp⁡(−|S|116)] ×10−8W Ω K−2 (|S| in units of μV/K)

Compared with L of the same SPB (λ = 0) as in the simulator, when η is varied from −10 to 50 the difference stays within the range from −3.6 % (near |S| = 36 μV/K) to +3.7 % (near |S| = 217 μV/K).

Misconception 4: do not estimate κL of a semiconductor with the metallic L0 The lattice thermal conductivity is obtained from the measured κ as κL = κ − LσT. If the metallic value L0 is used for L here, then for a semiconductor close to the nondegenerate limit κe is overestimated by up to a factor of π2/6 = 1.645 (for λ = 0), and κL is underestimated by the same amount. If the scattering mechanism is different, the direction of the error also changes: for a nondegenerate semiconductor with λ = 2 (ionized-impurity scattering), κe is conversely underestimated, at only 0.82 times its true value. At the top of the ZT peak of the room-temperature preset, L = 1.568×10−8 W Ω K−2, only 0.64 times L0.
Misconception 5: ZT does not keep rising with temperature indefinitely (bipolar effect) When a material with a small band gap is used at high temperatures, electron–hole pairs are thermally excited from the valence band to the conduction band. Because electrons and holes have opposite signs of S, they cancel and |S| decreases; in addition, pairs are created on the hot side and recombine on the cold side, which carries an energy equal to the band gap and adds an extra contribution to κ. The SPB model (the simulator), which treats only one type of carrier, does not include this effect, so it overestimates ZT at high temperatures.

9. Reducing the Lattice Thermal Conductivity

As Eq. (39) shows, B ∝ μ0/κL. If only κL can be lowered without lowering the mobility, the ZT peak becomes higher. We derive the idea behind this from the kinetic theory of phonons.

9.1 κL from the kinetic theory of gases

Regard phonons as a “gas of particles carrying heat,” and let C be the heat capacity per unit volume, v the speed of sound and Λ the mean free path. A phonon crossing the plane z = z0 in a direction making an angle θ with the normal brings an energy appropriate to the temperature at the position of its last scattering, z0 − Λ cos θ. The energy density there is lower than at z0 by CΛ cos θ dT/dz (the uniform part vanishes on averaging over directions), so multiplying by the z component of the velocity, v cos θ, and averaging over directions gives

(43) JQ=⟨vcos⁡θ·(−CΛcos⁡θdTdz)⟩ =−CvΛ⟨cos2⁡θ⟩dTdz =−13CvΛdTdz ⟹κL=13CvΛ

(Averaging over the sphere gives ⟨cos2 θ⟩ = 1/3.) C and v are largely fixed by the material, so what can be varied by design is the mean free path Λ.

9.2 Matthiessen’s rule and grain-boundary scattering

When several scattering processes occur independently, the scattering frequencies v/Λ add up, so

(44) 1Λ=1ΛU+1Λpd+1Λb+⋯

Here U denotes Umklapp scattering between phonons, pd denotes point defects such as the mass fluctuation in a solid solution, and b denotes grain boundaries. In a polycrystal of grain size d, Λb ≈ d. Tab ④ of the simulator lumps everything other than the grain boundaries into a single value Λph (the gray approximation) and takes 1/Λ = 1/Λph + 1/d. The electron mobility also satisfies μ ∝ τ ∝ Λ, so the same form holds with the electron mean free path Λe, and

(45) Λ=11/Λph+1/d =Λph1+Λph/d ⟹ κL(d)=κL,bulk1+Λph/d , μ0(d)=μ0,bulk1+Λe/d

9.3 PGEC and the minimum thermal conductivity

Slack described the ideal thermoelectric material as “a glass for phonons and a crystal for electrons” (Phonon Glass, Electron Crystal). Typical examples are filled skutterudites, in which guest atoms (Ba, Yb, etc.) placed inside the cage-like crystal structure rattle, and clathrates, which work by the same mechanism (rattling does not occur if the cages are left empty). However, no matter how much disorder is introduced, the mean free path cannot become shorter than the interatomic distance. Cahill et al. estimated the thermal conductivity in the limit where the lifetime of each vibration is shortened to half a period, and found that at high temperatures the minimum thermal conductivity is

(46) κmin≈12(π6)1/3 kBna2/3(vl+2vt)

(na: number density of atoms; vl and vt: speeds of sound of the longitudinal and transverse waves). For example, for na = 5×1028 m−3, vl = 3000 m/s and vt = 1800 m/s, κmin ≈ 0.50 W/mK. The κmin of the simulator is a parameter that represents this lower limit.

Misconception 6: the gray approximation tends to overestimate the effect of nanostructuring The actual phonon mean free paths are widely distributed from nm to μm and cannot be represented by a single Λph. Solid-solution alloying (mass fluctuation) mainly scatters short-wavelength phonons, nanoprecipitates medium wavelengths, and grain boundaries long wavelengths, so combining scatterers at the atomic, nano and meso scales is effective (the all-scale hierarchical architecture of Biswas et al.).

10. Deriving the Power-Generation Efficiency and the Cooling Coefficient of Performance

Once the ZT of a material is known, we derive how much efficiency is obtained when it is made into a thermoelectric device that combines p-type and n-type legs. This analysis based on the constant-property model has been the standard method since Ioffe’s textbook. Note that only in this section and in Problem 5 of §13, η denotes the conversion efficiency (not the reduced Fermi level).

10.1 The constant-property model and notation

As in Fig. 3, the p-type and n-type legs are joined at one end by an electrode on the hot side (Th), and a load resistance is connected across the ends on the cold side (Tc). We assume that S, σ and κ do not depend on temperature (the constant-property model; the Thomson heat in Eq. (8) is then 0). The whole device is described by the following three quantities.

  • the Seebeck coefficient of the pair, S = Sp − Sn (since Sn < 0, the magnitudes add up)
  • the sum R of the internal resistances of the two legs
  • the sum K of the thermal conductances of the two legs (even without a current, heat KΔT flows from the hot side to the cold side)

The figure of merit of the device is defined as follows.

(47) Z=S2RK

If the p and n legs have properties (|Sp|, σ, κ) of the same magnitude and differing only in the sign of S, and the same leg length ℓ and cross-sectional area A, then S = 2|Sp|, R = 2ℓ/(σA) and K = 2κA/ℓ, so Z = 4Sp2/(4κ/σ) = Sp2σ/κ, which coincides with the figure of merit of the material. Let the load resistance be mR (m: load ratio) and ΔT = Th − Tc.

Fig. 3. A thermoelectric power-generation device (p–n pair) and the flow of heat. Holes (p-type) and electrons (n-type) both flow from the hot side to the cold side, so the current flows downward in the p-type leg and upward in the n-type leg, and goes once around through the load. The heat Qh entering from the hot side is the sum of the Peltier heat SThI absorbed at the hot junction and the heat conduction KΔT, minus half of the Joule heat, I2R/2, that is generated inside the legs and returns to the hot side (Eq. (51)).

10.2 Heat balance on the hot side — why does half of the Joule heat come back?

The current flowing in the circuit is the electromotive force SΔT divided by the sum of the internal and load resistances, and the power taken out at the load is P = I2mR.

(48) I=SΔTR+mR=SΔTR(1+m) ,P=I2mR=S2ΔT2mR(1+m)2

To find the heat Qh entering the device from the hot side, consider the temperature distribution inside one leg of length ℓ and cross-sectional area A. Joule heat ρJ2 per unit volume is generated uniformly inside the leg (ρ = 1/σ, J = I/A), so the steady-state heat conduction equation (Eq. (8) with the Thomson heat set to 0) and its solution are

(49) κd2Tdz2=−ρJ2 ,T(0)=Th,T(ℓ)=Tc ⟹ T(z)=Th−ΔTℓz+ρJ22κz(ℓ−z)

(Differentiating twice gives −ρJ2/κ, and the boundary conditions are satisfied at z = 0 and ℓ.) The conducted heat flowing into the leg from the hot end z = 0 is

(50) −κA(dTdz)z=0 =−κA(−ΔTℓ+ρJ2ℓ2κ) =κAℓΔT−12I2ρℓA

(ρJ2Aℓ = I2ρℓ/A.) The first term on the right is the thermal conductance of the leg × ΔT, and the second term is half of the Joule heat generated in the leg’s resistance ρℓ/A. In other words, exactly half of the Joule heat generated inside the leg returns to the hot side, and the heat that must be drawn from the hot side is reduced by that amount (the other half flows to the cold side). Summing over the two legs and adding the Peltier heat absorbed at the hot junction (ΠI = SThI, from Eq. (6)) gives

(51) Qh=SThI−12I2R+KΔT

10.3 Writing the efficiency as a function of the load ratio

We put Eq. (48) into η = P/Qh. Here Qh = S2ThΔT/[R(1 + m)] − S2ΔT2/[2R(1 + m)2] + KΔT. Dividing both the numerator P and the denominator Qh by S2ΔTTh/[R(1 + m)2] and using RK/S2 = 1/Z, we have

(52) η(m)=PQh =ΔTTh· m(1+m)−ΔT2Th+(1+m)2ZTh

10.4 The optimal load ratio and the maximum efficiency

Writing the denominator as D(m) = (1 + m) − ΔT/2Th + (1 + m)2/ZTh, we have η ∝ m/D, and the condition is dη/dm ∝ (D − mD′)/D2 = 0. Using D′ = 1 + 2(1 + m)/ZTh, we obtain

(53) D−mD′=1−ΔT2Th +(1+m)2−2m(1+m)ZTh =1−ΔT2Th+1−m2ZTh=0 ⟹m2=1+Z(Th−ΔT2) =1+ZT‾,T‾=Th+Tc2

(The first line uses (1 + m)2 − 2m(1 + m) = (1 + m)(1 − m); the second line is obtained by multiplying the whole by ZTh and rearranging.) Therefore

(54) mopt=1+ZT‾≡M

At the optimum D = mD′, so η = (ΔT/Th)·M/D = (ΔT/Th)/D′(M). Using Z = (M2 − 1)/T̄, we have 2(1 + M)/ZTh = 2(1 + M)T̄/[(M2 − 1)Th] = (Th + Tc)/[(M − 1)Th], so

(55) ηmax=ΔTTh (M−1)Th(M−1)Th+Th+Tc =Th−TcTh·M−1M+Tc/Th
What to read off from Eq. (55) The first factor is the Carnot efficiency, and the second factor is the fraction determined by the material, which approaches 1 as ZT̄ → ∞ (M → ∞). The only material quantity that determines the efficiency is the average ZT̄; there is no need to know S, σ and κ individually. Tab ⑤ of the simulator draws this equation.

10.5 The coefficient of performance of cooling

This is the case where a current I is passed by a power supply and heat is pumped up from the cold side. The cooling capacity Qc is the Peltier heat STcI absorbed at the cold junction, minus half of the returning Joule heat and the conducted heat leaking in from the hot side. The electric power W supplied from outside is the sum of the work done against the Seebeck electromotive force and the Joule heat.

(56) Qc=STcI−12I2R−KΔT ,W=SΔTI+I2R

Multiplying the numerator and denominator of the coefficient of performance φ = Qc/W by R/S2, and using ξ = IR/S (which has the dimension of temperature) and 1/Z = RK/S2, we have

(57) φ(ξ)= Tcξ−ξ2/2−ΔT/ZΔTξ+ξ2

The condition dφ/dξ = 0 is N′W̃ − NW̃′ = 0, where N is the numerator and W̃ the denominator. When the two products are expanded and subtracted, the terms in ξ3 and TcΔT ξ cancel each other.

(58) N′W̃=(Tc−ξ)(ΔTξ+ξ2) =TcΔTξ+Tcξ2−ΔTξ2−ξ3 NW̃′=(Tcξ−ξ22−ΔTZ)(ΔT+2ξ) =TcΔTξ+2Tcξ2−ΔT2ξ2−ξ3−ΔT2Z−2ΔTZξ N′W̃−NW̃′=−(Tc+ΔT2)ξ2+2ΔTZξ+ΔT2Z=0 ⟹ZT‾ξ2−2ΔTξ−ΔT2=0 ⟹ξ=ΔT(1+M)ZT‾ =ΔT(1+M)M2−1=ΔTM−1

(The third line is obtained by multiplying by −Z and using Tc + ΔT/2 = T̄. The fourth line is the positive solution of the quadratic equation, with √(4ΔT2 + 4ZT̄ΔT2) = 2ΔT M.) We put this ξ back into Eq. (57). Using 1/Z = T̄/(M2 − 1), the numerator becomes TcΔT/(M − 1) − ΔT2/2(M − 1)2 − ΔT T̄/(M2 − 1), and the denominator becomes ΔT2/(M − 1) + ΔT2/(M − 1)2 = ΔT2M/(M − 1)2. Taking the ratio, canceling (M − 1)2/ΔT, and multiplying the numerator and denominator by M + 1, we obtain

(59) φ=Tc(M−1)−ΔT2−T‾(M−1)M+1ΔTM =Tc(M2−1)−ΔT2(M+1)−(Tc+ΔT2)(M−1)ΔTM(M+1) =TcM(M−1)−ΔTMΔTM(M+1) =TcM−ThΔT(M+1) ⟹ φmax=TcTh−Tc·M−Th/TcM+1

(The numerator in the second line is a rearrangement of Tc(M2 − 1 − M + 1) − (ΔT/2)·2M, and the last step uses Tc + ΔT = Th.) The first factor is the Carnot coefficient of performance Tc/(Th − Tc). For ZT̄ = 1, cooling from 300 K down to 270 K gives φmax = 1.130 (Carnot 9.00), and cooling down to 250 K gives 0.444 (Carnot 5.00), so widening the temperature difference lowers it sharply. Maximizing Qc/W by varying the current numerically gives the same value.

10.6 The maximum temperature difference

For φmax > 0 we need M > Th/Tc. Squaring both sides and substituting M2 = 1 + Z(Th + Tc)/2, we obtain

(60) 1+Z(Th+Tc)2>Th2Tc2 ⟹Z(Th+Tc)2>(Th−Tc)(Th+Tc)Tc2 ⟹ΔT<ZTc22

The same result can also be obtained directly by setting Qc = 0. From Eq. (56), KΔT = STcI − I2R/2, and the right-hand side is maximal where d/dI = STc − IR = 0, that is, at I = STc/R, so

(61) ΔTmax=1K(S2Tc2R−S2Tc22R) =S2Tc22RK =ZTc22

For example, for ZTc = 1 and Tc = 300 K, ΔTmax = 150 K. Conversely, to cool from the 300 K heat-rejection side down to 250 K (ΔT = 50 K), M > Th/Tc requires ZT̄ > (300/250)2 − 1 = 0.44.

Misconception 7: the efficiency of a real module is lower than Eq. (55) Eqs. (55) and (59) are ideal upper limits. In practice there are contact resistances with the electrodes, heat leakage from the sides of the legs, and the thermal resistance of the substrates. Moreover, S, σ and κ of real materials vary strongly with temperature, so the constant-property model that replaces ZT̄ by the “ZT at the average temperature” is only an approximation.

13. Exercises and Solutions

Problem 1 For a nondegenerate n-type semiconductor, S = −300 μV/K was measured. If the carrier concentration is doubled without changing the temperature or the effective mass, what is S?

Solution. From Eq. (27), |S| = (kB/e)[2 + λ + ln(Nc/n)]. Doubling n decreases ln(Nc/n) by ln 2, so |S| decreases by (kB/e) ln 2 = 86.17 × 0.6931 = 59.7 μV/K, and S = −240 μV/K. This change depends on neither λ nor Nc. (For λ = 0 the initial η is 2 − 300/86.17 = −1.48, which, as cautioned in §5, differs by a few percent from the value of the exact SPB.)

Problem 2 A pair consisting of a p-type leg with Sp = +200 μV/K and an n-type leg with Sn = −200 μV/K is used, and 100 such pairs are connected electrically in series, with a temperature difference of 50 K between the hot side and the cold side. What is the open-circuit voltage?

Solution. The Seebeck coefficient of one pair is Sp − Sn = 400 μV/K, so its electromotive force is 400 μV/K × 50 K = 20 mV. For 100 pairs in series it is 2.0 V. The basic configuration of a thermoelectric module is to connect the pairs thermally in parallel (all pairs receive the same ΔT) and electrically in series.

Problem 3 Take the electrical conductivity of copper to be σ = 5.96×107 S/m and estimate the electronic thermal conductivity at 300 K with the Wiedemann–Franz law.

Solution. From Eq. (21), κe = L0σT = 2.443×10−8 × 5.96×107 × 300 ≈ 437 W/mK. The thermal conductivity of copper at room temperature is known to be about 400 W/mK, which shows that in a metal, most of the heat is carried by electrons.

Problem 4 For a material with S = 200 μV/K, σ = 1000 S/cm and κ = 1.5 W/mK, find the power factor and ZT at 300 K.

Solution. Converting to σ = 105 S/m, S2σ = (2×10−4)2 × 105 = 4×10−3 W/mK2 (= 40 μW/cmK2). From Eq. (1), ZT = 4×10−3 × 300/1.5 = 0.80.

Problem 5 For a device with the hot side at 500 K, the cold side at 300 K and ZT̄ = 1, find the maximum power-generation efficiency and the load ratio at that point. What percentage of the Carnot efficiency is it?

Solution. From Eq. (54), M = √2 = 1.4142, so the load resistance is set to 1.41 times the internal resistance. From Eq. (55), ηmax = (200/500) × (1.4142 − 1)/(1.4142 + 300/500) = 0.4 × 0.4142/2.0142 = 8.23 %. This is 20.6 % of the Carnot efficiency of 40 %.

Problem 6 Taking the room-temperature preset of the simulator as the reference, compare two cases: (a) halving κL, and (b) doubling μ0. What happens to the maximum of ZT and to the optimal carrier concentration in each case?

Solution. By Eq. (39), B ∝ μ0/κL, so both (a) and (b) double B, from 0.442 to 0.883. By Eq. (38), ZT(η) is determined by B alone, so both the maximum of ZT and ηopt are the same in (a) and (b). Furthermore, since m* and T are the same, Nc is also the same, and nopt = Nc(2/√π)F1/2(ηopt) agrees as well. Calculating with the same code as the simulator gives, in both cases, a maximum ZT of 1.756 and nopt = 1.08×1019 cm−3 (the reference values are 1.098 and 1.58×1019 cm−3). In tab ② of the simulator, κL = 0.4 W/mK for (a) can be set exactly. The μ0 slider, however, has a logarithmic scale and cannot be set to exactly 600; at the nearest value, 603 cm2/Vs, the maximum of ZT is displayed as 1.761 (nopt is the same, 1.08×1019 cm−3). Since ηopt decreases as B increases (Table 2), the optimal carrier concentration moves to the lower-concentration side.

Problem 7 If the density-of-states effective mass is doubled while B is kept constant (for example, by multiplying μ0 by 2−3/2), what happens to the maximum of ZT and to nopt?

Solution. Since B is the same, the maximum of ZT and ηopt do not change. In nopt = Nc(2/√π)F1/2(ηopt), Nc ∝ m*3/2, so nopt becomes 23/2 = 2.83 times larger. If you choose “Vary m* and keep B constant” in tab ③ of the simulator, the “ZT max” values in the table agree in all four rows, and you can confirm that nopt moves in proportion to m*3/2.

Problem 8 For a free-electron-like metal (parabolic band, EF = 5 eV, τ independent of energy), show that σ(E) ∝ E3/2, and estimate S at 300 K with Mott’s formula. E is measured from the bottom of the band.

Solution. From Eq. (22), g ∝ E1/2 and v2 ∝ E, and since τ is constant, Eq. (13) gives σ(E) ∝ E3/2 (this corresponds to λ = 1/2 in Eq. (23)). Putting d ln σ/dE = 3/(2EF) into Eq. (20) gives S = −(π2/2)(kB/e)(kBT/EF) = −4.935 × 86.17 μV/K × (0.02585/5) ≈ −2.2 μV/K. Even when the integral of Eq. (15) is evaluated numerically without expanding it, the result is −2.2 μV/K, so Mott’s formula is a good approximation. This is two orders of magnitude smaller than the several hundred μV/K of semiconductors.

A.2 ① Seebeck effect

This tab lets you see the origin of the Seebeck effect, in which a temperature difference produces a voltage, as the motion of charged particles. The physics was explained in §1.1. The key points are the following three.

  • Charged particles on the hot side fluctuate vigorously, and those on the cold side fluctuate gently. The mean kinetic energy of one nondegenerate particle is (3/2)kBT, and the mean square of the speed is proportional to the temperature.
  • The vigorously moving particles on the hot side tend to diffuse toward the cold side, and charge accumulates on the cold side. In n-type material, electrons accumulate on the cold side, which becomes negative, while the positive charge of the donor ions that have released their electrons remains on the hot side, which becomes positive. In p-type material, holes accumulate on the cold side, which becomes positive, and the hot side becomes negative because of the negative charge of the acceptor ions.
  • The electric field created by the accumulated charge pushes the diffusion back, and the imbalance stops growing where the two balance. The potential difference that arises between the two ends in this way is the origin of the Seebeck voltage (the magnitude read by a voltmeter also includes the contribution of the temperature dependence of the Fermi level, as in §1.1).

The order in which to watch the animation. At first it is n-type, with Tc = 300 K, ΔT = 300 K (hot side 600 K), |S| = 200 μV/K and the switch OFF, and it starts moving immediately. The electrons move around in the rod while being scattered. The electrons on the hot side (left) have a darker color (large kinetic energy), move fast and leave long trails, while those on the cold side (right) have a lighter color and move slowly. The vibration of the immobile ions is also drawn larger toward the hot side. Within a few seconds a charge imbalance appears, positive at the end on the hot side and negative at the end on the cold side (the columns of red ⊕ and blue ⊖ just inside the electrodes, and the light red and blue shading of the rod), and the terminal voltage V settles around SΔT = −60 mV. At this point the number of electrons in the rod has hardly changed; only about 0.5 % of the total charge, in very thin layers at the two ends, is imbalanced (see “Model” below). When you turn the switch ON and connect the + side and the − side with an external wire, some of the electrons that have arrived at the electrode on the cold side enter the wire, go around the external circuit and return to the rod from the electrode on the hot side. Since the charge imbalance decreases and the electric field weakens, diffusion from the hot side to the cold side proceeds again in the rod, and the current keeps flowing as long as the temperature difference is maintained. This is thermoelectric power generation: the force with which heat pushes the charged particles out plays the role of the electromotive force of a battery. The current in the external circuit (in the direction of the flow of positive charge) goes from the + side to the − side; in n-type the hot side is +, and in p-type the cold side is + (this is why the current directions in the p-type leg and the n-type leg are opposite in Fig. 3 of the main text). When you turn the switch back OFF, charge accumulates again and the open-circuit state is restored. The ratio of the root-mean-square speeds, √(Th/Tc), is 1.41 by default. Model — units and temperature distribution. From here on we describe what the animation actually calculates (in this A.2 only, x is not the reduced energy but the position along the rod, corresponding to z in §1.1; likewise λ is not the scattering exponent but the screening length, L is not the Lorenz number but the length of the rod, and ℓ is not the leg length of §10 but the mean free path). The calculation is done in units in which the length L of the rod is the unit of length, T0 = 300 K is the unit of temperature, and the carrier mass m, kB and the magnitude of the charge |q| are all set to 1. The unit of time is L/√(kBT0/m), and the unit of potential is kBT0/|q|. The charge of a carrier is q = −|q| for n-type and q = +|q| for p-type. x is measured from the electrode on the hot side (x = 0 is the hot side and x = L is the cold side), and the temperature is assumed to vary linearly along the rod. This is the steady state of heat conduction, and changes of temperature due to the heat carried by the carriers, the Peltier heat and the Joule heat are not considered (passing a current does not change the temperature distribution).

Model — motion and scattering. The carriers are two-dimensional classical particles and move according to the equations of motion above between scatterings (time step Δt = 0.002). They are reflected like a mirror at the upper and lower walls and at the electrodes at the two ends (the aspect ratio of the rod is only a matter of appearance adjusted to the screen and does not affect the results of the calculation). At each step a carrier is scattered with probability Δt/τ = 0.1 (relaxation time τ = 0.02), and when it is scattered its two velocity components are redrawn from the Maxwell distribution at the local temperature T(x) (a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance kBT(x)/m for each component). This represents the exchange of heat with the lattice (phonons): a carrier scattered on the hot side becomes fast, and one scattered on the cold side becomes slow. As a result ⟨v2⟩ ≈ 2kBT(x)/m (two dimensions) at each position in the rod, and vrms ∝ √T. Because the relaxation time does not depend on energy, this corresponds to the case r = 0 of §1.1. The mean free path is ℓ = √(πkBT/2m)·τ ≈ 0.025L (300 K; 0.046L at 1000 K), and the carriers diffuse through the rod while being scattered many times. Because the system is two-dimensional, the mean kinetic energy per particle is kBT, but the readouts display the actual three-dimensional value (3/2)kBT (the speed ratio √(Th/Tc) does not depend on the dimension).

Model — ions and the electric field. The ions do not move and are treated as a background of charge distributed uniformly in the rod in the same number N as the carriers, so the whole is always electrically neutral. The rod is divided into 40 intervals, the carriers are distributed to the centers of the two neighboring intervals according to distance (the cloud-in-cell method), and the charge of interval j is ρj = q(nj − N/40). The electric field is obtained from the one-dimensional Gauss’s law and linearly interpolated to the positions of the particles to calculate the force (the field is not time-averaged; the particles are pushed by its instantaneous value).

dℰdx=ρ(x)ε ,ℰ(0)=ℰ(L)=0 , Vsim=φ(L)−φ(0)=−∫0Lℰdx , λ=εkBTq2n0

The field is ℰ = 0 at both ends because the whole is neutral and there is no electric field outside the rod. Vsim is the potential on the cold side minus the potential on the hot side, in the same direction as the sign convention of this page. The permittivity ε is chosen so that the screening length (Debye length) λ for the carrier density n0 = N/L is λ0 = 0.07L at 300 K, namely ε = λ02N (127.0 in the units above), so that λ(T) = 0.07L√(T/300 K) (0.064L at 250 K, 0.099L at 600 K, and 0.128L at 1000 K). The time for the charge imbalance to be canceled by the electric field (the dielectric relaxation time) is τd = λ2m/(kBTτ) = λ02/τ ≈ 0.25 (independent of temperature; about 0.6 s in real time), and the product of the plasma frequency and the relaxation time is ωpτ = τ/λ0 ≈ 0.29, so the charge imbalance settles without oscillating (overdamping).

Model — balance in open circuit and the electromotive force. When the switch is OFF and enough time has passed, the current becomes 0. Inside the rod the carrier density n is almost constant, so, as with the electrostatic part (a) of §1.1, the thermal pushing force on one carrier, −kBdT/dx, and the electric force qℰ balance, so that

qℰ=kBdTdx ⟹ φ(L)−φ(0) =−kBq(Tc−Th) =kBqΔT

holds. For n-type (q = −e) the potential on the cold side is lower by (kB/e)ΔT, and for p-type it is higher; the sign agrees with the sign of S. In both two and three dimensions the pressure in one direction per particle is m⟨vx2⟩ = kBT, so this result does not depend on the dimension. The charge that creates this electric field gathers, by Gauss’s law, in layers of thickness about λ at the two ends. In n-type, electrons are depleted at the hot end, leaving the positive charge of the donors, and electrons are in excess at the cold end (the reverse for p-type). The interior is almost neutral. The amount of imbalanced charge is about λ02ΔT/T0 of all the carriers, which for the default ΔT = 300 K is only 0.49 % (the equivalent of about 127 out of 25920). A difference of this size cannot be seen in the numbers of the 216 dots that are drawn, so it is shown by the columns of ⊕ and ⊖ inside the electrodes (schematic), the shading of the rod, and the bar graph in the lower figure.

However, ℰ = 0 at the electrodes, so inside the screening layers at the two ends the field is weaker than the interior value ℰb (in the Debye approximation, ℰ ≈ ℰb(1 − e−x/λh) from the hot-side end), and the potential difference between the terminals is c(kB/q)ΔT, that is, multiplied by a coefficient c ≈ 1 − (λh + λc)/L smaller than 1 (λh and λc are the screening lengths on the hot and cold sides). The simulator uses the following coefficient, fitted to numerical experiments, which also includes the kinetic effects near the ends and the coarseness of the intervals.

c=1−0.052−1.046×λ0L (ThT0+TcT0)

By default (600 K and 300 K), c = 0.771 (0.831 from the Debye-approximation formula). In numerical experiments Vsim/[(kB/q)ΔT] is 0.71–0.78 depending on the conditions, and dividing by c gives 0.97–1.00. The screening length of a real thermoelectric semiconductor is of the order of nm (about 1 nm when estimated with the nondegenerate formula for a carrier concentration of 1019 cm−3 and a relative permittivity of 10), which is negligible compared with a sample of mm size, and c can be regarded as 1. In this model the screening length is taken as large as 7 % of the length of the rod, so this correction is needed.

Converting to the on-screen voltage. As the balance above shows, the potential difference in this model is always (kB/e)ΔT × c regardless of the material, that is, c × 86 μV/K in terms of |S|. The real |S| depends on the material through the chemical-potential part (b) of §1.1 and the way the carriers are scattered, and in thermoelectric semiconductors it is much larger than this. So on the screen, the temperature difference ΔTeff calculated back from the potential difference of the particles is multiplied by S, which is the |S| in the controls panel with the sign of the type attached, to give the terminal voltage.

ΔTeff=qkBV‾simc , V=SΔTeff

V̄sim is the exponential moving average of Vsim with a time constant of 1 (2.5 s in real time). In the steady state of the open circuit, ΔTeff ≈ ΔT, so V ≈ SΔT (0.97–1.00 times SΔT in the numerical experiments); when the switch is ON and the charge imbalance decreases, ΔTeff becomes smaller and V goes down. The |S| slider only changes the mV scale on the screen; it does not change the motion of the particles, the charge imbalance or the relative value of the current. The potential φ(x) in the lower figure is also converted to mV with the same scaling (multiplied by |S| × T0/c). The fluctuation of the voltage becomes smaller as the number of simulated carriers N is increased (the thermal noise of the potential between the terminals is of the order of √(kBT/N)/λ in the units above (L = |q| = 1), or (kBT/|q|)√(L/N)/(λ/L) when the dimensions are restored. In numerical experiments with ΔT = 0, the standard deviation of the instantaneous Vsim is about 0.07–0.09 kBT0/|q|). If only the 216 drawn carriers were simulated, the instantaneous fluctuation would become larger than the default signal, so N = 25920 carriers are simulated and every 120th of them, 216 in all, is drawn (every 240th, 108 in all, on screens narrower than 560 px). Even so, the displayed voltage fluctuates by about 11–13 K in standard deviation when converted to ΔT (about 4 % of the default ΔT = 300 K).

The circuit rule. When the switch is ON, for a carrier reflected at an electrode we calculate the drop in potential energy when it moves through the wire to the opposite electrode, and the probability that it enters the wire:

u=q(φown electrode−φopposite electrode)kBT0 , probability of entering the wire=min(1,Gu)(u>0 only)

(The potentials are averaged with a time constant of 0.02.) Only when u > 0 is the carrier let into the wire, with this probability. The same rule is applied at both ends, and the direction of the current is not set by hand but is determined by the potential created by the particles. Since the number passing through the wire is proportional to the potential difference, this corresponds to Ohm’s law, and G plays the role of the ease with which the load conducts (the conductance). The light bulb has G = 0.08, and “wire (nearly a short circuit)” has G = 3. A carrier that has entered the wire returns to the rod from the electrode on the opposite side at that very instant (a metal wire is full of electrons, so it is treated as if the amount that enters at one end comes out at the other). The velocity on return is generated at the temperature of that electrode: the component pointing away from the electrode is drawn from a flux-weighted half-Maxwell distribution, and the transverse component from a Maxwell distribution. The number of carriers staying in the wire is always 0, and the total number of carriers in the rod, N, does not change.

Why the rule is “enter only when the potential falls” If, without looking at the potential, we let a carrier that hits an electrode enter the wire with a fixed probability, the current would flow in the wrong direction. This is because the number of hits on an electrode is proportional to n√T, so the electrode on the hot side is hit more often (the same as the Knudsen effusion in which a gas leaks out through a small hole). What drives the current in a real circuit is the potential difference between the two ends of the wire, so the rule has been made to use that potential difference.

The direction and magnitude of the current. The current I is the net number of carriers that moved through the rod from the hot side to the cold side (per unit time, averaged with a time constant of 1), divided by 150 to give a relative value. 150 is a unit chosen so that the value comes out close to 1 when the rod is short-circuited at ΔT = 100 K (the theoretical value of the short-circuit current obtained from the balance of the carrier flux is NτkBΔT/(mT0L2) = 173, and the numerical experiments give 153–159). The directions are as follows.

  • n-type: in the rod the electrons move from the hot side to the cold side, enter the wire at the electrode on the cold side, and go around the external circuit from the cold side to the hot side. The current (the flow of positive charge) is in the opposite direction, from the hot side (+) to the cold side (−) in the external circuit. The blue dots in the wire move from the cold side to the hot side.
  • p-type: in the rod the holes move from the hot side to the cold side, and the current in the external circuit goes from the cold side (+) to the hot side (−). The electrons in the wire move from the hot side to the cold side.

In the numerical experiments (Tc = 300 K), connecting the light bulb lowers the terminal voltage to about 0.43 times the open-circuit value, and the relative value of the current is 0.60, 1.19, 1.83 (default) and 2.42 for ΔT = 100, 200, 300 and 400 K (proportional to ΔT). With “wire”, the terminal voltage is about 0.02 times the open-circuit value, that is, nearly 0, and the current is 1.02, 2.09, 3.08 and 4.13 for ΔT = 100, 200, 300 and 400 K. The 0.43 for the light bulb and 0.60/1.02 ≈ 0.59 add up to almost 1 because the rod behaves as “a battery with electromotive force SΔT and an internal resistance”, so the terminal voltage falls linearly as SΔT − IRint. The brightness of the light bulb is made proportional to the power |IV|. When the switch is turned back OFF, the terminal voltage returns to the open-circuit value to within 1 %.

The animation is a schematic — what this model does not include A gas of two-dimensional classical particles, with neither Fermi statistics nor band structure (close to the electrons and holes of a nondegenerate semiconductor). The relaxation time is a constant independent of energy. What is calculated as the voltage is only the difference in electrostatic potential (the next box); the potential difference that comes out of the particles is c × 86 μV/K × ΔT regardless of the material, and the mV values on the screen are this rescaled by |S| in the controls panel (they are not the difference in electrochemical potential itself that a voltmeter actually measures). The screening length is taken to be much larger than in a real sample, and the coefficient c corrects for this. The temperature distribution is fixed, and the temperature does not change through Peltier heat or Joule heat even when a current flows. The motion in the wire is instantaneous, and the current and the brightness of the light bulb are relative values. The vibration of the ions is purely for visual effect.
What the motion of the particles shows is only a part of the voltage The part of the animation that shows “hot particles being pushed out and balanced by the electric field” corresponds to the (a) electrostatic part of §1.1 (the difference in electrostatic potential). The Seebeck voltage read by a voltmeter has, in addition to this, the (b) chemical-potential part (the temperature change of the Fermi level), and in a nondegenerate semiconductor (b) is in many cases the larger one ((a) : (b) = 1 : 3.5 in the example of §1.1). To find the magnitude of |S| correctly, the Boltzmann equation of §3–§5 is needed, and tabs ②–④ use its results.
Sign convention Potential on the cold side − potential on the hot side = SΔT (Eq. (2)). In n-type, electrons accumulate on the cold side and make it negative, so S < 0; in p-type, holes accumulate and make the cold side positive, so S > 0. It was confirmed in §1.1 that this is the same convention as S = −ΔV/ΔT in the lecture slides. Just by measuring the sign, you can tell the carrier type, so this is routinely used in the evaluation of oxide semiconductors.

Rough values of |S|. Metals have |S| ≈ a few μV/K, good thermoelectric semiconductors have 150–250 μV/K, and semiconductors close to insulators have several hundred μV/K or more. For example, a material with |S| = 200 μV/K and ΔT = 300 K (the default of ①) gives an electromotive force of 60 mV, whereas a metal (|S| = 5 μV/K) gives only 1.5 mV for the same ΔT. The reason why |S| of a metal is small is given at the end of §1.1 and in Mott’s formula in §4, and how |S| of a semiconductor decreases with the carrier concentration is explained by the Pisarenko relation in §5.

Thermocouples. A thermocouple is a thermometer that joins two materials A and B and measures the electromotive force V = (SA − SB)ΔT. If a circuit were made of only one material, the same temperature difference would also act on the wires leading to the voltmeter and the electromotive forces would cancel, so two materials with different S are combined. Most temperature measurements in industrial furnaces rely on this principle. The p–n pair of Problem 2 in §13 is also the same addition (Sp − Sn).

Viewing it as the difference between Fermi–Dirac distributions. The same thing can also be seen as the difference between the Fermi–Dirac distributions on the hot and cold sides (Fig. A1). For easy comparison, the figure puts the Fermi levels EF of the two sides together at the origin of the horizontal axis (in a real sample, EF − Ec itself also changes with temperature, as in the (b) chemical-potential part of §1.1).

Fig. A1. The Fermi–Dirac distribution f(E) = 1/[e(E − EF)/kBT + 1] on the hot side (red, Th = 400 K, kBT = 34.5 meV) and the cold side (blue, Tc = 300 K, kBT = 25.9 meV). The horizontal axis is the energy measured from the Fermi level (meV). The red fill is the part above EF that is more occupied on the hot side, and the blue fill is the part below EF that is more empty on the hot side; the two areas are equal.

  • The distribution on the hot side (red) is gentler around EF. That is, on the hot side, states at high energies above EF are also occupied, and these fast carriers diffuse toward the cold side. In a nondegenerate semiconductor, EF lies in the gap and there are no conduction-band states below EF (the range of the blue fill), so only the upper side counts. As a result, charge accumulates on the cold side and a potential difference arises.
  • The magnitude of S is a measure of “the entropy (heat) carried by one carrier” (Eq. (16) of §3.4: S is the average of E − EF carried by the carriers in the transport window, divided by eT). The farther EF is from the conduction band bottom (the fewer the carriers), the larger the energy that one carrier carries measured from EF becomes compared with kBT, and the larger |S| becomes (§5, Fig. 1 left).
  • In metals the parts above and below EF cancel, so |S| is only a few μV/K. However, the areas of the red fill and the blue fill in Fig. A1 are equal at any temperature for any substance, so this alone is not the reason for the cancellation. The cancellation occurs in a metal because there are states both above and below EF and σ(E) (the number of states and the speed) is almost the same on both sides. In a semiconductor there are no states below EF (in the gap), so there is no cancellation (§1.1, §4, Fig. 1 right). The dependence of S, σ and κ on the carrier concentration is examined quantitatively in ②.

  • Lattice thermal conductivity κL (×2, 1, 1/2, 1/4)

What to read off from the figure for each option. The “message” shows only the one bold sentence of each of the following items and a short supplement.

  • Effective mass m*: When m* is increased, the peak moves to the higher-concentration side and, at the same time, becomes higher. Because the density of states becomes larger, EF is farther from the conduction band bottom even at the same n, and S is kept large (since μ0 is not changed, B rises as B ∝ m*3/2). However, if a single valley is simply made heavier, the mobility also drops. With acoustic-phonon scattering μ0 ∝ m*−5/2, so B ∝ 1/m* and it actually goes down (the μ0 ∝ m*−3/2 of the next option is an artificial case chosen so that B is exactly constant). A realistic means of increasing Nc without lowering μ0 is to increase the number of equivalent valleys Nv (band degeneracy). Think of the operation in this option of raising m* without changing μ0 as the operation of increasing that Nv (§7.4).
  • Vary m* and keep B constant: If B is held constant, the height of the peak stays exactly the same and only its position moves, in proportion to m*3/2. Check that the ZT max in the table agrees in all four rows. In the SPB model the maximum of ZT is determined by the quality factor B alone. This is why one is told to “increase B” in the search for materials (§7.1, Problem 7 of §13).
  • Lattice thermal conductivity κL: Lowering κL makes the peak higher, and the top moves a little to the lower-concentration side. The smaller κL is, the more the relative weight of κe increases, so fewer carriers are advantageous in order not to increase κe (in Table 2, the larger B is, the lower ηopt is). We look at ways of lowering κL in ④.
  • Mobility μ0: Raising the mobility makes the peak higher, and the top moves to the lower-concentration side. Because the same σ can be obtained with fewer carriers, S can be kept high (Problem 6 of §13).
  • Temperature T: Raising the temperature increases ZT. Here μ0 ∝ T−3/2 and κL ∝ 1/T are assumed, so that B ∝ T3/2·μ0·T/κL ∝ T3/2·T−3/2·T·T = T2 in Eq. (39). It is not only that T multiplies in the definition of ZT; the quality factor B itself grows as T2, and this is what counts. With the room-temperature preset as the reference, B in the table is 0.442 at 300 K, 1.226 at 500 K, 2.404 at 700 K and 3.974 at 900 K, that is, 9 times as large at 900 K. In real materials, minority carriers are excited at high temperatures (the bipolar effect), S drops and ZT levels off, but this is not included in the model (“Misconception 5” of §8).

A.5 ④ Reducing κ (PGEC)

This tab draws how the maximum of ZT changes as the grains of a polycrystal are made smaller. The idea is in §9 and the equation used for the calculation is Eq. (62) of §11.

PGEC (Phonon Glass, Electron Crystal) The ideal material is disordered like a glass for phonons and ordered like a crystal for electrons (Slack, Reference 8). At the carrier concentrations of 1019–1020 cm−3 used by thermoelectric materials, the mean free path of phonons is often longer than that of electrons, so if interfaces of an intermediate size between the two are introduced, only the heat can be stopped selectively. However, no matter how much disorder is introduced, κL is hard to lower below the value κmin of an amorphous material (glass) (the minimum thermal conductivity, Eq. (46) of §9.3, Reference 6).

The figure “Smaller grains block the heat first”. The horizontal axis is the grain size d (log scale, 5 nm to 1 mm). The thick green line is the maximum of ZT (left axis) when the optimal carrier concentration is taken at each grain size, drawn by scanning the grain size at 91 points. The teal dashed line is κL divided by its original value (the κL in the controls panel), and the red dotted line is μ0 divided by its original value (both on the right axis, “Ratio to bulk”). The gray dotted line and the green dot are the grain size chosen in the controls panel and the maximum of ZT there.

Controls panel.

The message box, and what to read off from the figure. When the grain size is reduced to about Λph, phonons are scattered at grain boundaries and κL decreases. The “message” briefly tells which of the following four cases the settings fall under. The meaning of each case is as follows.

  • When there is a window (Λe < Λph, and ZT rises when the grains are made smaller): the electron mean free path Λe is shorter than this, so the mobility has hardly dropped yet. Within this “window” ZT rises. If the grain size is reduced further, κL approaches the lower limit κmin and no longer decreases, and only the mobility keeps falling, so ZT goes down. Lowering κmin moves the position of the peak toward smaller grain sizes. Please also check that if, with the default values Λph = 50 nm, Λe = 5 nm and factor 1.00, κmin is lowered to 0.05 W/mK, the optimal grain size reaches the left edge of the graph (5 nm) (ZT 2.42), and the descending slope on the left side of the peak can no longer be seen (ZT keeps rising up to the left edge).
  • When Λe ≥ Λph: the electron mean free path is as long as Λph or longer, so at grain boundaries electrons are scattered as much as, or more than, phonons. With these settings there is no window, and making the grains smaller does not raise ZT. Use these settings to check that PGEC holds only when Λe < Λph.
  • When κmin is equal to or larger than the original κL (aκL after alloying): even if the grains are made smaller, κL no longer decreases and only the mobility falls, so there is no window. Lowering κmin makes a window appear (if aκL after alloying is 0.05 W/mK or less and κmin cannot be lowered below it, raising κL or the factor from alloying makes a window appear).
  • Otherwise, when there is no window: Λe is shorter than Λph, but Λe is close to Λph or κmin is close to the original κL, so the loss from the lower mobility is larger than the gain from the lower κL. Lowering κmin (and, if Λe is close to Λph, shortening Λe as well) makes a window appear.
A caution on the gray approximation The grain-size dependence in ④ is merely the gray approximation, which represents the mean free path by a single value, with the lower limit κmin of the amorphous limit added. The mean free paths of real phonons are widely distributed from nm to μm, and representing them by a single Λ tends to overestimate the effect (“Misconception 6” of §9.3). Also, solid-solution alloying (mass fluctuation) scatters mainly short-wavelength phonons, nanoprecipitates scatter medium-wavelength ones, and the grain boundaries varied in ④ scatter long-wavelength ones, so combining scatterers of different sizes hierarchically is effective (Reference 13). Here we look at the heat conduction by phonons, seen in Lecture 10 (thermal properties), from the side of “how to stop it”.

A.8 The calculation model and cautions

All the figures and numbers of the simulator are calculated on the spot by the JavaScript of the page; they are not curves prepared in advance. However, this model is “for understanding trends” and does not quantitatively predict the ZT of a specific substance. The details of the implementation (the scan in η, the Fermi–Dirac integrals, the equations for the transport coefficients, how the top of the peak is found, the temperature options of ③, Eq. (62) of ④, and the minimum ZT of ⑤) are collected in §11, so here only the main points and what is not written in §11 are given.

  • The model of ②, ③ and ④. They are calculated with the single parabolic band (SPB) model and acoustic-phonon scattering (relaxation time τ ∝ E−1/2). The Fermi–Dirac integrals Fj(η) are integrated numerically with Simpson’s rule (only F0 is analytic), and the reduced Fermi level η is scanned from −10 to 50, to obtain the carrier concentration, S, the Lorenz number, the mobility, σ, κe and ZT (§11). Since Fj does not depend on the parameters, it is calculated only once on the grid of η, stored, and reused at every operation.
  • Checking the two limits. It has been confirmed that the model reproduces S = (kB/e)(2 − η) in the nondegenerate limit and the two limits of the Lorenz number, 2(kB/e)2 and (π2/3)(kB/e)2 (Table 1).
  • Units and constants. B of Eq. (39) is calculated in SI units; m* is entered with me = 9.1093837×10−31 kg, and μ0 is converted from cm2/Vs to m2/Vs. kB, e and h are the defined values of the SI.
  • The animation of ①. It is a schematic for seeing the picture of §1.1 with your own eyes and is independent of the calculation of the transport coefficients in ②–④. The contents of the model and its constants are collected in A.2 (Table A2).
  • The efficiency and cooling of ⑤. Eqs. (55) and (59) are drawn at 201 points of the average ZT from 0 to 4. Because it is a constant-property model, contact resistance, heat leaks and the temperature dependence of the properties are not included (“Misconception 7” of §10).
  • The table of ⑥. It is not a calculation but a list of textbook-level guides (A.7).
What this model cannot tell us Real materials have multiple valleys (band degeneracy), non-parabolic bands, multiple scattering mechanisms, and a contribution from minority carriers (the bipolar effect). The presets are educational values that give the order of magnitude and do not reproduce the measured ZT of a specific substance. The grain-size dependence of ④ is merely the gray approximation with the lower limit κmin added (A.5), and the ZT of ⑥ is a textbook-level guide that varies with composition and from paper to paper. For the whole of what can and cannot be said, see the box in §11.
