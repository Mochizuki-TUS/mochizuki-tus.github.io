AI Job Survival Score

Starting from what AI is good and bad at, this page defines seven criteria and scores 60 occupations from 1 to 5 points on each. The higher the score, the harder the work is for AI or robots to replace, and the more likely it is to remain as a job. The criteria fall into two groups: the “social barrier,” where people and institutions demand a human, and the “technical barrier,” where AI and robots are still weak.

Conclusions

Jobs that last combine body, heart, and responsibility

The top ranks go to surgeons, nurses, firefighters and paramedics, childcare workers, and politicians. The more a job requires physical work at a different site every time, closeness to people, and responsibility for the outcome all at once, the harder it is for either AI or robots to replace.

The most at risk are jobs where both input and output are entirely digital

The bottom ranks go to general office clerks, accounting clerks, bank tellers, translators, writers, and programmers. Creative jobs that forecasts a decade ago considered safe also land near the bottom, because they live in the digital space that generative AI can handle directly.

Jobs remain for two kinds of reasons: the “technical barrier” and the “social barrier”

Craft and on-site jobs remain because robots are still clumsy at them. Licensed professions remain because of responsibility and institutions. The technical barrier gets lower year by year, while the social barrier changes with laws and values.

“Reviewers remain,” but fewer are needed

Checking is finished faster than making something yourself. If reviewing an AI draft takes 1/4 of the time of doing it by hand, then even if demand grows 1.5 times, only about 40% of today's headcount is needed.

Robustness: even when the weight of each criterion is randomly varied between 0.5 and 2 times, a ±1-point error is added to the scores, and everything is recomputed 20,000 times, the 12 top occupations with 26 points or more land in the top third with a probability of about 90% or higher, and the 14 bottom occupations with 12 points or less land in the bottom third with a probability of about 95% or higher.

Weights

The multiplier applied to each criterion's score. Setting it to 0 ignores that criterion.

Discussion

Proposed characteristic 1, “Reviewers are needed”: correct, but fewer are needed

Because generative AI chooses the next word probabilistically, it cannot reduce errors (hallucinations) to zero. A role in which someone checks the output and takes responsibility will therefore remain. However, checking is finished faster than making something yourself, so for the same amount of work, fewer people are needed.

Required headcount (ratio, with today = 1) ≈ fraction of time done by humans × growth in demand
Example: 0.25 (checking takes 1/4 of the time of doing it by hand) × 1.5 (demand grows 1.5 times as it gets cheaper) ≈ 0.38

In some fields, such as testing programs, the verification itself can be automated. Review work remains strongest where someone must legally bear responsibility for errors. For this reason, criterion ① was scored to include not only review but also final responsibility.

Proposed characteristic 2, “Emotion is hard to reproduce”: the barrier is being an actual participant, rather than expression

When limited to written exchanges, AI is already quite good at expressing empathy. In a study in which health professionals compared answers to patients' questions, the share of answers rated “empathetic” was 4.6% for physicians versus 45.1% for the chatbot (Ayers et al., 2023). What is hard to replace is not the expression of emotion but being physically present at someone's side, together with the recipient's wish to have a human stay close to them. For this reason, relationships within the work were scored separately as ②, and the value of the work being done by a human as ⑦.

Proposed characteristic 3, “Tacit knowledge is hard for physical AI to reproduce”: a strong barrier, but not a permanent one

Craft and on-site jobs that score high on ③ tacit knowledge and ④ physical skill did in fact reach the top ranks. However, the real nature of this barrier is not that these skills “cannot be learned in principle” but that “the cost of collecting experiential knowledge as data and the cost of the robot hardware are high.” As research on general-purpose robots advances, the barrier will gradually come down. In the “Rapid physical AI” scenario, electricians, hairdressers, farmers, and others drop sharply in rank, while jobs protected by the social barrier, such as psychological counselors and executives, move up.

How far can the three proposed characteristics alone explain the results?

Even when scoring with ①–④ only, the rank correlation with the seven-criterion model was ρ = 0.97. The broad picture can be explained by the three proposed characteristics. The occupations whose rank moves substantially with the added criteria ⑤–⑦ are politicians, religious leaders, and traditional craftspeople (rising because of ⑦), and factory, warehouse, and cleaning jobs (rising because of ⑥). For the latter, ⑥ is a barrier against generative AI but not against industrial robots, so they may be overrated.

The forecast reversed within a decade

In 2013, Frey & Osborne estimated that about 47% of US employment could technically be replaced, and in 2015 the Nomura Research Institute estimated the same for about 49% of Japan's working population. Both rated routine clerical and manufacturing jobs as high-risk and jobs that require creativity or interpersonal skills as safe. The Nomura Research Institute's “100 occupations with low substitutability” even included graphic designers, copywriters, manga artists, game creators, and composers.

However, studies after the arrival of generative AI keep finding that the more highly educated the white-collar job, such as translation, writing, and programming, the greater the impact (Eloundou et al., 2023; Tomlinson et al., 2025). In US payroll data, employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in occupations exposed to AI is lower than it would have been had it moved at the same pace as in less-exposed occupations, and the gap has widened from 13% in the first version (August 2025) to 19% in the latest revision (August 2026) (Brynjolfsson et al.; descriptive results, not causal).

Matching Frey & Osborne's computerization probabilities with this model for 27 occupations gives a rank correlation of ρ = 0.60. The broad pattern that clerical work is at risk while healthcare and education are safe agrees; the disagreements are digital creative occupations and on-site skilled trades.

There are also examples in the opposite direction. In 2016, Geoffrey Hinton said that training radiologists should stop now, but since then the number of radiologists at the Mayo Clinic has grown 55% while they use more than 250 AI models (New York Times, 2025). When things become cheaper and faster, demand itself can grow, so even in jobs that AI does well, the number of people does not necessarily fall.

Limitations of this model

  • The scoring is subjective. The scores for 60 occupations × 7 criteria are a tentative proposal by Claude. Re-scoring based on the task descriptions of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's occupational information site (job tag, in Japanese) or the US O*NET would make it more objective.
  • Adding up scores underestimates a “single decisive barrier.” Professional shogi players score 16 points and sit toward the bottom, but even after shogi software beat the reigning Meijin (the top title holder) in two straight games in 2017, the profession has flourished thanks to the single criterion ⑦ (the value of a contest between humans).
  • “Does the occupation remain?” and “Is the same number of people still needed?” are separate questions. This model does not take growth in demand into account.
  • ④ and ⑥ are highly correlated (r = 0.82), which slightly favors jobs that use the body. If this concerns you, lower the weight of ⑥.
  • The model has not been calibrated to probabilities. The survival index is a measure of relative rank. It could be calibrated to probabilities by regressing on the actual changes in the number of workers by occupation.
  • The assumed time horizon is around 2035. Anthropic's CEO has warned that half of entry-level white-collar jobs could be lost within one to five years (Axios, 2025). If progress is that fast, the lower-ranked occupations will be affected sooner. Abrupt advances such as artificial general intelligence are outside what is assumed here.
