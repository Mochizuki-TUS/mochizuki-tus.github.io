How Does a Binary Phase Diagram Arise from Free Energy?

A phase diagram is not a collection of boundary lines drawn empirically. The starting point is the Gibbs free energy G(x,T,p) of each phase. Searching for the lowest free energy at each temperature determines the phase boundaries, and adding interfacial energy and the rate of mass transport to this takes us on to microstructure formation by the phase-field method. On this page we follow this single line of reasoning through equations and figures you can operate. We also cover the glass transition and crystallization (§7), in which the cooling rate decides between crystal and glass, and spinodal decomposition (§9), in which waves of composition grow without the formation of nuclei.

A phase diagram is the “destination”; a microstructure calculation is “the road to get there” What a phase diagram tells you is the equilibrium phases, equilibrium compositions, and phase fractions after a sufficiently long time. The size, shape, and arrangement of grains, and the time a change takes, are not determined by the phase diagram alone. For these you need kinetics: interfacial energy, diffusion coefficients, nucleation, elastic strain, anisotropy, and so on.

1. Why Look for the Minimum of G?

In a system whose temperature T and pressure p are held constant by the surroundings, a spontaneous change proceeds in the direction that lowers the total Gibbs free energy. When a material of composition x0 splits into an α phase and a β phase, the total free energy per 1 mol is

G=fα Gα(xα,T) +fβ Gβ(xβ,T) (1)

Here, the phase fractions and the mass balance of component B must always be satisfied:

fα+fβ=1 , x0= fαxα +fβxβ (2)

The equilibrium problem is therefore the problem of finding the values of fα, fβ, xα, xβ that minimize Eq. (1) under the constraints of Eq. (2). This minimization will later become visible geometrically as the common tangent.

2. What Determines the Temperature Dependence of the Free Energy?

2.1 The slope is the entropy, and the curvature is the heat capacity

For 1 mol of a single phase, the total differential of the Gibbs free energy is

dG=Vdp −SdT +∑iμidni (3)

If the pressure and the amount of substance are fixed, it follows immediately that

(∂G∂T)p =−S , (∂2G∂T2)p =−CpT (4)

In other words, the slope of the G–T curve is −S, and its curvature is −Cp/T. A liquid usually has a larger entropy than a solid, so the G of the liquid falls steeply with temperature, crosses the G of the solid at the melting point, and lies below that of the solid at higher temperatures.

2.2 Integrating the measured Cp gives G(T)

If H and S at a reference temperature T0 are known, then

H(T)=H(T0) +∫T0TCpdT S(T)=S(T0) +∫T0TCpTdT G(T)=H(T)−TS(T) (5)

gives G(T). If a phase transition occurs along the way, add the transition enthalpy ΔHtr to H and a jump of ΔHtr/Ttr to S. The pure-substance databases of CALPHAD store this thermochemical information as analytical expressions for each phase.

2.3 Melting as an example

Define ΔGfus = Gliquid − Gsolid. At the melting point Tm the two phases are in equilibrium, so ΔGfus(Tm) = 0, and ΔSfus(Tm) = ΔHm/Tm. If the difference in heat capacity between solid and liquid, ΔCp, is regarded as constant, integration gives

ΔGfus(T)= ΔHm(1−TTm) +ΔCp [(T−Tm) −TlnTTm] (6)

That is Eq. (6). The corresponding phase diagram simulator, to keep the mechanism easy to follow, sets ΔCp = 0 and uses the following linear expression.

ΔGfus(T)= ΔHm(1−TTm) (7) Pure-substance model of the simulator

For example, Al has Tm = 933.5 K and ΔHm = 10.711 kJ/mol. By Eq. (7), ΔGfus = +1.53 kJ/mol at 800 K, so the solid is stable; at 1000 K it is −0.763 kJ/mol, so the liquid is stable. The sign reverses at the melting point.

The linear expression is an “entry point near the melting point,” not a universal formula In reality Cp differs between solid and liquid, and magnetic transitions, structural phase transitions, and electronic excitations also contribute to G(T). 3. From a Pure Substance to a Binary Solution

Suppose that components A and B mix substitutionally in a phase φ. Letting x be the mole fraction of B, the free energy per 1 mol can be divided into the “end members,” “ideal mixing,” and “deviation from ideality.”

Gφ(x,T)= (1−x)gAφ(T) +xgBφ(T) +RT[(1−x)ln(1−x) +xlnx] +Gexφ (8)

3.1 The ideal mixing term comes from the “number of arrangements”

The number of ways of placing NA A atoms and NB B atoms on N lattice sites is W = N!/(NA!NB!). From S = kB ln W and Stirling’s approximation we obtain

ΔSmix= −R[(1−x)ln(1−x) +xlnx] (9)

For 0 < x < 1, ΔSmix > 0, so −TΔSmix always stabilizes mixing. Moreover, since its coefficient is T, the temperature effect that the higher the temperature, the more readily the components mix is built in naturally.

3.2 Expressing the reluctance to mix with a Redlich–Kister polynomial

In the phase diagram simulator, the deviation from an ideal solution is expressed as

Gexφ= x(1−x) [L0+L1(1−2x) +L2(1−2x)2] (10)

The simplest regular solution has L0 = Ω, L1 = L2 = 0. Ω > 0 is the case in which the energy rises when unlike atoms A–B are neighbors, which promotes phase separation. Ω < 0 promotes mixing or ordering. L1 and the higher terms express the left–right asymmetry of the curve.

In practical CALPHAD the coefficients are also functions of temperature Not only gAφ(T) and gBφ(T) but also Lkφ(T) may have the form a + bT + cT ln T + … Evaluating these coefficients from thermochemical data and phase equilibrium data is the CALPHAD method. The present simulator is a simplified teaching model and is not a CALPHAD thermodynamic assessment.

4. The Common Tangent — What It Means for the Chemical Potentials to Be Equal

Minimizing Eq. (1) subject to Eq. (2) by the method of Lagrange multipliers shows that, between coexisting α and β phases, the chemical potential of each component is equal.

μAα=μAβ , μBα=μBβ (11)

Using the tangent to the molar free energy G(x), we have

μA=G−xdGdx , μB=G+(1−x) dGdx (12)

If the slope and the intercept of the tangent are the same, Eq. (11) is satisfied simultaneously. Therefore the points where one and the same straight line touches two curves give the compositions of the two coexisting phases. The same holds when the line touches a single curve at two points; this is the case in which the same crystalline phase separates into two phases of different compositions.

4.1 The lever rule is nothing but the mass balance

Solving Eq. (2) for the phase fractions gives

fα= xβ−x0 xβ−xα , fβ= x0−xα xβ−xα (13)

For example, if xα = 0.20, xβ = 0.80, and the overall composition is x0 = 0.35, then fα = 0.75 and fβ = 0.25. The phase composition and the phase fraction are different quantities.

4.2 The binodal and the spinodal are not the same

For the regular solution Gmix = RT[x ln x + (1−x)ln(1−x)] + Ωx(1−x), the local stability is determined by the curvature.

d2Gdx2 =RTx(1−x) −2Ω (14)
  • Binodal (coexistence curve): the points of tangency of the common tangent. Beyond this point, if a nucleus of finite amplitude forms, separating into two phases gives a lower G.
  • Spinodal: d²G/dx² = 0. Inside it, a tiny composition fluctuation alone lowers G, and a continuous modulation of composition grows with no nucleation barrier.

The critical temperature of a symmetric regular solution is Tc = Ω/(2R), and the critical composition is x = 1/2. In Fig. 2, if you move T above Tc, the curve becomes convex everywhere and the common tangent disappears. How the separation proceeds inside the spinodal (which wavelengths of fluctuation grow) is derived in §9.

5. Stacking the Common Tangents of Each Temperature Gives the Phase Diagram

At a given temperature, draw the common tangent to the liquid GL(x) and the solid Gα(x), and record the points of tangency xL and xα. Repeating the same operation while changing the temperature slightly, xL(T) becomes the liquidus and xα(T) becomes the solidus.

As a rough guide, if Ω is negative for both liquid and solid the diagram is of the complete-solid-solution type; if it is positive for both, of the two-liquid-separation (monotectic) type; and if it is negative for the liquid and positive for the solid, of the eutectic type (Toshihiro Tanaka, “Alloys and Equilibrium Phase Diagrams” (in Japanese), Journal of the Japan Welding Society 77 (6), 576–581, 2008). However, the monotectic type arises only when Ω of the liquid is large enough that the critical temperature of liquid-phase separation (ΩL/2R for a regular solution) rises above the liquidus. If Ω is smaller than that, the separation of the liquid is hidden below the liquidus (metastable), and the diagram becomes eutectic or peritectic. If ΩL is larger still, a syntectic reaction (L1 + L2 → α), in which a solid forms from the two liquids, also appears. In addition, in a complete-solid-solution diagram with no three-phase reaction, if ΩL − ΩS is larger than ΔS(TB − TA) (entropy of fusion × difference between the melting points at the two ends; TB > TA, and the entropy of fusion is assumed to be the same at both ends), the liquidus and solidus have a maximum, and if it is smaller than −ΔS(TB − TA), they have a minimum; in between, a lens shape with no extremum results. Real, complicated phase diagrams, too, can be read as combinations of these basic forms and intermediate phases (compounds). With “Vary Ω (Fig. 3)” in simulator ②, you can check the 20 combinations and the free energy curves.
Whether metal or oxide, the same Ω determines the type The corresponding simulator lets you choose, side by side, metallic systems such as Cu–Ni and Ag–Cu and ceramic systems such as NiO–MgO, CaO–MgO, and Al2O3–Cr2O3. NiO–MgO is a complete solid solution and CaO–MgO is eutectic, yet both have the rock-salt structure. The crystal structure is not what separates the types. This is because the difference in cation radii produces a strain energy in the solid solution, and this strain energy determines the magnitude of L0 = Ω in Eq. (10). The larger Ω is, the more the G–x curve of the solid bulges up in the middle, and a single solid solution can no longer maintain a lower convex hull. The metals Cu–Ni (complete solid solution) and Ag–Cu (eutectic) show exactly the same contrast.

For a binary system at constant pressure, Gibbs’ phase rule gives F = C − P + 1 = 3 − P. With three phases coexisting, F = 0, so the eutectic and peritectic temperatures and the phase compositions cannot be moved freely. This is why they appear on a phase diagram as horizontal invariant reactions.

Reading the Pb–Sn eutectic in the language of free energy At 183 °C and 61.9 mass% Sn (about 73.9 mol% Sn), the liquid L changes simultaneously into α (Pb-rich) and β (Sn-rich). At this temperature a single tangent touches GL, Gα, and Gβ, and the chemical potentials of Pb and of Sn each coincide in the three phases.

5.1 If cooling is fast, the lever rule can no longer be used

So far we have assumed that the solid and liquid reach complete equilibrium at every temperature. In real solidification, however, diffusion in the solid is far slower, and the composition of the portion that solidified first remains frozen in. So, as the opposite limit, we make three assumptions: no diffusion occurs at all inside the solid, the liquid is always uniform, and local equilibrium holds only at the solid–liquid interface. When the solid fraction fS increases by dfS, the solute balance is

(CL− CS) dfS= (1−fS) dCL Scheil–Gulliver equation (differential form)

Here CL is the composition of the liquid and CS is the composition of the solid in equilibrium with it at the interface. Integrating this along the liquidus and solidus, two things happen. One is microsegregation (coring): the center, which solidified first, is rich in the high-melting-point component, and layers pile up in which the later-solidified outer regions are progressively richer in the low-melting-point component. The other is the nonequilibrium eutectic: in a system with a eutectic, since the liquid keeps getting richer in the low-melting-point component, even a composition that would end as a single phase in equilibrium finally reaches the eutectic temperature, and eutectic structure remains. Even in a complete-solid-solution system with no eutectic, the apparent solidus falls below the equilibrium solidus. Equilibrium cooling and Scheil are both extreme idealizations, and real solidification always falls between them. Homogenization heat treatment of cast material is done to smooth out these layers by diffusion.

5.2 Microstructure on slow cooling — primary crystals, eutectic structure, peritectic reaction, and precipitation

What a phase diagram tells you is the phases in equilibrium at each temperature and their proportions. The microstructure seen under a microscope adds to that a history: in what order those phases formed. Figs. 2-7 (eutectic system) and 2-8 (peritectic system) of Understanding Inorganic Materials Chemistry (in Japanese) depict how the microstructure changes when a melt is cooled slowly. “On cooling” in simulator ① and the equilibrium cooling in ③ reproduce this way of drawing by calculation. The numerical examples below are the values for the simulator’s “A–B” schematic systems shaped like textbook Figs. 2-7 and 2-8 (D = 18 %, E = 55 %, F = 84 %; for the peritectic system, α end 10 %, P ≈ 50.83 %, end of the liquid ≈ 84.33 %).

(a) Primary crystals and eutectic structure. When a melt of composition X (hypoeutectic), on the A side of the eutectic point E and on the B side of D, is cooled, primary α crystallizes in the liquid where the liquidus is crossed and grows as the temperature falls. At the eutectic temperature TE, the remaining liquid (composition xE) turns simultaneously into α (xD) and β (xF), forming a eutectic structure in which the two phases alternate. The primary crystals remain as they are, so the proportions of the two constituents that can be told apart in the microstructure are determined by the lever rule just above TE.

fprimary= xE−X xE−xD , feutectic= X−xD xE−xD , fαin eutectic= xF−xE xF−xD Fractions of primary crystals and eutectic structure

For a composition X1 = 24 %, the primary α is 83.8 %, the eutectic structure is 16.2 %, and the α inside the eutectic structure is 43.9 %. The overall fraction of α is 83.8 + 16.2 × 0.439 = 90.9 %, which agrees with the value obtained by applying the lever rule between α and β just below TE, (xF − X)/(xF − xD) = 90.9 % (combining the three equations above shows that this agreement holds in general). Note that the fractions of phases (α and β) and the fractions of constituents (primary crystals and eutectic structure) are different things. For a hypereutectic composition (between E and F), the roles of α and β are exchanged, and the microstructure is primary β + eutectic structure.

(b) Precipitation below the solvus. Below the solvus (solid solubility limit line), the amount that can stay in solid solution decreases as the temperature falls, so the part that has become supersaturated precipitates as a second phase. For the composition X2 of Fig. 2-7, β crystallizes in the liquid at T2 and becomes a β polycrystal at T3, and below T4 α precipitates inside the β grains and along the grain boundaries. The fraction of α that precipitates is given by the lever rule at that temperature T, (xβ(T) − X)/(xβ(T) − xα(T)). The same happens inside the hypoeutectic primary α (composition xD), and the fraction of β that precipitates in it is

fβin primary(T)= xD−xα(T) xβ(T)−xα(T) Precipitation inside the primary crystals

The fractions of α and β inside the eutectic structure change with temperature in the same way, and when these are added together, the overall fractions of α and β agree with the lever rule at that temperature.

(c) Peritectic reaction. In a peritectic system, when a composition X between the peritectic point P and the end of the liquid (composition xL) is cooled, primary α crystallizes and grows, and then at the peritectic temperature TP the peritectic reaction L + α → β takes place. β forms where α and the liquid are in contact and grows so as to enclose α (this is the origin of the name “peritectic”). With xa the composition of the end of α and xP the composition of the peritectic point, the fractions before and after the reaction are

Before the reaction (just above TP) fα= xL−X xL−xa After the reaction (xP<X<xL) fβ= xL−X xL−xP ,fα=0 After the reaction (xa<X<xP) fα= xP−X xP−xa ,fL=0 Before and after the peritectic reaction

For the composition X (70.5 %) of textbook Fig. 2-8, there are α 18.6 % and liquid 81.4 % before the reaction, and β 41.3 % and liquid 58.7 % after it, so all the α turns into β, and that β grows from the remaining liquid and becomes a β polycrystal at T2. For compositions on the A side of P, the liquid is used up first, and a microstructure in which β encloses a core of α remains. The simulator draws the fractions in the middle of the reaction (progress ξ) by moving them between the values before and after the reaction in proportion to ξ. The mass balance is preserved regardless of ξ. The actual peritectic reaction proceeds by diffusion through the β shell and is therefore slow, so with fast cooling such as in casting, the α core tends to remain (a nonequilibrium microstructure in which the peritectic reaction does not go to completion).

(d) The horizontal part of the cooling curve. At the eutectic and peritectic temperatures three phases coexist and F = 0, so the temperature stays constant and stops falling until the reaction ends (thermal arrest). This is because the heat released by the reaction cancels the cooling, and the length of the horizontal part is proportional to the amount that reacts (for a eutectic, the fraction of liquid just above TE). This is why the eutectic and peritectic temperatures can be determined from cooling curves and differential thermal analysis (DTA). (In simulator ③, for ease of viewing, the time during which the temperature stops is made “a fixed time + a time proportional to the amount that reacts.”) For a pure substance or for the composition of a congruently melting compound too, the liquid solidifies all at once into the solid without changing composition, so the temperature stops in the same way.

(e) Lamellae or rods? The two phases of a eutectic structure can be either layers (lamellae) or rods. Let f be the volume fraction of the minority phase and compare at the same spacing λ: the interfacial area per unit volume is 2/λ for lamellae and, for rods on a square array (radius r, f = πr2/λ2), 2πr/λ2 = 2√(πf)/λ. If the interfacial energy is isotropic, the smaller interfacial area is favored, so the rule of thumb is rods for f < 1/π (about 0.32) and lamellae for larger f (a commonly used rule of thumb; comparing in the same way with rods on a triangular lattice gives about 0.28). In practice, anisotropy of the interfacial energy and the growth rate also matter, and quite a few systems depart from the rule of thumb (Ag–Cu is a typical lamellar system even though its minority-phase fraction is about 0.32, and the Si in Al–Si forms irregular plates). The simulator draws Pb–Sn, Ag–Cu, and Al–Si, whose shapes are known, as lamellae, and uses this rule of thumb to choose between lamellae and rods for the other systems (mole fractions are substituted for the fractions).

(f) How a eutectic structure grows as layers. At the eutectic temperature, α first nucleates, β forms next to it, and α and β grow side by side (the same idea as Fig. 7 of Tanaka 2008). Since α is rich in A and β is rich in B, when α grows, B is left over in the liquid ahead of it, and when β grows, A is left over. The leftover B diffuses along the interface to the front of the neighboring β and is used there, and the leftover A diffuses to the front of the neighboring α. This side-by-side growth of two phases that exchange components is called cooperative growth.

Since neighboring phases exchange components, the two phases form alternating layers (or rods) that are elongated in the direction of growth. The narrower the layer spacing λ, the shorter the diffusion distance and the faster the growth, but the α/β interface per unit volume increases and the interfacial energy cost goes up. The spacing is determined by this balance, and λ2v is nearly constant for a growth rate v (the faster the cooling, the finer the microstructure), as is known from the analysis of Jackson and Hunt (1966).

(g) Microstructure of a monotectic (two-liquid-separation) system. In a system with a large positive Ω of the liquid, inside the dome below the critical point (the region of two-liquid separation), the liquid splits into two liquids of different composition, L1 (A-rich) and L2 (B-rich), and the minority liquid forms droplets. At the monotectic temperature TM the monotectic reaction L1 → α + L2 proceeds at constant temperature, leaving droplets of L2 inside α (for compositions in which L2 is the majority, conversely, grains of α end up scattered in L2). As it cools, L2 becomes richer in B while crystallizing α, and finally solidifies at the eutectic temperature near the melting point of B. In Cu–Pb, the microstructure consists of Pb grains dispersed in Cu, which is used for plain bearings (Kelmet). In practice, it is also known that the droplets rise or sink because of the density difference between the two liquids, so the microstructure tends to be biased vertically (gravity segregation). For compositions in which primary α appears first, the combination of phases stays “α + liquid” at the monotectic temperature; the composition of the liquid, however, jumps from L1 to L2.

(h) Crystallization and precipitation, eutectoid and peritectoid. The emergence of a solid from a liquid is called crystallization, and the emergence of another solid from inside a solid is called precipitation (Tanaka 2008). When a reaction of the same form as the eutectic or peritectic takes place entirely among solids, it is called eutectoid (example: the eutectoid reaction of steel, γ → α + Fe3C, which gives the microstructure called pearlite) or peritectoid. If you read primary crystals as proeutectoid phases and the eutectic structure as the eutectoid structure, the way of reading the microstructures in this section (the lever rule included) applies.

Conventions in the simulator’s microstructure pictures (1) A small number of primary-crystal nuclei (16, including ones outside the screen) are placed, and they grow at the same rate with their birth times shifted slightly (the Johnson–Mehl model). Because of this, the grain sizes are not uniform. (2) Assuming that regions solidify in the order in which the growth from the nuclei reaches them, areas are painted in proportion to the lever-rule fractions. The thin liquid film between grains that have collided solidifies last, and that becomes the grain boundary. (3) The eutectic structure changes the direction of the layers from one region of aligned orientation (colony) to the next, and the ratio of layer widths is matched to the fractions of α and β inside the eutectic structure. For systems drawn with rods, the minority phase is drawn as rods arranged on a triangular lattice, and the fraction is matched by the thickness of the rods. (4) Precipitates are placed inside grains (elongated particles in two orientations aligned grain by grain) and along grain boundaries. In every case the area ratios agree with the lever-rule answers (the difference checked in all 15 systems was within 0.5 %), but the shapes, sizes, and spacings of grains are schematic. Also, mole fractions are substituted for the area ratios (the area seen under a microscope is the volume fraction, so it deviates slightly if the molar volumes differ). (5) “On heating” is drawn as the microstructure when a polycrystal is heated above the solidus: in each grain, melting starts from the part far from the nucleus (near the grain boundary), and the original grain boundaries are left as dotted lines (they fade as the liquid fraction approaches 0.8). (6) Bands narrower than 0.8 K in which the same phase appears twice side by side (a minor by-product of the model) are skipped. Genuinely narrow regions, such as the thin L + α region just below the liquidus, are kept.

6. A Phase Diagram Alone Does Not Determine Grain Shape or the Time a Change Takes

Even when the equilibrium phases are determined, creating a new phase requires creating an interface. In the simplest classical nucleation theory, in which a spherical nucleus of radius r forms in a uniform parent phase, the free-energy change is

ΔG(r)=4πr2γ +43πr3Δgv (15)

If the new phase is stable, the volumetric free-energy difference is Δgv < 0, but the interfacial energy γ > 0 tries to eliminate small nuclei. The critical radius and the barrier are

r*=2γ|Δgv| , ΔG*=16πγ3 3Δgv2 (16)

Increasing the supercooling increases |Δgv| and makes nucleation easier. Furthermore, the growth rate after a nucleus has formed depends on the diffusion of heat and solute, the interface reaction, crystal orientation, and elastic strain. This is why even materials with the same phase diagram can have greatly different microstructures if their heat-treatment histories differ. How nucleation and growth compete with the cooling rate is treated in detail in §7, as a story of the glass transition and crystallization.

7. Glass Transition and Crystallization — How Fast You Cool Decides Where You End Up

At the end of §6 I noted that even materials with the same phase diagram can change their microstructure with heat-treatment history. The most extreme example is glass. When a melt of SiO2 or B2O3 is cooled, the crystal is stable below the melting point Tm on the phase diagram, yet the melt solidifies without becoming a crystal. In this section we trace through equations what separates “becoming a crystal” from “becoming a glass,” taking up the enthalpy diagram, the viscosity, the rates of nucleation and growth, and the TTT diagram in that order.

7.1 Distinguishing four states in the enthalpy–temperature diagram

Plot the enthalpy H of 1 mol of the substance against temperature. Just as in Eq. (4), at constant pressure dH/dT = Cp, so the slope of a line is the heat capacity. Regarding the heat capacity as constant in each state and taking the enthalpy of the crystal at its melting point as the zero reference,

Hc(T)= Cp,c(T−Tm) Hl(T)= ΔHm+ Cp,l(T−Tm) (17)

Here the subscript c denotes the crystal and l the liquid. The line for the liquid extends below Tm unchanged, and this is the supercooled liquid. The supercooled liquid has a higher G than the crystal and so is not stable, but until a crystal nucleus forms it keeps internal equilibrium as a liquid (metastable). On further cooling, at some temperature the rearrangement of the atomic configuration can no longer keep up, and the structure freezes as that of the liquid at that temperature. If the temperature that represents the frozen structure is called the fictive temperature Tf (Tool 1946), the enthalpy of the glass is

Hg(T)= Hl(Tf)+ Cp,g(T−Tf)Cp,g≈ Cp,c (18)

In a glass the atomic vibrations remain, but the rearrangement of the atoms (the configurational degrees of freedom) stops, so the heat capacity falls to about that of the crystal, and the slope of the line bends at Tf. The temperature of this bend is the glass transition temperature Tg. On the other hand, if a crystal is born during cooling, H drops by the enthalpy of fusion ΔHm and moves onto the line of the crystal.

7.2 The glass transition is not a phase transition but the “freezing of relaxation”

Melting was an equilibrium phase transition that occurs at a definite temperature where Gl and Gc become equal (§2.3). The glass transition is not like that. It is a question of rate: the structure freezes at the point where the time needed for the structure of the liquid to follow a change in temperature (the structural relaxation time) τ becomes longer than the time scale of the cooling. A rough estimate of τ follows from the Maxwell model of viscoelasticity. If a spring (high-frequency shear modulus G∞) and a dashpot (viscosity η) are connected in series, the stress

σ(t)=σ0exp(−t/τ)τ=ηG∞ (19)

relaxes in this way. Here σ is the stress (a different quantity from the interfacial energy γ of Section 7.5). When the temperature is lowered at q [K/s], the time it takes to cool through the temperature width δT that the structure has to follow is δT/q. If τ is shorter than this, the liquid stays in equilibrium; if it is longer, the structure freezes. The condition at the boundary is

τ(Tg)≈δTq⟹η(Tg)≈G∞δTq (20)

Using the order of magnitude of the shear modulus of silicate glass, G∞ ≈ 1010 Pa, η = 1012 Pa·s gives τ ≈ 100 s. When cooling at 10 K/min, it takes 60 s to pass through 10 K, so the structure freezes right around here. This is the meaning of the commonly used rule of thumb that “Tg is the temperature at which η ≈ 1012 Pa·s.” This, however, is a conventional definition, and Tg changes by several tens of K depending on the measurement method (DSC, dilatometer) and the cooling and heating rate.

What is important in Eq. (20) is that increasing q lowers the η at which the structure freezes, that is, raises Tg. A glass cooled faster has the structure of a liquid at a higher temperature, and both its H and its volume are larger (Moynihan et al. 1976 measured the cooling-rate dependence of the fictive temperature). Tg is not a material constant but a temperature determined by the cooling rate.

7.3 Temperature dependence of viscosity — the VFT equation and “strong” and “fragile” liquids

The viscosity of glass-forming liquids often rises more steeply than the Arrhenius type, and is often well described by the Vogel–Fulcher–Tammann (VFT) equation (Fulcher 1925, Tammann & Hesse 1926).

log10(η/Pa·s)= A+BT−T0 (21)

In Eq. (20), take η(Tg) = 1012 Pa·s at 10 K/min (qref = 1/6 K/s). Regarding G∞δT as a constant, η(Tg) ∝ 1/q, so log10η(Tg) = 12 − log10(q/qref). Substituting this into the left-hand side of Eq. (21) and solving for T gives

Tg(q)=T0+ B12−log10(q/qref)−A (22)

Inserting the VFT parameters of B2O3 (Table 1), we get Tg ≈ 537, 555, and 574 K for q = 10−3, 1/6, and 10 K/s, so Tg shifts by about 10 K for every order-of-magnitude change in the cooling rate (values in this model).

Another indicator is Angell’s fragility index m, which is the slope of log10η against the temperature normalized by Tg (Angell 1995). Setting s = Tg/T gives T = Tg/s and dT/ds = −Tg/s2, so

Liquids with a small m that are close to the Arrhenius type are called “strong liquids” (SiO2 is the typical example), and liquids that become viscous abruptly near Tg are called “fragile liquids.”

7.4 The driving force for crystallization

When a crystal forms from a supercooled liquid, the free energy per 1 mol changes by ΔG = Gc − Gl = −ΔGfus. Using Eq. (7) (the approximation ΔCp = 0) and rewriting in terms of the degree of supercooling ΔT = Tm − T, this is −ΔHm(1 − T/Tm) = −ΔHmΔT/Tm. Dividing by the molar volume Vm to get a value per unit volume, we obtain

Δgv=− ΔHmΔTTmVmΔT=Tm−T (24) Turnbull approximation

The greater the supercooling, the larger the driving force. There are also approximations, such as Hoffman’s, that partly incorporate the effect of ΔCp at large supercooling.

7.5 Homogeneous nucleation — the barrier W* and the nucleation rate I

The free-energy change when a spherical crystal of radius r forms in a supercooled liquid is, as in Eq. (15), ΔG(r) = 4πr2γ + (4/3)πr3Δgv (γ is the interfacial energy between the crystal and the liquid, and Δgv < 0). The first term goes as r2 and the second as r3, so for small nuclei the interfacial loss wins, and for large nuclei the volume gain wins. We find the top of the hill that marks the boundary between them.

dΔGdr= 8πrγ+4πr2Δgv=0⟹r*=−2γΔgv

Substituting this back into ΔG(r),

W*=ΔG(r*)= 4πγ4γ2Δgv2 −43π8γ3Δgv2 =16πγ33Δgv2

we obtain Eq. (16). Substituting Eq. (24) into it,

W*= 16πγ3Tm2Vm2 3ΔHm2(ΔT)2 ∝1(ΔT)2 (25)

As Tm is approached, the barrier becomes infinite and no nuclei can form. The frequency with which critical nuclei form is proportional to the Boltzmann factor exp(−W*/kBT), and the frequency with which atoms attach to a critical nucleus is proportional to the diffusion coefficient. If the diffusion coefficient is related to the viscosity through an Eyring (or Stokes–Einstein) type relation D ≈ kBT/(d0η) (d0 is the size of a structural unit; numerical coefficients are omitted), the steady-state nucleation rate (the number of nuclei per unit volume and unit time) takes the following form (the form used by Cassar et al. 2020).

I(T)= γkBTd05η(T) exp(−W*kBT) (26)

On the high-temperature side W* is too large, and on the low-temperature side 1/η drops steeply, so I(T) has a sharp peak at a temperature well below Tm (Fig. 4, left). Whether the peak lies above or below Tg depends on the material. In the model of this page, for LS2, which has a small Tg/Tm (it crystallizes easily), the peak lies a little above Tg (783 K; the Tg calculated in simulator ④ for cooling at 10 K/min is about 727 K), while for SiO2 and B2O3, which have a large Tg/Tm (they form glass easily), the peak lies below Tg (in the temperature range where the structure freezes) (1357 K versus about 1459 K for SiO2, and 531 K versus about 559 K for B2O3). If the peak lies below Tg, the motion of the atoms freezes during cooling before nuclei can form, so the material becomes a glass more easily. LS2 is a material often used in studies of homogeneous nucleation; for example, in the undoped sample of Thieme et al. 2016 the maximum of the nucleation rate is 16 mm−3s−1 (1.6×1010 m−3s−1) at about 469 °C. In the model of this page (the values of Table 1 with γ = 0.151 J/m2 fitted), the maximum is 1.0×1011 m−3s−1 at 783 K: the order of magnitude is close, but the temperature comes out higher. It is a much-discussed point that, when classical nucleation theory is combined with the viscosity, the maximum tends to shift to the high-temperature side (Nascimento et al. 2011, Cassar et al. 2020).

7.6 Crystal growth rate u

A crystal that has formed grows larger by advancing its interface. Consider the difference between the frequency at which structural units on the liquid side of the interface attach to the crystal side and the frequency at which they conversely leave the crystal. Let the distance of a single hop be d0 and the frequency be D/d02, and suppose that hops in the reverse direction are made less likely by the driving force ΔGm = ΔHmΔT/Tm (per mole). Then the growth rate is

u(T)=fDd0 [1−exp(−ΔHmΔTRTTm)]D≈kBTd0η (27) Wilson–Frenkel form

where f is the fraction of sites at the interface where atoms can attach. When the supercooling is small, the exponent ε = ΔHmΔT/(RTTm) of Eq. (27) is much smaller than 1, so 1 − exp(−ε) ≈ ε and u ∝ ΔT/η, which is the same form as the result of Wagstaff 1969, who organized the growth of cristobalite in silica in the form u ∝ ΔT/η. The peak of u(T) lies on the higher-temperature side (just below Tm) of the peak of I(T). The maximum growth rate of LS2 is measured to be 6.87×10−5 m/s (at 1210 K; the table of Jiusti et al. 2020), and in the model of this page (fitted with f = 0.23) it is 6.8×10−5 m/s (at 1242 K).

7.7 Crystal volume fraction — the JMAK equation

Suppose that nuclei are born at random in the liquid and grow isotropically. The volume fraction of crystals counted while ignoring the collisions between crystals (“allowing them to overlap”) is called the extended volume fraction Xext. The volume fraction dX that actually increases is only the part of dXext that falls on the portion that is still liquid (fraction 1 − X), so

dX=(1−X)dXext⟹X=1−exp(−Xext) (28) Johnson–Mehl–Avrami–Kolmogorov

These relations hold (Avrami 1939, 1940, among others). If I and u are constant at constant temperature, a nucleus born at time t′ has radius u(t − t′) at time t, so

Xext= ∫0tI4π3 u3(t−t′)3dt′ =4π3Iu3t44 =π3Iu3t4 (29)

We thus obtain Eq. (29) (Avrami exponent 4). When cooling at a constant rate q, I and u change with time, so they are kept inside the integral, and with t = (Tm − T)/q and dt = −dT/q the time integral is converted into an integral over temperature.

Xext=4π3 ∫0tI(t′) [∫t′tu(s)ds]3dt′ =Cq4C=4π3 ∫TTmI(T′) [∫TT′u(T″)dT″]3dT′ (30)

The outer dt′ gives a factor 1/q, and the ds inside the brackets gives (1/q)3, so that the extended volume fraction is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the cooling rate (exact as long as I and u are functions of temperature alone). While X is small, X ≈ Xext, so cooling 10 times faster reduces the amount of crystal to about 1/104.

7.8 The TTT diagram and the critical cooling rate

Uhlmann 1972 took “a crystal can be detected once it has formed at a volume fraction X = 10−6” as the criterion and considered the conditions for forming a glass. The time needed to reach X when the sample is held at temperature T is, from Eq. (29) solved for t,

t(T)= [3XπI(T)u(T)3]1/4 (31)

When this is plotted on a temperature–time (logarithmic) diagram, it takes the shape of a “nose” jutting out sideways (Time–Temperature–Transformation, the TTT diagram). This is because I is small at high temperatures and η is large at low temperatures. The critical cooling rate Rc can be defined in two ways: by a simple estimate using the nose temperature Tn and time tn, and by the rate at which Eq. (30) gives X = 10−6.

Rcnose≈Tm−TntnRccontinuous cooling=(CX)1/4 (32)

Here C is the value of the integral in Eq. (30) carried out to a sufficiently low temperature (the temperature at which crystallization practically stops; Tlo in the simulator). Since Rc is a quantity determined by the detection limit X = 10−6, for q > Rc a glass forms, while for q < Rc a detectable amount of crystal begins to appear, and if q is sufficiently smaller than Rc, crystallization proceeds (since X ∝ q−4, reducing q to 1/10 increases X by a factor of about 104). The nose-based estimate counts as if the sample stayed for a long time only at the nose temperature during cooling, so it gives a larger value than the integral for continuous cooling (Weinberg et al. 1989 discuss the comparison of the two).

7.9 Why SiO2 and B2O3 form glass easily — Zachariasen’s rules

Zachariasen 1932 gave the following empirical rules as the conditions under which an oxide AmOn can form a continuous random network without long-range order.

  1. Oxygen is not bonded to more than 2 A atoms.
  2. The number of oxygens surrounding A is small (3 or 4).
  3. The oxygen polyhedra share only corners, not edges or faces.
  4. To build a three-dimensional network, each polyhedron shares at least 3 of its corners with other polyhedra.

In SiO2, SiO4 tetrahedra are connected by sharing corner oxygens (bridging oxygens), and in B2O3, BO3 triangles are connected by sharing corners. Corner-sharing polyhedra can extend the network without large strain even without periodicity, by changing bond angles such as Si–O–Si. Oxides that form networks in this way (SiO2, B2O3, GeO2, P2O5, and so on) are called network-forming oxides. On the other hand, Li2O and Na2O are modifier oxides that break Si–O–Si bonds and create non-bridging oxygens, lowering the viscosity and making crystallization easier. This is one reason why LS2 is an “example that crystallizes easily.” It also matters that LS2 is a congruently melting compound, so that a crystal of the same composition as the glass can form and homogeneous nucleation can occur without a change in composition, and that Tg/Tm is small, about 0.55 (Table 1). Note that Zachariasen’s rules are empirical rules for network-forming oxides alone, and for glasses containing modifier oxides an extension is needed in which the modifier ions enter the gaps in the network (Zachariasen 1932 himself discussed it in this form). They do not apply to metallic glasses.

7.10 Putting crystallization to use — crystallized glass (glass-ceramics)

In Fig. 4, when LS2 is selected, one can see that the peak of I (just above Tg) and the peak of u (just below Tm) are separated in temperature. So if one first makes a glass and then carries out a two-stage heat treatment in which the glass is held at the temperature of the nucleation peak to create many nuclei, and then heated toward the growth peak to grow the crystals, one obtains a material in which fine crystals are uniformly dispersed throughout the glass. This is the basic idea of crystallized glass (glass-ceramics), and the Li2O–SiO2 system is one of its representative systems (for an overview, see the book by Höland & Beall). That adding a nucleating agent changes Rc has also been investigated for LS2 (Huang et al. 1986).

7.11 Connection with the “Quench” of simulator ③

In simulator ③, pressing “Quench” during cooling freezes the remaining liquid as glass. That was based on the assumption that there is “no time to become a crystal.” In the language of this section, quenching is an operation that makes the cooling rate q sufficiently larger than the critical cooling rate Rc. Rc differs greatly between materials; even the three materials of Table 2 alone differ by more than 6 orders of magnitude (the value for B2O3 is from the model). Some materials, like SiO2, turn into glass even when cooled slowly in a furnace, while others, like metals, need an extremely large cooling rate. The phase diagram (equilibrium) tells which phase is stable, and Rc (kinetics) tells whether the system can get there.

What kind of state is glass, after all? Glass is not an equilibrium state but a nonequilibrium state in which the structure is frozen. Zanotto & Mauro 2017 define glass, in essence, as “a nonequilibrium, noncrystalline condensed state that shows a glass transition, with a structure resembling that of the original supercooled liquid, that spontaneously relaxes toward the supercooled liquid and, in the limit of infinitely long time, crystallizes.” Also, there is a temperature at which the entropy of a supercooled liquid, extrapolated to low temperature, would become smaller than that of the crystal (Kauzmann 1948), but in reality the glass transition occurs before that and the extrapolation stops. The interpretation of this “Kauzmann paradox” has not yet been settled (Debenedetti & Stillinger 2001).

(1) The model — the equations used

  • Viscosity and relaxation time. The viscosity is given by the VFT equation (21), and the relaxation time by τ = η/G∞ from the Maxwell Eq. (19), with G∞ = 1010 Pa (a model value matched to the order of magnitude of the shear modulus of silicates).
  • Glass transition (fictive temperature). The idea of Eq. (20) is expressed by a single-relaxation equation in which the fictive temperature Tf chases the temperature T with relaxation time τ.
dTfdt= T−Tfτ(T)

The Tf that freezes when cooling from high temperature at a constant rate q is called Tg(q) in the simulator. 8. The Phase-Field Method — Holding the Interface Not as a Line but as a Smooth Field

8.1 Adding a gradient energy to the local free energy

In the phase-field method, the composition c(r,t) and an order parameter representing the phase, η(r,t), are placed continuously in space. For example, take η = 0 as the liquid phase and η = 1 as the solid phase, and let it change smoothly from 0 to 1 at the interface. We take the total free-energy functional to be

ℱ[c,η]= ∫V[ fchem(c,η,T) +κc2|∇c|2 +κη2|∇η|2 ]dV (33)

Here fchem is the bulk free energy that gave the phase diagram, and κc|∇c|²/2 and κη|∇η|²/2 are interface terms that penalize rapid spatial variation. As a result the interface acquires a finite thickness, and the interfacial energy appears naturally.

8.2 Conserved and nonconserved quantities obey different evolution equations

Composition is a conserved quantity that cannot disappear on its own, so with the chemical potential

μ=δℱδc =∂fchem∂c −κc∇2c (34)

it flows down its gradient, with flux J = −M∇μ. This is the Cahn–Hilliard equation.

∂c∂t =∇·[M∇μ] =∇·[M∇ (∂fchem∂c −κc∇2c)] (35) Cahn–Hilliard

On the other hand, the order parameter η, which represents order or disorder of a crystal or liquid versus solid phase, is a nonconserved quantity that can change locally from 0 to 1. The steepest-descent time evolution is given by

∂η∂t =−Lδℱδη =−L( ∂fchem∂η −κη∇2η) (36) Allen–Cahn

For periodic boundaries or zero-flux boundaries, with M > 0 and L > 0, dℱ/dt ≤ 0 holds, and the system changes its microstructure while dissipating free energy. This is the heart of building a “time evolution that does not violate thermodynamics.”

8.3 Smoothly connecting the free energies of the liquid and solid

For the solidification of a binary alloy, one can take, for example,

fchem= h(η)fS(c,T) +[1−h(η)] fL(c,T) +Wg(η) (37)

Here the solid- and liquid-phase free energies of the phase diagram are connected by an interpolation function h(η), and the double well g(η) = η²(1−η)² stabilizes the pure liquid and solid phases. By coupling the temperature field, latent heat, interface anisotropy, and elastic field, the method can be extended to dendrites, eutectic growth, precipitation, grain growth, and so on.

9. Spinodal Decomposition — Phase Separation in Which Composition Waves Grow Without Forming Nuclei

In §4.2 we saw that inside a two-phase region there are two boundaries, the binodal and the spinodal. In this section we derive spinodal decomposition, which occurs inside the spinodal, following the flow of §8.2 of Kingery, Bowen, and Uhlmann, Introduction to Ceramics, 2nd edition. It is also an account of linearizing the phase-field method of §8 (the Cahn–Hilliard equation) around the uniform state. Next to each equation number we add the corresponding equation number of Kingery.

9.1 The G–x curve and phase diagram of two-liquid immiscibility

For simplicity we use the same symmetric regular solution as in §4.2. Let g be the free energy per molecule (per structural unit), and use the critical temperature Tc = Ω/(2R) to write Ω = 2RTc; then Eq. (14) becomes

∂2g∂x2 =kB[Tx(1−x)−4Tc] (38)

Here the per-mole Eq. (14) has been divided by NA, using R/NA = kB. Two curves follow from this.

  • Spinodal (points of zero curvature, the inflection points): from Eq. (38) = 0, x(1 − x) = T/(4Tc).
  • Binodal (points of tangency of the common tangent): since the curve is symmetric about x = 1/2, the common tangent is horizontal, and the points of tangency satisfy dg/dx = 0. Putting Ω = 2RTc into RT ln[x/(1 − x)] + Ω(1 − 2x) = 0 gives

The names of the three regions Outside the binodal: a uniform solution is stable. Between the binodal and the spinodal: a uniform solution is metastable. It is stable against infinitesimal fluctuations, but if a nucleus larger than a certain size forms, it separates into two phases (nucleation and growth). Inside the spinodal: a uniform solution is unstable. Small fluctuations of sufficiently long wavelength grow without a barrier. The “two liquid phases” here lie, in BaO–SiO2 and the like, in a metastable region below the liquidus, and are not necessarily the two-phase region of the equilibrium phase diagram.

9.2 Free energy including the gradient energy

Eq. (38) is the value for a uniform solution. When the composition varies from place to place, at places where the composition changes sharply the energy of a “nascent interface” is added. Following Cahn & Hilliard 1958, we express it by the square of the composition gradient, and the free energy of a system of volume V is

G=Nv∫V [g(C)+κ(∇C)2]dV (40) Kingery Eq. 8.8

where C is the composition (the same as the mole fraction x) and Nv is the number of molecules per unit volume, and κ > 0 is the gradient-energy coefficient. This is the same idea as κc|∇c|2/2 of Eq. (33) in §8, and only the way the coefficient is set differs (it corresponds to κc = 2Nvκ).

9.3 Does G go up or down under a small fluctuation?

Suppose that a small composition fluctuation C − C0 has formed in a uniform solution of average composition C0. Expanding g(C) in a Taylor series around C0, we get

g(C)=g(C0) +(∂g∂C)C0(C−C0) +12(∂2g∂C2)C0(C−C0)2+⋯

Put this into Eq. (40) and subtract G = NvVg(C0) of the uniform solution. The first-order term vanishes, since the amount of substance is conserved and hence ∫(C − C0)dV = 0. (Kingery explains that in an isotropic solution the odd-order terms vanish. For the first-order term, the reason is conservation of the amount of substance.) If the terms of third and higher order are dropped as small, we obtain

ΔG=Nv∫V [12(∂2g∂C2)C0 (C−C0)2 +κ(∇C)2]dV (41) Kingery Eq. 8.9

The second term is always positive. Therefore, if (∂2g/∂C2)C0 > 0 (outside the spinodal), then ΔG > 0 for any small fluctuation, and the uniform solution is stable against fluctuations. Inside the spinodal the first term becomes negative, and ΔG < 0 for fluctuations that satisfy

12|∂2g∂C2|C0 (C−C0)2 >κ(∇C)2 (42) Kingery Eq. 8.10

that is, fluctuations whose gradient is sufficiently gentle (long wavelength). A solution inside the spinodal is always unstable against some fluctuation.

9.4 A sinusoidal fluctuation and the critical wavelength λc

Take the fluctuation to be a single sine wave and examine which wavelengths are unstable.

C−C0=Acosβx (43) Kingery Eq. 8.11

β is the wavenumber, and the wavelength is λ = 2π/β. ∇C = −Aβ sin βx, and the average over one wavelength is ⟨cos2βx⟩ = ⟨sin2βx⟩ = 1/2. Putting this into Eq. (41) and dividing by the volume, we get

ΔGV=Nv [12g″A22 +κA2β22] =NvA24 [g″+2κβ2] (44) Kingery Eq. 8.12

Hereafter we abbreviate g″ = (∂2g/∂C2)C0. Kingery’s Eq. 8.12 is written in the per-molecule form without the coefficient Nv, but the argument about the sign is the same. For g″ < 0, the bracket is negative for waves with β2 < −g″/(2κ).

βc=[−12κg″]1/2λc=2πβc =[−8π2κg″]1/2 (45) Kingery Eq. 8.13a, b

All fluctuations with wavelengths longer than λc are unstable, and at shorter wavelengths the gradient energy wins and ΔG > 0. The more negative g″ is (the deeper inside the spinodal), the shorter λc is, and the finer the scale on which decomposition can occur. If the system separates into two phases on a scale finer than λc, it is by nucleation and growth.

∂C∂t= M~Nv ∇2[g′(C)−2κ∇2C] (46) Cahn–Hilliard (the same form as Eq. 35)

Experimental clues are the initial stage in which, in small-angle X-ray scattering or light scattering, the position (wavelength) of the scattering peak stays almost constant while the intensity grows, and the difference in behavior inside and outside the spinodal. For example, it has been reported that in TiO2–SnO2, a modulated structure forms inside the coherent spinodal for [001], but no modulation was seen between the coherent spinodal for [001] and the equilibrium curve (Stubican & Schultz 1970). Linear theory (Eq. 48) holds only in the initial stage, and in practice the scattering peak often shifts toward lower angles (longer wavelengths) with time, so the effects of thermal fluctuations (Cook 1970) and nonlinear effects have been discussed (Cahn & Hilliard 1971).

9.7 Interconnected microstructures, and the effect of the average composition

After enough time has passed, the composition field becomes a superposition of waves of various directions with wavelengths near λm. Cahn 1965 superposed 100 sinusoidal waves of wavenumber magnitude βm with random directions and phases, and computed a 50:50 microstructure obtained by dividing the field into two phases according to the sign of its value. Looking at successive cross sections, he showed that this is a microstructure in which both phases are interconnected in three dimensions, and that it resembles the microstructures observed in phase-separated glass (Fig. 8.4 of Kingery).

Fig. 8 below is our own two-dimensional calculation of the same idea. An interconnected microstructure alone does not prove “it was spinodal decomposition” In a two-dimensional cross section, a phase that is connected in three dimensions can look like islands. Conversely, an interconnected microstructure also forms when grains born by nucleation coalesce. Seward et al. 1968b heated a thin film of BaO–SiO2 inside an electron microscope and observed isolated grains coalescing into an interconnected structure, and stated that judging the mechanism from the final microstructure alone is insufficient. Haller 1965 also discussed the possibility that an interconnected microstructure can be explained by the overlap of nucleated spheres.

9.8 Subsequent coarsening, and what happens when the temperature is raised

Once the amplitude has grown to the equilibrium composition difference, linear theory no longer holds. After that, the microstructure becomes coarser so as to reduce the interfacial area (coarsening). In the phase separation of alkali borosilicate glass, the interfacial area decreases in proportion to the −1/2 power of time, and an apparent activation energy of 40 kcal/mol (about 167 kJ/mol) has been reported (Haller 1965).

When the temperature is raised above Tc, Eq. (38) becomes positive at every composition, and R(β) of Eq. (48) becomes negative for every β. All the composition waves decay, and the system returns to a uniform solution. Even below Tc, if the system moves outside the binodal, the two phases eventually dissolve into each other. Since the material has to be carried by diffusion, however, the coarser the microstructure, the longer it takes (the time is of the order of λ2/D̃). Haller et al. 1970 used the temperature at which the milky opalescence of glass (light scattering by phase separation) disappears on heating to determine the range of metastable immiscibility in the Na2O–B2O3–SiO2 system. In glass, on the other hand, diffusion effectively stops below Tg, so a phase-separated microstructure can be carried out to room temperature as it is (§7).

In crystals, strain lowers the spinodal In a crystal whose lattice constant changes with composition, a coherent strain energy is added to the composition wave, and the condition for decomposition changes to the requirement that g″ plus a strain term be negative (Cahn 1962). As a result, the coherent spinodal shifts to the low-temperature side of the chemical spinodal, and waves in the elastically soft direction are selected. In TiO2–SnO2 (tetragonal, rutile type) the lowering is smallest along [001] (105 K in a regular-solution calculation), and a plate-like modulated structure perpendicular to [001] forms (Park et al. 1975, Stubican & Schultz 1970). Isotropic liquids and glasses do not have this term.

9.10 Applications

  • Porous glass and high-silica glass. If one of the two interconnected phases produced by phase separation is dissolved in acid, a porous glass with connected pores results. Sintering it gives a highly heat-resistant glass of about 96 % silica (Vycor process). The pore size is adjusted by the heat treatment of the phase separation (λm and coarsening).
  • Age hardening of hard coatings. (Ti,Al)N films decompose spinodally at the high temperatures reached in use to form a coherent nanostructure, which maintains or even raises the hardness. They are used as coatings for cutting tools.
  • Magnetic materials. In CoFe2O4–Co3O4, for example, fine composition fluctuations produced by decomposition increase the coercivity.
  • Sometimes it is a nuisance. For materials that need a uniform composition, such as the light-emitting layer of InGaN, growth conditions that avoid phase separation are required.

9.11 What simulator ⑤ shows

Simulator ⑤ numerically solves the Cahn–Hilliard equation, Eq. (46), in two dimensions, and draws how the composition field grows in a material cooled from a high temperature (or heat-treated) in four figures at the same time: the atom arrangement, the position on the phase diagram, the free-energy curve, and the amplification factor R(β). The numbers below were checked by computing the same functions as those in the simulator, using node.

(1) The model — free energy

Borosilicate glass and TiO2–SnO2 are the same symmetric regular solutions as in Section 9.1. Because the immiscibility region of BaO–SiO2 is skewed toward about 10 mol% BaO, it is represented as a mixture of two kinds of structural units of different sizes (the same form as the Flory–Huggins type for polymer solutions). Assuming that a SiO2 unit occupies one lattice site and a BaO-containing unit occupies N sites, and writing the volume fraction of BaO-containing units as φ = Nx/[1 + (N − 1)x] (x is the mole fraction), the free energy per lattice site, in units of RTc, is given by

g(φ)RTc= TTc[φNlnφ+(1−φ)ln(1−φ)] +χcφ(1−φ) φc=11+N, χc=12(1+1N)2 (52)

Here χc is chosen so that ∂2g/∂φ2 = ∂3g/∂φ3 = 0 at the critical point (φc, Tc). For N = 1, φ = x and χc = 2, which reduces to the regular solution of Eq. (38) (Ω = 2RTc). For BaO–SiO2, the critical composition xc = 1/(N3/2 + 1) = 0.10 gives N = 92/3 ≈ 4.33. N is a phenomenological parameter for fitting the shape of the immiscibility region, and does not claim to be the size of an actual structural unit. The value per mole is Gm(x) = [1 + (N − 1)x]g(φ), and its curvature is ∂2Gm/∂x2 = N2(∂2g/∂φ2)/[1 + (N − 1)x]3; the free-energy curve on the screen and the readout ∂2g/∂C2 are these per-mole values (kJ/mol). The binodal is found by bisection on the slope of the common tangent, and the spinodal as the two roots of ∂2g/∂φ2 = 0.

(2) The model — time evolution

Taking ℓ = (κ/RTc)1/2 as the unit of length and τ0 = ℓ2/[M(Tc)RTc] as the unit of time (the same nondimensionalization as in Fig. 7, written per mole), Eq. (46) becomes

∂φ∂t= m(T)∇2[g′(φ)−2∇2φ] +(coherent strain)+ξ m(T)=M(T)M(Tc)= exp[−EaR(1T−1Tc)] (53)

Here g is in units of RTc, t in units of τ0, and ∇ in units of ℓ. The mobility is of Arrhenius type and decreases as the temperature falls, so the lower the temperature, the more slowly the microstructure changes. ξ is the thermal fluctuation (Cook 1970); to conserve the amount of substance it is added with a variance proportional to the square of the wavenumber, and its strength is 2.5×10−3 × T/Tc (a schematic value). Linearizing this about a uniform state gives, in the same form as Eq. (48), R(β) = −mβ2[g″ + e(θ) + 2β2] (g″ = ∂2g/∂φ2), λc = 2π[−(g″ + e)/2]−1/2, λm = √2 λc, and Rm = m(g″ + e)2/8, and the readouts and the figure of R(β) are calculated with these formulas.

e(θ) is the coherent-strain term, included only for the crystal (TiO2–SnO2) (the box in Section 9.8, Cahn 1962). To match the calculation of Park et al. 1975, in which the coherent spinodal at x = 0.5 is lowered by 105 K along [001] and by 387 K along <100>, we set e1 = 4 × 105 K/Tc ≈ 0.247 ([001], horizontal in the figure) and e2 = 4 × 387 K/Tc ≈ 0.909 ([100], vertical in the figure), and interpolate for directions in between by e(θ) = e2 + (e1 − e2)cos2θ (θ is the angle between the wavevector and [001]). With this interpolation the lowering along <101> comes out as 302 K. For a regular solution, g″ at x = 0.5 is 4(T/Tc − 1), so e = 4ΔT/Tc is the amount that lowers the spinodal by ΔT. In the phase diagram, the coherent spinodal for the softest direction, [001], is drawn as a dotted line, and the region inside it is labeled “Unstable.”

(4) Numerical method

  • Eq. (53) is solved by a semi-implicit Fourier method on a 128 × 128 square lattice with periodic boundaries (lattice spacing ℓ). In one time step h we take [1 + mhk2(A + e + 2k2)]φ̂n+1 = φ̂n − mhk2ℱ[g′(φn) − Aφn]. A is a constant for stabilization, namely the larger of g″ at the 0.2 % point and the 99.8 % point of the composition distribution. The k = 0 component does not change, so the average composition (amount of substance) is conserved to within rounding error.
  • For φ < 0.004 and φ > 0.996, g′ is extended linearly, so that the calculation does not break down even if φ overshoots numerically to outside 0–1 (when BaO–SiO2 is cooled to around 950 °C, the minimum overshoots to about −0.02 (depending on conditions such as the holding time). In the display it is rounded to 0.)
  • The step h is chosen as Rmh ≤ 0.03 inside the spinodal, Amh ≤ 6 when there is a microstructure with a large composition difference, and mh ≤ 50 otherwise, and while the temperature is being changed, it is limited so that the temperature changes by at most 2 K per step.
  • Clock. At speed ×1, the time tdec = 6/Rm in which the preset’s initial composition decomposes at the initial holding temperature (the time in which the amplitude grows by a factor of e6 ≈ 400) advances in about 4 seconds (240 frames). The speed of “Cool slowly” and “Reheat” is a constant rate that goes from Thi to Tmin in 2.5 tdec. ×10 and ×100 are multiples of this, and if the calculation of one frame takes more than 5 ms, the actual progress is slower than this.

(5) Controls

  • Choice of material. When you choose again, the display returns to the uniform state above Tc and stops.
  • Average composition C0 slider. When you move it, the calculation starts over from a uniform state at the current temperature.
  • Holding-temperature slider. If you move it while holding (after a quench), the temperature jumps to that value immediately.
  • “Homogenize at high temperature.” Makes the state uniform at Thi (above Tc).
  • “❄ Quench to this temperature and hold.” Drops straight to the holding temperature and advances time.
  • “Cool slowly.” Cools at a constant rate down to the holding temperature (down to Tmin if the holding temperature is higher than the current one).
  • “Reheat.” Heats at a constant rate up to Thi.
  • “● Place a nucleus.” In the metastable region, places one circular nucleus with a large composition difference. The composition of the nucleus is the point of tangency of the tangent parallel to the tangent at the current composition (the composition at which the driving force for nucleation is largest), and the same amount is taken from the surroundings to keep the average composition. In a 128 × 128 box, placing the nucleus moves the composition of the surroundings (the matrix) away from the average and reduces the supersaturation, so the radius is chosen as follows. For the radius r*(φm) of the critical nucleus at the matrix composition φm = φ0 − (φnucleus − φ0)πr2/(1282 − πr2) after a nucleus of radius r has been placed, the smallest r satisfying r ≥ r*(φm) + 1.5 ℓ + max(0, δ − 8 ℓ) is searched for in steps of 0.5 ℓ in the range 10–20 ℓ (δ is a measure of the interface thickness, obtained by dividing the difference between the two binodal compositions by the square root of the height of the free-energy hump; near Tc, where the interface is blurred, r* for a sharp interface is too small, so δ is added). For compositions where none is found in this range, the button is disabled and reads “● Place a nucleus (no growing nucleus can be placed at this composition).” That a nucleus placed by this rule grows has been checked by calculation at several temperatures and compositions for the three materials. Growth in the metastable region is slow, so the speed should be ×10 or higher. (Inside the spinodal and in the stable region the button can also be pressed, and then a nucleus of radius 10 ℓ is placed.)
  • Display. Switches between “Atom arrangement” and “Composition shading.” The speed is ×1, ×10, or ×100.

(6) How to read the display

  • Atom arrangement. 64 × 64 atoms are drawn, and one atom corresponds to 2 × 2 calculation lattice points. The average composition φ of each 2 × 2 block is mapped to the “probability of being B,” v, and compared with a random number fixed for each atom to decide whether it is B (orange) or A (blue), so places of the same composition get the same color every time and do not flicker. The mapping is a display that emphasizes composition. In the two-phase region (inside the binodal), φ is mapped to 0 at the composition of phase a, to the average mole fraction x0 at the average composition φ0, and to 1 at the composition of phase b, and the segments between φ0 and each phase are joined by a cubic (pushing values toward the ends the farther they are from φ0). Elsewhere v is the mole fraction x(φ) itself. As a result, in a uniform state the fraction of B equals the average composition C0 (mole fraction), and the separated phases become almost a single color. In BaO–SiO2 the calculation variable φ is the volume fraction of BaO-containing units and is related to the mole fraction x by φ = Nx/[1 + (N − 1)x], so the colors are matched to x rather than φ. The atom arrangement and the figure of the amplification factor R(β) are drawn across the full width of the screen. The composition shading is laid faintly underneath. For glass, the atoms are drawn slightly displaced from the lattice to look amorphous, and during playback a small vibration proportional to √(T/Tc) is added. This is a schematic in which the atomic positions themselves are not calculated; what is calculated is the composition field. In TiO2–SnO2, the horizontal direction of the figure is [001].
  • Composition shading. The 128 × 128 composition field is turned directly into colors.
  • Phase diagram. It shows the binodal (solid line), the chemical spinodal (dashed line), for TiO2–SnO2 the coherent spinodal for [001] (dotted line), the critical point, the current state (●), and the holding temperature (○). On the right, the temperature history is drawn (the horizontal axis is elapsed time).
  • Free-energy curve. This is Gm(x) at the current temperature with the common tangent (or the tangent, for a single phase) subtracted (kJ/mol), and it shows the points of tangency of the common tangent, the inflection points (○), the inside of the spinodal (shaded), the point of C0, and a chord representing a small fluctuation around C0. If the chord lies below the curve, G decreases with only a slight separation (inside the spinodal), which corresponds to the sign of the first term of Eq. (41). In TiO2–SnO2, when the chord is below the curve but G goes up once the strain is included, the display says so.
  • Amplification factor R(β). On the same curve as Fig. 7 (unit 1/τ0; for TiO2–SnO2 the solid line is [001] and the dashed line is [100]), vertical lines are drawn at βm and βc. The dots are the growth rates of the structure factor S(β) of the simulation (the squared Fourier components of the composition field, summed over rings of wavenumber). While the sample is held at the same temperature, ln S(β) is recorded every 0.1/Rm, and R is taken as half the least-squares slope over the most recent interval of 1.5/Rm (at least 5 points) (S is the square of the amplitude, so the slope is multiplied by 1/2). A dot is drawn only for wavenumbers at which S has exceeded 5 times its value when the holding began (well above the floor of the fluctuations). The early dots scatter around the curve while roughly tracing its shape. This is a sign that the system is in the stage of linear theory (Eq. 48). However, because the amplitude of the thermal fluctuations is finite, the linear stage lasts only a few e-foldings, and the dots scatter around the curve because of the fluctuations. When the width of the composition distribution exceeds half the composition difference between the two phases, comparison with linear theory is meaningless, so no dots are drawn and the display says “Nonlinear stage (points to compare with the linear-theory R(β) are not drawn).” The ▼ is the mean wavenumber ⟨k⟩ of the structure factor; inside the spinodal it is averaged only over wavenumbers smaller than βc (the growing waves), so that it is not dragged by fluctuations of short waves.

(7) Readouts

  • Current state. Material, temperature (°C and K), Tc and T/Tc, average composition C0, region (stable, metastable, unstable), and elapsed time t (τ0).
  • Curves at this temperature. The binodal and spinodal compositions, for TiO2–SnO2 the coherent spinodal for [001], and ∂2g/∂C2 at C0 (kJ/mol; for the crystal, also the value including strain).
  • Wavelength and growth. λc, λm (in ℓ, with a guide value in nm), Rm, the dominant wavelength 2π/⟨k⟩ measured from the structure factor, the mobility ratio M(T)/M(Tc), and Ea.
  • Microstructure. The volume fractions of the two phases (the value counted with the midpoint of the two binodal compositions as the threshold, and the lever-rule value), the shape of the microstructure, the radius r* of the critical nucleus in the metastable region, the length conversion, and the measured modulation wavelength (TiO2–SnO2 only).
  • Judging the shape of the microstructure. The clusters of the minority phase are counted. If “a cluster spanning the system from end to end makes up at least half of the minority phase” or the elongation measure Q = Σ(perimeter2/16)/Σarea ≥ 5, the shape is “maze-like”; if Q ≥ 2.5, “elongated particles”; otherwise, “droplet-like” (the perimeter is counted as the number of edges of the calculation lattice, so Q is 1 for a square and about 1.3 (4/π) for a circle, which differs from the π/4 of a circle as a continuous figure). In TiO2–SnO2, if the strength of the waves along [001] exceeds three times that along [100], the shape is “lamellar.”
  • Radius of the critical nucleus. A two-dimensional classical-theory estimate r* = σ/(−Δf), where the interfacial energy is σ = 2∫√Δg dφ (the formula of Cahn & Hilliard 1958 written in units of ℓ), and Δf is the driving force at the composition of the nucleus. In parentheses, the radius of the nucleus placed by the rule above is shown (if none can be placed, “no growing nucleus can be placed in this box”). For TiO2–SnO2, the composition of the nucleus and σ are obtained from the free energy ge that includes the strain emin of the softest direction. A round nucleus cannot become a fully formed plate perpendicular to [001], so the strain part of the driving force Δf is estimated with a value between emin and the direction average eavg, namely eN = emin + 0.4(eavg − emin). The coefficient 0.4 is a value chosen to agree with calculations of whether a placed nucleus actually grows, and was not derived from theory. When Δf ≥ 0, the display says “G does not decrease for a coherent nucleus.”
  • Frozen. When the mobility becomes smaller than 1/100 of its value at the preset’s holding temperature, the one-line comment changes to say that the microstructure is “frozen.”

Limits of simulator ⑤ (i) It is a two-dimensional calculation. The “microstructure in which both phases are interconnected in three dimensions” of Cahn 1965 cannot be reproduced in two dimensions (in two dimensions, the two phases cannot both be connected vertically and horizontally at the same time). For this reason the shape of the microstructure is judged using the elongation Q as well. (ii) The time τ0 and length ℓ are teaching conversions. Because measured values of the interdiffusion coefficient and κ have not been confirmed, no conversion to seconds is given. The ℓ = 1 nm for glass is an assumption, and ℓ for TiO2–SnO2 is a value matched to a single measurement. (iii) Borosilicate glass is a schematic that treats a three-component system as pseudo-binary, and effects such as rotation of the tie lines are not included. The critical temperature of 760 °C is a rough guide. (iv) The 200 kJ/mol for Ea, the strength of the fluctuations, and N ≈ 4.33 are schematic or phenomenological values. (v) The coherent strain is approximated by a constant for each direction. The size of a nucleus that grows is a rough guide estimated with the strain eN between those of the softest direction and the direction average (using the fitted coefficient 0.4). In real materials, nuclei that have lost coherency can also form. (vi) λm and Rm of linear theory are quantities for the initial stage only, and the lengthening of the wavelength in the coarsening stage appears only in the numerical calculation. (vii) In the metastable region, nuclei do not form spontaneously. Because the thermal fluctuations are weak and the box is small, within the time range of the screen the compositions outside the spinodal (the metastable region) stay uniform. In real materials, phase separation occurs in the metastable region as well, by (often heterogeneous) nucleation and growth (§8.2 of Kingery). The button that places a nucleus is there to do this on the user’s behalf. (viii) As cautioned in Section 9.7, a resemblance between the microstructure on the screen and a real one is not evidence that spinodal decomposition occurred in the real material.

11. How to Proceed in an Actual Materials Analysis

  1. Choose a thermodynamic model. Prepare Gφ(x,T) for each phase from experiments, first-principles calculations, or a CALPHAD database.
  2. Verify the equilibrium. Check that the phase boundaries obtained from the common tangent, the eutectic and peritectic temperatures, the activities, and the enthalpies of formation are consistent with known data.
  3. Supply kinetic data. Give the diffusion coefficients or atomic mobilities, the interface mobility, the interfacial energy, and the anisotropy.
  4. Choose the fields. Couple only the degrees of freedom you need, such as the composition c, the phase field η, the crystal orientation, the temperature, and the elastic displacement.
  5. Verify lengths and times. Check that the answer converges when the interface width and lattice spacing are changed, and that mass conservation and the decrease of free energy are satisfied.
  6. Compare with experiment. Compare not only the phase fractions but also the dendrite arm spacing, the precipitate radius, the composition profile, and the growth rate.

12. Common Misconceptions

MisconceptionThe correct reading
If the free energy is negative, the state is stableThe zero point is arbitrary. When compared at the same T, p, and overall composition, the lower state is the stable one.
The point of tangency of the common tangent is the phase fractionThe points of tangency are the compositions of the phases. The phase fractions are obtained from the lever rule using the overall composition.
Everything inside the two-phase region is unstableBetween the binodal and the spinodal the state is metastable, and there is a nucleation barrier.
The transformation happens the instant you cross a line of the phase diagramA phase diagram describes equilibrium. In practice there are supercooling, nucleation, and diffusion delays, and the start of the transformation depends on the history.
mol% and mass% can simply be read interchangeablyThe numbers change depending on the atomic weights. The composition units of the mass balance and the thermodynamic model must be matched.
The glass transition temperature is a material constant like the melting pointTg is the temperature at which structural relaxation freezes, and it changes with the cooling rate and the measurement method (§7.2). It is not an equilibrium transition temperature like the melting point.
Glass is a substance that does not crystallize however slowly it is cooledWhether it crystallizes is a matter of the cooling rate q compared with the critical cooling rate Rc. Rc differs greatly from substance to substance; even the three substances in Table 2 differ by more than six orders of magnitude (§7.8).
An interconnected two-phase microstructure means spinodal decompositionAn interconnected structure also forms when grains formed by nucleation coalesce. Judge from the process, such as whether the initial wavelength is uniform and the amplitude grows (§9.7).
If a phase-field image resembles an experiment, it is correctVerification is needed of mass conservation, energy dissipation, interface-width dependence, physical parameters, and the time and length scales.

13. Summary

  1. The G(T) of a pure substance is determined by the entropy and the heat capacity. dG/dT = −S and d²G/dT² = −Cp/T.
  2. For a binary solution, add the end members, the entropy of mixing, and the excess free energy. At high temperatures the RT mixing term dominates, and a positive Ω promotes phase separation.
  3. Equilibrium is determined by the lower convex hull. The common tangent is the geometric expression of the equality of the chemical potentials of both components between phases.
  4. Stacking the points of tangency at each temperature gives the phase diagram. The composition of a phase is the point of tangency, and the fraction of a phase is given by the lever rule.
  5. A phase diagram shows the final candidates for the microstructure, but not its shape or the time it takes. Only when interfaces, diffusion, and nucleation are included does it become microstructure formation.
  6. The phase-field method is a theory that extends the same free energy into space. A conserved composition follows Cahn–Hilliard and a nonconserved order parameter follows Allen–Cahn, and both evolve while dissipating the free energy.
  7. The glass transition is the freezing of relaxation. Tg changes with the cooling rate, and whether the liquid becomes a crystal or a glass is decided by the critical cooling rate Rc, obtained from the nucleation rate I and the growth rate u.
  8. Inside the spinodal, composition waves grow without a barrier. The wave with λm = √2 λc, at which the amplification factor R(β) = −(M̃β2/Nv)[g″ + 2κβ2] is maximal, sets the scale of the microstructure.
