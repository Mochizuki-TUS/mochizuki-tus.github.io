Why Does Perturbation Theory Work When We Proceed “Cautiously”?

This page writes out the derivation of Lecture 4 (Rayleigh–Schrödinger perturbation theory) step by step, along a single straight path: λ expansion → equations order by order → first-order energy → first-order wave function and second-order energy → application to He → when the series breaks down. We treat the time-independent, nondegenerate case.

0. Setup — Mixing an Unsolvable Term into a Solvable Problem with λ

To a solvable problem Ĥ⁽⁰⁾ (all of its eigenvalues En⁽⁰⁾ and eigenfunctions ψn⁽⁰⁾ are known), we want to add a troublesome term ĤP. So we write ĤP = λĤ′ and turn the knob λ cautiously from 0 to 1.

H^= H^(0) +λH^′ , ψn= ψn(0) +λψn(1) +λ2ψn(2) +⋯ , En= En(0) +λEn(1) +λ2En(2) +⋯

For He, Ĥ⁽⁰⁾ is “two electrons without repulsion” (two hydrogen-like atoms, exactly solvable), and ĤP is the electron–electron repulsion e0²/4πε0r12. λ is an auxiliary line of the theory that we set to 1 at the end; it is not a real knob.

1. The Conclusion First (30-Second Version)

  1. Insert the λ expansion into the Schrödinger equation and sort by powers of λ; each order gives one equation.
  2. First order: En⁽¹⁾ = ⟨ψn⁽⁰⁾|Ĥ′|ψn⁽⁰⁾⟩. We do not correct the wave function; we simply take the expectation value of the perturbation.
  3. First-order wave function: other states are mixed in with the coefficient ci = −⟨i|Ĥ′|n⟩/(Ei⁽⁰⁾−En⁽⁰⁾). The larger the matrix element and the closer the levels, the more is mixed in (the denominator effect).
  4. Second order: En⁽²⁾ = −Σi≠n |⟨i|ĤP|n⟩|²/(Ei⁽⁰⁾−En⁽⁰⁾). Always negative for the ground state (the energy goes down).
  5. He: E⁽¹⁾ = −(5/4)Zε1s. For Z = 2, E ≅ −108.85 + 34.02 = −74.833 eV (experiment −79.005 eV, error 5 %). Adding the second order (−4.29 eV) gives −79.12 eV.
  6. The series has a range of validity (radius of convergence). For the two-level model it is λc = Δ/2V. With degeneracy (Δ = 0) it cannot be used at all.

2. The Equations Order by Order

Substitute the expansions into (Ĥ⁽⁰⁾ + λĤ′)ψn = Enψn and compare the coefficients of λ⁰, λ¹, λ² … on both sides (since λ is arbitrary, the equation must hold independently at each order).

H^(0) ψn(0) = En(0) ψn(0) … ① (λ⁰: unperturbed) H^′ ψn(0) + H^(0) ψn(1) = En(1) ψn(0) + En(0) ψn(1) … ② (λ¹) H^′ ψn(1) + H^(0) ψn(2) = En(2) ψn(0) + En(1) ψn(1) + En(0) ψn(2) … ③ (λ²)

Equation ① is just the unperturbed problem. The unknowns are En⁽¹⁾ and ψn⁽¹⁾ in ②, and En⁽²⁾ and ψn⁽²⁾ in ③. We expand the correction to the wave function in a complete set (the same idea as a Fourier series).

ψn(1) = ∑i ci ψi(0)

3. The First-Order Energy — Applying ⟨ψn⁽⁰⁾| from the Left

Insert the expansions into ② and apply ⟨ψn⁽⁰⁾| from the left. Using Ĥ⁽⁰⁾ψi⁽⁰⁾ = Ei⁽⁰⁾ψi⁽⁰⁾ and the orthogonality ⟨ψn⁽⁰⁾|ψi⁽⁰⁾⟩ = δni, the terms containing ψ⁽¹⁾ cancel against each other in the components with i = n and vanish, leaving

En(1) = ⟨ ψn(0) |H^′| ψn(0) ⟩ ⟹ En≅ En(0) + ⟨ ψn(0) |H^P| ψn(0) ⟩ … ④
First order “measures without breaking” Equation ④ contains no ψ⁽¹⁾. Before correcting the wave function, we measure the perturbation in the uncorrected state—that is first order. It is the same operation as the “point Z′ = Z” we saw on the variational-principle page, so for He the two values coincide.

4. The First-Order Wave Function and the Second-Order Energy — Where the Denominator Effect Comes From

Now apply ⟨ψj⁽⁰⁾| (j ≠ n) to ② from the left. This determines cj:

cj = − ⟨ψj(0) |H^′| ψn(0)⟩ Ej(0) − En(0) … ⑤

The larger the matrix element, and the smaller the denominator (the level difference), the more strongly that state is mixed in. This equation holds the most important intuition of perturbation theory. Substituting ⑤ into ③ and repeating the same operation gives the second-order energy.

En≅ En(0) + ⟨ψn(0) |H^P| ψn(0)⟩ − ∑i≠n |⟨ψi(0) |H^P| ψn(0)⟩|2 Ei(0) − En(0) … ⑥
A note on sign conventions The lecture (Lecture 4) follows the convention, as in ⑥, of pulling the minus sign out in front of the sum and writing the denominator as Ei⁽⁰⁾ − En⁽⁰⁾. Some textbooks write +Σ|…|²/(En⁽⁰⁾ − Ei⁽⁰⁾) instead, but it is the same equation. For the ground state (n = 0) all the denominators are positive, so the second-order correction is always negative—it can be read as “if there are states it can mix with around it, the ground state always gains.”

5. Applying It to He — A Six-Fold Integral Reduces to a Single Formula

The unperturbed state is a product of hydrogen-like 1s functions, ψ⁽⁰⁾ = φ⁽⁰⁾(r₁)φ⁽⁰⁾(r₂), φ⁽⁰⁾ = (1/√π)(Z/aB)3/2e−Zr/aB, E⁽⁰⁾ = 2Z²ε1s. The first-order correction ④ is a six-fold integral, but the integral formula

∬ e−a(r1+r2) r12 dV1dV2 = 20π2a5 (a=2Z/aB)

settles it in one stroke (the derivation is in Lecture 4: treat r2 as a constant and integrate over r1 first). Rearranging gives

E(1) = 5e02Z 32πε0aB = −54Zε1s , E0≅ (2Z2−5Z4) ε1s … ⑦

The second-order coefficient is a constant independent of Z (1/Z expansion) In atomic units (Hartree), the series for a He-like ion is E = −Z² + (5/8)Z − 0.15767 + 0.00870/Z + …. That is, E⁽²⁾ = −0.15767 Hartree = −4.291 eV is the same for every ion. The first order goes as the first power of Z and the second order as the zeroth power of Z—the fact that the perturbation gets “relatively” smaller as 1/Z shows up in the Z-dependence of the order. That is why perturbation theory works better for heavier ions.

6. When Does the Series Break Down? — Exactly, with a Two-Level Model

The perturbation series is not a cure-all. We can check this with the exactly solvable two-level model Ĥ(λ) = [[0, λV], [λV, Δ]]. The exact solution is E∓ = (Δ ∓ √(Δ² + 4λ²V²))/2. The point where the expression under the √ becomes zero, λ = ±iΔ/2V (a complex number!), is a singularity, so the radius of convergence of the power series is λc = Δ/2V. For λ < λc, going to higher order gives increasingly accurate results; for λ > λc, going to higher order makes the series diverge.

The perturbation series for the ground-state energy of the two-level model, summed to second order, fourth order, …: the partial sums (bars) and the exact solution (horizontal line). Make λ/λc smaller or larger than 1 and watch the behavior of the series change completely.

⚠ With degeneracy, it cannot be used from the start This is because the denominators Ei⁽⁰⁾ − En⁽⁰⁾ in ⑤ and ⑥ become zero. Within a degenerate subspace, we first build the matrix of the perturbation and diagonalize it (= solve the secular equation), and only then rebuild the series (degenerate perturbation theory). “Build a matrix and diagonalize it” is exactly the operation on the secular equation and diagonalization pages.

7. The Physics of Second-Order Perturbation — Moving Even the Structure

The second-order correction ⑥ is a treasure trove of applications. One that Lecture 4 also touches on is the second-order Jahn–Teller effect (pseudo Jahn–Teller effect, SOJT): when the crystal is distorted by a displacement Q, the electronic system feels a perturbation λĤ′ ∝ Q. The ground state is then lowered at second order by −Q²|⟨e|Ĥ′|g⟩|²/Δ. If this wins over the increase in elastic energy +½KQ², the distorted structure is more stable—the smaller the gap Δ between the occupied and unoccupied levels, the more easily a material distorts. It is a tool for discussing the origin of spontaneous polarization in ferroelectrics.

And when we want to “sum the perturbation systematically to infinite order,” the bookkeeping device is Feynman diagrams and the Dyson equation. The first-order Hartree and Fock diagrams, the ten second-order diagrams—the Green’s function page can be read as a continuation of this page.
