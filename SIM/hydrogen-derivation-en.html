How Do We Solve the Schrödinger Equation of the Hydrogen Atom?

This page writes out the derivation in lecture notes (15)(16) step by step. Along a single path — separation of variables → three ordinary differential equations → the boundary condition of each — the three quantum numbers n, l and m come out entirely from boundary conditions. The names 1s, 2s, 2p, … and the restriction l ≤ n − 1 are also settled along the way.

0. Setup

An electron of mass me moves in the Coulomb potential created by a proton placed at the origin:

V(r)= − 14πε0 e02r

The equation we solve is the stationary-state Schrödinger equation

− ℏ22me ∇2ψ+Vψ=Eψ

In addition, for the azimuthal angle φ we impose the condition that the wave function returns to itself after one full turn.

ψ(r,θ,φ) = ψ(r,θ,φ+2π)
All of the quantization comes from boundary conditions It does not come from anything mysterious; it comes from the following three boundary conditions.
• The condition that the function returns to itself after one turn in φ → m is an integer
• The condition of no divergence at θ = 0, π → l is a non-negative integer and |m| ≤ l
• The condition of vanishing as r → ∞ → n is a positive integer and l ≤ n − 1
It is exactly the same logic as what we did for the one-dimensional well and the harmonic oscillator.

1. The Conclusion First (30-Second Version)

  1. Because of the spherical symmetry, we switch to polar coordinates and separate ψ = R(r)Y(θ,φ). The separation constant is set to l(l+1).
  2. Next we separate Y = Θ(θ)Φ(φ). The separation constant is set to m2.
  3. Φ″ + m2Φ = 0 gives Φ = eimφ. For it to return to itself after one turn, we need m ∈ ℤ.
  4. The equation for Θ is the associated Legendre equation. For the solution not to diverge at the two poles, l must be a non-negative integer and |m| ≤ l.
  5. For R, factoring out the far-field form e−κr gives the associated Laguerre equation. For the series to terminate after finitely many terms, n must be a positive integer and l ≤ n − 1.
  6. In that case E = −mee04/8ε02h2n2 = −13.606 eV/n2.
How the names are assigned n = 1, 2, 3, … is the principal quantum number, and l = 0, 1, 2, 3, … corresponds to s, p, d, f. That is, 1s means (n,l) = (1,0) and 3d means (3,2). Since l ≤ n − 1, 1p and 2d do not exist — this too comes out in the course of the derivation. Also, l is called the azimuthal quantum number, and m = −l, …, 0, …, +l is called the magnetic quantum number. In the simulator, the orbital buttons correspond to choosing the pair (n, l), and the buttons below them choose m.

2. Separation of Variables — Lecture Notes (15)

2.1 The Laplacian in polar coordinates

∇2= 1r2 ∂∂r (r2 ∂∂r) + 1r2sin⁡θ ∂∂θ (sin⁡θ ∂∂θ) + 1r2sin2⁡θ ∂2∂φ2

2.2 Separating r from (θ,φ)

Substitute ψ = R(r)Y(θ,φ), multiply both sides by −2me/ℏ2, then multiply by r2 and divide by RY. The equation splits into one in r only and one in (θ,φ) only.

1R (r2 d2Rdr2 +2r dRdr) + 2meℏ2 ( e024πε0r +E) r2 ⏟ depends on r only = − [ 1Y ( ∂2Y∂θ2 + 1tan⁡θ ∂Y∂θ) + 1sin2⁡θ 1Y ∂2Y∂φ2 ] ⏟ depends on θ, φ only

The left side is a function of r only and the right side a function of θ, φ only. Because two functions of independent variables are identically equal, both must equal the same constant. Following the notes, we set this constant to l(l+1) (we will see later that l is an integer, so this choice makes the equations neat).

2.3 Separating θ from φ

Next we separate Y = Θ(θ)Φ(φ), multiply by sin2θ and rearrange. This time the equation splits into one in θ only and one in φ only. Setting this separation constant to m2, we obtain three ordinary differential equations.

1R (r2 d2Rdr2 +2rdRdr) + 2meℏ2 (e024πε0r +E)r2 =l(l+1) … ① sin2⁡θΘ ( d2Θdθ2 + 1tan⁡θ dΘdθ) +l(l+1) sin2⁡θ =m2 … ② 1Φ d2Φdφ2 = −m2 … ③

3. The Radial Direction ① — Where n Is Born

3.1 Setting R = S/r to get a one-dimensional form

Multiplying ① by R and rearranging gives r2R″ + 2rR′ + [ … ]R = 0. Now set R(r) = S(r)/r, and the first-derivative term disappears neatly.

r2 d2Rdr2 +2r dRdr =r d2Sdr2

Now, defining κ and τ by 2meE/ℏ2 = −κ2, mee02/2πε0ℏ2 = τ (the electron is bound, so E < 0 and therefore κ2 > 0), we obtain

d2Sdr2 + [ −κ2 +τr −l(l+1)r2 ]S=0
This is exactly a one-dimensional Schrödinger equation With Veff(r) = −e02/4πε0r + ℏ2l(l+1)/2mer2 as the effective potential, this equation describes a one-dimensional particle moving in that potential. The second term is exactly the centrifugal potential of classical mechanics, and the larger l is, the less the electron can approach the origin. This is why, in tab ②, orbitals with larger l have a thinner inner tail.

3.2 Looking at the far-field form first

At very large r, both 1/r and 1/r2 are negligible, so S″ − κ2S = 0, that is, S ≃ Aeκr + Be−κr. Since S → 0 must hold as r → ∞, we need A = 0, so S ∝ e−κr is required. So we factor out this factor and set S(r) = X(r) e−κr, which gives

d2Xdr2 −2κ dXdr + [τr −l(l+1)r2 ]X=0

Making it dimensionless with x = 2κr puts the equation in a tidy form.

d2Xdx2 − dXdx + [ τ2κ 1x − l(l+1)x2 ]X=0

3.3 Expanding in a series (Frobenius)

Since x = 0 is a regular singular point, there is a solution of the form X(x) = Σk≥0 akxk+λ, and substituting it in and taking the coefficient of the lowest order (xλ−2) gives

{λ(λ−1) −l(l+1)} a0=0 ⟹ λ=l+1, −l

As for λ = −l, if l ≥ 1 then |X| → ∞ as x → 0, and even for l = 0, if X(0) ≠ 0 then R = S/r ∼ 1/r fails to satisfy the equation at the origin (∇2(1/r) produces a delta function), so in either case we discard this solution. Hence λ = l + 1, that is, X = xl+1F(x). Putting this into the original equation and dividing by xl gives

x d2Fdx2 + {2(l+1)−x} dFdx + {τ2κ −(l+1)} F=0

This is the associated Laguerre equation xy″ + (N+1−x)y′ + (m′−N)y = 0 once we identify N = 2l+1 and m′ = τ/2κ + l.

3.4 Quantization happens here

The series must terminate after finitely many terms The solution of the associated Laguerre equation is a polynomial (terminating at a finite order) only when m′ is an integer greater than or equal to N. If it does not terminate, F behaves like ex, so S = Xe−κr ∝ e+κr and it diverges as r → ∞. We want a normalizable solution, so this is not allowed.

So we set m′ = n + l (n ∈ ℕ). Then

τ2κ=n , m′≥N ⟹ n+l≥2l+1 ⟹ l≤n−1
This is why 1p and 2d do not exist l ≤ n − 1 comes from the condition for the series to terminate. For n = 1 only l = 0 is allowed (1s only), for n = 2 we have l = 0, 1 (2s, 2p), and for n = 3 we have 3s, 3p, 3d. This is the starting point of the structure of the periodic table.

Solving τ/2κ = n for κ gives κ = τ/2n. Going back to κ2 = −2meE/ℏ2, we obtain

En= − ℏ22me κ2= − mee04 8ε02h2 1n2 = − 13.606eVn2

E is determined by n alone and depends on neither l nor m. This is why 2s and 2p have the same energy, and 3s, 3p and 3d have the same energy. Counting l = 0, 1, …, n−1 and m = −l, …, +l, the states with the same n are Σ(2l+1) = n2-fold degenerate (2n2-fold if spin is included). The “degeneracy” shown in the simulator’s readout is this number.

Seeing the quantization. We integrate the equation for u(r) = rR(r), u″ = {l(l+1)/r2 − 2/r − 2E}u (atomic units), numerically outward from the origin, and plot how much of the far tail remains relative to the height of the peak, namely u(rf)/max|u| (the overall size of the wave function is fixed by normalization, so only this ratio is meaningful). The tail dies away only when E = −1/2n2 exactly, and rises if it is off even slightly. The horizontal axis is ν = 1/√(−2E), so the eigenvalues line up at equal spacing, ν = 1, 2, 3, …, and you can also see that there are no eigenvalues at ν ≤ l (l ≤ n − 1). It is the same experiment as the one on the harmonic oscillator page.

4. The Polar Angle Direction ② — Legendre

Rewriting ② in terms of Θ gives sin2θ Θ″ + sinθcosθ Θ′ + {l(l+1)sin2θ − m2}Θ = 0. Now change variables to x = cosθ. Using dΘ/dθ = −sinθ·dΘ/dx twice, we get

(1−x2) d2Θdx2 −2x dΘdx + {l(l+1) −m21−x2} Θ=0

This is the associated Legendre equation. The solution that can remain bounded at both poles is, using Rodrigues’ formula,

Θ(x)= D0 (1−x2) |m|/2 d|m| dx|m| { 12ll! dldxl (x2−1)l }
The restrictions on l and m appear here For the solution not to diverge at x = ±1 (θ = 0, π, that is, the north and south poles), l must be a non-negative integer. Moreover, since after differentiating l times we differentiate |m| more times, |m| > l would make the result identically 0. Therefore |m| ≤ l. This is the origin of the degeneracy of 2l+1 states, m = −l, …, 0, …, +l.

5. The Azimuthal Direction ③ — m Becomes an Integer

③ is exactly the equation of simple harmonic motion: Φ(φ) = c0eimφ + d0e−imφ. If we impose the boundary condition Φ(φ) = Φ(φ+2π) for all φ, then, since eimφ and e−imφ are linearly independent, coefficient by coefficient we get

c0= c0ei2mπ , d0= d0e−i2mπ ⟹ e±i2mπ=1 ⟹ m∈ℤ

The sign of m does not matter; if we push the plus and minus signs onto m itself, everything can be combined into one single form: Φ(φ) = c0eimφ (m = 0, ±1, ±2, …)

“Returning after one turn” alone quantizes the angular momentum Applying Lz = −iℏ∂/∂φ to this Φ gives LzΦ = mℏΦ. In other words, the z-component of the angular momentum can only be an integer multiple of ℏ. The reason the cloud rotates when you select “Complex m = ±|m|” in tab ① of the simulator is that this angular momentum shows up as a real flow of probability (|ψ|2 itself does not depend on φ, so the shape of the distribution does not change even as it rotates; see Section 8 for details).

6. Multiplying the Three Together

Putting ①②③ together gives ψ(r,θ,φ) = R(r)Θ(θ)Φ(φ). The constants are fixed by the normalization ∫|ψ|2dV = 1, and with the Bohr radius aB = 4πε0ℏ2/mee02 = 0.52918 Å we obtain the form in lecture notes (16).

Rnl(r)= 1aB (n−l−1)! n2{(n+l)!}3 e−r/aBnr ∑k=2l+1n+l (−1)k {(n+l)!}2 (k−2l−1)! k! (n+l−k)! (2rnaB) k−l
About the overall sign This sum starts from the term k = 2l+1, so (−1)2l+1 = −1, and as it stands R < 0 as r → 0. The table in lecture notes (16) (1s = 2aB−3/2e−r/aB, etc.) starts from a positive value, so in the simulator the overall sign is flipped to match the table. This comes from a difference in convention for the Laguerre polynomials (the old definition versus the modern one) and has no effect at all on |ψ|2.

8. Real and Complex Orbitals — Which One “Rotates”?

For m ≠ 0 the table above is written in the form cosmφ, sinmφ, that is, as the sum and difference of e+imφ and e−imφ. Chemistry uses this form because the shapes are real and easy to draw, but these are not eigenstates of Lz.

The flow of probability (the probability current density) is

j= ℏme Im⁡ (ψ*∇ψ) ⟹ vφ= ℏmmersin⁡θ

For a complex orbital ψnlm ∝ eimφ, this does not vanish. Writing the distance from the z axis as r⊥ = rsinθ, the angular velocity is dφ/dt = ℏm/mer⊥2, that is, a differential rotation that is faster toward the inside. Since |ψ|2 does not depend on φ, this flow does not change the distribution at all (the continuity equation is satisfied automatically). Integrating gives exactly ⟨Lz⟩ = mℏ.

For real orbitals the flow is zero px and dxy are superpositions of +|m| and −|m|, so the oppositely directed flows cancel completely and j = 0. The same holds for the m = 0 orbitals (s, pz, dz² …). A probability flow corresponding to the picture of “the electron circling the proton” exists only when you choose a complex orbital. Even so, the “flow” here is the j that takes care of the conservation of probability density, and it does not mean that the electron traces out a single classical orbit. Tab ① of the simulator displays this distinction exactly as it is.

10. Summary

  • All three quantum numbers come out of boundary conditions. m ∈ ℤ comes from the condition “returns to itself after one turn,” |m| ≤ l and l = 0, 1, 2, … from “does not diverge at the two poles,” and l ≤ n − 1 and n ∈ ℕ from “vanishes as r → ∞.”
  • En = −13.606 eV/n2 is determined by n alone. 2s and 2p are degenerate because the Coulomb potential is exactly 1/r, and in many-electron atoms this degeneracy is lifted.
  • The shells are the “radial distribution function” P(r) = r2|R|2. Looking at |R|2 alone, 1s is largest at the origin, but multiplying by the area of the spherical shell ∝ r2 produces a peak at r = aB. This is where the Bohr radius appears.
  • The number of nodes is always n − 1. There are n − l − 1 radial nodes and l angular nodes (surfaces where Y vanishes), and adding them gives n − 1 regardless of l. Counting l angular nodes is valid for the real orbitals shown in the simulator and in the table in Section 7. For a complex orbital Y ∝ eimφ, |Y| vanishes only on l − |m| cones (Section 8).
  • The electron is not running around on a thin spherical surface. It is smeared out with some thickness, and the density of that smear is |ψ|2. In tab ① of the simulator, thousands of points are scattered according to that distribution.
