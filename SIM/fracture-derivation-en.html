The Theory of Fracture — Deriving the Theoretical Strength, Griffith’s Equation, Fracture Toughness, and Weibull Statistics

This page is the companion explanation to fracture-simulator-en.html. The simulator showed “a gap of tens to hundreds of times between the theoretical strength and the actual strength,” “strength that falls as the −1/2 power of the crack length,” and “strength that scatters even when specimens are made under the same conditions, and the Weibull modulus m.” This page derives where these come from, without skipping intermediate steps. All you need is Hooke’s law, the elastic strain energy σ2/2E, finding a maximum by differentiation, and the first rule of probability that “the probability of independent events is a product.” The page is written with second- and third-year undergraduates in mind. The physics of the phenomena that the simulator makes visible — the necking of a tensile specimen, the stress field around an elliptical hole, the plastic zone at a crack tip and the atomic picture, the distribution of strengths that builds up as specimens are broken one after another, the stress distribution in a bending test, the temperature and thermal-stress distributions in thermal shock, and the Vickers indentation with the cracks running out from its corners — is also derived in §1.1–§1.5, §3.2–§3.5, §5.5–§6.7, §8.5, §9.5–§9.6, §10.2–§10.5, and §12 (for the complex functions of §3.2 and the numerical method of §10.4, it is enough to understand the idea and how to use the result).

The backbone of this page The overall picture of how ceramics fracture becomes clearer if it is built up in the following three steps. ① The strength needed to tear the bonds apart (the theoretical strength: about 44 GPa for alumina-equivalent values) is tens to hundreds of times the actual strength (hundreds of MPa) — about 125 times for alumina. ② Cracks fill that gap, and the strength is set by the size of the most dangerous crack (Griffith: the energy balance). ③ The size of the crack scatters from specimen to specimen, so strength can be stated only in terms of probability (Weibull: the weakest link). Of these, “deriving Griffith’s equation from the energy balance” (§4) and “deriving the Weibull modulus m from the distribution of flaw sizes” (§7) are the core of this page.

1. Introduction — How Ceramics Break

Drop a teacup on the floor and it breaks. Drop a metal spoon and it may bend, but it will not break. A tensile test makes the difference clear: a ceramic breaks suddenly at some stress while stress and strain are still proportional. The strain just before fracture is only 0.092 % for alumina and 0.071 % for soda-lime glass. A metal, after yielding, stretches a great deal by plastic deformation and fails only after necking (§1.1–§1.5).

The root of this difference is whether dislocations can move. Metallic bonds have no directionality, and atomic planes slip over one another relatively easily. In ionic crystals, ions of the same sign come face to face partway through slip, which costs a lot of energy, and in covalent crystals the bonds are directional, so slip requires the bonds to be switched over. As a result, in polycrystalline ceramics at room temperature dislocations hardly move and there are too few independent slip systems (single crystals such as MgO do slip a little even at room temperature), so the crack tip is never rounded off by plastic deformation. A crack whose tip stays sharp therefore governs the strength.

The elastic energy stored per unit volume up to fracture is the area of the triangle under the stress–strain line, σf2/2E. It is 0.16 MJ m−3 for alumina, 0.018 MJ m−3 for glass, and even for Y-TZP only 2.4 MJ m−3. In a metal the work of plastic deformation is added, so it absorbs far more energy than this before it fails. This page uses the values in Table 1 as examples.

1.1 The tensile test — nominal stress and true stress

Tab ① of the simulator carries out a student-laboratory tensile test on the screen. A specimen whose parallel section has length (gauge length) L0 and cross-sectional area A0 is pulled with a load F, and the elongation ΔL is measured. What the testing machine records directly is F and ΔL, so the first quantities we obtain are these divided by the original dimensions.

(1) σn=FA0 , e=ΔLL0 (nominal stress and nominal strain)

However, as the specimen is pulled it becomes thinner, so the stress actually carried by the current cross section A is F/A. The strain, likewise, is defined by adding up the increments dL/L as the length grows from L to L + dL. These are the true stress σt and the true strain εt.

(2) σt=FA , εt=∫L0LdL′L′ =ln⁡LL0=ln⁡(1+e)

In the plastic deformation of a metal the atoms merely rearrange, and the volume hardly changes. Setting AL = A0L0, we have A = A0L0/L = A0/(1 + e), so

(3) σt=FA0·A0A =σn(1+e)=σneεt , εt=ln⁡A0A

In the elastic range the volume does change slightly, but the strain is only about 0.1 %, so the difference can be neglected. After necking begins, the cross section is no longer uniform along the specimen, so the true stress and true strain are taken from the cross section at the thinnest place Amin, as σt = F/Amin, εt = ln(A0/Amin) (the readouts of the simulator use this definition, too).

1.2 Work hardening — Hollomon’s equation

Even after a metal yields, dislocations become tangled with one another and harder to move, so a larger stress is needed to keep deforming it (work hardening). The relation between true stress and true strain is well described for many metals by the following power function (Hollomon, 1945).

(4) σt=Kεtn

n is called the work-hardening exponent and K the strength coefficient (the K and n in this section are different quantities from the stress intensity factor KI in §5 and the number of flaws n in §7). In the simulator, after the elastic line σt = Eεt reaches the yield stress σy, we use σt = max(σy, Kεtn). Applying Eq. (4) as it stands at the yield strain σy/E gives 209 MPa for structural steel, which falls below σy = 250 MPa, so the stress is held constant at σy up to the strain ε1 = (σy/K)1/n at which Kεtn catches up with σy (0.36 % for steel, 0.47 % for the Al alloy). This is a formal stand-in for the yield-point elongation seen in mild steel (the flat part while a Lüders band travels along the specimen). n and K are determined, using the result of §1.3 below, from the tensile strength in Table 1 and the uniform elongation (18 % for structural steel and 8 % for the aluminum alloy; rough values for teaching).

1.3 When does necking begin? — The Considère condition

Work hardening makes the material stronger, but at the same time the cross section gets thinner. How the load F = σtA changes is decided by the competition between these two effects. Differentiating the product gives

(5) dF=Adσt+σtdA

Differentiating the constant-volume condition AL = constant gives L dA + A dL = 0, that is, dA/A = −dL/L = −dεt. Putting this in, we get

(6) dF=A(dσt−σtdεt)

The load is at its maximum (dF = 0) when

(7) dσtdεt=σt (Considère condition)

(Considère, 1885). The meaning is as follows. Suppose that one place somewhere in the specimen happens to become slightly thinner. The stress rises there, but that place is also strained extra, so it becomes stronger through work hardening. As long as the work-hardening rate dσt/dεt is greater than σt, “getting stronger” wins, the thin place stops deforming, and the deformation spreads over the whole specimen (uniform elongation). When the work-hardening rate falls below σt, “getting thinner” wins and the deformation concentrates at that one place. This is necking. As Eq. (6) shows, this coincides with the point where the load turns from increasing to decreasing, that is, with the highest point of the nominal stress–nominal strain curve.

For Hollomon’s equation (4), dσt/dεt = nKεtn−1 = (n/εt)σt, so Eq. (7) becomes

(8) nεtσt=σt ⟹ εt=n , σUTS=σn|εt=n =Knne−n

(the second is obtained by putting εt = n into σn = σte−εt from Eq. (3)). This is a simple and important result: the true strain at which necking begins is equal to the work-hardening exponent n. Conversely, if the uniform elongation eu (the nominal strain at the highest point) is known, n = ln(1 + eu), and if the tensile strength σUTS is known, K = σUTS/(nne−n). For structural steel (eu = 18 %, σUTS = 400 MPa), n = 0.166 and K = 636 MPa, and the true stress at the onset of necking is 472 MPa, and the work-hardening rate there is indeed 472 MPa as well. For the aluminum alloy (8 %, 310 MPa), n = 0.077 and K = 408 MPa, and both quantities are 334.8 MPa. The Al alloy, with its small work-hardening exponent, necks earlier. Rewriting in terms of the nominal strain gives dσt/de = σt/(1 + e), so on the graph of σt versus e, the point of tangency of the tangent line drawn to the curve from the point e = −1 is the highest point. This is the Considère construction found in many textbooks. In the simulator, if you check “Also show true stress–true strain and the work-hardening rate (Considère condition),” the same point is shown as the intersection of the dσt/dεt curve (green) and the σt curve.

1.4 Stress inside the neck — Bridgman’s correction

Once a neck forms, the outer profile at the thinnest cross section is curved, and that cross section is sandwiched from above and below by the thicker parts. The thin part tries to contract sideways, but the thicker material around it holds it back, so inside the cross section tensile stresses (radial and circumferential) arise in addition to the stress along the axis. As a result, the axial stress σzz is no longer uniform across the cross section but higher at the center and lower at the surface. Bridgman (an analysis collected in his 1952 book) derived, for the neck of a round bar, with a the radius of the smallest cross section and R the radius of curvature of the neck profile, the following distribution (r is the distance from the center and σeq is the flow stress (equivalent stress) of the material).

(9) σzz(r)=σeq [1+ln⁡(1+a2−r22aR)] , σrr=σθθ=σeqln⁡(1+a2−r22aR)

Let us compute the average stress σ̄ = F/πa2, the load F divided by the minimum cross section πa2, from this distribution. Substituting u = a2 − r2 (du = −2r dr), ∫0a ln(1 + (a2 − r2)/2aR) 2r dr = ∫0a2 ln(1 + u/2aR) du = 2aR[(1 + x) ln(1 + x) − x] (with x = a/2R; we used ∫ ln t dt = t ln t − t), so

(10) σ¯=1πa2∫0aσzz2πrdr =σeq[1+(1+x)ln⁡(1+x)−xx] =σeq(1+2Ra)ln⁡(1+a2R)

The coefficient on the right-hand side, B = (1 + 2R/a) ln(1 + a/2R), is at least 1 and returns to 1 as a/R → 0 (no neck). In other words, the F/Amin measured after necking makes the flow stress of the material look higher by the factor B, and dividing it back, σeq = σ̄/B, is Bridgman’s correction. For the structural steel model just before fracture (a/R = 0.646), B = 1.146, and in Eq. (9) the value at the center is 1.12 times σ̄ and the value at the surface is 0.87 times σ̄. The fact that the stress is highest at the center and is tensile in all three directions is the reason that ductile fracture starts at the center (see §1.5 below).

Bridgman’s equation is an approximation Eq. (9) is the solution for a round bar that assumes the strain is uniform within the minimum cross section, the neck profile is a circular arc near the minimum cross section, and the material yields according to the von Mises condition, and it departs from finite-element calculations as the neck deepens. The specimen in the simulator is a flat plate, but to show the shape of the distribution within the cross section, the round-bar equation is borrowed along the width direction. The load F itself is calculated from the extended Hollomon equation F = AminKεtn, and B is used only to redistribute the stress within the cross section (for the reason and the model of the neck shape, see Appendix A.9).

1.5 Ductile fracture and brittle fracture

Ductile fracture (metals). Metals contain scattered inclusions such as oxides and sulfides, and second-phase particles. As plastic deformation proceeds, particles crack or the interfaces between particles and the matrix peel apart, and small cavities (voids) form. The voids are stretched and grow with plastic deformation, and they grow faster where the stress is tensile in all three directions. The center of the neck is, as Eq. (9) shows, exactly where those conditions are met, so the voids grow and join at the center and become a central crack perpendicular to the axis. Finally the outer ring slips along a surface at about 45° and tears off, producing the fracture surface called a cup-and-cone. Seen in an electron microscope, the fracture surface is covered with pits left by the voids (dimples). A model that explains the magnitude of ductility from the amount of inclusions and the nucleation and growth of voids was presented by Le Roy et al. (1981). The enlarged (schematic) view in the simulator draws this sequence, but the way the voids grow is shown only as a shape; the growth equation is not solved.

Brittle fracture (ceramics). Dislocations hardly move, so the stress rises along a straight line, and at the instant the stress intensity factor at the tip of a small crack that was there from the start reaches the fracture toughness (§5), the crack crosses the cross section while accelerating. The fracture surface is a flat surface perpendicular to the direction of the load, with no trace of plastic deformation. Taking the values in Table 1 and a surface crack (Y = 1.12), the crack depth a = (KIC/1.12σf)2/π (Eq. (43)) that breaks at the strength σf is 25 μm for alumina, 16 μm for Y-TZP, and 57 μm for soda-lime glass. The enlarged view in the simulator draws a crack of this depth.

The energy absorbed before fracture (the area under the nominal stress–nominal strain curve) is, in the simulator’s model, 94 MJ m−3 for structural steel and 35 MJ m−3 for the aluminum alloy, which are about 590 times and about 215 times that of alumina (0.16 MJ m−3), respectively. Although the strength itself is higher for alumina (350 MPa) than the yield stress of steel (250 MPa), what differs decisively is the energy that can be absorbed before fracture.

2. Estimating the Theoretical Strength (Orowan)

First we estimate the stress needed to pull apart atomic planes in a perfect crystal with no cracks or flaws — the theoretical strength. Let the spacing of the atomic planes be a0 and the displacement of a plane from its equilibrium position be x. As x increases, the stress first rises according to Hooke’s law and, after reaching a maximum σth, returns to 0 as the bonds break one after another. We approximate this hump by a half period of a sine function (Orowan’s approximation).

(11) σ(x)=σth sin⁡2πxλ (0≤x≤λ/2)

λ/2 is the displacement at which the stress returns to 0 (the bond is completely broken). There are two unknowns, σth and λ, so we prepare two conditions.

2.1 Condition 1: Hooke’s law at small displacements

For x ≪ λ, sin θ ≈ θ, so Eq. (11) gives σ ≈ σth·2πx/λ. On the other hand, the strain is ε = x/a0, so Hooke’s law reads σ = Eε = Ex/a0. Equating the coefficients of x, we get

(12) σth2πλ =Ea0 ⟹ σth=Eλ2πa0

2.2 Condition 2: The work of pulling apart equals the energy of the two new surfaces

The work done by the stress in pulling an atomic plane of unit area completely apart is the area under the curve of Eq. (11). Pulling apart creates two surfaces, so this is set equal to 2γs.

(13) ∫0λ/2 σthsin⁡2πxλdx =σth [−λ2πcos⁡2πxλ]0λ/2 =σthλ2π(1+1) =σthλπ =2γs

(We used cos π = −1 and cos 0 = 1.) Therefore λ = 2πγs/σth.

2.3 Eliminating λ

Substituting this λ into the right-hand expression of Eq. (12), we get

(14) σth=E2πa0 ·2πγsσth =Eγsa0σth ⟹ σth2=Eγsa0
(15) σth=Eγsa0

Putting in the alumina-equivalent values E = 380 GPa, γs = 1.0 J m−2, and a0 = 0.2 nm gives

(16) σth= 380×109×1.00.2×10−9 =1.9×1021 ≈4.36×1010Pa =43.6GPa≈E8.7

The strength of alumina in Table 1 is 350 MPa, so the theoretical strength is about 125 times the actual strength. Here λ = 2πγs/σth = 0.144 nm, and the displacement λ/2 at which the bond is completely broken is 0.072 nm, which is 0.36 times a0 — a reasonable size: the bond breaks when the interatomic distance is stretched by a little under 40 %.

Misconception 2: The coefficient “E/8.7” in Eq. (16) changes with how the approximation is made The sine function in Eq. (11) is a rough stand-in for the shape of the bond-force curve. With a more realistic interatomic potential, or with different values of γs or a0, the coefficient changes. However, the conclusion that the theoretical strength is tens to hundreds of times the actual strength does not change, whatever the coefficient. It was Griffith’s experiment (1921) that showed that the strength of thin glass fibers, which have few flaws, is far higher than that of thick rods.

3. Stress Concentration (Inglis)

The first clue to explaining this large gap is that stress concentrates at the tip of a hole or crack. Inglis (1913) obtained the elastic solution for an elliptical hole of major axis 2a and minor axis 2b in an infinitely wide plate under a uniform tensile stress σ applied perpendicular to the major axis, and showed that the stress at the end of the major axis is

(17) σmax=σ(1+2ab)

For a circular hole (a = b) this is 3σ, the well-known “stress concentration factor of 3.” Near the end (a, 0) of the major axis of the ellipse (x/a)2 + (y/b)2 = 1, the ellipse can be approximated by the parabola y2 ≈ 2(b2/a)(a − x); comparing this with y2 ≈ 2ρ(a − x) for a circle of radius ρ gives the radius of curvature ρ = b2/a. From this, b = √(aρ), and therefore 2a/b can be rewritten as 2a/√(aρ) = 2√(a/ρ).

(18) σmax=σ(1+2aρ) ≈ 2σaρ (a≫ρ)

What determines the degree of concentration is not the size of the hole itself but “the ratio of the length to the sharpness of the tip.” For example, if the tip of a crack with a = 10 μm is as sharp as a single atom (ρ = a0 = 0.2 nm), then √(a/ρ) = √(5×104) = 223.6, and the concentration factor is 1 + 2 × 223.6 ≈ 448. Merely applying σ = 100 MPa from outside brings the stress at the tip to 44.8 GPa — which exceeds the theoretical strength of 43.6 GPa in Eq. (16).

3.1 Combining Orowan and Inglis

Assume that the specimen breaks when the stress at the tip reaches the theoretical strength, that is, σmax = σth, and set ρ = a0. From the approximate form of Eq. (18) and from Eq. (15), we get

(19) σf≈σth2a/a0 =12Eγsa0 ·a0a =12Eγsa

a0 has dropped out. For a = 10 μm, σf ≈ 97 MPa, so the order of magnitude of real strengths comes out. Even the form “the strength is inversely proportional to the square root of the crack size” appears.

Misconception 3: It is not true that “knowing the tip radius of curvature ρ determines the strength” Eq. (19) is the result of fixing ρ = a0 by assumption. Elasticity theory is a continuum theory, so it cannot treat accurately the stress at a tip as sharp as a single atom, and the actual ρ cannot be measured either. Griffith set the condition for fracture using only the energy flowing in and out as the crack extends, without asking for the value of the stress at the tip (§4). The result has the same form √(Eγs/a) as Eq. (19); only the coefficient changes from 1/2 to √(2/π) ≈ 0.80.
Eq. (18) also shows that a round pore (large ρ) is less dangerous than a crack of the same size. However, if there is a small crack at the edge of the pore, the pore and the crack together act as a crack of their combined size (§11).

3.2 The stress field around an elliptical hole — conformal mapping and complex potentials

Tab ② of the simulator draws not only the “value at the single point at the tip” of Eq. (17) but the stress all around the elliptical hole as a color map. Here is an outline of how that is computed. Consider an infinitely wide plate (the in-plane stresses do not depend on ν, in either plane stress or plane strain) with an elliptical hole of semi-axis a in the x direction and semi-axis b in the y direction, with no force on the edge of the hole (a free surface). Far away, uniform stresses σxx = Sx, σyy = Sy, σxy = 0 are applied. In ②, the load “vertical” is (Sx, Sy) = (0, σ), “horizontal” is (σ, 0), and “equibiaxial” is (σ, σ).

Step 1: Express the stress with two complex functions. It is known that a two-dimensional stress state satisfying the equilibrium equations and the compatibility condition can be written, using analytic functions φ(z) and ψ(z) of z = x + iy, as follows (the Kolosov–Muskhelishvili equations; for the derivation, see textbooks on elasticity theory: Muskhelishvili, 1953).

(20) σxx+σyy=4ReΦ(z) , σyy−σxx+2iσxy =2[z¯Φ′(z)+Ψ(z)] , Φ=φ′,Ψ=ψ′

For example, if φ = Γz and ψ = Γ′z (Γ and Γ′ real), then Φ = Γ, Φ′ = 0, and Ψ = Γ′, so we get the uniform stress σxx + σyy = 4Γ, σyy − σxx = 2Γ′, σxy = 0. To match the stress far away, it suffices to choose Γ and Γ′ as follows:

(21) Γ=Sx+Sy4 , Γ′=Sy−Sx2

With the hole present, φ and ψ approach this form far away and are determined so that the force at the edge of the hole is 0.

Step 2: Map the outside of the ellipse onto the outside of a circle. Imposing conditions on the edge of an ellipse is awkward, so we use a function that maps the outside of the unit circle |ζ| ≥ 1 in the plane of another complex number ζ onto the outside of the elliptical hole.

(22) z=ω(ζ)=R(ζ+mζ) , R=a+b2 , m=a−ba+b

Let us check. At a point ζ = eiθ on the unit circle, 1/ζ = e−iθ, so z = R[(1 + m) cos θ + i(1 − m) sin θ]. Since R(1 + m) = (a + b)/2 × 2a/(a + b) = a and likewise R(1 − m) = b, z = a cos θ + ib sin θ — exactly the edge of the ellipse. If b = a, then m = 0 and the hole is circular; as b → 0, m → 1 and it becomes a crack of length 2a. A function that maps shapes while preserving angles in this way is called a conformal mapping.

Step 3: Determine the functions for which the force on the edge is 0. In the ζ plane, express the functions that take the form of Step 1 far away as powers of ζ and 1/ζ, and determine the coefficients so that the force on the edge is 0 on the unit circle; this gives the following form.

(23) φ(ζ)=R(Γζ+Aζ) ,A=−(Γ′+mΓ) ψ(ζ)=R[Γ′ζ−Γζ+mΓζ −(1+mζ2)(Γζ2−A)ζ(ζ2−m)]

Inglis (1913) solved the same problem using elliptic coordinates. Eq. (23) is a rewriting of that solution in the form of complex potentials, and the simulator inverts the mapping to find ζ from z (of ζ = (z ± √(z2 − a2 + b2))/2R, the root with the larger |ζ|), and substitutes Φ = φ′(ζ)/ω′(ζ) and Ψ = ψ′(ζ)/ω′(ζ) into Eq. (20) to compute the stress at each point. That the force is 0 at the edge of the hole, that the stress returns to uniform far away, and that the equilibrium and compatibility equations are satisfied have been checked numerically (Appendix A.9).

Step 4: The stress at the edge of the hole. At a free edge the stress normal to the edge σnn is 0, so the stress along the edge σtt equals the sum σxx + σyy = σnn + σtt, which does not depend on the orientation of the coordinates, that is, 4 Re Φ. At ζ = eiθ, Φ = φ′/ω′ = (Γ − Ae−2iθ)/(1 − me−2iθ). To make the denominator real, multiply the numerator and denominator by (1 − me2iθ); the denominator becomes 1 − 2m cos 2θ + m2, and the real part of the numerator is Γ + mA − (A + mΓ) cos 2θ. Using A + mΓ = −Γ′ and Γ + mA = Γ(1 − m2) − mΓ′, we obtain

(24) σtt(θ)= 4[Γ(1−m2)−mΓ′+Γ′cos⁡2θ] 1−2mcos⁡2θ+m2 (point on the edge (acos⁡θ,bsin⁡θ))

For the vertical load (Γ = σ/4, Γ′ = σ/2, A = −σ(2 + m)/4), calculating at the tip θ = 0 gives a numerator of 4(1 − m)(Γ − A) = (1 − m)(3 + m)σ and a denominator of (1 − m)2, so σtt = σ(3 + m)/(1 − m). Putting in 1 − m = 2b/(a + b) and 3 + m = (4a + 2b)/(a + b) gives σ(4a + 2b)/2b = σ(1 + 2a/b) — Eq. (17) has come out. At the top and bottom edges θ = π/2 (cos 2θ = −1), the numerator is −(1 + m)2σ and the denominator is (1 + m)2, so σtt = −σ (compression).

3.4 The direction of the principal stress and the direction in which the crack advances

When the stress at a point (σxx, σyy, σxy) is viewed on a plane whose normal is rotated by an angle ϑ from the x axis, the stress normal to that plane is

(26) σϑ=σxx+σyy2 +σxx−σyy2cos⁡2ϑ +σxysin⁡2ϑ

(this follows from writing the force balance on a small triangular element). Differentiating with respect to ϑ and setting the result to 0 gives tan 2ϑp = 2σxy/(σxx − σyy), and putting the resulting cos 2ϑ and sin 2ϑ back gives the maximum and minimum values. These are the principal stresses.

(27) σ1,2=σxx+σyy2 ±(σxx−σyy2)2+σxy2

The default display of simulator tab ② is the maximum principal stress σ1, and the short gray lines show its direction (ϑp). A crack in a brittle material advances perpendicular to the direction of the maximum principal stress near its tip. Erdogan and Sih (1963) compared the way an inclined crack turns with experiments under the condition that the crack extends in the direction in which the circumferential stress around the tip is maximum (the maximum circumferential stress criterion), and Gol’dstein and Salganik (1974) presented the principle of local symmetry, that a crack advances in the direction in which the field at the tip has no shear component (mode II). Both are expressions of the same intuition that “a crack advances perpendicular to the tension that opens its tip.” The green arrow in ② is drawn in this direction (the outward normal of the edge) from the point on the edge where σ1 is maximum. For the vertical load the crack extends in the x direction from the tip, and for the horizontal load it extends in the y direction from the top and bottom edges. With equibiaxial loading on a circular hole the whole edge has the same 2σ, so where the hole breaks is decided by the small flaws present there, and no arrow is drawn.

Right next to a free edge, the principal stress directions are always along the edge and perpendicular to the edge. This is because at the edge both the normal stress to the surface and the shear stress are 0, so the stress along the edge σtt and the zero normal stress are the principal stresses. If σtt > 0, σ1 runs along the edge, and a crack advancing perpendicular to it meets the edge at a right angle. If you check “Overlay the direction of σ₁ as short lines” in ②, you can confirm that the lines near the edge of the hole line up along the edge.

3.5 Discussion: why do glass fragments seem to have so many triangles?

This subsection is a discussion A systematic study of the statistics of the shapes of broken-glass fragments could not be found here. Below, we line up the facts that can be confirmed in the literature (the direction in which cracks advance, how they intersect, and branching), and separate what they seem to imply from what they do not.

Fact 1: A crack advances perpendicular to the local σ1 (§3.4). The shapes of the fragments therefore trace the pattern of principal-stress directions in the plate at the moment of fracture.

Fact 2: A crack that arrives later meets an earlier crack at almost a right angle. The surface of an earlier crack is a free surface, so near it σ1 runs along the surface, as in §3.4, and a later crack turns to meet the surface perpendicularly. When cracks form one at a time, in sequence and divide a region, as in the cracking of a ceramic glaze or of drying mud, T-shaped junctions are common, and it has been reported that the number of sides of the resulting cells averages 4 (Bohn, Douady, Couder, 2005; Bohn, Pauchard, Couder, 2005). This means that “if the region is simply divided in sequence, the fragments tend to be quadrilaterals rather than triangles.”

Fact 3: A fast-running crack branches. When a material such as glass breaks while still holding a large amount of stored elastic energy, the crack accelerates and splits into two, and the branches split further. Yoffe (1951) showed that at the tip of a fast-running crack the direction of maximum circumferential stress deviates obliquely from the direction of travel, and this is regarded as one of the classical explanations of branching (the cause of branching is still under study). The fractography literature summarizes that the branching angle varies with the stress state, being about 30–45° for uniaxial tension (with large scatter) and larger for biaxial tension (Quinn, 2020). It is also well known that when a point impact or bending is applied to a plate, cracks that extend radially from the load point (radial cracks) and circumferential cracks that cross them form (Quinn, Swab, Patel, 2021; Bradt, 2011).

What the facts seem to imply. When a third crack crosses the narrow wedge between two cracks that emerge from one point at a sharp angle (the two branches of a branching crack, or adjacent radial cracks), a roughly triangular fragment is formed. That the branching angle is sharp, about 30–45°, is consistent with triangular fragments with a sharp corner at the tip of the wedge being easily formed. On the other hand, if the T-shaped junctions of Fact 2 continue, the fragments approach quadrilaterals. Therefore, if it does feel as though there are “many triangles,” the natural interpretation is that many branches and radial cracks were formed in a fast, high-energy fracture. However, whether triangles really are in the majority, and by how much in which kinds of breakage, cannot be said without counting the fragment shapes. Please also note that the elliptical-hole figure in simulator tab ② is a static elastic solution and does not represent fast-running cracks or branching themselves. A question for students: In ②, change the direction of the load, predict from the pattern of σ1 lines “where the crack will turn and in which direction,” and compare your prediction with photographs of actually broken glass.

4. Griffith’s Energy Balance [Core]

Griffith’s (1921) idea was not to ask what happens at the tip of the crack but to look only at the energy bookkeeping when the crack extends a little. When a crack extends, (i) new surfaces are created, which costs energy, and (ii) the material around the crack relaxes, so elastic strain energy is released. If (ii) exceeds (i), the crack keeps extending without anything being done from outside.

We set the situation as follows. In an infinitely wide plate of thickness 1 (unit thickness) there is a through-crack of full length 2a, and a uniform tensile stress σ is applied far away, perpendicular to the crack. Plane stress is assumed. We find (i) and (ii) as functions of a for the case in which the crack is introduced, and add them.

4.1 The shape the crack opens into

According to elasticity theory (the limit b → 0 of the Inglis elliptical hole in §3), this crack opens into an elliptical shape. Taking the center of the crack as the origin and the x axis along the crack, the displacement of the upper crack face is

(28) v(x)=2σE a2−x2 (−a≤x≤a)

(the lower face moves by −v(x)). This result is borrowed from elasticity theory. The opening is greatest at the center, 4σa/E, and even for a = 10 μm and σ = 156 MPa it is only 16 nm.

4.2 The elastic energy released when a crack is introduced

Consider the following procedure. Starting from a plate without a crack that carries σ, we cut along the surface where the crack is to be introduced, but on the cut surface we leave the stress σ that was originally acting there as a “holding force.” While it is held, nothing changes. If this holding force is slowly reduced from σ to 0, the crack faces open from 0 to v(x). The work that the material does against the holding force at this time is the energy released by introducing the crack.

In a linear elastic body the holding force and the opening change in proportion, so the work is “initial force × final displacement × 1/2” (the triangle under the force–displacement line). Adding up the upper and lower faces, we get

(29) UE=2× ∫−aa 12σv(x)dx =σ∫−aa 2σEa2−x2dx =2σ2E ∫−aaa2−x2dx

The last integral is the area under y = √(a2 − x2) (the upper half circle of radius a), that is, the area of a half circle, πa2/2. Therefore

(30) UE=2σ2E·πa22 =πσ2a2E
Constant external force or fixed grips If the external force is held constant (constant load), the external force also does work when the crack opens, and the strain energy actually increases. If the grips are fixed (constant displacement), the strain energy itself decreases. They appear to be opposite, but the amount by which the “potential energy of the system,” the strain energy minus the work of the external force, decreases can be shown to be the same in both cases for a linear elastic body. Please read Eq. (30) as this quantity.

4.3 The energy of the newly created surfaces

The crack length is 2a, the thickness is 1, and there are two faces, upper and lower.

(31) US=2×(2a×1)×γs =4aγs

4.4 Finding the maximum of the total energy

The change in the energy of the system relative to the state without a crack is obtained by subtracting the released part of Eq. (30) and adding Eq. (31):

(32) U(a)=−πσ2a2E +4γsa

The first term decreases as a2, and the second increases as a. For small a the second term wins, and for large a the first wins, so U(a) has the shape of a hill. Differentiating with respect to a, we get

(33) dUda= −2πσ2aE+4γs

As long as dU/da > 0, the energy increases when the crack extends, so it does not extend. Once dU/da < 0, the energy decreases the more it extends, so it does not stop. The boundary dU/da = 0 is

(34) 2πσ2aE=4γs ⟹ σ2=2Eγsπa

Reading this as the stress (strength) at which a crack of a given a breaks, or as the half-length of the crack that starts to extend under a given σ, gives two forms.

(35) σf=2Eγsπa , ac=2Eγsπσ2

The second derivative is d2U/da2 = −2πσ2/E < 0, so this point is a maximum. The height of the hill, using πσ2ac/E = 2γs from the left-hand expression of Eq. (34), is U(ac) = −2γsac + 4γsac = 2γsac. With the alumina-equivalent values and a = 10 μm, we get

(36) σf= 2×380×109×1.0π×10×10−6 =2.42×1016 ≈1.56×108Pa =156MPa

This is 1/280 of the theoretical strength of 43.6 GPa. For plane strain (E → E/(1 − ν2), ν = 0.22) it becomes 159 MPa. Conversely, to explain the strength of alumina, 350 MPa, with γs = 1.0 J m−2 unchanged, it suffices to have a crack with a = 2.0 μm.

What to read from Eq. (35) ① The strength goes as the −1/2 power of the crack size. If the crack becomes 4 times larger, the strength is halved. On a log–log plot it is a straight line of slope −1/2.
② There are two things the material side can do. One is to make the cracks (flaws) smaller — reducing powder agglomerates, coarse pores, and grinding scratches — and the other is to increase the “resistance of the crack to extension” that takes the place of γs (§5 and §6).
③ The critical point is an unstable equilibrium. For a crack that has passed the maximum, dU/da becomes more negative the more it extends, so it accelerates and does not stop. This is why ceramics break suddenly without warning.
Misconception 4: a is not the “full length of the crack” The a in Eq. (35) is half of the full length 2a for a crack that goes through the plate, and the depth for a crack that enters from the surface. If the full length is put in for a, the crack is treated as twice its actual size, so the strength is estimated at 1/√2 (about 0.71 times) of its true value and the critical size (tolerable crack) at half its true value. For estimating strength or tolerable cracks this is an error on the safe side, but if instead KIC is obtained from a measured strength and crack length, it comes out √2 times too large, which is on the unsafe side. When comparing with values in the literature, the discrepancy is a factor of 2 in crack size and a factor of √2 in strength or KIC. When using values from the literature, always check which definition is used.

5. Stress Intensity Factor and Energy Release Rate

Griffith looked at the energy of the whole system. Irwin (1957) moved the viewpoint to the crack tip and expressed the “strength” of the stress field near the tip with a single number. They are two faces of the same fracture condition.

5.1 The stress field at the tip and the stress intensity factor K

In a linear elastic body, at distances r from the tip that are sufficiently smaller than a, the tensile stress on the extension of the crack plane is

(37) σyy(r)≈ KI2πr (r≪a)

It diverges like 1/√r as r → 0, but the form of the divergence is the same regardless of the crack length or the shape of the specimen, and all of the differences go into the coefficient KI. This is called the stress intensity factor (the subscript I denotes mode I, the opening mode, in which the crack faces are pulled apart). For a wide plate,

(38) KI=Yσπa (Y=1.0: through-crack,  Y≈1.12: surface or edge crack)

Y is the geometry factor. A crack with a straight front that enters from the edge of a plate (a free surface) opens more easily because there is no constraint on one side, so Y is 1.12, greater than 1. For a small semicircular crack in the surface, Y runs along the crack front from about 0.66 (at the deepest point) to 0.73 (where it meets the surface), and the representative value often used is Y ≈ 0.71, the coefficient 2/π of a penny-shaped crack multiplied by the surface correction 1.12. In any case it is smaller than 1. On this page and in the simulator, for simplicity, a surface crack is represented by Y = 1.12 (a conservative treatment that overestimates KI). The unit of KI is MPa m1/2.

Isn’t it a problem that the stress becomes infinite at the tip? Eq. (37) is the result of continuum elasticity theory; at a real tip the stress is finite, because of the rounding at the scale of an atom and a small region of slight nonlinearity (the process zone). KI is a quantity that expresses the strength of the elastic field outside that small region, surrounding it. If the outer field is the same, what happens inside should be the same too, and this is the justification for discussing fracture in terms of K.

5.2 The energy release rate G

The elastic energy released when the crack advances by a unit area is defined as the energy release rate G. A crack of total length 2a has two tips, and when a increases by da each end advances by da, so the crack area (for unit thickness) increases by 2da. Using Eq. (30), we get

(39) G=dUEd(2a) =12dda(πσ2a2E) =12·2πσ2aE =πσ2aE

Squaring Eq. (38) for Y = 1 gives KI2 = πσ2a, so this can be written directly as

(40) G=KI2E (plane stress; for plane strain,  G=KI2(1−ν2)/E)

The energy of the whole system (G) and the stress field at the tip (K) are now connected by a single equation.

5.3 The two fracture conditions are the same

The surface-energy side is counted by the same rule. When the crack area increases by a unit amount, the surface increases by two sheets, upper and lower, so the energy required is 2γs. Writing the condition “fracture occurs when G reaches 2γs,” we get

(41) πσ2aE=2γs ⟺ σ=2Eγsπa

This agrees with Eq. (35). In the language of K, the condition is “fracture occurs when KI reaches the critical value KIC.” If we replace G in Eq. (40) by the critical value Gc, the relations between the two are as follows.

(42) Yσπa=KIC , KIC=EGc , Gc=2γeff

Gc is called the fracture energy (resistance to crack growth), and γeff = Gc/2 the effective surface energy (some references call the γeff per fracture surface on one side the fracture energy γf, so watch out for the factor of 2 when comparing values). KIC is a material value that can be measured directly in a test, and this is the fracture toughness. For an ideally brittle body γeff = γs, and with alumina-equivalent values we would expect KIC = √(2Eγs) = 0.87 MPa m1/2. The 3.5 MPa m1/2 in Table 1 is 4.0 times that, or 16 times in terms of Gc. The origin of this gap is the subject of §6.

5.4 The tolerable crack size

Solving the left-hand equation of Eq. (42) for a gives the largest crack that can be tolerated under a stress σ.

(43) ac=1π (KICYσ)2

For a design stress σ = 300 MPa and a surface crack (Y = 1.12), this is 34.5 μm for alumina, 1.6 μm for soda-lime glass, and 180 μm for Y-TZP. Because ac is proportional to the square of KIC, a 2.3 times larger KIC (alumina → Y-TZP) allows a crack 5.2 times larger. The 1.6 μm for glass is a size that even a light scratch on the surface exceeds. Compared with the estimate seen at the end of §4, that “for γs = 1.0 J m−2, a crack of 2.0 μm is enough to give 350 MPa” (a through-crack), the crack size at which real alumina (KIC = 3.5 MPa m1/2) fractures at 350 MPa is 32 μm for the same through-crack (16 times, the same as the ratio of Gc), and 25 μm for a surface crack (Y = 1.12).

5.5 The whole stress field around the crack tip — Westergaard’s solution and the K field

Eq. (37) held only on the extension of the crack plane. The upper figure of simulator tab ③ colors σyy over the whole region around the tip. We derive that field by the method of Westergaard (1939). For a wide plate containing a crack of length 2a (y = 0, |x| < a) under a remote stress σ, we use the complex function

(44) Z(z)=σzz2−a2 , σyy=ReZ+yImZ′ , σxx=ReZ−yImZ′ , σxy=−yReZ′

With this function, √(z2 − a2) is purely imaginary on the crack faces (y = 0, |x| < a), so that σyy = σxy = 0, and Z → σ far away (this Z is a biaxial solution that also makes σxx equal to σ far away, so for uniaxial tension σ is subtracted from σxx; σyy and σxy do not change). On the extension of the crack (y = 0, x = a + r), σyy = σ(a + r)/√((a + r)2 − a2), which is the same as the exact solution for a crack in §3.3.

Near the tip we use polar coordinates centered on the tip, z = a + reiθ (r ≪ a). z2 − a2 = (z − a)(z + a) ≈ reiθ × 2a, so we get

(45) Z≈σa2are−iθ/2 =KI2πre−iθ/2 , Z′≈−KI22πr−3/2e−3iθ/2

(We put KI = σ√(πa); Z′ is the derivative of Z ∝ (z − a)−1/2.) Re Z = (KI/√(2πr)) cos(θ/2), y Im Z′ = r sin θ × (KI/2√(2π))r−3/2 sin(3θ/2), and putting these together with sin θ = 2 sin(θ/2) cos(θ/2), we obtain the mode I K field.

(46) σyy=KI2πrcos⁡θ2[1+sin⁡θ2sin⁡3θ2] , σxx=KI2πrcos⁡θ2[1−sin⁡θ2sin⁡3θ2] σxy=KI2πrcos⁡θ2sin⁡θ2cos⁡3θ2

For θ = 0 this returns to Eq. (37). The angular functions depend on neither a nor σ, which enter only through KI — this is what is meant by “K expresses the strength of the field at the tip with a single number.” Substituting into Eq. (27), we get (σxx + σyy)/2 = (KI/√(2πr)) cos(θ/2), ((σxx − σyy)/2)2 + σxy2 = (KI2/2πr) cos2(θ/2) sin2(θ/2)[sin2(3θ/2) + cos2(3θ/2)], so

(47) σ1=KI2πrcos⁡θ2(1+|sin⁡θ2|)

Putting s = sin(θ/2), the angular part becomes √(1 − s2)(1 + s); setting its derivative with respect to s equal to 0 gives s = 1/2, that is, θ = ±60°, where the maximum value is 1.30. At the same distance, σ1 is 30 % higher in the direction 60° off to the side than straight ahead of the crack (1.00), so in the color map of σ1 in ② the high-stress region has a kidney shape bulging above and below. The σyy painted in the upper figure of ③ (the component that opens the crack) also has a shape bulging above and below the line of the crack, since the angular part of Eq. (46), cos(θ/2)[1 + sin(θ/2)sin(3θ/2)], is 1.30 at θ = ±60° (1.3 times its value straight ahead). The differences from σ1 are that σyy is only the component in the direction that opens the crack, and that it decreases faster from the side of the tip toward the region behind it (at θ = 90°, σyy is 1.06 and σ1 is 1.21; at θ = 120°, σyy is 0.50 and σ1 is 0.93).

5.6 The size of the plastic zone — Irwin’s estimate

Eq. (46) goes to infinity at the tip, but in a metal plastic deformation occurs where the stress exceeds the yield stress σy. Finding the distance along the extension at which KI/√(2πr) = σy, we get

(48) rp=12π(KIσy)2 (plane stress)

However, the part r < rp cannot support a stress larger than σy, so the corresponding force is pushed outward, and the plastic zone spreads to about twice that size, 2rp. Irwin corrected for this by regarding the crack as “behaving as if it were longer by rp” (the derivation of this correction is summarized in Anderson, 2017). In simulator tab ③ this plastic zone is shown by a dashed circle of diameter 2rp centered a distance rp ahead of the tip. In plane strain (the interior of a thick plate), triaxial constraint makes yielding more difficult, and the plastic zone becomes about 1/3 as large.

Calculating rp for KI = KIC with the values of Table 1 gives 6.4 mm for structural steel and 2.0 mm for the aluminum alloy. In ceramics dislocations hardly move at room temperature, so a yield stress is hard to define, but if we use the rough relation between the hardness H and the yield stress, σy ≈ H/3 (Tabor, 1951; §12.4; since a hydrostatic pressure acts under an indentation, this is a nominal value estimated from the plastic deformation that occurs there), we get 69 nm for alumina (H ≈ 16 GPa, σy ≈ 5.3 GPa), 27 nm for soda-lime glass, and even for Y-TZP only 0.6 μm — at most about the size of a grain, orders of magnitude smaller than the crack size (tens of μm), that is, effectively 0. Note that H ≈ 3σy holds for metals with a large E/σy; in ceramics H/σy is smaller, about 1.5–2 (Marsh, 1964). H/3 underestimates σy, so the rp here is a rough upper bound (the actual value is about 1/2 to 1/4 of it). The tip of a ceramic has almost no room to be rounded by plasticity.

Substituting KI = Yσ√(πa) into Eq. (48) gives rp/a = Y2σ2/(2σy2), which becomes as large as 0.63 at σ = σy. Fracture can be discussed in terms of K only when the plastic zone is sufficiently small compared with the dimensions of the crack and the specimen (small-scale yielding); for metals at stresses close to the yield stress it remains a rough guide (Misconception 5 in §6). With a plastic zone the tip opens and is blunted, and in the strip-yield model the crack-tip opening displacement is δ ≈ KI2/(Eσy) (Anderson, 2017). For structural steel at KI = KIC, δ ≈ 48 μm, and as seen in Problem 2 of §15, blunting of the tip greatly lowers the stress concentration.

6.1 Where toughness comes from — shielding at the tip

When actions that tend to close the crack operate around or behind the crack tip, the stress intensity factor that the tip actually feels is smaller than the externally applied KI. This is called shielding; writing the shielding part as Ksh, we have

(49) Ktip=KI−Ksh ⟹ KIC=K0+Ksh

The material at the tip itself fractures when Ktip reaches its intrinsic value K0 (the toughness needed just to break the bonds), so the fracture toughness seen from the outside is K0 + Ksh. Because it is hard to strengthen the bonds at the tip, the toughening of ceramics aims to increase Ksh. There are four representative methods (transformation toughening, crack deflection, bridging, and microcracking).

6.2 Transformation toughening — zirconia

When cooled from the high-temperature tetragonal form (t phase), ZrO2 transforms to the monoclinic form (m phase), and its volume increases by about 4 % (the literature gives a range of 3–5 %). A sintered body of pure ZrO2 cracks itself during cooling because of this expansion. If a small amount of Y2O3 or the like is dissolved in it and the grains are kept small, the t phase can be retained even at room temperature in a metastable state. This is Y-TZP (yttria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia polycrystal).

When a crack approaches such a material, the large tensile stress at the tip triggers the t → m transformation in a band-shaped region around the crack. The transformed grains try to expand but are held in by the surrounding untransformed material, so a compressive stress that tends to close the crack faces arises, and this is the Ksh of Eq. (49). Garvie et al. (1975) showed that a transformation of the same type as the martensitic transformation that occurs in the quenching of steel can be used for toughening, and titled their paper “Ceramic steel?” The mechanism of transformation toughening and the design of zirconia-based materials that exploit it are summarized in the review by Hannink et al. (2000). The KIC = 8.0 MPa m1/2 of Y-TZP in Table 1 is 2.3 times that of alumina (the KIC of zirconia depends strongly on the measurement method, and with the standardized notched-beam method values of about 4–6 MPa m1/2 may be obtained).

Being easy to transform is a double-edged sword The metastable t phase can also transform at places other than the crack tip. When Y-TZP is left for a long time at moderate temperatures in an environment containing water vapor, the t → m transformation can advance from the surface and reduce the strength and toughness. This is called low-temperature degradation (Chevalier et al., 2009). In applications such as the femoral heads of artificial joints, measures are taken to suppress it through composition and processing.

6.3 Crack deflection and bridging

Besides transformation toughening, there are several other ways to create shielding through the microstructure (the review by Evans, 1990, is detailed).

  • Deflection. When a crack bends in a zigzag along weak grain boundaries or a second phase, the opening component at the tip decreases and the area of the fracture surface also increases.
  • Bridging. When elongated grains, coarse grains, or fibers remain connected behind the crack without breaking, they exert a force that pulls the crack faces together. The high KIC of silicon nitride is due to the large bridging by the columnar grains that grow during sintering.
  • Microcracking. Many microcracks form around the tip, dissipating energy and relaxing the stress at the tip.

6.4 The R-curve — a crack that becomes harder to extend the more it extends

The bridging and transformation zones accumulate behind the crack as it extends, so the shielding grows larger the longer the crack becomes. The curve along which the resistance to crack growth R (with the same dimension as Gc) increases with the crack extension Δa is called the R-curve. Since G = πσ2a/E of Eq. (39) is a straight line proportional to a, the condition for fracture to become unstable is

(50) G(a)=R(a) and dGda=πσ2E ≥dRda

If R is constant, dR/da = 0, so the crack becomes unstable the moment G reaches R (the sudden fracture of §4). In a material in which R rises, even when G = R the crack extends stably by a small amount and stops, and it becomes unstable only when the stress is raised further and the straight line of G touches the R-curve. As a result, there is the advantage that the strength becomes insensitive to the size of the initial crack and the scatter tends to be small. On the other hand, for small cracks the shielding has not yet developed, so they do not enjoy as much benefit as the steady-state KIC suggests.

Misconception 5: Do not compare the KIC of a metal and a ceramic directly Most of the toughness of metals comes from plastic deformation at the tip (dissipation of energy in the plastic zone, and blunting of the tip). If the plastic zone is not small compared with the specimen, the premise of organizing the problem in terms of K (that the nonlinear region at the tip is small) breaks down. The thickness guideline for obtaining a valid plane-strain KIC, 2.5(KIC/σy)2 (ASTM E399), calculated with the values of Table 1, comes to as much as 100 mm for steel and 31 mm for the Al alloy. The metal values in Table 1 are meant for “comparing orders of magnitude.”

6.5 Emitting dislocations or breaking bonds — the Rice–Thomson picture

The plastic zone of §5.6 was a continuum view. Looking at the same problem on the atomic scale, it becomes a competition over which happens first at a crack tip that is sharp down to a single atom. (i) The bond at the tip breaks and the crack advances by one atom (cleavage). (ii) A dislocation is emitted from the tip and escapes along a slip plane. If (ii) occurs first, each time a dislocation is emitted the tip is displaced by one step and becomes rounded (blunting), the stress concentration drops, and the material behaves in a ductile manner. If (i) comes first, the tip advances while remaining sharp and the material fractures in a brittle manner. This view was put forward by Rice and Thomson (1974), and Rice (1992) quantified it using the Peierls model.

The condition for bond breaking is the same as in §5.3, G = 2γs. The condition for dislocation emission is determined by the height of the barrier that has to be crossed when the material above and below a slip plane is displaced by one atom — the unstable stacking energy γus — and, for a slip plane inclined at an angle θ to the crack plane (mode I), it is given by

(51) Gcleave=2γs , Ge=8γus(1+cos⁡θ)sin2⁡θ =θ=60°649γus≈7.1γus

(At θ = 60°, 1 + cos θ = 3/2 and sin2 θ = 3/4, so the denominator is 9/8.) If Ge < 2γs, dislocation emission comes first (ductile); if Ge > 2γs, bond breaking comes first (brittle). To convert to a stress intensity factor, use K = √(GE/(1 − ν2)) for plane strain. 6.6 Why it is hard for dislocations to move in ceramics

We look at the reasons stated in §1 a little more quantitatively.

  • The Peierls stress is large. When a dislocation moves by one atomic spacing, the atoms in the core cross a barrier while rearranging their bonds. The shear stress needed to cross that barrier is the Peierls stress, and in the Peierls–Nabarro model, with the spacing of the slip planes d, the magnitude of the Burgers vector b, and the shear modulus μ, it is estimated as τP ≈ [2μ/(1 − ν)] exp[−2πw/b], with the core width w = d/(1 − ν) (Hull and Bacon, 2011). The key point is that it depends exponentially on the core width. In metallic bonding, which has no directionality, the core is wide and τP is small, whereas in strongly directional covalent bonding the core is narrow and τP is larger by orders of magnitude (the numerical values from this formula are only rough estimates).
  • In ionic crystals, the directions in which slip can occur are limited. Only planes and directions in which ions of the same sign do not face each other during slip can be used. The main slip system of rock-salt-type crystals is {110}⟨110⟩.
  • There are not enough independent slip systems. For the grains of a polycrystal to deform into arbitrary shapes without gaps between them, five independent slip systems are needed (the von Mises condition). The rock-salt-type {110}⟨110⟩ has only two independent ones (Groves and Kelly, 1963). Even if single-crystal MgO slips a little at room temperature, in a polycrystal the deformation does not match at the grain boundaries and cracks form.
  • The Burgers vector is long. In crystals with large unit cells such as Al2O3, b is long and the energy of a dislocation (∝ μb2) is large.
  • Glass has no crystalline dislocations. The arrangement of atoms has no periodicity, so the very notion of a slip plane does not apply.

All of these relax as the temperature rises, and at high temperatures ceramics also show plastic deformation and creep. The fact that dislocations “cannot move” at room temperature is the background of the large Ge in Eq. (51) and of the effectively zero rp of §5.6.

6.7 The shape of the transformation zone and the shielding

The t → m transformation of §6.2 is one in which the volume increases, so it occurs more easily where the tensile part of the hydrostatic stress (the mean stress σm) is large. In plane strain σzz = ν(σxx + σyy), so from Eq. (46) σm = (σxx + σyy + σzz)/3 = (1 + ν)(σxx + σyy)/3. Substituting σxx + σyy = 2(KI/√(2πr)) cos(θ/2) and setting it equal to the critical value σmc at which the transformation occurs, the boundary of the transformation zone is

(52) σm=2(1+ν)3KI2πrcos⁡θ2 ⟹ r(θ)=2(1+ν)29π(KIσmc)2cos2⁡θ2

This gives a shape bulging out in front of the tip (McMeeking and Evans, 1982). What is interesting is that a transformation zone ahead of the tip alone does not give shielding; McMeeking and Evans showed that shielding that closes the tip arises only when the crack has extended and the transformation zone remains behind the crack as a band. With the half-width of the band h, the volume fraction transformed Vf, and the volumetric strain of the transformation eT (about 0.04), the steady-state shielding takes the form ΔK ∝ EVfeT√h/(1 − ν), with a coefficient found to be about 0.2 (McMeeking and Evans, 1982; Budiansky et al., 1983). The more the band extends, the more the shielding develops, so Y-TZP shows the R-curve of §6.4. In simulator tab ③, in the atomic picture for Y-TZP, the atoms in a region of the shape of Eq. 7. Deriving the Weibull Distribution from the Weakest Link [Core]

In §4–§6 we saw that the strength is set by the size of the most dangerous crack. But the sizes of the cracks inside specimens are not uniform, even among specimens made by firing the same powder under the same conditions. That is why strength scatters. Moreover, a larger specimen is more likely to contain a larger crack, so the strength decreases on average. Weibull statistics treats these two effects quantitatively.

7.1 The weakest-link idea

A chain breaks as a whole when its weakest link breaks. If each of n links independently withstands a stress σ with probability ps(σ), the probability that the whole chain withstands it is the product of the probabilities of the independent events.

(53) Ps=ps×ps×⋯×ps⏟n links =psn

If we divide a specimen of volume V into V/V1 small pieces of volume V1 and make the same statement, Ps(V) = [Ps(V1)]V/V1 holds. Taking the logarithm, ln Ps(V) = (V/V1) ln Ps(V1); that is, ln Ps is proportional to the volume. So if we define the “risk” per unit volume, φ(σ) = −ln Ps(V1)/V1 (≥ 0), we obtain

(54) Ps(V,σ)=exp⁡[−Vφ(σ)]

Ps is the probability that the specimen survives (does not break) when a stress σ is applied. Everything up to here follows only from the weakest-link assumption that “the small pieces are independent, and if any one of them breaks, the whole breaks.” The form of φ(σ) is not determined by this assumption. Weibull (1939) proposed the following form by taking φ to be a power function, and in a 1951 paper showed that it fits the data of many materials.

(55) Pf=1−Ps =1−exp⁡[−VV0 (σσ0)m]

m is the Weibull modulus (a measure of how small the scatter is), and σ0 is the characteristic strength of a specimen of volume V0. For V = V0, Pf = 1 − 1/e = 63.2 % at σ = σ0. The power function was chosen as a simple form that fits experiments well, and Weibull himself did not derive it from theory. Next we show that this form can be derived from the distribution of flaw sizes.

7.2 Deriving it from the distribution of flaws — the model of the simulator

We assume the following four things. Simulator tab ④ creates and breaks specimens exactly according to these assumptions.

  1. Number of flaws. The number n of flaws contained in a specimen of volume V follows a Poisson distribution with mean λV (the flaws are scattered independently of one another).
  2. Size of flaws. The size a of each flaw is independent of the others and, above amin, follows a Pareto law (a power law). The larger k is, the less likely large flaws are to occur.
  3. Strength of each flaw. A flaw of size ai becomes the origin of fracture at the stress σi = KIC/(Y√(πai)) at which KI = KIC in Eq. (42).
  4. Weakest link. The strength of the specimen is the smallest of the σi of all the flaws.
(56) Pr(n)=(λV)ne−λVn! , Pr(size>a)=(amina)k (a≥amin)

What to read from Eq. (61) ① m is not a property of the material itself but is set by the shape of the tail of the flaw-size distribution. The less likely large flaws are (the larger k is), the larger m is, and the more uniform the strengths are. The “2” in m = 2k comes from the strength being set by a−1/2 (Eq. (43)).
② σ0 is set by the number of flaws and the smallest flaw. It is lower the more flaws there are (the larger λV is), and higher the smaller amin is. A larger specimen has a larger λV, so σ0 decreases as V−1/m (the volume effect of §9).
③ Doubling KIC doubles both σ* and σ0, but m does not change. Toughness raises the level of the strength, while controlling the flaw distribution (powder processing, forming, grinding) reduces the scatter (this is for a model with constant KIC; in a material with an R-curve, toughening also tends to reduce the scatter: §6.4).

Misconception 6: It is not true that “a material with a larger m is stronger” m is a measure of how small the scatter is, and σ0 is the level of the strength; they are different quantities. However, in designs that keep the probability of fracture very small (§8), m has a strong effect. For example, for a material with “m = 10, σ0 = 400 MPa” and one with “m = 20, σ0 = 300 MPa,” the mean strength is higher for the former (381 MPa versus 292 MPa), but the design stress that allows Pf = 10−6 is 100 MPa for the former and 150 MPa for the latter, so the order is reversed.

8. The Weibull Plot and Estimating m

8.1 Making it a straight line

If we fix the specimen size at V = V0, Eq. (55) becomes 1 − Pf = exp[−(σ/σ0)m]. Taking the logarithm of the reciprocal gives ln[1/(1 − Pf)] = (σ/σ0)m, and taking the logarithm once more gives

(66) ln⁡ln⁡11−Pf =mln⁡σ−mln⁡σ0

If we take x = ln σ on the horizontal axis and y = ln ln[1/(1 − Pf)] on the vertical axis, we get a straight line of slope m whose x at y = 0 is ln σ0. This is the Weibull plot.

8.2 From the data of N test specimens

Arrange the strengths of N specimens in ascending order, σ1 ≤ σ2 ≤ ⋯ ≤ σN, and estimate the fracture probability of the i-th specimen by

(67) Fi=i−0.5N

If we fit a straight line to the points (xi, yi) = (ln σi, ln ln[1/(1 − Fi)]) by least squares, then the slope, together with the fact that the line passes through the mean point (x̄, ȳ) (ȳ = m x̄ − m ln σ0), gives

8.4 Design strength — choosing the fracture probability and finding the allowable stress

Solving Eq. (55) (V = V0) for σ, the stress at which a fracture probability Pf is allowed is

(69) σd=σ0 [−ln⁡(1−Pf)]1/m ≈ σ0Pf1/m (Pf≪1)

Using the estimates of Fig. 3 (mest = 9.34, σ0,est = 447 MPa) gives 102 MPa, so underestimating m by just 7 % lowers the design stress by 9 %.

Misconception 7: It is not true that “testing 100 specimens tells us the fracture probability of 10−6” What 100 tests directly reveal extends only to about F1 = 0.5 %, that of the weakest specimen. 10−6 is an extrapolation 3.7 orders of magnitude beyond that, and there is no guarantee that the tail of the distribution really lies on the same straight line. If the Weibull plot bends partway (when there are two or more kinds of flaws, such as grinding scratches on the surface and pores inside), you must not extrapolate with a single straight line. Furthermore, ceramics undergo delayed fracture, in which cracks grow slowly when a load is applied continuously in an environment with moisture, so the decrease of strength with time must also be considered in design.

8.5 Breaking specimens one at a time brings the histogram closer to the theoretical distribution — the law of large numbers

In simulator tab ④, the specimens lined up on the shelf are put into the testing machine one at a time, or ten at a time, and the strengths are piled up in a histogram. The red curve is the theoretical expected number for the number n of specimens broken so far; with the bin width Δσ and the probability density of the strength p(σ),

(70) (expected number per bin)≈nΔσp(σ) , p(σ)=−dPsdσ =mσ0(σσ0)m−1exp⁡[−(σσ0)m]

(p is the derivative of Ps = exp[−(σ/σ0)m], and Ps is the probability of surviving a stress σ.) With the first few specimens the bars do not match the curve at all, but the more specimens are broken, the closer they come to the curve. Let us estimate how they approach it.

Let q (≈ Δσ p) be the probability of falling into a given bin. The quantity Xi, which is 1 if the i-th specimen falls into that bin and 0 if it does not, has mean q, and since Xi2 = Xi, its variance is q − q2. The specimens are independent, so the mean and variance of the number N = ΣXi of the n specimens that fall into that bin are the respective sums, so

(71) ⟨N⟩=nq , Var(N)=nq(1−q) ⟹ Var(N)⟨N⟩=1−qnq

The relative scatter of the height of a bar decreases as 1/√n. For a bin with q = 0.1, it is 1 ± 0.95 specimens for n = 10 (scatter 95 %), 10 ± 3 for 100 specimens (30 %), and 100 ± 9.5 for 1000 specimens (9.5 %). The fact that the fraction N/n approaches the probability q — this is the law of large numbers, and the reason why the shape of the histogram approaches the red curve. Checking with N = 20000 specimens, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic (the maximum difference between the empirical and theoretical distributions) was 0.0073, below the critical value 0.0096 at the 5 % significance level.

Scatter of the points of the Weibull plot. (72) ⟨F(i)⟩=in+1 , Var(F(i))=i(n−i+1)(n+1)2(n+2)

The Fi = (i − 0.5)/n of Eq. (67) uses a value close to this mean as a representative. For n = 100 and i = 1 (the weakest specimen), the standard deviation of F is 0.0098, the same as its mean, so the scatter of y is 1 or more (1.29 by numerical calculation). It is 0.14 for i = 50 (the middle) and 0.23 for i = 100 (the strongest specimen). 9. Volume Effects and Effective Volume

9.1 The volume effect under uniform tension

Apply uniform tension to specimens of the same material with volumes V1 and V2, and compare the stresses σ1 and σ2 at which they have the same fracture probability. If Pf in Eq. (55) is equal, the contents of the exponent are equal, so

(73) V1V0(σ1σ0)m =V2V0(σ2σ0)m ⟹ σ1σ2=(V2V1)1/m

For a component with 1000 times the volume of the test specimen, the strength is 1000−1/10 = 0.501 times for m = 10 and still only 0.708 times for m = 20. For a volume 10 times larger, it is 0.794 times for m = 10. The median (the stress at Pf = 1/2) is σ0(ln 2)1/m, so the ratio of the medians also follows Eq. (73). In simulator tab ⑤ you can confirm this by actually breaking specimens of V1 and 10V1 with the model of §7.2 (the meaning of the symbols and a schematic figure are in §9.5).

9.2 When the stress is not uniform — the effective volume

In a bending test the stress differs from place to place. Going back to the fact that ln Ps in §7.1 was the sum of the contributions of the small pieces, we add up the risk for each small piece of volume dV using the stress σ(r) at its location (the compressed part σ ≤ 0 does not break, so it is not included).

(74) Pf=1−exp⁡ [−1V0∫σ>0 (σ(r)σ0)mdV]

Factoring out the maximum stress σmax in the specimen, we get

(75) Pf=1−exp⁡ [−VeffV0(σmaxσ0)m] , Veff=∫σ>0 (σ(r)σmax)mdV

Veff is “the volume that gives the same risk if the maximum stress were applied uniformly.” Because of the m-th power, effectively only the places near the maximum stress are counted.

9.3 Finding the effective volume in 3-point bending by integration

A load is applied at the center of a rectangular bar with support span L, width b, and height h. The bending moment is 0 at the supports, maximum at the center, and varies linearly in between. The bending stress in the cross section is proportional to the distance y from the neutral axis. Measuring the position x from a support and taking the tensile side as y > 0, in the left half (0 ≤ x ≤ L/2) we have

(76) σ(x,y)=σmax ·2xL·2yh (0≤x≤L/2,0≤y≤h/2)

The right half is symmetric, so we take twice the left half, and the compressed side y < 0 is not included. Substituting into Eq. (75), the integrals over x and y separate, and we get

(77) Veff=b·2 ∫0L/2(2xL)mdx ·∫0h/2(2yh)mdy

(78) Veff=b·2·L/2m+1 ·h/2m+1 =bLh2(m+1)2

Misconception 8: It is not true that “the catalog bending strength can be used to design a large component” The strength in a catalog is usually the value of 3-point or 4-point bending of a small specimen. If the origin of fracture is on the surface, like grinding scratches, the same argument is made with the effective area instead of the volume.

9.5 The meaning of V1, V2, σ1, and σ2 — the volume effect seen through the largest crack

Let us confirm the meaning of the symbols once more. V1 is the volume of the small specimen (for example, the specimen whose strength was measured), V2 is the volume of a larger specimen or component (V2 = nV1), and σ1 and σ2 are the stresses at which each breaks with the same fracture probability; in simulator tab ⑤ they are matched at a fracture probability of 50 % (the median). When the stress is not uniform, as in a bending test, read V as the effective volume Veff (§9.2).

(81) Pr(amax≤a)=exp⁡[−λV(amina)k]

Checking with 4000 Monte Carlo runs, the ratios were 1.567 for the largest crack and 0.799 for the strength at 10 times, and 2.511 and 0.631 at 100 times. 9.6 The stress distribution in a bending test and the volume under high stress

We confirm from beam theory the bending stress σ ∝ M(x)y that was used in §9.3. The cross section of a bent beam rotates while remaining plane, and the strain at a distance y from the neutral axis is ε = y/ρ, where ρ is the radius of curvature. The bending moment obtained by integrating σ = Ey/ρ over the cross section is M = ∫σy dA = (E/ρ)∫y2 dA = EI/ρ (I = bh3/12 for a rectangle of width b and height h), so eliminating E/ρ gives

When the origin of fracture is a flaw on the surface, the same conversion is made with an effective area instead of a volume (Quinn and Morrell, 1991; Quinn, 2003). Beam theory does not include the contact stresses under the loading points and supports, and it also ignores the stress concentration at the shoulders of the grip section of a tensile specimen.

10. Thermal Shock

A hot ceramic put into cold water can crack. When it is cooled quickly, only the surface cools first and tries to contract, but it is held back by the interior, which is still hot, and cannot contract. As a result, a tensile stress arises at the surface. Ceramics are weak in tension, and the surface also has cracks such as grinding scratches, so the ceramic breaks under this stress.

10.1 Deriving the thermal stress at the surface

Let the surface be the x–y plane and the normal to the surface the z direction. Suppose that the temperature of a thin layer at the surface suddenly drops by ΔT relative to the interior. If it were free, this layer would shrink by αΔT in every direction, but we assume that (i) because it is connected to the thick interior, its in-plane dimensions cannot change (εx = εy = 0), and (ii) nothing restrains it in the direction normal to the surface (σz = 0). Adding the thermal strain −αΔT to Hooke’s law gives the following for the two in-plane directions (a tensile stress σy in the y direction contracts the x direction by νσy/E through the Poisson effect, so that amount is subtracted).

(85) εx=σx−νσyE−αΔT , εy=σy−νσxE−αΔT

(Because σz = 0, there is no −νσz/E term.) The in-plane behavior is isotropic, so σx = σy ≡ σ. Setting εx = 0 gives

(86) σ−νσE−αΔT=0 ⟹ σ(1−ν)E=αΔT
(87) σ=EαΔT1−ν (tension)

A bar constrained in only one direction would have σ = EαΔT, but here the layer is constrained from two in-plane directions, so the stress is multiplied by 1/(1 − ν) (1.28 times for alumina). The temperature difference at which this stress reaches the strength σf is the limit at which cracking begins.

(88) ΔTc=R= σf(1−ν)Eα

We call this R (a quantity different from the R of the R-curve in §6.4) the thermal shock resistance parameter (it was Kingery (1955) who organized the resistance to thermal stress in terms of such a parameter). Calculated with the values in Table 1, it gives the following.

Misconception 9: It is not necessarily true that “a material with a larger R also has a higher strength after thermal shock” R is an index of “whether a crack starts to run.” As Hasselman (1969) showed, how far a crack extends once it has started to run, and how much the strength drops as a result, is a separate question: the stronger a material is and the more elastic energy it stores, the longer the crack tends to run and the greater the damage. For materials that undergo thermal shock repeatedly, such as refractories, the design emphasizes “not being devastated even if it cracks” over “not cracking.”

10.2 Temperature inside the sample — transient heat conduction and the Fourier number

Eq. (87) was the value at the instant when “only the surface has cooled by ΔT and the interior has not cooled yet.” Simulator tab ⑦ calculates how the temperature spreads through a square sample over time and how the thermal stress changes. Let us start with the temperature. Consider the heat entering and leaving a small volume dV. The heat flux (the heat that flows per unit area per unit time) is given by Fourier’s law q = −k∇T (k is the thermal conductivity), and the net heat that flows in, −∇·q dV, raises the temperature (density ρ, specific heat c), so ρc ∂T/∂t = k∇2T, that is

(89) ∂T∂t=κ(∂2T∂x2+∂2T∂y2) , κ=kρc

where κ is the thermal diffusivity. (This is a two-dimensional equation: we consider the cross section of a long square bar and assume that the temperature does not change along its length.) Dividing the coordinates by the side length L (x̂ = x/L, ŷ = y/L) and measuring time by

(90) Fo=κtL2 ⟹ ∂T∂Fo=∂2T∂x^2+∂2T∂y^2

gives an equation that depends on neither the material nor the size. The Fourier number Fo is time measured in units of “the time L2/κ that heat needs to spread over a distance L.” The problem of ⑦ is a square cross section at a uniform initial temperature T0; at time 0 the top face is held at Ttop and the bottom face at Tbottom, and the side faces are adiabatic (no heat enters or leaves). This corresponds to the most severe quench or sudden heating, in which the surface temperature changes in an instant (the case of an infinite Biot number in the box of §10.1).

Eq. (89) is linear in the temperature, so if we find one solution φ(ŷ, Fo) for “the top face at 1, the initial state and the bottom face at 0,” we can write, by superposition, T = T0 + (Ttop − T0)φ(ŷ, Fo) + (Tbottom − T0)φ(1 − ŷ, Fo). Because the side faces are adiabatic and the initial temperature is uniform, the temperature is a function of ŷ only (one-dimensional). φ settles into the straight line 1 − ŷ as time passes, so putting φ = 1 − ŷ − u, u is the solution of heat conduction that is 0 at both ends and starts from 1 − ŷ. 10.3 Thermal stress — it arises only while the temperature deviates from a straight line

Thermal stress in a free plate. Let y be the thickness direction, and consider a free plate that is sufficiently wide in the plane (x, z). Let the temperature change be ϑ(y) = T − T0. The plate is a single body in its plane, so the in-plane strain is restricted to a linear function of y, εx = εz = c0 + c1y (a cross section stays plane), and nothing restrains it in the thickness direction (σy = 0). The in-plane behavior is isotropic, so σx = σz ≡ σ, and from Hooke’s law with the thermal strain added, εx = (σx − νσz)/E + αϑ, we get

(92) σ(y)=E1−ν[c0+c1y−αϑ(y)] = −Eα1−ν[ϑ(y)−ϑlin(y)]

Here ϑlin = (c0 + c1y)/α is the straight line determined by the conditions that no external force or moment acts, ∫σ dy = 0 and ∫σy dy = 0, and it is the same as the straight line fitted to ϑ(y) by least squares (because these two conditions are exactly the normal equations of the least-squares fit). If the temperature is a linear function of position, then ϑ = ϑlin and the thermal stress is 0. This is because a free body can respond to a linear temperature distribution with a uniform extension and a bending. Therefore, even if we keep heating the top and cooling the bottom, the thermal stress disappears once the temperature has settled into a straight line (Fo ≳ 0.5). Thermal stress arises only during the transient, while the temperature deviates from a straight line.

Immediately after a quench, only a thin layer at the surface has ϑ = −ΔT and the interior has ϑ = 0, so the fitted line is almost 0 and the surface stress is σ = EαΔT/(1 − ν) — in agreement with Eq. (87). When the upper and lower faces are quenched at the same time, ϑlin is just the average temperature change by symmetry, and from the symmetric form of Eq. (91) the surface stress is

(93) σsurface(Fo)EαΔT/(1−ν) =1−(average temperature change)/ΔT =∑n=1,3,5,⋯8n2π2e−n2π2Fo

Here the solution for both faces held at 0 has the form 1 − Σodd n(4/nπ) sin(nπŷ)e−n2π2Fo, and we used the fact that the integral of sin(nπŷ) from 0 to 1 is 2/nπ. At Fo = 0, Σodd 8/(n2π2) = (8/π2)(π2/8) = 1, which recovers Eq. (87), and the ratio then falls to 0.995 at Fo = 4×10−6, 0.968 at 2×10−4, 0.929 at 10−3, 0.733 at 0.014, 0.496 at 0.05, 0.302 at 0.1, 0.042 at 0.3, and 0.006 at 0.5. We see that the moment right after the quench is the most dangerous, and if the ceramic is going to crack, it cracks then, and that the maximum stress EαΔT/(1 − ν) depends on neither the size of the sample nor κ (when the surface temperature changes instantly). Size and thermal conductivity matter when the heat transfer is finite and the surface temperature does not change at once (the box in §10.1; Kingery, 1955; Hasselman, 1969).

Square cross section (plane strain). The simulator solves the cross section of a long square bar as plane strain (strain along the length εz = 0). In a thin layer at the surface, setting εx = 0, σy = 0, and εz = 0, we obtain from εz = [σz − νσx]/E + αϑ = 0 that σz = νσx − Eαϑ, and putting this into εx = [σx − νσz]/E + αϑ = 0 gives σx(1 − ν2) = −Eαϑ(1 + ν), that is, σx = −Eαϑ/(1 − ν), which for ϑ = −ΔT is the same EαΔT/(1 − ν). In plane strain the in-plane problem becomes “a two-dimensional elastic body with a thermal strain (1 + ν)αϑ,” and the in-plane stress is again 0 for a linear temperature distribution. However, the side faces of a square sample are free, so the stress relaxes more easily than in an infinitely wide plate, and for a symmetric quench of the upper and lower faces the stress at the center of the surface (divided by EαΔT/(1 − ν)) is 0.988 against the plate’s 0.995 at Fo = 4×10−6 just after the quench, 0.71 against 0.87 at Fo = 3.2×10−3, and 0.49 against 0.73 at Fo = 1.4×10−2; as time passes it falls further below the plate value (we have confirmed that when the same calculation is done with the width of the cross section widened four times, the difference from the plate value at these times is 0.001 or less). The heated face tries to expand and is put in compression, and tension arises in the interior, on the opposite face, and near the corners.

11. Pores and Strength

Pores left in a sintered body greatly reduce its strength. There are three reasons. ① The cross-sectional area that supports the load decreases. ② Stress concentrates around a pore (by a factor of 3 at a circular hole in a plate in §3, and by about 2 even for a spherical pore). ③ A pore acts as a large flaw and becomes the origin of fracture (the pore together with the small cracks at its edge acts as the size a). In addition, Young’s modulus E also decreases.

The following empirical equation is often used for the relation between the porosity P (volume fraction) and the strength.

(94) σf(P)=σf0exp⁡(−bP) (Ryshkewitch–Duckworth equation)

σf0 is the strength of the material without pores, and b is a constant of about 4–7 (the default value in the simulator is 5). Ryshkewitch (1953) reported that the compressive strength of porous alumina and zirconia roughly halves with every 10 % increase in porosity. Setting exp(−b × 0.1) = 1/2 corresponds to b = ln 2/0.1 = 6.9. Taking the logarithm of both sides gives ln σf = ln σf0 − bP, which is a straight line on a semilogarithmic graph. When P is small, exp(−bP) ≈ 1 − bP, so the strength drops by about b % for every 1 % of porosity (for b = 5, exp(−0.05) = 0.951).

At a relative density of 95 % (P = 0.05) and b = 5, exp(−0.25) = 0.779 — just 5 % porosity reduces the strength by more than 20 %. For b = 4–7 the range is 0.819–0.705, and for P = 0.1 it is 0.607. This is why we care about raising the relative density by even a few more percent in sintering. How to reduce pores is covered in the theory of sintering (sintering-simulator-en.html).

Misconception 10: Eq. (94) is not a theoretical equation Eq. (94) is an empirical equation that organizes measured strengths of porous sintered bodies, and b changes with the shape, size, and connectivity of the pores. Also, strength is governed by the weakest link (§7), so even at the same porosity, a single large pore is more dangerous than small pores scattered uniformly. Do not forget that not only the porosity but also the size of the largest pore acts as the a in Eq. (43).

12. Vickers Hardness and Indentation Fracture

The most widely used method for measuring the hardness of ceramics is the Vickers hardness test. The tip of a square-based diamond pyramid (the indenter) is pressed into the sample under a given load, and the hardness is obtained from the size of the dent (the impression) that remains after the load is removed. The procedure is simple, but in ceramics a large load makes cracks run out from the corners of the impression, and there the stress intensity factor of §5 appears as it is. In this section we derive the equation for hardness from the geometry of the indenter (§12.2), look at the key points of the standards (§12.3) and the differences from metals (§12.4), and then treat the equation for the length of the cracks that extend from the impression and the limits of using it to estimate fracture toughness (§12.5–§12.7). In the simulator this corresponds to tab ⑥ (Appendix A.7).

12.1 What is hardness? — Brinell, Vickers, and Knoop

Hardness is “resistance to indentation”; it is not a single material property like Young’s modulus or the yield stress. Under an indenter, elastic deformation occurs first, and as the pressure rises plastic deformation is added (in ceramics, flow or densification in a very small region). When the load is removed the elastic part recovers, and the plastic part remains as the impression. The hardness value is calculated from this remaining impression, so it mainly expresses the resistance to plastic deformation, but it is also affected by the elastic recovery. JIS R 1610 also notes that the impressions in metals are made mainly by plastic deformation, whereas in ceramics elastic–plastic deformation is involved, so care is needed when comparing with the hardness of metals.

The name of the test changes with the shape of the indenter. Brinell hardness is obtained by pressing in a hard sphere and dividing the load by the surface area of the dent (part of a sphere). With a sphere, the shape changes as the dent gets deeper (the ratio of the dent diameter d to the sphere diameter D changes), so the value can change when the load is changed. Vickers hardness (Smith and Sandland, 1922) uses a square pyramid indenter, and the shape of the impression is similar at any load, so ideally a value independent of load is obtained. Knoop hardness uses an elongated rhombic pyramid (the ratio of the diagonals is about 7 : 1) and makes a shallow impression. JIS R 1610 notes that cracks appear more easily with a Vickers indenter than with a Knoop indenter.

Hardness is not “strength.” You may come across the expression “Vickers strength,” but what a Vickers test measures is the Vickers hardness, which is a different quantity from the tensile strength, fracture strength, or fracture toughness. As the table below shows, hardness is the resistance to the plastic deformation left by indentation, strength is the stress at which a specimen breaks, and fracture toughness is the resistance to crack growth. A harder material is not necessarily stronger or harder to break: SiC is harder than silicon nitride, yet its KIC in Table 1 is on the low side (Misconception 11).

12.2 Geometry of the indenter — 136° and the contact area

A Vickers indenter is a pyramid with a square base, and the angle between two opposite faces (the face-to-face angle) is 136°. The angle between the axis of the pyramid and each face is half of that, 68°, and the angle between the surface of the sample and a face is 90° − 68° = 22°. Seen from above, the impression is a square, and its diagonal length is denoted d (Fig. 5 (b)).

HV is conventionally written as the number in kgf/mm2 without the unit, and “1500 HV 1” means that HV = 1500 was obtained at a test force of 9.807 N (1 kgf). In the notation of ISO 6507-1, the hardness value comes first, and after the symbol HV comes the number giving the test force in kgf. The symbol of the test force itself is F (unit N), which is separate from the hardness symbol HV. Therefore “HV 10” alone expresses the test conditions (test force 98.07 N), not a hardness value. For example, if a measurement at 294.2 N (30 kgf) gives 640, it is written “640 HV 30” (the number in this example is not the value of any material, so that it will not be the answer to ⑥). Since 1 kgf/mm2 = 9.80665 MPa, the SI hardness HV is 0.009807 times HV in GPa (1500 HV is 14.7 GPa). Depth. In Fig. 5 (a) the axis and the slope make an angle of 68°, so tan 68° = (s/2)/h. Putting in s = d/√2 gives

Pressing glass with HV = 5.5 GPa at 9.807 N gives d = 57.5 μm. Even if the load is increased tenfold, the diagonal increases only by a factor of √10 = 3.16, and the shape stays similar.

Why 136°? In the Brinell test, a load that makes the diameter of the dent 0.25–0.5 times the diameter of the sphere is preferred. Drawing tangents to the sphere at the edge of an impression with the middle value d/D = 0.375, the angle between the two opposite tangents is, from sin φ = 0.375, φ = 22.0°, and 180° − 2 × 22.0° = 136.0°. The 136° of the Vickers indenter is usually explained as having been chosen to match this angle, so that the Vickers hardness and the Brinell hardness take close values in the range where the hardness is not very high. However, the main text of the original paper by Smith and Sandland (1922) could not be checked. The calculation itself holds as shown above.
Note also that Eq. (96) divides by the area of the slopes, not by the area seen from directly above (the projected area) d2/2. The mean pressure H = 2F/d2, obtained by dividing by the projected area, is 2/1.8544 = 1.0785 times HV, and care is needed because this is often the one used in the indentation fracture equations of §12.5 (Quinn, 2006).

12.4 Metals and ceramics — Tabor’s relation and the indentation size effect

Metals. The impression in a metal is made by plastic flow alone, and the metal does not crack. The material directly under the indenter is constrained by its surroundings and cannot flow out freely, so a mean pressure far higher than the uniaxial flow stress is needed to start plastic deformation. Tabor (1951) analyzed the plastic deformation under an indenter and showed that for metals the following relation holds:

(100) H≈3σr

Here σr is the flow stress at a representative strain (for a Vickers indenter the representative strain is about 8 %). For a fully work-hardened metal, σr is almost the same as the yield stress. For a work-hardening metal, σr is larger than the yield stress σy, so H/3 overestimates σy. The σy ≈ H/3 used in §5.6 is this relation used in the reverse direction as a rough guide. Ceramics and glasses have a small E/H and a large fraction of elastic deformation around the indenter, so H/σy is smaller than 3, about 1.5–2 (Marsh, 1964; §5.6).

Ceramics. The hardness of ceramics (about 10–25 GPa) is an order of magnitude higher than that of steel (about 1–2 GPa; about 7 GPa for hardened steel; all rough guides). As we saw in §6.6, dislocations hardly move in crystals with covalent or ionic bonding, so plastic deformation under the indenter itself hardly occurs. For the same reason, however, stress cannot be relieved by plastic deformation, and with a large load, cracks run out from the corners of the impression. After the load is removed, the diagonals hardly recover, while the depth recovers elastically (Lawn and Marshall, 1984). For that reason, the HV calculated from the diagonals is thought to be less affected by elastic recovery than a hardness calculated from the depth.

Indentation size effect. For an ideal pyramid HV should not depend on the load, but in practice the apparent hardness is often higher at smaller loads, and this is called the indentation size effect. When the relation between load and diagonal is written with Meyer’s equation

(101) F=AMdn ⟹ HV=1.8544Fd2=1.8544AMdn−2

then HV is constant if n = 2, and if n < 2, HV becomes larger the smaller d is (the smaller the load). Li and Bradt (1993) considered that part of the load is used against a resistance near the surface (proportional to d) and proposed the form

(102) F=A1d+A2d2 ⟹ HV=1.8544(A2+A1d)

For large d, HV approaches the constant value 1.8544A2 (a hardness independent of load), and for small d it is higher by A1/d. There are several explanations for the cause: rounding of the indenter tip, a work-affected layer at the surface, cracking around the impression, and so on (Bull et al., 1989). For the silicon nitride standard sample in Table 9, it was 17.3 GPa at 9.8 N and 16.0 GPa at 98 N. When comparing the hardness of ceramics, the load must always be matched.

Misconception 11: It is not true that “the harder the material, the more resistant it is to cracking” Hardness expresses resistance to plastic deformation under indentation, and fracture toughness expresses resistance to crack growth; they are different quantities. SiC is harder than silicon nitride, but its KIC in Table 1 is on the low side. If anything, a hard material in which dislocations hardly move cannot relieve stress at the crack tip, and so tends to be brittle. The brittleness index H/Kc of §12.7 is the ratio of these two.

12.5 Indentation fracture — residual stress and the half-penny crack

Types of cracks. When ceramics or glass is pressed with a sharp indenter, the following cracks form. Radial cracks extend from the corners of the impression along the extensions of the diagonals at the surface; median cracks form directly under the impression in the plane containing the axis; half-penny cracks are made when the two connect, appearing as one crack when seen from the surface and semicircular in cross section (Fig. 5 (c)); lateral cracks spread almost parallel to the surface and cause chipping of the surface; and Palmqvist cracks form shallowly around the corners only when the load is small. Real cracks branch and intersect, and a neat semicircle is only an idealization (Cook and Pharr, 1990).

Where does the force that opens the cracks come from? Marshall and Lawn (1979) and Lawn, Evans, and Marshall (1980) regarded the plastic zone under the impression as “a cavity expanded inside the surrounding elastic body.” The surrounding elastic body has to accommodate the volume displaced by plastic deformation, so even after the load is removed the plastic zone keeps pushing outward on its surroundings (residual stress). This spreading action opens the faces of the cracks surrounding the plastic zone like a wedge. In observations of soda-lime glass, during loading the cracks grow mainly downward, and the radial and lateral cracks at the surface spread mainly while the load is removed. In air they keep growing for a while even after the load has been removed (Lawn and Marshall, 1984).

Determining the form of K by dimensional analysis. When the crack is sufficiently larger than the impression (c ≫ a), the plastic zone looks to the crack like a force concentrated at a single point at the center. For a disc-shaped crack of radius c pulled apart at its center by forces of magnitude Pr on the upper and lower faces, the stress intensity factor is, as a standard solution of fracture mechanics,

(103) KI=Pr(πc)3/2

If we ignore the coefficient, this can be seen from dimensions alone. The unit of K is Pa m1/2 = N m−3/2, and if the only quantities available are a force Pr [N] and a length c [m], the only combination that makes N m−3/2 is Pr/c3/2. The force Pr of the residual stress is proportional to the indentation load F, so we put Pr = (constant) × F, and writing χ for the constant combined with the shape coefficient, we get

(104) KI=χFc3/2 , dKIdc =−32χFc5/2<0

This is where it differs greatly from the cracks of §4 and §5. Under uniform tension, KI = Yσ√(πa) increases with the crack, so once it reaches KIC the crack runs without stopping (unstable). For an indentation crack, KI decreases as the crack grows, so the crack stops stably at the length where KI = Kc. The length of the stopped crack is determined by the toughness of the material, so measuring it lets us estimate the toughness — this is the idea of the indentation fracture method (IF method).

The equation of the IF method. In the expanding-cavity model, the size of the plastic zone and the strength with which it pushes on its surroundings are determined by E/H, giving χ ∝ (E/H)1/2 (Lawn, Evans, and Marshall, 1980; the derivation is omitted). The equation of Anstis et al. (1981) is obtained by fitting the proportionality coefficient ξ to eight ceramics and glasses whose toughness was known from other methods.

(105) Kc=ξ(EH)1/2Fc3/2 ,ξ=0.016±0.004 (H=F2a2=1.0785HV)

c is the length of the crack measured from the center of the impression (half of the length measured from end to end at the surface), and a = d/2 is the half-diagonal. The Japanese standard JIS R 1607:2015 gives priority to the single-edge precracked beam (SEPB) method, and as an option for when that cannot be used it keeps the following equation, which uses the coefficient of Miyoshi et al. (1985) and HV.

(106) KC=0.018(EHV)1/2Fc3/2

With the same measured values, Eq. (106) gives a value 0.018/0.016 × √1.0785 = 1.17 times larger than Eq. (105) (Quinn, 2006). JIS R 1607 requires that the cracks extend from the corners along the extensions of the diagonals, that the lengths of the cracks in the two orthogonal directions differ by 10 % or less of the mean, and that the crack length c (measured from the center of the impression) be at least 2.5 times the half-diagonal a (c/a ≥ 2.5). Note also that the symbol of the result is KC, not KIC.

How does the crack length grow with the load? Putting KI = Kc in Eq. (104) and solving for c gives

(107) c=(χFKc)2/3∝F2/3 , ca∝F2/3F1/2=F1/6

where a ∝ F1/2 is Eq. (99). As the load is raised, the crack grows faster than the impression. A tenfold load increases c/a by a factor of 101/6 = 1.47. For example, for the alumina of Table 1 (E = 380 GPa, Kc = 3.5 MPa m1/2) with HV = 15.2 GPa (H = 16.4 GPa), Eqs. (105) and (107) give c ≈ 36 μm at 9.807 N (a = 17 μm, c/a ≈ 2.1) and c ≈ 170 μm at 98.07 N (a = 55 μm, c/a ≈ 3.1). JIS R 1607 asks for the load to be chosen as large as possible and sets the condition c/a ≥ 2.5, which also brings us closer to the premise c ≫ a of Eq. (104). Note that the condition Anstis et al. (1981) themselves gave for Eq. (105) was a well-developed crack (c ≥ 2a), and 2.5 is the value of JIS R 1607. In the supplement of ⑥, the check is made against 2.5, and this is also written on the screen.

Anstis et al. themselves limited their estimate to saying that the error of the method would probably be within about 30–40 %. Quinn and Bradt (2007) and others have pointed out the following problems.

  • There are dozens of IF equations, and the values they give from the same measurement differ greatly. The coefficient ξ depends on the material.
  • Real cracks are not ideal half-penny cracks; they branch and have lateral cracks.
  • It is difficult to read the position of the crack tip at the surface. Since K ∝ c−3/2, reading c 10 % too long makes K 1.1−3/2 = 0.87 times as large, that is, as much as 13 % lower.
  • If there is slow crack growth due to moisture, c can change with the time between indentation and measurement (JIS R 1607 requires measuring within 10 minutes of indentation).

Quinn of NIST and Bradt of the University of Alabama (2007), taking these into account, concluded that the IF method is not reliable as a fracture test and cannot be clearly defined as to what it measures, so it should not be accepted as a fracture toughness test for ceramics (according to the abstract of the paper published by NIST). JIS R 1607 also gives priority to the SEPB method and keeps the IF method only as a method for when the SEPB method cannot be used. Treat the value from the IF method as an “indentation fracture resistance,” not a value from a standard KIC test, and if it is used at all, limit it to comparisons among materials measured with the same equation and the same test force, or to a guide for quality control (this is the position of this page, not words from the abstract of Quinn and Bradt). For the design of components and the specification of materials, use values measured on specimens with a precrack, such as by the SEPB method.

Misconception 12: “The fracture toughness KIC can be measured from the cracks of a Vickers impression” Eq. (105) is an estimating equation made of a physical idea (a half-penny crack with the force of the residual stress concentrated at its center) and an empirical coefficient. As Table 9 shows, from the same impression the different equations give values that differ by as much as a factor of 1.5, from 3.7 to 5.5 MPa m1/2. When you see a value from the IF method in a paper or catalog, check which equation was used and what c/a was.

12.7 The load at which cracking starts, and the brittleness index

If the load is small, an impression forms but no cracks appear. Lawn and Evans (1977) estimated the load at which cracks begin to appear, F*, from the condition under which a small flaw at the edge of the plastic zone starts to extend under the stress of the plastic zone. The form without the coefficient can be derived from dimensions. If we use only the hardness H [Pa] and the toughness Kc [Pa m1/2] as material quantities, the only combination that makes a length is (Kc/H)2, so the size of the impression when cracks begin to appear is a* ∝ (Kc/H)2. The load at that point follows from the definition of hardness, F ∝ Ha2, and we get

(108) a*∝(KcH)2 , F*∝H(a*)2∝Kc4H3

Since F* is proportional to the fourth power of Kc, a small difference in toughness has a large effect. Comparing with the KIC of Table 1 and the HV of Table 10 (alumina 15.2 GPa, glass 5.5 GPa), the F* of alumina is (3.5/0.75)4 × (5.5/15.2)3 = about 22 times that of glass. In glass, the size of the impression at which cracks begin to appear is a few tens of μm, and in the presence of water the load at which cracks begin to appear can drop by as much as two orders of magnitude (Lawn and Marshall, 1984). The proportionality coefficient could not be confirmed in the original source, so only the ratio is shown here. In polycrystalline materials the effects of grain boundaries and pores are large, and this ratio does not necessarily hold as it is.

Lawn and Marshall (1979) called H/Kc in Eq. (108) the brittleness index.

(109) B=HKc [m−1/2]

Since H is the “resistance to deformation” and Kc is the “resistance to cracking,” a material with a larger B is more likely to crack before it deforms when it is pressed. Calculating with the KIC of Table 1 and the HV of Table 10 gives 5.5×109/0.75×106 ≈ 7300 for soda-lime glass, and ≈ 6600 for silicon carbide, ≈ 4300 for alumina, ≈ 2300 for silicon nitride, and ≈ 1500 m−1/2 for Y-TZP. The brittleness index shown in the supplement of simulator tab ⑥ uses the HV of Table A3 instead of Table 10 (alumina 16 GPa, silicon nitride 15 GPa), so it comes out slightly larger: 4600 m−1/2 for alumina and 2500 m−1/2 for silicon nitride. Quinn and Quinn (1997) proposed a method of obtaining B from the relation between the sizes of the impression and of the cracks when the load is varied.

What this model can and cannot tell you What it can tell you are the trends “strength falls as the −1/2 power of the crack size,” “the higher the toughness, the larger the tolerable crack, as the square of the toughness,” “the distribution of flaw sizes determines m, and the larger the volume, the weaker the specimen,” and “materials with high strength and small Eα withstand quenching well”, as well as the relative comparison between materials. What it cannot tell you is a quantitative prediction of the strength or lifetime of a specific product.
The material constants in Table 1 are rough values for teaching, and even materials with the same name differ greatly with the manufacturing method, grain size, and purity. The flaw model is idealized: it does not consider the orientation or shape of cracks, the distinction between surface scratches and internal pores, interactions between cracks, or the slow growth of cracks caused by moisture (delayed fracture). Obtaining a valid plane-strain value of the KIC of a metal requires a very thick specimen (§6), so the values in Table 1 cannot be compared directly with those of ceramics.

Problem 1 Use the SiC-equivalent values E = 410 GPa, γs = 1.0 J m−2, and a0 = 0.2 nm. (1) Find the theoretical strength σth with Eq. (15) and compare it with the strength of 400 MPa in Table 1. (2) Keeping γs = 1.0 J m−2, what is the half-length of the through-crack that breaks at 400 MPa when Eq. (35) is used? (3) Using KIC = 3.5 MPa m1/2 from Table 1, find the depth of the surface crack that breaks at 400 MPa with Eq. (43) (Y = 1.12). What does the difference from (2) mean?

Solution. (1) σth = √(410×109 × 1.0/(0.2×10−9)) = √(2.05×1021) = 4.53×1010 Pa = 45.3 GPa (E/9.1). This is about 113 times the actual strength.
(2) ac = 2 × 410×109 × 1.0/[π × (400×106)2] = 1.63×10−6 m = 1.63 μm.
(3) ac = (1/π)[3.5/(1.12 × 400)]2 m = 1.94×10−5 m = 19.4 μm, which is 11.9 times the value in (2). This is because in an actual SiC the resistance to crack growth is γeff = KIC2/2E = 14.9 J m−2, nearly 15 times the surface energy. (The factor 11.9 equals γeff/γs = 14.9 divided by Y2 = 1.25 for a surface crack.)

Problem 2 A plate with a crack of half-length a = 20 μm and tip radius of curvature ρ = 0.5 nm is loaded in tension with σ = 50 MPa. (1) Find the maximum stress at the tip with Eq. (18). (2) If the tip is rounded to ρ = 5 nm, by what factor does the maximum stress decrease?

Solution. (1) √(a/ρ) = √(20×10−6/(0.5×10−9)) = √(4×104) = 200, so the concentration factor is 1 + 2 × 200 = 401. σmax = 50 × 401 = 2.005×104 MPa = 20.05 GPa.
(2) √(20×10−6/(5×10−9)) = √4000 = 63.2, so the concentration factor is 1 + 2 × 63.2 = 127.5 and σmax = 6.37 GPa. 401/127.5 = 3.15, so it is about 1/3.15. For a ≫ ρ, σmax ∝ ρ−1/2, so we can also estimate that making ρ 10 times larger reduces it to 1/√10 = 1/3.16. This is why a metal becomes stronger when the tip of a crack is rounded by plastic deformation.

Problem 3 (1) In Eq. (35), by what factor does the strength change if the crack half-length is multiplied by 4? (2) With alumina-equivalent values (E = 380 GPa, γs = 1.0 J m−2), find the critical half-length ac at σ = 200 MPa and the height of the peak in Eq. (32). (3) What is ac in plane strain (ν = 0.22)?

Solution. (1) σf ∝ a−1/2, so the strength becomes 4−1/2 = 1/2 of the original.
(2) ac = 2 × 380×109 × 1.0/[π × (200×106)2] = 6.05×10−6 m = 6.05 μm. The height of the peak is, from §4.4, U(ac) = 2γsac = 1.21×10−5 J m−1 = 12.1 μJ m−1 (per unit thickness).
(3) E is replaced by E/(1 − ν2), so ac becomes 1/(1 − 0.222) = 1.051 times as large, 6.36 μm.

Problem 4 Consider a component under a tensile stress of σ = 500 MPa, and assume the cracks are surface cracks (Y = 1.12). (1) Find the tolerable crack ac of alumina, silicon nitride, and Y-TZP with Eq. (43). (2) Suppose that nondestructive inspection can always find a crack 50 μm deep or more. For which material is there no risk that a component that passed the inspection will break at 500 MPa? (3) Find Gc and γeff of silicon nitride and Y-TZP.

Solution. (1) From ac = (1/π)(KIC/560 MPa)2: alumina 12.4 μm, silicon nitride 36.5 μm, Y-TZP 65.0 μm.
(2) The cracks that the inspection can miss are those shallower than 50 μm. The only material whose ac is larger than that is Y-TZP, while alumina and silicon nitride may break from a missed crack shallower than 50 μm.
(3) Silicon nitride: Gc = (6.0×106)2/(310×109) = 116 J m−2, γeff = 58.1 J m−2. Y-TZP: Gc = (8.0×106)2/(210×109) = 305 J m−2, γeff = 152 J m−2. They are 58 and 152 times γs = 1.0 J m−2, respectively, and represent the magnitude of the shielding by bridging and transformation (§6).

Problem 5 The strength of a certain material follows a Weibull distribution with m = 10 and σ0 = 400 MPa (the specimen size is constant). (1) Find the probability of breaking at 300 MPa. (2) Find the design stress that keeps the fracture probability at 10−4. (3) Find the mean strength and the coefficient of variation. What percentage breaks when a stress equal to the mean strength is applied?

Solution. (1) (300/400)10 = 0.0563, so Pf = 1 − exp(−0.0563) = 0.0548 ≈ 5.5 %.
(2) From Eq. (69), σd ≈ 400 × (10−4)1/10 = 400 × 0.398 = 159 MPa.
(3) From Eq. (63), ⟨σ⟩ = 400 × Γ(1.1) = 400 × 0.95135 = 381 MPa, and from Eq. (65) CoV = 0.120. At 381 MPa, (0.95135)10 goes into the exponent and Pf = 0.455, so about 46 % of the specimens break. The “mean strength” is not a number that can be used for design.

Problem 6 In the model of §7.2, starting from the default values (KIC = 3.5 MPa m1/2, Y = 1.12, amin = 5 μm, k = 5, λV = 300; with these, σ* = 788.5 MPa, m = 10, and σ0 = 446 MPa), what happens to m and σ0 if we make the following changes? (1) The powder processing is improved so that large flaws become less likely, giving k = 7.5. (2) The specimen volume is doubled. (3) The smallest flaw size is made amin = 2.5 μm.

Solution. From Eqs. (58) and (61), m = 2k, σ0 = σ*(λV)−1/m, and σ* ∝ amin−1/2.
(1) m = 15. σ0 = 788.5 × 300−1/15 = 788.5 × 0.684 = 539 MPa. Not only does the scatter decrease, but the characteristic strength also rises.
(2) λV becomes 600, m stays 10, and σ0 = 446 × 2−1/10 = 446 × 0.933 = 416 MPa (this is just the volume effect of Eq. (73)).
(3) σ* becomes √2 times larger, 1115 MPa, and σ0 also becomes √2 times larger, 630 MPa (calculated from the unrounded values). m stays 10.

Problem 7 A small square bar made of a material with m = 10 was tested in 3-point bending, and a strength of 600 MPa was obtained for a certain fracture probability. For the same fracture probability, (1) what is the strength, in MPa, if the same bar is tested in uniform tension? (2) What is it if the same bar is tested in 4-point bending (inner spacing L/2)? (3) What is it for a component in which uniform tension acts on a volume 100 times the volume between the supports in the 3-point bending test?

Solution. (1) From Eq. (80), 600/2421/10 = 600/1.731 = 347 MPa.
(2) From Eq. (79), Veff(4-point bending)/Veff(3-point bending) = (1/40.3)/(1/242) = 6.0, so 600/6.01/10 = 600/1.196 = 502 MPa.
(3) The effective volume of the component is 100V and that of 3-point bending is V/242, so the volume ratio is 24200. From Eq. (73), 600/242001/10 = 600/2.744 = 219 MPa. This is about 0.36 times the catalog 3-point bending strength (a little more than one third).

Problem 8 (1) The alumina and silicon carbide of Table 1 are quenched by ΔT = 150 K. Use Eq. (88) to judge whether they crack. (2) The relative density of a sintered alumina body is 95 % (P = 0.05, b = 5). Find the strength from Eq. (94) and the value of R when E and α are left unchanged. (3) In reality, pores also lower E. Is the estimate of R in (2) larger or smaller than the real value?

Solution. (1) Alumina has R = 90 K < 150 K, so it cracks. Silicon carbide has R = 205 K > 150 K, so it does not crack. However, R is the lower limit for infinitely fast quenching, so whether the alumina actually cracks also depends on the cooling rate and the size of the sample (see the note in §10).
(2) σf = 350 × exp(−0.25) = 350 × 0.779 = 273 MPa. R = 273×106 × 0.78/(380×109 × 8.0×10−6) = 70 K (down from 90 K for the dense body).
(3) Since R ∝ σf/E, a lower E raises R. The estimate in (2) is off on the side of being smaller than the real value (part of the loss of strength is canceled).

Problem 9 (1) A ceramic was pressed with a Vickers indenter at a test force of 9.807 N, and the diagonals of the impression were d1 = 34.4 μm and d2 = 34.8 μm. Does this impression satisfy the requirement of JIS R 1610 that “the difference between the two diagonals is less than 5 % of the mean”? (2) d = 2a = 140 μm = 1.40×10−4 m, so HV = 1.8544 × 196/(1.40×10−4)2 Pa = 363.5/1.96×10−8 Pa = 1.85×1010 Pa = 18.5 GPa. c/a = 300/70.0 = 4.29 (2.5 or more). The blue frame is the range of the magnified view on the right. The right shows σyy around the tip of an edge crack (depth a from the surface), painted blue–white–red from −2σref to +2σref (σref is σf for ceramics and σy for metals). The figure shows the crack and the dimension line for a, Irwin’s plastic zone (a dashed circle of diameter 2rp centered at a distance rp ahead of the tip; for ceramics, “plastic zone almost 0”), the position of the tolerable crack ac (green dashed line), and the state (safe / fractured (cleavage) / blunted by the plastic zone / general yielding). What you see (lower figure: the atomic scale, schematic). Atoms of a triangular lattice are moved by the displacement of Eq. (46) and colored by the stretch of the bonds (stretched: red; compressed: blue; for glass and silica glass the lattice is shifted to make it look amorphous). In Y-TZP, atoms in the transformation zone (the shape of Eq. (52)) turn orange and remain as a band behind the crack. A.7 ⑥ Vickers hardness

What you see in this tab. A square diamond pyramid (face-to-face angle 136°) is pressed into the sample by the testing machine, and after the load is removed you measure the diagonals d1 and d2 of the remaining impression yourself, calculate HV = 0.1891F/d2 (F [N], d [mm]; Eq. (97)) from d = (d1 + d2)/2, and finally check your answer against the automatic measurement — that is, you run the procedure of the Vickers hardness test itself (§12.1–§12.3). At the same test force, a soft metal is left with a large impression and a hard ceramic with a small one. In ceramics and glass, when the test force is large, cracks run from the corners of the impression, and their length grows as the 2/3 power of the load, faster than the impression does (§12.5, Eq. (107)). The IF method, which estimates the toughness from the crack length, is kept apart from the hardness measurement in a collapsible “Supplement” section, and together with its limits (§12.6) it is an advanced topic. What you learn by measuring. Since HV is proportional to the −2 power of d, reading the mean d 1 % too long lowers HV by about 2 %. For an impression with d ≈ 100 μm, misreading d by 1 μm changes HV by only about 2 %, but for an impression with d ≈ 10 μm (the size made by pressing a hard ceramic at 0.1 kgf) the same 1 μm changes it by as much as −17 % to +23 %. You can get a feel for why small impressions at low test forces are hard to measure, and why the standards cover d ≥ 20 μm (Table 8). The error in reading by the graduations follows the same logic: reading an impression with d ≈ 100 μm in a 500 μm field (1 division = 10 μm) gives −17 % to +23 % for ±1 division, but with “Zoom to the impression,” which makes the field 200 μm (1 division = 5 μm), this shrinks to −9 % to +11 % (you can check the numbers in the “If d is misread” rows on the screen of ⑥). Model and approximations. Everything is given by closed-form equations; no numerical computation is involved.

  • Hardness and impression: HV is given for each material (Table A3); the diagonal is d = √(1.8544F/HV) (Eq. (99)), the depth is h = d/7.0006 (Eq. (98)), and the true HV is HV[GPa]/0.009807. The measured HV is calculated from the mean d of the measuring lines by HV = 0.1891F/d2 (Eq. (97)), and the “automatic measurement” of the answer check is the value obtained by putting the model’s d (the corners of the impression at ±a) into the same equation (= the true HV). The indentation size effect (§12.4) is not included, so the true HV is the same at every test force, the two diagonals are equal, and a difference between d1 and d2 arises only from measurement error.
  • Crack length: It is c = [ξ(E/H)1/2F/KIC]2/3, obtained by solving Eq. (105) for c, with ξ = 0.016 and H = 2F/d2 = 1.0785HV (the hardness divided by the projected area; see the box in §12.2). The values of Quinn in Table 9 can be reproduced within ±1.2 % by both equations, Eq. (105) calculated with the projected-area H and Eq. (106) with HV, if E = 320 GPa is assumed, so the H in Eq. (105) is taken as the hardness based on the projected area. Equation (105) assumes c ≫ a, so the lengths of short cracks with c/a < 2.5 are extrapolated rough values. Since c ∝ KIC−2/3, taking KIC 20 % lower makes c 1.16 times larger. The KIC values in Table 1 are rough values that vary with the manufacturing method and composition even for materials of the same name, so please treat the crack lengths as rough values as well.
  • Conditions for drawing cracks: For ceramics, cracks are drawn only when F ≥ Fon and c > a; they are of the half-penny type if c/a ≥ 2.5 (the condition of JIS R 1607) and short cracks if 1 < c/a < 2.5. Fon is a guide for display. For glass, in line with the statement that the impression at which cracks begin to appear is a few tens of μm (Lawn and Marshall, 1984; §12.7), cracks are made to appear from HV 0.2 (d = 26 μm). The coefficient of F* ∝ Kc4/H3 in Eq. (108) could not be confirmed, so the Fon of the other materials is not determined from this equation either. The chipping warning applies from 49 N for glass (a rough guide) and from 196 N for silicon carbide. The value for silicon carbide is based on the statement in the manuscript of Quinn (2006) that severe chipping and shattering occur in SiC and B4C at 196 N and 294 N. Metals do not crack.
  • Size of the plastic zone (used only for the size in the figure; the value is not displayed): For ceramics, the form of Lawn, Evans, and Marshall (1980), b/a = (E/H)1/2(cot ψ)1/3 (H = 1.0785HV, ψ = 74°), is used. For the half-penny type, so that the crack can be seen extending beyond the plastic zone, the radius of the plastic zone drawn in the figure is limited to bd = min(b, 0.65c) (the b/a in Table A4 is the model value before this limitation). Whether ψ should be 74° or 68° could not be confirmed in the original source, so b has an uncertainty of a factor of 1.12. For metals, Johnson’s (1970) expanding cavity model, (b/ae)3 = E tan β/[6(1 − ν)Y] + 2(1 − 2ν)/[3(1 − ν)], is used with ae = a√(2/π) (the radius of the circle with the same projected area), tan β = √(2π)/7.0006 = 0.358 (β = 19.7° is the angle between the surface and the face of a cone with the same projected area and depth), and Y = HV/3 (the rough value of Eq. (100)). During loading, a and b are made to grow as the 1/2 power of the fraction of the load.
  • Recovery of depth (a rough guide for the appearance of the figure): The depth after unloading is taken as hf/h = 1 − 5HV/Er (lower limit 0.3) and 1/Er = (1 − ν2)/E + (1 − νi2)/Ei, where Ei = 1141 GPa and νi = 0.07 for diamond are the values used by Oliver and Pharr (1992). The coefficient 5 is not a literature value but a provisional value to show the tendency that the larger H/E is, the more the depth recovers. During unloading the depth is recovered linearly, and the diagonal is not recovered (§12.4).
  • How the cracks grow (schematic): For materials of the half-penny type, the median crack during loading is taken as a disk with its center at depth (bd + c)/2 and radius (c − bd)/2, and grown as c ∝ g2/3 (Eq. (107)) and bd ∝ g1/2 with the fraction of the load g (only when gF ≥ Fon and c > 1.15bd). Since bd ≤ 0.65c, the latter condition always holds at maximum load (g = 1), and at intermediate loads it holds from g ≈ 0.17 (= (1.15 × 0.65)6) at the latest. During unloading, the center of the disk is moved toward the surface while its radius is expanded to c, and the length of the surface crack is extended linearly from a to c.
  • Time: The loading time of 5 s follows the 2–8 s of ISO 6507-1 and the holding time of 15 s follows the holding time of JIS R 1610 (Table 8); the unloading time of 3 s and the approach and departure times are values chosen for display.
  • Material values: Among the HV values, 16 GPa for alumina, 23 for silicon carbide, 15 for silicon nitride, and 12 for Y-TZP are rounded rough values that agree within 10 % with the manufacturer’s values of Table 10 (15.2, 23.0, 14.0, and 12.3 GPa) and with the 16.0 GPa of SRM 2100 at 98 N. The 5.5 GPa for soda-lime glass is in the middle of the rough range of 5–6 GPa in Table 10; the 8.0 GPa for silica glass, 1.3 GPa for structural steel, and 1.0 GPa for the aluminum alloy are general rough values whose sources could not be confirmed. The same values are used as for σy ≈ H/3 in ③ (§5.6, A.9).
  • Material categories: sintered oxides (alumina, Y-TZP), sintered non-oxides (silicon carbide, silicon nitride), glass (soda-lime glass, silica glass), and metals (structural steel, Al alloy). The manufacturing methods of the four ceramics have not been verified against sources, so “(tentative)” is attached to them (§12.8). The category is used only for display and color coding, not in any calculation.
  • Graduations and sensitivity to misreading: One division δ is 1/50 of the width of the field of view rounded to the series 1, 2, 2.5, 5 (see the text above). The change in HV is (d/(d + δ))2 − 1 (reading too long) and (d/(d − δ))2 − 1 (reading too short), where d is the mean measured by the student.

A.9 Models used in the calculations, and cautions

Which equations each tab computes on the spot is summarized in §13. Here we add the settings that are not written in §13 and the cautions for reading the numbers on the screen.

  • Necking of the metals in ①: The distribution of the true strain in the necked part is taken as ε(ζ) = n + Δ/[1 + (ζ/ℓ)2] (ζ is the distance from the center of the gauge length and ℓ = 1.5 mm is a rough value of the order of the thickness), and outside the neck ε = n is kept as it is (elastic recovery is ignored). The extra elongation due to necking, ∫en(eΔL(ζ) − 1)dζ (with L(ζ) = 1/[1 + (ζ/ℓ)2]; Simpson integration), is matched to L0(ef − eu) to determine Δf at fracture by bisection (0.627 for steel and 0.370 for the Al alloy). The uniform elongation eu and the elongation at fracture ef are rough values of 18 % and 26 % for structural steel and 8 % and 12 % for the aluminum alloy. The minimum cross section is Amin = A0e−(n+Δ) (the width and thickness shrink at the same rate) and the load is F = AminKεn, which decreases monotonically after the maximum-load point. From the geometry, the shape of the neck is a/R = a2Δe−2ε/ℓ2 (with a = (w0/2)e−ε/2), and the value 0.646 for steel at fracture is close to the 0.690 given by the empirical equation of Le Roy et al. (1981), a/R = 1.1(ε − n). Bridgman’s coefficient B is not multiplied into the load because, in this model in which a/R increases with Δ, doing so would make the load rise slightly just after necking begins and would break the agreement between the Considère condition and the maximum-load point. B is used only for the redistribution within the cross section (Eq. (9)), and along the length it is weakened by the weight L(ζ). The nominal stress at fracture is 0.69 times the tensile strength for steel and 0.79 times for the Al alloy. The stress concentration factor of the shoulder, Kt = 1.08 (a rough value for a shoulder with a diameter ratio of 2 and a radius ratio of 1.5), is applied with a weight within ±3 mm of the shoulder, and for metals it is removed once the plastic strain exceeds 0.2 %. The absorbed energy is the trapezoidal integral of the tabulated curve (it agrees with an integral using 200,000 subdivisions), and the deformation of the shoulders and the grip section is ignored. In the magnified view, slip lines appear in each grain at a plastic strain of 0–2 % with a spacing of 0.45d/(1 + 30εp). The growth of the voids is meant only to show their shape, and no growth equation is solved.
  • Verification of the stress field in ②: The residual force on the edge of the hole (as a ratio to σ) is 4×10−9 for b/a = 1 and 2×10−6 for 0.1; at a far point (300a, 400a) the stress agrees with the uniform stress to 10−5; the values at the tip are 3, 5, 9, 21, and 101 for b/a = 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.1, and 0.02; for a circular hole it agrees with Kirsch’s equation (25) to 10−15; and the residuals of the equilibrium (div σ) and compatibility conditions checked by finite differences were of the size of the truncation error. The energy balance is the value for a plane-stress through-crack converted to a plate thickness of 1 m.
  • Stress field of the edge crack in ③: The edge crack is approximated by σyy ≈ σ[1 + Y(σW/σ − 1)] (Y = 1.12), in which the perturbation part of Westergaard’s solution σW for a central crack is multiplied by Y. It agrees with the K field of Eq. (46) at the tip and returns to σ far away, but it does not satisfy the condition at the free surface x = 0 exactly, and −0.12σ remains on the crack faces. Y = 1.12 is the value in the limit where the crack is shallow compared with the width of the plate; at a/W = 0.1 (a = 5 mm) it actually rises to about 1.19. The displacements in the atomic picture are those of the plane-strain K field (ν = 0.25), with √r capped at four times the atomic spacing; the amplitude at which the bond at the tip is stretched by exactly 100 % is found by bisection and multiplied by εb = 0.2 to give the amplitude at Ktip = Kcleave. Since the stretch is not proportional to the amplitude, the stretch at that point is about 19 % (the bond color is 0.95 on a scale on which 20 % is 1). The number of dislocation pairs is 1 + floor(3 ln(K/Ke)/ln(KIC/Ke)) (at most 4), and the Burgers vectors are 0.248 nm for Fe and 0.286 nm for Al. The rough values of the hardness H are 5.5 GPa for glass, 8 for silica glass, 16 for alumina, 23 for SiC, 15 for Si3N4, and 12 for Y-TZP (shared with ⑥; Table A3), and σy ≈ H/3 is 5.3 GPa for alumina and 7.7 GPa for SiC. γs and γus are those of Table 4. The cracked state is determined from the current σ and a, so if you lower σ or a after cracking, it returns to a new specimen.
  • The specimens in ④ and ⑤: Each contains cracks, λV in number on average (Poisson distribution), and the crack size a (the depth of a crack entering from the surface) follows the Pareto distribution P(> a) = (amin/a)k; each crack causes fracture at σ = KIC/(Y√(πa)), and the largest crack (the one that starts to extend at the lowest stress) determines the strength of the specimen; this is the model (§7.2). The strengths in ④ use mulberry32(seed) in the same order and the same number of times as the calculation in §8.2, so they agree exactly, and the random numbers for drawing are a separate sequence (the exclusive OR of the seed and 0x5bd1e995). The number of bins of the histogram in ④ is 1.6√N rounded into the range 8–24. Schematic 1 of ⑤ has λV1 = 8 cracks, and the crack length is 2.2 px × a/amin (capped at 42 px; not to scale), the cracks in the omitted part number about 8V2/V1 − (the number drawn), and their largest is chosen from umin = 1 − R1/N (R is a uniform random number). V1 and V2 use separate random number sequences, so even at V2/V1 = 1 the cracks drawn are not the same. In the Monte Carlo of ⑤, random numbers from a single seed are used in the order of 200 specimens of volume V1 followed by 200 specimens of V2 = 10V1.
  • Calculation in ⑥: All the physical quantities are given by the closed-form equations of Appendix A.7 and are uniquely determined once the material and test force are chosen. What was checked: HV = 18.5 GPa from the calculation example of JIS R 1607 (196 N, d = 140 μm), 1549 HV 1 from SRM 2831 (9.807 N, d = 34.6 μm), agreement of the 12 test forces shown to four digits with the test-force values of ISO 6507-1 (0.9807–490.3 N), reproduction of the values in Table 9 with Eqs. (105) and (106) to within ±1.2 % for E = 320 GPa, the IF-method Kc with the true d returning to KIC to a precision of 10−9, and its ratio to Eq. (106) being constant at 1.1683. For the plastic zone of steel, Johnson’s (1970) mean pressure pm = (2/3)Y[1 + ln(E tan β/3Y)] becomes 1.46 GPa at Y = 0.43 GPa, which agrees within 4 % with the projected-area hardness 1.0785 × 1.3 = 1.40 GPa. The scale of the cross-section view and the field of view of the microscope are determined from the impression and cracks at the maximum test force and do not change during playback. The figure comparing materials draws only the post-test state at the current test force.
  • Calculation in ⑦: The temperature is the exact solution (the table for display has 9 × 241 points), and the finite element mesh has 33 × 77 nodes (horizontally the mesh is made finer near the sides by the coordinate s − 0.75 sin(2πs)/2π, and vertically finer near the surfaces by widening the elements by a factor of 1.15 each from a surface element of 5×10−4L); rigid-body motion is removed by setting to zero both displacement components of the lower-left node and the vertical displacement of the lower-right node (since the load is self-balanced, the stress is unchanged, and the reaction forces are of the order of 10−12 or smaller). The starting points of cracks are chosen as up to six nodes with σ1 ≥ σf, taken in decreasing order of σ1 and at least 0.2L apart from each other, and at each time the longest crack is displayed. The time of cracking is obtained by linear interpolation in log Fo between frames. κ is the rough value of Table 7, and the porosity equation (94) is an empirical one that does not include the effect of pores on E and α (Problem 8 (3)).
