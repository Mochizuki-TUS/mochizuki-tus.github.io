Calcined gypsum (hemihydrate gypsum) and cement harden when water is added, not because the water evaporates, but because the powder dissolves in the water and a different solid that has taken up water (a hydrate) precipitates and joins up. This page explains that mechanism in the following order: the reaction equations and the mass and volume balance, solubility and supersaturation, nucleation and growth, the heat-release curve, and Powers’ model of volumes. How to read the simulator screen, and the equations and values used in the calculations (distinguishing literature values, empirical formulas, and provisional values), are collected in Appendix A; the items we would like to confirm with Prof. Mochizuki are collected in Items to Confirm.

The backbone of this page ① Why does it harden? The dissolved components precipitate as a solid that has taken up water as water of crystallization or structural water, and these solids join up (hydration). ② Where does it precipitate, and in what form? In gypsum, needle-like dihydrate crystals form in the supersaturated liquid and interlock; in cement, C–S–H and other products grow from the particle surfaces and bind the particles to one another. ③ What happens to the excess water? Water that is not used in the reaction remains as pores, so the more water is added, the more pores there are (§5, §9).

1. Introduction — What “Hardening When Water Is Added” Means

Both calcined gypsum and cement lose their fluidity within tens of minutes to a few hours of being mixed with water (setting), and keep getting stronger afterward (hardening). During this process the powder dissolves in the water, and the dissolved components precipitate as a different solid that has taken up water. In gypsum this solid is dihydrate gypsum, in which water molecules have entered the crystal (water of crystallization); in cement it is C–S–H, calcium hydroxide, and the like, in which water has entered the structure. Even if you dry the material after it has hardened, this water does not leave. Only the water that was not used in the reaction is removed by drying.

Another important point is the route by which the material dissolves first and then precipitates (dissolution–precipitation). The powder particles do not simply swell and stick together; the material first passes through the liquid and then precipitates in a different place and in a different form. This idea goes back to Le Chatelier (1887), and today it is observed in situ by synchrotron diffraction and X-ray CT (Refs. 6–10). The way matter is transported is completely different from sintering (sintering simulator), in which heating joins powder particles to one another by atomic diffusion.

2.2 Heat release and volume

The hydration of hemihydrate to dihydrate is an exothermic reaction, and the code of CEMHYD3D (NIST’s cement hydration model) uses 132 J per 1 g of β-hemihydrate (Ref. 4). Calculating from the heats of formation gives ΔH ≈ −19.2 kJ/mol, which agrees with this value. The value from a thermodynamic database that corresponds to the α form gives −17.0 kJ/mol (118 J/g) (Ref. 3). When the temperature is actually measured in a container that is not adiabatic, it rises from 20 °C to about 30 °C for the β form (Ref. 6). The simulator assumes that the temperature is held constant in a thermostatic bath.

As for volume, hemihydrate 52.97 cm³/mol (density 2.74 g/cm³) + water 27.0 cm³ → dihydrate 74.21 cm³/mol (density 2.32 g/cm³), so the product is smaller than the reactants by 5.8 cm³/mol (7.2 %; the value with the density of water taken as 1 g/cm³) (chemical shrinkage). On the other hand, looking at the solid alone, the volume increases by a factor of 1.40, and this gradually fills the gaps between the grains.

3. Solubility and Supersaturation — Why Calcined Gypsum Hardens

Hemihydrate gypsum is more soluble in water than dihydrate gypsum. At 25 °C the solubility of the dihydrate is 15.1 mmol/kg (PHREEQC Example 2) and that of the hemihydrate is about 58 mmol/kg, so the ratio is about 3.9 (Table 2). When the hemihydrate dissolves, the liquid immediately becomes supersaturated about fourfold with respect to the dihydrate. When the dihydrate precipitates from the supersaturated liquid, the liquid becomes more dilute, and more hemihydrate dissolves to make up for it. This repetition of “hemihydrate dissolves ⇄ dihydrate precipitates” is what dissolution–precipitation really is.

For this reason, warming does not necessarily make it harden faster. The rate coefficient of the reaction increases with temperature, but the driving force (supersaturation) becomes smaller. For dental and building gypsum, it is often explained that raising the temperature of the mixing water makes setting faster up to around 40 °C and slower above that (primary sources were not checked in this survey).

What happens when water is added to uncalcined dihydrate powder Even when dihydrate gypsum powder dissolves in water, the liquid becomes concentrated only up to the solubility of the dihydrate (S = 1). The liquid does not become supersaturated, so no new crystals form and the grains do not join up. Calcined gypsum hardens because it is dihydrate that has been heated to drive out part of its water, turning it into hemihydrate, which dissolves more readily than the dihydrate. You can check this with comparison condition G in simulator tab ①.

4. Nucleation, Growth, and Setting

4.1 Nucleation

For a crystal to form from a supersaturated liquid, the supersaturation must be large enough to outweigh the surface-energy cost of a small crystal. In classical nucleation theory, the nucleation rate J takes the form

(4) J=J0exp⁡(−B(ln⁡S)2) ,S=ccDH

(B is a constant proportional to the cube of the surface energy). When S is small, J drops sharply, so almost no nuclei form at high temperature or in dihydrate powder. The simulator uses this form, and J0 and B are provisional values for teaching. Nuclei are assumed to form in proportion to the amount of surface of the hemihydrate grains (the sum of their perimeters) (a schematic of heterogeneous nucleation). La Bella et al. (2023) report that the contribution of dihydrate forming directly and epitaxially on the surface of the hemihydrate is small (Ref. 7). The simulator therefore does not attach nuclei directly to the grain surface but places them in the concentrated liquid around a grain. One grain is chosen with a weight given by its original perimeter (a grain that has completely dissolved is not chosen, and the next grain is taken), and the nucleus is placed at a distance from the grain center between rL − 0.3δ and rL + δ (rL is the radius of the grain at w/p = 0.40, and δ = 14 px is the width of the concentrated liquid around a grain; schematic). Because the position is the same regardless of w/p, it is easy to compare the setting times when w/p is changed (when w/p is large the grains are small, so this band shifts outside the original outline r0. The shift is at most about 5 px at w/p = 1.0, and for large grains the lower edge of the band also lies outside the original outline). The fraction of nuclei that form inside the space left by a dissolved grain depends on the time (counting the nuclei whose center lies inside the original outline of some grain; 20 °C). At the moment of setting it is about 10 % or less for any w/p (4–11 %), with no trend in w/p. At 120 min it is about 20 % for w/p = 0.4 and less than 10 % for 0.7 and above, so in the later stage the larger w/p is, the smaller the fraction. When the center would fall inside the current grain, the nucleus is placed again outside the current edge (up to rL + δ), and when it would fall inside a neighboring grain, it is moved out beyond the edge of that grain. In this way the nuclei form either in the space left by a dissolved grain or in the concentrated liquid outside, that is, in the liquid. The number that selects the grain and the direction are not random but come from a sequence with golden-ratio steps, which distributes the nuclei evenly among the grains and over the directions (to reduce the scatter of the setting time caused by the random seed; §4.3). Because the grain is chosen with a weight given by its original perimeter, in the later stage, when the grains have become small, this deviates slightly from the formula for the nucleation rate (proportional to the current perimeter) (the difference up to about the time of setting is small). A grain that has completely dissolved is not chosen, so once a grain has dissolved away, no nuclei form near it. However, the grains are packed densely, most of the liquid outside the grains lies within δ of a grain, and the needles grow to many times δ in length, so the bias in the positions of the needles is hardly visible in the figure.

4.2 Growth and degree of reaction

The dihydrate that forms grows into needles and later becomes thicker (Refs. 7, 8). In the simulator, for each representative needle, the length ℓ and the thickness w are grown by

(5) dℓdt=gLk(T)(S−1)1+ℓ/ℓc, dwdt=gWk(T)(S−1)

and the volume fraction of the dihydrate is expressed by the fraction of the area occupied by the needles in the figure, Σℓw/A (the area fraction in the two-dimensional figure is regarded as the volume fraction). The rate at which the hemihydrate dissolves is d nHH/dt ∝ −(grain perimeter)·(cHH − c), and the concentration of the liquid c is determined by the difference between the amount dissolved and the amount precipitated. E = 30 kJ/mol in k(T) = exp[−(E/R)(1/T − 1/293.15 K)] is also a provisional value. The curve of the degree of reaction α obtained in this way (the fraction of the hemihydrate that has become dihydrate) is S-shaped, and it is known to be well described by the Avrami form α = 1 − exp[−k(t − t0)n] (n ≈ 3 for the β form; Ref. 6).

The coefficients were chosen to be close to “about 25–30 min to reach 50 % and about 60–70 min to exceed 90 %” for the β form of Gurgul et al. (2019) (w/p = 0.8, about 20 °C) (read from the abstract; to be confirmed), and to the initial set of 10.4 min and the final set of 16.0 min of building plaster (w/p = 0.5) in Darmawan et al. (2020). In the simulator’s representative condition (w/p = 0.8, 20 °C), it takes 27 min to reach 50 % and 52.5 min to reach 90 %, and the setting at w/p = 0.5 is at 14.6 min (Appendix A.5). The induction period (until α reaches 1 %) is about 3 min, which is shorter than the 9 min of Gurgul et al.

4.3 Setting = connection of the needle network

When the needles cross one another and form a network connected from one end of the sample to the other, the paste begins to hold its shape. Morgado et al. (2019) report that the fraction of needles belonging to the percolating (connected) cluster is a good indicator of the onset of setting and agrees with the rise of the shear modulus (Ref. 10). In the simulator, needles that cross as line segments (or whose half-thicknesses touch) are regarded as belonging to the same cluster, and the time at which a cluster connecting the left and right edges of the window forms is defined as “setting.” It is known that line segments of length ℓ scattered in two dimensions become connected over an infinite extent when the number density exceeds Nℓ²/A ≈ 5.64 (Ref. 23). From this we see that, for the same amount of dihydrate, the longer and thinner the needles, the earlier they connect. When w/p is increased, the amount of dihydrate in the window decreases, so more reaction is needed before the needles connect, and setting is delayed. Because the needles form around the grains (§4.1), how they connect also depends on the arrangement of the grains. In the simulator the grains are placed with the same random sequence regardless of w/p (they are placed for the amount of powder at w/p = 0.40, and only the radii are shrunk to match w/p), so that only the spacing between grains changes when w/p is changed. When nuclei are placed near the grains, the setting time depends on the arrangement of the grains and nuclei (the random seed). Over 80 different seeds, the setting at w/p = 0.60 and 20 °C scatters over 12.4–22.4 min (median 15.6 min; 80 % of the runs lie within 13.2–19.4 min). The property that setting becomes later as w/p increases from 0.40 to 1.00 (in steps of 0.1) is preserved in 73 of the 80 seeds, and even for the seeds in which it breaks down, the reversal is 1.2 min or less. The default seed is a sequence chosen to be close to this median, and the setting at w/p = 0.60 is 15.4 min (this is not a value matched to the 15.6 min of the previous version, but a value obtained by choosing a seed near the median). The progress of the degree of reaction does not depend on the positions of the nuclei, but because the arrangement of the grains (the size distribution and the perimeters) was changed to a sequence independent of w/p, the time to reach α = 0.9 shifted by at most 1.5 min from the previous version (Table A1).

6. Composition of Portland Cement

Portland cement is made by adding a few percent of gypsum to clinker, which is limestone, clay, and the like fired at about 1450 °C, and grinding the mixture. In cement chemistry notation, C = CaO, S = SiO₂, A = Al₂O₃, F = Fe₂O₃, S̄ = SO₃, and H = H₂O, and the four main minerals are C₃S (alite), C₂S (belite), C₃A (aluminate), and C₄AF (ferrite). Paraphrasing the key points of lecture 01, slide 14: alite (C₃S) develops strength from an early age over a long period, belite (C₂S) reacts slowly and contributes to the strength after many years, and aluminate (C₃A) is a mineral with a large heat of hydration (the role of ferrite (C₄AF) is not given on the slide). Low-heat cement has more C₂S and less C₃A in order to use this property to suppress heat release. The simulator’s default values are the mineral compositions of lecture 01, slide 14 (Table 3). Comparing with the source data of Prof. Mochizuki’s bachelor’s thesis (Ref. 26), the values for moderate-heat and low-heat cement correspond to values calculated with Taylor’s equation (without the SO₃ correction), and those for ordinary cement correspond to values from Bogue’s equation (including the SO₃ correction, the same equation as in the table of the bachelor’s thesis). Aligning with Bogue’s equation gives 47.8/30.5/2.3/13.0 for moderate-heat cement and 34.8/48.0/1.5/9.2 for low-heat cement (C₃S/C₂S/C₃A/C₄AF, %). The simulator uses the values on the slide as they are.

8. Heat-Release Curve and Stages of Hydration

The heat-release rate of cement measured with an isothermal calorimeter can be divided into the following stages. A sharp peak just after mixing (surface dissolution and the formation of AFt; it reaches its maximum within a few minutes and becomes small in about 1 hour) → the induction period with little heat release (a few hours; the paste is still fluid) → the acceleration period, in which C–S–H and CH precipitate through the hydration of C₃S → the main peak → the deceleration period, in which the layer of products becomes thicker and the reaction slows down. Right after the main peak, a further peak (a shoulder) overlaps, in which the gypsum (sulfate) is used up and the reaction of C₃A speeds up again. Cement manufacturers adjust the amount of gypsum so that the time at which this sulfate runs out comes after the main peak of C₃S (Ref. 12). For the CEM I 42.5R of Jagličić et al. (2025) (w/c = 0.5, 20 °C), the end of the induction period was tis = 3.7 h, the maximum of the acceleration was at tm = 7.0 h (1.3 mW/g, slope 0.27 mW/(g·h)), and the main peak was at tmax = 12.6 h with 2.2 mW/g; the cumulative heat release was 41.4 J/g at 6.2 h, 45.1 J/g at 7.0 h, and 81.8 J/g at 12.6 h (Table 1 of Ref. 12; the sample was placed in the calorimeter 5 min after mixing for the measurement).

8.1 The simulator’s rate equations (for teaching)

The degree of hydration αi of C₃S, C₂S, and C₄AF is expressed as the sum of an initial fast reaction, nucleation and growth (Avrami form), and a slow diffusion stage (three-parameter exponential form).

(7) αi(θ)= a0(1−e−θ/τ0) +A[1−e−(k(θ−ti))n] +Be−(τD/(θ−ti))βD

The second and third terms are added only for θ > ti. For C₃A, the reaction is held back by the AFt layer formed on the surface as long as gypsum remains, and it speeds up again once the gypsum is used up (the “shoulder” in §8). To keep this order (the gypsum runs out → C₃A speeds up) inside the model as well, only C₃A was given the following form.

(7a) αC₃A(θ)= a0(1−e−θ/τ0) +Bs(1−e−θ/τs) +Bxe−τx/min(θ,θsd) +A[1−e−(k(θ−θsd))n] +Be−(τD/(θ−θsd))βD
(7b) a0(1−e−θsd/τ0) +Bs(1−e−θsd/τs) +Bxe−τx/θsd =ngypsum3nC₃A

The fourth and fifth terms are added only for θ > θsd. The third term is a slow term in which C₃A reacts slowly through the AFt layer even while gypsum remains; we set Bx = 1 − a0 − Bs = 0.494 and τx = 300 h (provisional), and after the gypsum is used up it is added with the value at that moment held fixed. Its magnitude at θ = 15 h is 10⁻⁸ or less, so it has almost no effect on the results for ordinary cement and CEM I, in which the gypsum runs out at around 15 h. θsd is the clock of the reaction that uses up the gypsum, and it is calculated for each composition from Eq. (7b) as the time at which the gypsum used for the formation of AFt (C₃A + 3CS̄H₂ → AFt, Table 4) reaches the whole amount (n is in mol per 1 g of cement). If the right-hand side is at least a0 + Bs + Bx = 1, the gypsum does not run out and θsd = ∞. In this case too, C₃A reacts slowly through the third term while forming AFt, and approaches 1 with time (α of C₃A in moderate-heat and low-heat cement is 0.58 at 7 days and 0.81 at 28 days). For ordinary cement (right-hand side 0.313) the gypsum runs out at 14.8 h, and for CEM I 42.5R (0.314) at 15.0 h, both after the main peak (12.6 h); for moderate-heat (1.02) and low-heat (1.57) cement there is little C₃A, so gypsum remains even at 28 days (the amounts used by 28 days are 79 % and 52 %). This equation expresses the role of gypsum as a “setting regulator”: the less gypsum there is relative to the amount of C₃A, the earlier it runs out. In reality the sulfate is also used for the hydration of C₄AF and for adsorption on C–S–H, but the model does not count this (§6).

The coefficients are set so that a0 + A + B = 1 for C₃S, C₂S, and C₄AF and a0 + Bs + A + B = 1 for C₃A (while gypsum remains, a0 + Bs + Bx = 1), so that, as long as the water does not run out, every phase approaches αi → 1 with time (even while gypsum remains, C₃A heads toward 1 through the third term; what stops it is the water constraint of §8.2). The Avrami equation (Ref. 20) describes nucleation and growth, and the three-parameter exponential form exp[−(τ/t)β] is an empirical formula often used for the degree of hydration of concrete (Ref. 21; the original source has not been checked). The idea of stages, “slowing down by diffusion after nucleation and growth,” follows the model of Parrott and Killoh (1984) (original source not checked). The coefficients (Table A3) were fitted to the CEM I 42.5R values of Jagličić et al. The heat release is Q = ΣfiHiαi (fi is the mass fraction and Hi is the phase-specific value in Table 4), and the overall degree of hydration is α = Σfiαi/Σfi. For simplicity, the reaction of C₃A after the gypsum has run out (the formation of AFm) is also counted with the same 1672 J/g as for AFt.

8.2 Temperature and the water constraint

θ is the “clock” of the reaction, and, including the effects of both temperature and water, it is advanced as

(8) dθdt= exp⁡[−ER(1T−1293.15 K)] ·1−e−wcap/WS1−e−(w/c)/WS

where E = 40 kJ/mol is the apparent activation energy recommended by CEMHYD3D (citing ASTM C1074, Ref. 4). While there is enough water (when the second factor is about 1, for example right after mixing or under water curing), this is the same as the age converted to 20 °C (equivalent age), and the rate is 0.41 times as fast at 5 °C and 2.2 times as fast at 35 °C. wcap is the capillary water available for the reaction (per 1 g of cement); it is w/c − 0.42α for sealed curing and w/c − 0.36α for underwater curing (§9), and for dry curing the evaporated water Eev is further subtracted. The curing (sealed, underwater, dry) and the temperature are decided before mixing and are not changed midway (if the sample were moved underwater midway, a separate calculation to replenish the evaporated water would be needed). For dry curing, dEev/dt = 0.06 h⁻¹ × wcap (provisional value) is used, so the capillary water leaves in about 1 day and hydration stops. The second factor φ is a “multiplier for the progress of the reaction due to water,” normalized to be 1 immediately after mixing (wcap = w/c). We set WS = 0.12 for sealed and dry curing. This is a schematic of the fact that when the capillary water decreases, the internal humidity drops (self-desiccation) and the reaction slows down even before the water runs out. For underwater curing, water keeps entering from outside, so we set WS = 0.02 so that the reaction does not slow down until just before the voids are filled with hydrates (both are provisional values). As a result, α (28 days) of ordinary cement at 20 °C is 0.62 for sealed curing at w/c = 0.30 (φ falls to 0.31), 0.71 for underwater curing, and 0.69 for sealed curing at w/c = 0.50. This expresses the tendency that the lower the w/c, the more the curing water matters (the amounts are provisional).

8.4 Fluidity — amount of water and water-reducing agent (apparent viscosity)

How easily the paste flows before it sets changes greatly with the amount of water. This is the flip side of lecture 01, slide 15, “reducing the mixing water reduces the voids in the hardened body and makes it stronger”: when water is reduced, the paste flows less easily and can no longer be packed into a mold. A water-reducing agent is therefore used to reduce the water while keeping the paste flowing as easily. In Prof. Mochizuki’s bachelor’s thesis (Ref. 26) and the measurements made in preparation for it, flow curves (shear rate versus shear stress) were measured with a stress-controlled rotational viscometer (double cylinder), changing the shear stress 0.54 → 200 → 0.54 Pa over 240 s (20 °C). The apparent viscosity is the slope (stress ÷ shear rate) of the straight line joining the origin and the 200 Pa point of the flow curve, that is, the viscosity when the paste is regarded as a Newtonian fluid. It allows even pastes with a yield value to be compared with a single number.

9. Powers’ Model — Voids and the Gel/Space Ratio

Powers and Brownyard divided the volume of cement paste into “unhydrated cement,” “hydration products (gel: solid and gel water),” and “capillary pores” (Refs. 14, 15). Taking the specific volume of cement as 0.32 cm³/g, the non-evaporable water wn = 0.23α (specific volume 0.72 cm³/g), and the gel water wg = 0.19α (specific volume 1 cm³/g), the hydration of 1 g of cement produces gel with a volume of

(9) Vgel=0.32α+0.72×0.23α+0.19α≈0.68α

The volume fractions relative to the total volume of the paste, w/c + 0.32, are

(10) φunhyd=0.32(1−α)w/c+0.32, φgel=0.68αw/c+0.32, φcap=w/c−0.36αw/c+0.32

The sum of the three is 1. The “unhydrated cement” in the readout of tab ② is calculated from the degree of hydration of each phase and the consumption of gypsum (the part used for AFt), so it differs slightly from the first of Eqs. (10) (for ordinary cement at w/c = 0.5: 0.356 at 1 h (0.371 by the equation), 0.345 at 6 h (0.365), and 0.110 at 28 days (0.120); the difference is at most 2 percentage points). The “hydration products” are the remainder (1 − unhydrated − capillary pores), so in the early stage they come out larger than in Eq. (10) (0.056 at 1 h versus 0.041 by the equation). X and the model strength are calculated with Eq. (11) (X at 28 days is 0.653; from the ratio of products to capillary pores it would be 0.657). In sealed curing, w/c − 0.42α of the capillary pores is filled with water, and the remaining 0.06α is empty voids created by chemical shrinkage (0.23α × (1 − 0.72) ≈ 0.064α). Hydration stops when the capillary water is gone, so complete hydration is possible in sealed curing only for w/c ≥ 0.42, and for underwater curing only for 0.36 or more (upper limit αmax ≈ (w/c)/0.42). Brouwers (2004) has re-evaluated this as about 0.39 for present-day CEM I (Ref. 14). In reality, in sealed curing the internal humidity drops and the reaction slows down before the water runs out (self-desiccation), so α at 28 days falls below this upper limit (§8.2).

10. Setting — Connecting the Solids

Bentz (2006/2007) calculated the connectivity (percolation) of the solids with CEMHYD3D and compared it with the setting measured by the Vicat needle. He reports that at the time of setting only 4–8 % of the cement has hydrated, but the skeleton that joins the particles has already formed (Ref. 13). The wider the spacing between particles (the larger w/c), the more products are needed before they connect, so setting is delayed. Jagličić et al. (2025) also state that the initial set by the Vicat needle agrees well with the time tm of maximum acceleration and with the ASTM onset time tASTM (for CEM I 42.5R, tm = 7.0 h and tASTM = 6.2 h; Ref. 12).

Simulator tab ② regards the particles as connected when the C–S–H “fuzz” extending out from a particle reaches the neighboring particle, and defines the time at which the left and right edges of the window are connected as setting. In a two-dimensional figure the particles look farther apart than in three dimensions, so the length of the fuzz is set to κ = 33 times “the thickness when C–S–H is spread uniformly over the surfaces of all particles.” The amount of C–S–H is the volume obtained from the amounts of C₃S and C₂S that have reacted, using a molar volume of 108 cm³/mol (C₁.₇SH₄, Table 4). However, the C–S–H formed in the reaction just after mixing (the a0 term of Eq. (7)) is a thin layer on the particle surface and is not counted as reaching the neighboring particle. Only C–S–H is counted toward the amount of fuzz; the hydrates formed from C₃A (AFt and AFm) and the hydrates of C₄AF are assumed not to form part of the skeleton. Because C–S–H precipitates from the liquid and attaches to the surface of every particle, the fuzz is attached with the same length to all particles other than gypsum (including C₃A), and it is drawn in the inside figure with the same length on the same particles as in the setting criterion. κ is a provisional value chosen so that the setting of CEM I 42.5R comes close to tm = 7.0 h; the setting in the model occurs at 7.5 h (α = 7.5 %). The particles are placed with enough spacing that they do not overlap even when they are enlarged to match w/c = 0.30 (if the particles were touching from the start, they would become connected right after mixing when w/c is small). For ordinary cement at 20 °C, setting occurs at 6.3 h, 7.3 h, and 8.0 h for w/c = 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7, and the degrees of hydration at those times are 6.7 %, 7.7 %, and 8.4 %, which roughly agree with Bentz’s “4–8 %” (α includes about 3 % for the reaction of C₃A and C₄AF just after mixing; Appendix A.5). The setting time also changes with the arrangement of the particles (the random-number seed). With 20 different seeds, setting is spread over 6.1–7.0 h, 7.0–7.8 h, and 7.6–8.4 h for ordinary cement at w/c = 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7, and over 5.8–9.3 h for all four types together (standard deviation 0.14–0.41 h). The differences among the types at w/c = 0.5 (ordinary 7.3 h, moderate-heat 7.4 h, low-heat 7.8 h, CEM I 7.5 h) are about the same as this spread, so the model cannot distinguish differences in setting among the types of cement. Compared with the initial set of 2 h 10–15 min of a product example (Ref. 16; standard consistency, w/c ≈ 0.27), the model’s 6.3 h at w/c = 0.3 is about 2.9 times as late, so the setting time should be taken only as a guide (provisional).

12. The Simulator’s Model and Its Simplifications

  • A two-dimensional representative window. The window of the inside figure (480 × 300 px, roughly 480 × 300 µm as a guide) is regarded as representative of 1 cm³ of paste, and the fraction of the area in the figure is treated as a volume fraction. Each needle or particle stands for many crystals or particles that were born at about the same time.
  • Constant temperature. The reaction is assumed to take place in a constant-temperature bath, and the temperature rise due to the heat of reaction is not included in the calculation.
  • The rate coefficients for gypsum are provisional. The rate of dissolution, nucleation (J0, B), growth (gL, gW, ℓc), and the temperature coefficient E are values chosen for teaching to match times in the literature (Table A2). The difference between the α and β forms (the α form needs less water and gives higher strength, but its dissolution and setting are reported to be slower than those of the β form; Abu Zeitoun et al., ZKG International 10/2020) is not treated.
  • The rate equations for cement are of an empirical form. Eq. (7) was not derived from a mechanism; it is an equation for reproducing the shape of the heat-release curve. The coefficients are provisional, and the rate of each phase is assumed to be the same even when the composition changes (the difference in composition appears in the mass fractions and in the time at which the gypsum is used up, which is determined by the ratio of the amounts of C₃A and gypsum (Eq. (7b))). Particle size, specific surface area, and admixtures are not treated (for the effect of the water-reducing agent, one can only view the measured heat release in ② overlaid on the model and the measured viscosity in ③ (§8.3, §8.4)). The amount of gypsum is fixed at 4.9 % for ordinary cement and 4.5 % for the others (provisional).
  • The shapes and arrangement of the products are schematic. The total area of the products in the inside figure is matched to Powers’ volume fraction of gel, and its breakdown (C–S–H, CH, AFt, AFm, and the hydrates of C₄AF) is determined from the molar volumes in CEMHYD3D. The volume ratio of 2 for the hydrates of C₄AF is provisional. In the appearance that matches the lecture figures, the products other than AFt are grouped into an ocher layer, and the extent of the layer is adjusted so that the area of light blue + white in the painted figure matches the capillary porosity (water + empty) (Appendix A.3).
  • The color showing the concentration of the gypsum liquid is schematic. In the calculation the concentration of the liquid is uniform (a single value). Of the dense liquid around a grain in the inside figure, only the two ends, the concentration at the surface (the solubility of the hemihydrate) and the concentration of the liquid as a whole, are quantities of the model; the distribution between them (width δ) and the flow of ions are schematic.
  • The setting criterion is provisional. It is the time at which the needle network (gypsum) or the connected fuzz (cement) joins the left and right edges of the window. Because the window is finite, the value changes with the arrangement of the particles and needles (the random-number seed is fixed). For cement, changing the seed in 20 ways gives 6.1–7.0 h for ordinary cement at w/c = 0.3 and 5.8–9.3 h over the four types and w/c = 0.3–0.7 together, and the differences among the types of cement are about the same as this spread (§10).
  • The water constraint is schematic. The form of φ in Eq. (8) and WS (0.12 for sealed and dry curing, 0.02 for underwater curing) are provisional values for expressing the difference between self-desiccation and water supplied from outside; the internal humidity is not actually calculated.
  • Boundaries between stages. The boundaries of the “initial, induction, acceleration, and deceleration periods” in ② were obtained by fitting 1.2 h, 3.7 h (tis), and 12.6 h (main peak) of the CEM I heat-release curve with the reaction clock θ converted to 20 °C, and they are common to all compositions.
  • Strength. It is not displayed for gypsum. For cement, Powers’ empirical equation 234X³ is displayed as the “model strength.” Phenomena such as the decrease in long-term strength when curing at high temperature are not treated.
  • Needle penetration. The penetration of the needle in the apparatus figure is a schematic for seeing the state of the network and connection by eye; it is not a measured value of the Vicat test. At the moment setting is judged to occur, it is set to 39 mm, near the bottom of the mold, which is 40 mm deep, and afterward it becomes shallower with the time elapsed since setting. The initial set of the standard (DIN EN 196-3) is when the needle tip stops at a position a few mm above the base plate (6 ± 3 mm as far as I recall; the text of the standard has not been checked), which corresponds to a penetration of about 34 mm, so 39 mm is a schematic value slightly closer to the bottom than that. The time to reach 0.5 mm is a schematic matched to about 1.1 h in terms of the reaction clock θ for cement (the difference between the initial set of 2 h 10–15 min and the final set of 3 h 20 min of the product example; Ref. 16) and to about 5.6 min at 20 °C for gypsum (the difference between the initial set of 10.4 min and the final set of 16.0 min of Darmawan et al.; Ref. 11), and it becomes slower the lower the temperature. Because the setting time itself is only a guide (§10), the time at which the penetration reaches 0.5 mm is also only a guide to the time of final set.
  • Conditions are fixed before mixing. In both ① and ②, the type of powder, the water ratio, the temperature, and the curing can be changed only before “① Add powder.” If they were changed midway, the explanation of the results up to that point (how to compare the effects of temperature and curing) would no longer hold. To compare, press “Keep result,” then Reset and run again.
