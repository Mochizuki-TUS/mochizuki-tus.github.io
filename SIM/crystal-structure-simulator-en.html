Crystal Structures — From Sphere Packing to Pauling’s Rules

Metals and ionic crystals alike come down, in the end, to a question of how spheres are packed. First we examine the 7 crystal systems and the 14 Bravais lattices while rotating them, then work out the packing fractions of sc, bcc, fcc, and hcp in three steps: “① find the contact direction, ② write r in terms of a, ③ substitute.” Next we focus on the 25.95 % of empty space that remains even in the closest packing — the tetrahedral and octahedral holes — and derive from geometry that the upper limit for a sphere placed there is a radius ratio of 0.225 / 0.414. In ④ we recast 10 representative crystal structures in the sentence pattern “parent lattice + which holes + how many,” and in ⑤ we build the perovskite tolerance factor t from the periodic table and check why BaTiO3 distorts. ⑥ is a check calculation of Pauling’s electrostatic valence rule.
What we cover in class (Lectures 2 and 3) is ★ ①②③④; ★★ ⑤⑥ are a bridge from the second half of Lecture 3 to Lecture 14 (ferroelectrics).

All figures are computed and drawn on the spot. The lattice vectors are built in general form, even for triclinic cells, from a, b, c, α, β, γ, and drawn in orthographic projection (atoms are shown as hard spheres). The packing fractions, coordination numbers, critical radius ratios, tolerance factors, and electrostatic bond strengths are all computed from the values on the screen; they are not constants embedded in a table.

Ionic radii are the widely used values from Shannon’s tables (note that the value differs with the coordination number). The structure models use textbook-level ideal coordinates; in real materials the lattice constants and atomic coordinates shift with temperature and composition. The spinel unit cell contains 56 atoms, so only two kinds of coordination polyhedra are extracted and shown.
