YBCO Synthesis and Superconductor Levitation — Solid-State Reaction, Oxygen Content, and Flux Pinning Explained

Back to: Mochizuki Research Group Education — Inorganic Materials Science > Virtual Experiments, Inorganic Materials Science resources

This page is the explanation that pairs with ybco-simulator-en.html. Concerning the solid-state synthesis of YBa2Cu3O7−δ (hereafter YBCO) carried out in basic experiments A1–A3 of the Materials Science Experiments (Inorganic) course, and the observation of magnetic levitation of the sintered body, this page summarizes the reaction equation and mass balance, what happens inside the material during calcination, sintering and oxygenation, the relation between the oxygen content δ and the superconducting transition temperature Tc, and “why it levitates and why its position is stable,” together with the equations used in the simulator and their sources. For the principles of superconductivity itself (GL theory and BCS theory), see superconductivity-simulator-en.html; for Cooper pairs, see cooper-pair-simulator-en.html. How to read the screen of each tab of the simulator is explained in Appendix A.

The backbone of this page The YBCO experiment is easier to follow if you divide it into three stages: ① making the phase (solid-state reaction: calcination), ② making the shape (pressing and sintering: densification), and ③ putting in oxygen (oxygenation: making δ smaller). Whether the sample levitates is not decided only by whether it is “below Tc”; it changes with the superconducting volume, the size over which the shielding current circulates (a grain, or the whole sample), the strength of the flux pinning, and the cooling history.

2. Raw Materials and Weighing — Reaction Equation and Mass Balance

The overall reaction can be written in a form in which CO2 leaves the carbonate and oxygen is taken up at the end.

12Y2O3 +2BaCO3 +3CuO +(14−δ2)O2 →YBa2Cu3O7−δ +2CO2 (1)

The numbers of oxygen atoms balance: on the left, 1.5 + 6 + 3 + (1/2 − δ) = 11 − δ; on the right, (7 − δ) + 4 = 11 − δ. The molar masses are 225.809 for Y2O3, 197.335 for BaCO3, 79.545 for CuO, 666.191 − 15.999δ for YBa2Cu3O7−δ, and 44.009 g/mol for CO2 (calculated from the table of atomic weights). The raw materials for 1 mol of YBCO weigh 0.5 × 225.809 + 2 × 197.335 + 3 × 79.545 = 746.21 g, so, writing m0 for the total mass of the raw materials, the mass of each reagent is as follows.

The mass balance is an identity: the mass of the solid m equals the weighed mass minus the part that left as CO2, plus the net gain or loss of O2.

m=m0−mCO2+mO2 (2)

The simulator records this in a ledger at every step of the reaction and displays it as the “balance difference” in the readouts (even in a check run with node, the difference is 10−15 g or less). In pressing, each term of the ledger is scaled down by the same factor as the fraction of powder used, and in the polishing of ⑩ by the same factor as the fraction removed. When all of the BaCO3 has decomposed, the CO2 released is 2 × 44.009 / 746.21 = 11.80 % of the raw materials (by mass), and the gain or loss of oxygen (the change in δ) is added to this. Under the representative conditions, 0.3539 g of CO2 leaves after calcination and 0.0219 g of O2 enters, so 3.000 g becomes 2.668 g. Counting the theoretical yield by the method of lab manual §6.1, for the weighed values 0.454 / 1.587 / 0.959 g we have Y:Ba:Cu = 1:2.000:2.998, so Cu is very slightly the limiting reagent, and the yield is 2.645 g as YBa2Cu3O6.5 (the composition counted from the oxygen of the raw materials alone), or 2.677 g if taken as YBa2Cu3O7. The theoretical mass loss and theoretical yield are shown in the “mass balance” readout of ① (they change if a BaCO3 weighing error is entered).

3. Course of the Solid-State Reaction — Decarbonation, Green Phase, Main Reaction, Peritectic Decomposition

Simply mixing the powders does not make them react. On heating, atoms diffuse at the contact points between particles, and YBCO forms through the following stages (the reaction scheme of VEL thermal_ybco.js, carried over as it is).

  1. Decarbonation (from about 600 °C): BaCO3 + CuO → BaCuO2 + CO2. This CO2 is why the mass of the sample decreases.
  2. Green phase (from about 780 °C): Y2O3 + BaCuO2 → Y2BaCuO5 (Y211). Only when the charge is short of Ba (Ba/Y < 1.95) does Y2O3 + 2 CuO → Y2Cu2O5 (the blue phase) also form.
  3. Main reaction (diffusion-limited): Y211 + 3 BaCuO2 + 2 CuO → 2 YBa2Cu3O6.5. The layer of product separates the reactants, so diffusion through the layer sets the rate.
  4. Peritectic decomposition (above about 1015 °C in air): 2 YBCO → Y211 + liquid (3 BaCuO2 + 2 CuO) + O2. It occurs if the sintering temperature is raised too high.

The rates are first-order for decarbonation and the green phase, and follow the Ginstling–Brounshtein (GB) law for the main reaction. α is “the fraction of Y that has become YBCO.”

dξdt=kn, k=Aexp⁡(−EaRT) (3)
1−23α−(1−α)2/3 =2Dr2t, D=D0exp⁡(−EaRT) (4)

r is the particle radius, and t ∝ r2 (a powder of half the size reacts 4 times faster) is the reason mixing and grinding are done carefully (for the solid-state reaction and the Jander equation, see sintering-derivation-en.html §2). The mixing uniformity h (0–1) multiplies the rate by 0.25 + 0.75h2 and caps the attainable α at 0.62 + 0.40h (with poor mixing, Y211 and BaCuO2 remain).

4. Mixing and Grinding — Wet Mixing in Ethanol Is a Pretreatment

Mixing in a mortar is an operation that makes the particles finer (radius r) and brings the three kinds of particles closer together (mixing uniformity h); YBCO does not form the instant you mix. The model uses the regrind of VEL as it is (t is in minutes).

r=rfloor+(r0−rfloor)e−t/τR, h=1−(1−h0)e−t/τH (5)

For wet mixing (with ethanol), rfloor = 0.6 µm, τR = 16 min and τH = 8 min; for dry mixing, 0.7 µm, 22 min and 20 min (VEL estimates). Before mixing, the particle radius is taken to be 3.0 µm and the uniformity 0.30, as if the powders had only been combined on weighing paper (the values of the VEL preset), and the ethanol evaporates at 0.055 mL/min during mixing (VEL mortar_bench.js). The boundaries between the states (liquid → slurry → paste → dry powder) are a schematic judgment based on the ratio of the volume of ethanol to the volume of powder. Once the remaining ethanol falls below 0.05 mL, the mixing is treated as dry (for 1.0 mL, the last 2.7 min or so of the 20 min). The time of switching from wet to dry is computed, so the same sample results whether you advance one step at a time on the screen or compute everything at once with a preset or the “standard procedure” of ②③. Under the representative conditions (3 min dry → 20 min wet), the uniformity goes 0.30 → 0.40 → 0.94 and the particle radius 3.0 → 1.24 µm.

In a powder that has been calcined, the reacted and unreacted portions have turned into hard lumps, so compositional unevenness should be hard to remove by regrinding. Therefore, in regrinding a calcined powder, the time constant of the uniformity τH is lengthened by up to a factor of 3 (increased smoothly from 650 °C to 850 °C; a schematic of our own, with a provisional coefficient). If mixing is omitted (0 min dry, 0 min wet), the uniformity remains at about 0.80 even after 30 min of regrinding, and even after sintering YBCO is only about 94 % (the rest is Y2BaCuO5 and BaCuO2). Because of this, for the VEL “poor mixing” condition (5 min dry, then 20 min dry after calcination), this simulator gives 86.5 % compared with 94.1 % in VEL (a deliberate difference).

If calcination is skipped: the powder is pressed with BaCO3 still in it, so decarbonation (Eq. (1)) takes place inside the pellet while the temperature rises for sintering, and CO2 comes out of the pellet. The simulator records the fraction fCO2 of the theoretical amount of CO2 that has been released inside the pellet, and includes the following schematics (all of our own, with provisional coefficients; VEL does not have them).

  • Increase of pores (swelling): the outer volume is expanded by a factor of (1 − 0.10 fCO2)−1 according to the released fraction fCO2 (the mass is unchanged, so the relative density falls by up to 10 %, and the subsequent densification is delayed by the same amount). The mass lost as CO2 escapes lowers the relative density separately from this, without changing the outer shape. Because the part of the pressed powder that leaves as CO2 reduces the amount of solid, the dimensions after sintering are smaller than for the standard sample. The text on the screen also says “the pores and cracks increase, which hinders densification.”
  • Cracks: for fCO2 > 0.30 the pellet is regarded as having cracked and is treated as a weak link.
  • Carbonate at grain boundaries: multiplies the connectivity of the grain boundaries by 1 − 0.6 fCO2 and widens the transition by 12 fCO2 K. It is not included in the mass fractions of the phases.

A sample calcined for 0 h, even with 100 % YBCO and δ = 0.098, has a relative density of 79.0 % (standard 81.7 %) and a grain-boundary connectivity of 0.40, and its resistance does not reach zero even when cooled to 40 K. The dimensions just after firing are φ 8.66 × 1.80 mm (standard φ 8.96 × 1.84 mm), and the mass is 0.534 g (standard 0.601 g). Levitation is determined by the currents inside the grains, so it still levitates, but the levitation height with field cooling falls to 1.8 mm (standard 4.0 mm) (in the arrangement with the sample on top of the magnet, magnet φ 20 × 10 mm; in the default arrangement of the lab manual, a φ 6 × 3 mm magnet levitates 0.71 mm (standard 1.29 mm)). Insufficient calcination such as 800 °C for 2 h produces the same effect in proportion to the BaCO3 that remains (18 % in the preset “Under-calcination and under-sintering”).

6. Pressing and Sintering — Densification and Grain Growth

The relative density of the green compact is assumed to rise in proportion to the logarithm of the pressure P on the sample (greenDensity of VEL sinter.js; an estimate).

ρgreen=0.60+0.185log10⁡P98.1 MPa+0.030log10⁡r1.5 µm (6)

(Limited to 0.30–0.78. Multiplied by 0.997 for a 1-min hold.) The ram of the press in the lab manual is 2.86 cm in diameter, so the hydraulic gauge reading when 98.1 MPa is applied to the sample in the die (φ 1.000 cm) is 98.1 × (1.000/2.86)2 = 11.99 ≈ 12.0 MPa (shown in the figure for step ⑦ in ①; the gauge reading is the oil pressure, not the pressure on the sample). Sintering uses the same equations as the Coble intermediate-stage model borrowed in sintering-simulator-en.html (sintering-derivation-en.html §13), and the YBCO coefficients are the VEL estimates A = 1.03×1016, Qd = 350 kJ/mol, B = 5.73×1012, Qg = 260 kJ/mol, a pore breakaway grain size of 3 µm, and ρmax = 0.96.

dρdt=Ae−Qd/RTTG4(ρmax−ρ)φ, dGdt=Be−Qg/RTTG2ψ (7)

The relative density is referenced to the theoretical density of YBCO (a first-order approximation in δ for the lattice constants, ā = 3.8571 − 0.01092(x − 6.5) Å, c = 11.7422 − 0.15325(x − 6.5) Å, x = 7 − δ, giving 6.39 g/cm3 at δ = 0 and 6.12 g/cm3 at δ = 1). The outer shape is always adjusted so that the relative density equals “mass ÷ (outer volume × theoretical density at the current δ),” so the dimensions, mass, theoretical density and relative density in the readouts are all consistent with one another. When CO2 escapes, the relative density is lowered without changing the outer shape; for the gain or loss of oxygen, the relative density (the volume fraction of solid) is kept and the outer shape is stretched or shrunk to follow the change in the volume of solid (mass ÷ theoretical density). VEL changes the relative density in proportion to the mass even for the gain or loss of oxygen, so oxygenation makes the sample appear to become denser. This is not done here, so with the same regrinding as VEL (dry, 20 min) the standard relative density comes out about 1 % lower than in VEL (a deliberate difference). With the default regrinding (wet, 30 min) the powder is finer and densification goes further, giving 81.7 %, about 5 % higher than in VEL (Appendix A.5). Difference in the reference for relative density: the lab manual uses as the denominator a constant true density derived from the lattice constants at δ = 0.49 and the composition O6.5. The denominator in this simulator is the theoretical density at the current δ, so for a sintered body with δ ≈ 0.1 the denominator differs by about 2 %, and the relative density differs from that of the manual’s method by the same amount (the manual’s true density is 6.2517 g/cm3, obtained by dividing the formula weight of YBa2Cu3O6.5, 658.19 g/mol, by the volume of the lattice at δ = 0.49 in Table 6-1 of the manual, 174.8262 Å3, and by NA. For the polished sintered body of the representative conditions (bulk density 5.20 g/cm3), the relative density is 81.7 % in the model and 83.1 % by the manual’s method. Both are shown in the readouts of ①). The sintered body of the representative conditions has a relative density of 81.7 %, a grain radius of 2.1 µm, and, just after firing, dimensions of φ 9.0 mm × 1.8 mm and a mass of 0.60 g. In the polishing of ⑩, 0.1 mm in total (a provisional value) is removed from top and bottom, and only the thickness and mass are reduced without changing the relative density (after polishing, φ 9.0 mm × 1.7 mm and 0.57 g; the bulk density W/(πD2h/4) is the same 5.20 g/cm3 before and after polishing). The “YBCO sintered body” in ②③ is the sample after polishing. The “grain-boundary connectivity” (0–1) in the readouts has a value only for a sintered body that has been heated to 800 °C or above after pressing; the YBCO fraction and the carbonate factor of the grain boundaries are multiplied by a smooth step, 3u2 − 2u3 (u = (ρ − 0.6)/0.2), that is 0 at a relative density of 60 % and 1 at 80 % (schematic). Powder and green compacts have no necks, so 0 is displayed. It is 1.00 for the standard sintered body, 0 for sintering at 850 °C for 0 h (relative density 60 %), and 0.57 for the preset “Under-calcination and under-sintering” (71.9 %). This value affects the grain-boundary step of the resistance (§10) and does not enter the levitation force.

7. Oxygen Content δ — Structure, Tc, and Speed of Oxygenation

7.1 Equilibrium oxygen content

The oxygen in the Cu–O chains of YBCO goes in and out depending on temperature and oxygen partial pressure. At high temperature it leaves and δ becomes large (tetragonal); at low temperature it takes up oxygen and δ becomes small (orthorhombic). The equilibrium oxygen content is expressed by a sigmoid (VEL’s estimate; its shape was matched to the curves of Specht et al. and is not a fit to a table).

xeq=7−δeq=6.06+0.921+exp⁡[(T−T1/2)/105], T1/2=700+65log10⁡pO2 (8)

(T is in °C and pO2 in atm.) The equilibrium δ in air is 0.094 at 400 °C, 0.133 at 450 °C, 0.360 at 600 °C, 0.575 at 700 °C and 0.871 at 920 °C, and 0.098 at 450 °C in 1 atm O2 (Fig. 1). In the literature, there is an order–disorder transition from tetragonal to orthorhombic at about 700 °C in pure oxygen (Jorgensen et al. 1987, Ref. 8), and oxygenation at 450 °C in flowing O2 gives Tc = 91 K and an oxygen content of 6.94 (Heber et al. 1987, Ref. 7).

Comparison with the figure in the lab manual: the lab manual has a figure of isotherms of δ versus oxygen partial pressure (quoted from Ref. 21). Compared with its air line, at 500–900 °C the equilibrium δ of the model is larger by 0.05–0.15, so the model leans toward the side where oxygen leaves more easily. The readings of the air line in the figure (by eye, about ±0.02) are 0.12 at 500 °C, 0.26 at 600 °C, 0.46 at 700 °C and 0.60 at 800 °C, while Eq. (8) of the model gives 0.190, 0.360, 0.575 and 0.754, respectively (at 400 °C the figure gives 0.085 and the model 0.094, nearly in agreement). For this reason, the δ at which the exchange of oxygen stops (freezes) during cooling may in reality be smaller than in the model (“furnace cooling without a hold” in §7.2).

The high-temperature crystal system is schematic: the model fixes the boundary of the orthorhombic phase at the room-temperature value x = 6.40 at all temperatures. As a result, on the equilibrium curve of Eq. (8), the switch between tetragonal and orthorhombic occurs at about 712 °C in air and about 756 °C in 1 atm O2, which is higher than in the literature (about 700 °C in pure O2, and lower in air). This is because at high temperature the boundary moves to around x ≈ 6.5. Also, at δ = 0.60–0.65 (x = 6.35–6.40), Tc of 0–10 K remains even though the structure is tetragonal (a mismatch between the Tc vanishing point of VEL, 6.35, and the lattice boundary, 6.40). Neither affects the judgment of levitation at 77 K.

7.2 Speed of oxygen uptake — diffusion distance set by the microstructure

δ approaches its equilibrium value as a first-order process, and the time constant is set by the slowest mode of the diffusion equation.

τ=Leff2π2D, D=D0exp⁡(−EaRT) (9)

Ea = 93.6 kJ/mol (0.97 eV) is the value of oxygen tracer diffusion by Rothman et al. (Ref. 9). The pre-exponential factor D0 = 6.0×10−6 cm2/s is a calibration value of VEL (about 1/23 of the value in the ab plane of a single crystal; adjusted so that the relaxation time of porous ceramics at 450 °C is 1–2 h), and must not be cited as a measured value. The diffusion distance Leff is taken to be the grain radius for open pores (relative density ≲ 0.90) and half the thickness of the pellet for closed pores (≳ 0.96), with a smooth logarithmic interpolation between them (a modeling assumption). For a grain radius of 2 µm, τ = 3.4 h at 400 °C and 1.1 h at 450 °C. That is why a hold of “400 °C, 10 h” is effective, and why, if the hold is omitted and the sample is furnace-cooled, oxygen exchange freezes near 500 °C and δ ≈ 0.19 (the equilibrium δ in the lab manual figure is smaller than the model in this temperature range, so the actual frozen δ can be smaller than this; §7.1). This δ ≈ 0.19 lies in the range where Tc changes steeply with δ (§7.3), and doubling D0 gives a δ of 0.151 (a Tc of 89.8 K), so a sample that does not levitate in ② is replaced by one that does. “It does not levitate without the 400 °C hold” is a result under the calibration value of this model (Table A2). If overfiring makes the grains grow to 5.8 µm, δ remains at about 0.30 even with the same hold. Effective diffusion coefficients in the literature scatter over orders of magnitude, and a value of 1.7×10−7 cm2/s (400 °C) has even been reported for a 94 % dense polycrystal (Ref. 10). It differs greatly from the value in the model (3.3×10−13 cm2/s at 400 °C), so the absolute value of τ is only a rough guide. The oxygen uniformity u = 1 − 0.35 log10(Leff/3 µm) (0.25–1) affects the superconducting volume fraction and the width of the transition (VEL; a modeling assumption).

7.3 Tc and δ

Tc(δ) is the piecewise-linear function of VEL (an estimate, ±3 K), with a 90 K plateau (x ≥ 6.85), a 60 K plateau (6.55–6.75), and 0 for x ≤ 6.35 (δ ≥ 0.65). Literature values differ greatly even at the same δ, depending on how the sample was made, quenching, and aging at room temperature (Table 5, Fig. 2). Superconductivity disappears at δ ≈ 0.65, near the tetragonal–orthorhombic transition (Jorgensen et al. 1990, Ref. 11; Liang et al. 2006, Ref. 12). The lab manual states that “superconductivity appears for δ < 0.6, and Tc is 90 K or higher for δ < 0.15.” The 90 K boundary agrees with the model (90 K at δ = 0.15), and the δ at which superconductivity disappears differs by 0.05 between the manual (0.6) and the model (0.65) (within the scatter of the vanishing points in the literature).

8. Superconductivity and Levitation — Meissner Effect and Flux Pinning

8.1 Meissner state and mixed state

A superconductor expels a weak magnetic field from its interior by shielding currents at the surface (the Meissner effect). YBCO is a type II superconductor, and when the field exceeds the lower critical field Hc1 (a few mT to a dozen or so mT at 77 K), it enters the mixed state, in which magnetic flux penetrates as quantized flux (vortices) of Φ0 = h/2e = 2.07×10−15 Wb each (for the distinction between type I and type II, see superconductivity-simulator-en.html ①). The surface of the NdFeB magnet in the levitation experiment is at 0.1–0.5 T, so the inside of the sintered body is in the mixed state. Most of the flux has entered the sample, and only a part is expelled.

The flux that has entered gets caught on second phases (such as Y2BaCuO5), defects and grain boundaries and cannot move (flux pinning). Because the flux cannot move, a shielding current flows when you try to change the magnetic field, and its upper limit is the critical current density Jc. With perfect diamagnetism alone, the lateral position above a flat superconductor is unstable, and the penetration of flux and pinning make the position stable (Hellman et al. 1988, Ref. 13). It is not that “it floats because the resistance is zero”; the magnitude of the shielding current and the force that holds the flux in place determine how it floats.

8.2 Zero-field cooling (ZFC) and field cooling (FC)

  • ZFC: cool away from the magnet and then bring it close. As it approaches, the field increases, and the shielding current opposes that increase, so there is repulsion.
  • FC: cool with the magnet and sample stacked. The flux present at the moment of cooling is frozen in as it is. When you try to move the sample from there, the change in the field is opposed, so pushing gives repulsion and pulling apart gives attraction, and it is returned to its original position both vertically and laterally. A material with strong pinning (a melt-grown bulk) does not rise when cooled, and when you lift the upper one, the lower one hangs from it. A sintered body of fine grains with weak pinning has part of its flux pushed out during cooling, and rises by that amount (Kordyuk et al. 1999, Ref. 14).
  • Pressing down (“forcing the magnet down” in lab manual §6.4, and video A3): when the ZFC sample and magnet are pressed against each other to push flux in (in the manual, the magnet is forced onto the cooled sintered body), pinning resists the change in the returning direction, and when the magnet is moved the sample follows.
A note on terminology The background section of the lab manual does not call the diamagnetism seen in ZFC the Meissner effect, because it can also be derived from perfect conductivity, and instead calls the expulsion of flux when the sample goes below Tc in FC the Meissner effect. The distinction made in this page and the simulator (repulsion by shielding currents in ZFC; the Meissner effect is the part of the flux pushed out in FC) is the same as this. On the other hand, the procedure section of the manual and the captions of the lab video call the repulsion in ZFC the “Meissner effect” (an inconsistency in usage).

8.3 Magnetic field of the magnet

The magnet is a uniformly magnetized cylinder (remanent flux density Br, radius a, thickness b), and only the field on the central axis is used (z is the distance from the face of the magnet).

Bz(z)=Br2 [z+b(z+b)2+a2−zz2+a2] (10)

The default magnet is a neodymium magnet φ 6 mm × 3 mm matched to the arrangement of lab manual §6.4 (a magnet placed on the cooled sintered body), with Br = 1.25 T (provisional; the manual gives no material or dimensions; §12), a surface-center field of 0.44 T and a mass of 0.64 g (weight 6.2 mN). In this model only a small magnet levitates when placed on the sintered body (with field cooling, 1.29 mm for φ 6 × 3 mm and 0.05 mm for φ 8 × 3 mm), and the size can be changed with a slider. A magnet with a diameter larger than the sample (φ 8.95 mm for the standard sintered body), that is, φ 10 mm or more, does not levitate regardless of its thickness at the default Br = 1.25 T. The effective area Aeff in Eq. (12) is set by the smaller cross-section of the sample and the magnet, so the part sticking out beyond the sample does not contribute to the repulsion (and the larger the diameter, the gentler the field gradient on the axis), while only the weight of the magnet increases as the square of the diameter. Compared at a thickness of 3 mm, the repulsion on contact (ZFC) falls once the diameter exceeds the sample: 19.7 mN for φ 8, 20.0 mN for φ 9, 16.4 mN for φ 10, 11.2 mN for φ 12 and 3.2 mN for φ 20 (for φ 10 it falls below the weight of 17.3 mN). Although outside the range of the slider, φ 10 × 1 mm is lighter than the default magnet (weight 5.8 mN) but gives a repulsion of only 4.7 mN and does not levitate. Conversely, φ 8 × 5 mm is heavy (weight 18.5 mN), but its repulsion is 26.7 mN, so it levitates by 1.66 mm in ZFC. 8.4 Force model (a schematic for teaching, of our own)

The diamagnetic magnetization of the sample m (A/m, positive when opposite to the field) is expressed as the sum of a reversible part and an irreversible part due to pinning.

m=fsc(T) [Hp(T)HH2+Hp2η +Mcφ], Mc=Jc(T,B)ℓ3 (11)
  • First term (reversible): the Meissner state in weak fields (m ≈ H), saturating at Hp, a measure of the equilibrium magnetization, in strong fields. Hp(T) = Hp0(1 − (T/Tc)2). In FC, only the fraction of flux that could be expelled, η = pexp, contributes (η = 1 in ZFC).
  • Second term (irreversible): in the Bean critical-state model, the saturation magnetization of a cylinder of radius ℓ, Jcℓ/3. ℓ is the radius over which the shielding current circulates, and is the grain radius for a sintered body (the grain boundaries are weak links, so the current cannot cross from grain to grain) and the radius of the sample (a single domain) for a melt-grown bulk.
  • History φ: tanh(H/Mc) for approaching in ZFC, tanh((H − Hcool)/Mc) for FC, and φt − (φt + 1) tanh((Hpress − H)/((φt + 1)Mc)) after pressing down and returning (φt = tanh(Hpress/Mc)). All of them are the Bean-model property “the change of the field is shielded with slope 1 and saturates at ±Mc,” smoothed with tanh.
  • Jc(T, B) = Jc77 [(1 − T/Tc)/(1 − 77 K/Tc,ref)]1.5 × (1 − B/Birr)2, and Birr also changes with the same temperature factor. Tc,ref is the standard Tc of the material (91 K for YBCO); for a sample whose Tc has fallen because of oxygen deficiency, Jc at 77 K drops greatly.

The fsc of a sintered body is the superconducting volume fraction of the model in ① (mass fraction of YBCO × oxygen uniformity × transition sigmoid) multiplied by a porosity coefficient (ρrel/0.85)1.5 (capped at 1). It is a coefficient expressing that the superconducting volume decreases by the amount of the pores, and that mutual shielding between grains becomes weaker when contact between grains is small; the factor of meissnerTest in VEL properties.js was borrowed (a modeling assumption). Without it, an unsintered porous sample would repel more strongly than a dense one. For the sample sintered at 850 °C for 0 h (relative density 60 %) the coefficient is 0.59, and the ZFC force on contact is 8.4 mN (standard: 12.3 mN, when placed on a φ 20 × 10 mm magnet).

The force is obtained by integrating over the thickness of the sample the force with which the diamagnetic magnetization is pushed toward the weaker field (Aeff = π min(Rs, a)2).

F(z)=Aeff∫zz+tm(z′)|∂Bz∂z|dz′ ,F(zeq)=W (12)

The levitation height zeq is the point where F crosses the weight W from above to below, searching upward from contact (z = 0) (W is the weight of the sample in the arrangement with the sample on top of the magnet, and that of the magnet in the arrangement with the magnet on top of the sample; the density of NdFeB is 7.5 g/cm3). If F(0) < W, it does not levitate. Lateral stability is judged by the ratio S of the restoring force of pinning, kpin ∝ Mc χ (χ: 1 for FC and after pressing down, 0.25 for ZFC), to kdia ∝ 0.15 × the first term, the part of the reversible diamagnetism that pushes outward from the center (stable at 1.2 or more, slides off below 0.8). This lateral judgment is a schematic that expresses only the tendency “the stronger the pinning, the more stable,” and does not calculate the lateral force due to the shape of the magnet. The coefficients 0.15 and χ = 0.25 are provisional values, and whether a sintered body placed in ZFC slides off is determined by how these coefficients are chosen. In fact, in ZFC of a sintered body, S ≈ 0.5 regardless of the dimensions, Br and arrangement of the magnet (for magnets of φ 6–20 mm, and also for a ferrite magnet). S is also proportional to Mc ∝ Jcℓ, so it changes greatly with the intragrain Jc (provisional value 105 A/cm2) and the grain radius (about 2 µm). How a sintered body levitates also depends strongly on the magnitude of the reversible diamagnetism Hp0 (10 mT, provisional) and on the fraction of flux pushed out in FC, pexp (1, provisional). 8.5 Comparison with the ideal case (our own derivation)

When there is a vertical point magnetic dipole md (A·m2) at height z above an infinitely wide perfect diamagnet, the image that satisfies Bz = 0 at the boundary is an oppositely directed dipole at −z, and the repulsive force becomes as follows (method of images, our own).

FZFC=3μ0md232πz4, FFC=3μ0md22π[1(2z)4−1(z+z0)4] (13)

The FC expression was derived by ourselves from the description of Kordyuk’s frozen-image method (Ref. 17), which adds a “frozen image,” the mirror image at the cooling position z0 with its magnetization reversed, and it has not been checked against the equation in the original paper. At z = z0, F = 0; for z > z0 the force is attractive (hanging); and as z0 → ∞ it returns to the ZFC expression. Example: for a magnet of φ 10 mm × 5 mm and Br 1.2 T (md = 0.375 A·m2, 2.9 g), the ZFC equilibrium is at about 21 mm (a gap of 18 mm), which is far larger than the actual value for a sintered body (a few mm). This is because the sample is finite, the grain boundaries are weak links, and flux penetrates. Calculating a melt-grown bulk (Mc ≫ H) with Eqs. (11) and (12) approaches this ideal.

8.6 Why the force differs by orders of magnitude between a sintered body and a melt-grown bulk

  1. Weak links at grain boundaries: at randomly oriented grain boundaries Jc falls exponentially with the misorientation, so the intergrain Jc of a polycrystal is small (Hott’s review, Ref. 15).
  2. The current loops close inside the grains: since Mc ∝ Jcℓ, the difference in size between a grain (µm) and a domain (mm to cm) becomes a difference of a factor of about 103 as it is (Kordyuk’s granular model, Ref. 14).
  3. Pinning sites: a melt-grown bulk has fine particles of Y2BaCuO5 dispersed in it to strengthen the pinning.
  4. Pores, second phases and oxygen unevenness reduce the superconducting volume and the current paths.

10. Temperature Dependence of Resistance by the Four-Probe Method

In the four-probe method, which separates the current terminals from the voltage terminals, the resistance of the sample can be measured without including the contact resistance (video A3, lab manual §6.5). In the lab, four electrodes are attached with silver paste to a sample cut into a strip (the outer two for the current I, the inner two for the voltage V, with L the distance between the inner terminals), a constant current of 30 mA is passed, and the sample, immersed in liquid nitrogen, is warmed by lowering the Dewar flask with a jack while the voltage and temperature are recorded from about 77 K to about 173 K (−100 °C). Step ⑪ of ① draws this measurement as R/R(300 K) from the model of the sample of ① (the absolute value of the resistivity is not treated). In a sintered body the resistance drops in two stages, the transition inside the grains (Tc) and the transition of the weak links at the grain boundaries (Tc − transition width), and with many second phases or pores the curve is one in which “the resistance starts to drop but does not reach zero” (resistivityCurve of VEL properties.js, simplified by normalizing to R/R(300 K)). The curves of the other materials are schematics that represent only the position of the transition (zero resistance of Bi-2223 at 107 K: Ref. 20). Zero resistance and levitation are separate properties; Bi-2223 has zero resistance at 77 K, but its levitation is unstable.

  • The constants for the reaction, sintering and oxygen are VEL estimates, not fits to measurements. The numbers for the phase fractions, δ and the relative density are for seeing trends.
  • Partial melting from 940 °C in the presence of CuO, carbon at the grain boundaries from residual BaCO3, and oxygen deficiency at the grain boundaries are not included.
  • The lattice constants and the theoretical density are first-order approximations in δ (they deviate by 0.1–0.4 % at the end values).
  • The particles in the interior schematic are two-dimensional circles, and their number and shape are schematic. Only the size follows the scale bar (display upper limit 40 px). The colors are a schematic for telling the phases apart (in reality Y2BaCuO5 is green, and YBCO and CuO are black).
  • The levitation force is calculated only from the field on the central axis of the magnet; edge effects of the sample, the demagnetizing field, and flux creep (the force weakens with time) are not included. The judgment of lateral stability is schematic, and the coefficients (0.15; χ = 0.25 for ZFC) are provisional values. How a sintered body levitates also depends strongly on Hp0, pexp and the intragrain Jc (provisional) (§8.4).
  • The tetragonal–orthorhombic boundary at high temperature is a schematic fixed at the room-temperature value (x = 6.40) (§7).
  • The porosity coefficient of the sintered body (ρrel/0.85)1.5 (borrowed from VEL), the swelling, cracks and grain-boundary carbonate caused by decarbonation inside the pressed pellet, and the difficulty of recovering the uniformity when a calcined powder is reground are schematics of our own, with provisional coefficients (§4, §5, §8.4).
  • The speed of the temperature change (a time constant of 8 s for cooling in liquid nitrogen and 1500 s for warming) is a provisional value chosen for display.
  • Numbers for arbitrary conditions are rough guides, and except for the representative conditions with literature values (Table 7) they are not quantitative predictions.

A.3 ② Cool and levitate

The sample is chosen from “Sample made in ①” (selectable once ① has been advanced to the polishing of ⑩), four kinds of YBCO sintered body (ready-made samples calculated with the same model as ①), and melt-grown YBCO, GdBCO and Bi-2223 (assumed to be cut out to φ 10 × 2 mm). The arrangement can be switched between “Magnet placed on top of the sample (lab manual §6.4)” (the default; neodymium magnet φ 6 × 3 mm, provisional) and “Sample placed on top of the magnet (for reference: lab video A3)” (magnet φ 20 × 10 mm, provisional). In the arrangement of the lab manual the magnet is not cooled but kept outside the container (at room temperature). In the arrangement of the video, in ZFC the magnet is also cooled in the same polystyrene foam container (this is shown in the figure “View of the experiment” and in the comment). The control panel notes that the liquid nitrogen is poured by a TA, that leather gloves (not cotton work gloves) and bamboo tweezers are to be used, and warns about frostbite and oxygen deficiency. The order of operations becomes the history: pouring liquid nitrogen away from the magnet gives ZFC; stacking the magnet and the sample at room temperature and then pouring gives FC; pressing the cooled sample and the magnet together (in the arrangement of the manual, forcing the magnet onto the sintered body) gives “pressing down.” When the sample is warmed, Jc and the superconducting volume fraction decrease and the levitation height drops, the sample loses its lateral stability or can no longer support the weight, and above Tc the history is erased. Time runs about 12 times faster (0.2 s per frame). The time constants of cooling and warming (8 s, 1500 s) are provisional values. In the “magnet on the sample” arrangement (the default), it is the magnet that levitates or is deflected, so the subject of the comment is the magnet, and the buttons become “Gently place the magnet on the cooled sintered body” and “Force the magnet down (press it in to pin the flux).” As in lab manual §6.4, the button for moving is “Move the magnet sideways (does the sintered body follow?)”, and it picks up the upper magnet with tweezers and moves it by ±7 mm. When the pinning keeps the relative position (S ≥ 1.2 after field cooling or after pressing down), the sintered body follows by sliding along the bottom of the container (on the base in the figure); if it is levitating and 0.8 ≤ S < 1.2, it follows with a delay (after the magnet returns, it keeps moving until it catches up with the original relative position); otherwise the sintered body stays where it is and only the magnet moves. When it does not follow, the distance moved is reduced to (sample radius − 1 mm) so that the center of the magnet does not go beyond the edge of the sample. Even when the magnet is just gently placed after zero-field cooling, if it is levitating and S ≥ 1.2 (a sample with strong pinning, such as melt-grown YBCO or GdBCO), the sample follows because of the pinning of the flux that entered near the surface as the magnet was brought close. The comment in this case does not say “pushed in” but says that this is a condition not in the procedure of lab manual §6.4 (with the sintered body of the manual the magnet is deflected, as in [2]). The comment after the sintered body has been pressed down is written from the calculated equilibrium height and the breakdown of the force (the first and second terms): the flux pushed in is not strong enough to hold the position, but it acts as a force pulling back in the direction away from the pressed position, so the sample stops at almost the same height as with field cooling (lower than in ZFC). The quantities compared are the pinning magnetization Mc and the reversible magnetization mrev (§8.4). When the force is still short of the weight even after pressing down, the comment says “does not levitate even when pressed down,” and the lateral stability S is displayed only while the sample is levitating. When the sample has slid off, the comment says that the lateral judgment is decided by the provisional coefficients (0.15; χ = 0.25 for ZFC) and does not depend on the dimensions or arrangement of the sample and the magnet. When it has slid off after pressing down or field cooling, no next operation is recommended. In the breakdown of the force in the readouts, when the pinning term is in the pulling-back direction (negative), no percentage is given and it is shown with its sign. In the arrangement with the sample on the magnet, if “Move the magnet sideways” is pressed for a sample that is not levitating (or is still almost in contact), the sample moves together with the magnet, remaining on top of it. If the sample is superconducting and the lateral stability S is 1.2 or more after field cooling or after pressing down, the comment says “the pinned flux keeps the relative position” (as in [3] and [4] of lab manual §6.4); otherwise it says “this is due to friction, not pinning.” The lateral judgment in the comment after pressing down is also written differently with the same thresholds (stable at S ≥ 1.2, wobbling at 0.8 or more). For a sample that stops very close to contact after being pressed down, the second term reverses in the direction away from the pressed position to pull back and cancels part of the repulsion, so the comment says that it balances the weight very close to contact (it is the pinning that holds it back), and shows Mc compared with mrev (if a magnet made φ 8 × 3 mm with the slider is pressed onto the standard sintered body, it stops at 0.15 mm, where the first term is 15.34 mN and the second term is −4.29 mN; with the first term alone it would levitate to 1.77 mm). As in the table and the readouts of ③, S in the comment is written as “10 or more” when it exceeds 10. The readouts show the breakdown of the force into reversible diamagnetism and pinning. The scales of the figure “View of the experiment” and of the flux figure, the horizontal axis of the force graph, and the axis of the levitation height change to suit the levitation height and the dimensions of the magnet (the scale of the figure “View of the experiment” is shown at its lower right). Whether the sample is superconducting is judged by T < Tc and a superconducting volume fraction above 2 % (the smooth tail of fsc remains above Tc, but the force is 0 for T ≥ Tc).

A.5 Notes on the calculation and checks

  • The time steps of the heat treatment are on a common grid counted from time 0 (60 s, and 2 s in the quench interval), and the steps are always also split at the boundaries between the intervals of the furnace program. Each step advances in the order decarbonation → formation → peritectic decomposition → oxygen → sintering. Furnace cooling follows Newton’s law of cooling (time constant 3 h, down to 25 °C + 15 °C).
  • Using node, we calculated under the same conditions as the representative presets of VEL (only the regrinding was matched to VEL, dry for 20 min; everything else at the default values) and confirmed approximate agreement with the VEL records (standard δ 0.098, Tc 90.7 K; no hold δ 0.184 (0.185 here); quench δ 0.861 (0.861); over-sintering δ 0.292 (0.293), relative density 0.842 (0.834), grain 5.6 µm; under-calcination YBCO 14 %, over-firing 31 % (30 %)). Under-calcination was calculated for the calcined powder only, and over-firing, as in VEL, without the 400 °C hold (the 40 % for over-firing in Table A2 includes the hold). With the default regrinding (wet, 30 min) the powder is finer, so densification goes further and δ also becomes slightly larger (Table A2: no hold 0.192, quench 0.862, over-sintering 0.302, standard relative density 0.817). The poor-mixing case differs from VEL because the regrinding of the calcined powder was made slower (§4). The relative density is 0.772 against 0.779 in VEL, because oxygen gain and loss are treated as not changing the relative density (§6; an intended difference).
  • A sample advanced one step at a time on the screen and a sample calculated all at once by a preset or in ② and ③ agree, regardless of the playback speed (×1, ×2, ×8) and of “Compute the result right away,” for all the presets and for calcination 0 h and sintering 0 h (the difference is 3 × 10−14 or less with node; for the standard, relative density 0.8166, δ 0.1009, and quench δ 0.8618). This is because the end of each frame on the screen is aligned to the 60 s grid.
  • Even when the conditions of ⑨ are changed after ⑧ has finished, the result agrees with the one calculated all at once (δ 0.0884 for O2 at 450 °C for 12 h with slow cooling, and 0.8618 for quenching without a hold).
  • The difference in the mass balance (Eq. (2)) is 10−15 g or less, the total weighed amount is 3.0000 g, and Y:Ba:Cu = 1:2:3.
  • All inputs are limited to their ranges (clamped), and values that are abnormal as numbers are not displayed.
