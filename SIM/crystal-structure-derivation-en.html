Crystal Structure Theory — Deriving the Packing Fraction, Critical Radius Ratios, the Tolerance Factor, and Pauling’s Rules

This page is the companion explanation to crystal-structure-simulator-en.html. The quantities you saw by running the simulator — the packing fraction 0.7405, the critical radius ratio 0.414, the tolerance factor t, and the electrostatic bond strength s — are derived here from scratch, without skipping any intermediate steps. All you need is the Pythagorean theorem and the formula for the volume of a sphere; no prior knowledge of crystallography is required. The page is intended for second- and third-year undergraduates.

The backbone of this page At bottom, discussions of crystal structure are built in the following three stages. Explain as much as possible with ① geometry (how to pack the spheres), balance the charges with ② electrostatics (Pauling’s rules), and fill in whatever still does not fit with ③ energy (lattice enthalpy, covalency, and the ligand field). This page covers ① and ②. ③ is handed over to Introduction to Solid State Physics (in Japanese) and to textbooks of inorganic chemistry.

1. Introduction — The Problem of Packing Spheres

In the fruit section of a supermarket, mandarin oranges are always stacked in the same way, even though nobody has talked it over with anybody. The problem “pack spheres of equal size as densely as possible” has a definite answer, and that same answer appears, unchanged, as the crystal structure of metals.

This page starts from this simple question. First we define a crystal by translational symmetry (§2), and set up the framework for describing it (§3–§5). With that in place we calculate the packing fraction (§7), and then turn our attention to the 25.95 % of gaps that remain even in the closest packing (§10). The size of these gaps determines the coordination number of ionic crystals (§11) — this is the core of this page.

Notation a, b, c, α, β, γ: lattice constants. Z: number of formula units per unit cell (the number of atoms, for an elemental crystal). r: atomic radius. R: radius of the larger sphere (the anion). CN: coordination number. APF: packing fraction (atomic packing factor). Where a symbol takes a different meaning in a different section, we will say so each time.

2. Crystals and Lattices — Starting from Translational Symmetry

There are many ways to define a crystal, but the one that is most useful in working with materials is the definition by translational symmetry.

Definition (crystal) If there exist three linearly independent vectors a, b, c such that, for any integers n1, n2, n3, a translation by R = n1a + n2b + n3c leaves the structure completely unchanged, the substance is called a crystal.

In other words, “the scenery around a point looks exactly the same when viewed from a point displaced by R.” The set of all these “points with the same view” is the lattice.

Misconception 1 — Lattice points are not atoms A lattice point is a mathematical point, not an atom itself. A lattice point is an “address” at which atoms are to be placed. If you leave this distinction vague and press on, you will surely get stuck later on the question “why does the unit cell contain 8 atoms even though the lattice is fcc?” The correct relation is the following single line.
Lattice (a set of points) + basis (the cluster of atoms placed at each lattice point) = crystal structure
For example, rock-salt NaCl is the “face-centered cubic lattice” plus “a two-atom basis with Na+ at (0,0,0) and Cl− at (½,0,0).” There are 4 lattice points, but 8 atoms.

An important consequence follows at once from this definition. When we discuss the properties of a crystal, we do not need to deal with an infinite number of atoms. Once a single basis and the way it is repeated are fixed, the whole crystal is fixed. This is why computational science can handle crystals, and why X-ray diffraction can determine structures.

3. The Unit Cell and the Lattice Constants — Deriving the General Formula for the Volume

The parallelepiped spanned by the fundamental translation vectors a, b, c is called the unit cell. The shape of this parallelepiped is determined by the lengths a, b, c of the three edges and the angles between them:

(1) α=∠(b,c) , β=∠(c,a) , γ=∠(a,b)

Altogether, these six numbers determine the shape uniquely. The six are collectively called the lattice constants.

3.1 Obtaining the volume from the metric tensor

The volume is given by the scalar triple product V = a·(b×c), but we want to write it in terms of the lattice constants alone. So let us consider the metric tensor

(2) G= ( a2 abcos⁡γ accos⁡β abcos⁡γ b2 bccos⁡α accos⁡β bccos⁡α c2 )

Let A be the matrix whose columns are a, b, c. Then, since G = ATA,

(3) detG= (detA)2 =V2

Expanding the determinant and taking the square root, we obtain the following.

(4) V=abc 1 −cos2α −cos2β −cos2γ +2cos⁡α cos⁡β cos⁡γ
There is no need to memorize Eq. (4) In practice, only the cubic and hexagonal cases are used by hand. For a cubic crystal (α=β=γ=90°) the quantity under the root becomes 1, so V = a3; for a hexagonal crystal (α=β=90°, γ=120°), cosγ = −½, so the quantity under the root becomes ¾ and V = (√3/2)a2c. It is worth remembering, however, that “the further the angles deviate from a right angle, the more the volume shrinks for the same a, b, c.” This is because the quantity under the root takes its maximum value 1 at α=β=γ=90°.

Depending on which constraints are imposed on the six lattice constants, the shape of the unit cell falls into one of 7 types.

Why does “high symmetry” mean “few independent parameters”? High symmetry means that there are many operations (rotations, reflections, and so on) that bring the structure into coincidence with itself. The more operations there are, the more equalities are imposed among the lattice constants. A cubic crystal is fixed in shape by the single parameter a — this is the flip side of having many 4-fold rotation axes and 3-fold rotoinversion axes. A triclinic crystal, conversely, has only the identity operation and inversion, so all six numbers can vary independently. “A small number of independent parameters” is a practical yardstick of how high the symmetry is.
Classifying the symmetry operations systematically gives the 32 point groups, and including translations gives the 230 space groups. Readers who want to go that far should refer to Introduction to Solid State Physics, Chapter 4 (in Japanese) and Chapter 5 (in Japanese) (for university members only; a password is required).

5. The 14 Bravais Lattices — Why 14 and Not 28?

The crystal system fixes the “shape of the box,” but there is still freedom in where to put the lattice points inside that box. There are four ways to place them: P (primitive: corners only), I (body-centered), F (face-centered), and C (base-centered), so a simple multiplication gives 7×4 = 28 possibilities. In reality, however, there are only 14.

Why does it drop to 14? — Two reasons (a) The case where it coincides with one already counted. Consider, for example, a “face-centered tetragonal lattice.” If we re-choose the lattice in the ab plane of the tetragonal system so as to include the face-centered lattice points, we obtain a smaller body-centered tetragonal lattice with edge length a/√2. It is not a new lattice, so we do not count it.
(b) The case where the symmetry of the crystal system is destroyed. For example, if we put a base-centering into a cubic cell, only the c axis is singled out, and the lattice no longer has the symmetry of the cubic system (four 3-fold axes). This is not a cubic lattice.
“Remove the duplicates and those that break the symmetry” — carrying this sorting through to the end was the work of Auguste Bravais (1848), and that is why the 14 types are called Bravais lattices.

Bravais lattices matter again in later chapters A Bravais lattice is more than a classification. As shown in The Theory of X-ray Diffraction, §9, the positions at which diffraction peaks appear are determined only by the Bravais lattice and the lattice constants, and do not depend on which atoms are placed there. The basis affects only the peak heights. In other words, the Bravais lattice is a quantity that is directly visible in a diffraction experiment.

With this, Z becomes a mere addition.

(5) Zsc=8×18=1 , Zbcc=8×18+1=2 , Zfcc=8×18+6×12=4
Z will be used from here on Z appears every time, not only in the packing fraction (§7) but also in the calculation of the density, the structure factor in X-ray diffraction, the normalization of defect concentrations, and the calculation of the lattice enthalpy. “How many formula units are contained in the unit cell?” — make sure you can always answer this question immediately.

6.1 A check by density

Whether a structural model is correct can be judged by calculating the density and comparing it with the measured value.

(6) ρ= ZM NAVcell

For example, Cu is fcc with a = 3.615 Å and M = 63.55. Converting a to cm gives 3.615×10−8 cm, so Vcell = 4.725×10−23 cm3, and putting in Z = 4 gives ρ ≈ 8.94 g cm−3 (measured: 8.96).

Misconception 2 — Forgetting to convert Å to cm This is the most common mistake with Eq. (6). If you cube a in Å as it is, you will be off by a factor of 1024. 1 Å = 10−8 cm.
In addition, when the measured density is lower than the theoretical value, there are vacancies. Discrepancies of a few percent between the theoretical and measured values for TiO and FeO led to the discovery of nonstoichiometric compounds.

7. Deriving the Packing Fraction — Three Steps

When atoms are regarded as rigid spheres, the fraction of the unit-cell volume occupied by the spheres is called the packing fraction.

(7) APF= Z·43πr3 Vcell
Three steps to find the packing fraction ① Find the contact direction. Along which direction do the spheres touch? For sc, along the edge; for bcc, along the body diagonal; for fcc, along the face diagonal. If you start from the equations without confirming this in a picture, you will always go wrong.
② Write r in terms of a. Set up the equation “the length in that direction = the sum of the diameters of the spheres lined up.”
③ Substitute into Eq. (7). a cancels completely, leaving only a number.
If a remains after step ③, there is a mistake somewhere, because the packing fraction is a dimensionless quantity.

7.1 Simple cubic (sc)

The spheres touch along the edge, so a = 2r, Z=1, CN=6.

(8) APFsc= 43πr3 (2r)3 =π6≈0.5236

Only a little over half the space is filled. That is why almost no elemental substance takes the simple cubic structure (α-Po is almost the only example).

7.2 Body-centered cubic (bcc)

The atom at the body center touches the 8 corner atoms along the body diagonal. The body diagonal has length √3 a, along which a radius r + a diameter 2r + a radius r = 4r are lined up.

(9) 3a=4r ⟹ APFbcc= 2·43πr3 (4r/3)3 =3π8 ≈0.6802

7.3 Face-centered cubic (fcc)

The atom at the center of a face touches the 4 corners of that face along the face diagonal. The face diagonal is √2 a.

(10) 2a=4r ⟹ APFfcc= 4·43πr3 (22r)3 =π32 ≈0.7405

Fig. 2. Contact directions. The spheres touch along the edge for sc (a=2r), along the body diagonal for bcc (√3a=4r), and along the face diagonal for fcc (√2a=4r). Whether you can draw this one figure decides whether you can find the packing fraction.

Theorem (Kepler conjecture) When spheres of equal size are packed in three-dimensional space, the upper limit of the packing fraction is π/(3√2) = 0.74048…, and this upper limit is attained by fcc (and hcp).
Kepler conjectured it in 1611, Hales gave a computer-assisted proof in 1998, and a formal proof was completed in 2017. That “spheres cannot be packed more densely than the way mandarin oranges are stacked, however hard you try” has thus been settled mathematically.
Misconception 3 — How should we think about bcc being “widespread even though it is not close-packed”? bcc (0.680) is not close-packed (0.7405). Even so, many metals such as Fe and W take the bcc structure. The rigid-sphere model alone cannot explain this. The reason is that metallic bonding is not directional, so factors other than “packing the spheres densely” — specifically, the relation between the electronic density of states and the position of the Fermi level — come into play. The rigid-sphere model is a starting point, not an endpoint.

8. Close Packing — The Fork Between hcp and fcc

That fcc and hcp have the same packing fraction is no accident. Both are close-packed, and they differ only in the order of stacking.

First make one layer. When spheres are arranged on a plane as densely as possible, each sphere is surrounded by 6 others in a honeycomb-like pattern (layer A). Above layer A there are two kinds of depressions — let us call them the B positions and the C positions. The spheres of the second layer can use only one of the two kinds of depression (they cannot sit on both at the same time). Up to here there is no freedom of choice. The fork occurs at the third layer.

Table 4. Classification by stacking sequence
Where the third layer is placedStacking sequenceNamePeriodExamples
Return to directly above AABAB…Hexagonal close-packed, hcp2 layersMg, Zn, Ti, Co
Use the remaining CABCABC…Cubic close-packed, ccp (= fcc)3 layersCu, Al, Au, Ag, Ni

Fig. 3. The depressions of layer A are of two kinds, B and C. The second layer can use only one of them. If the third layer returns to A, we get hcp (ABAB…); if it uses C, we get fcc (ABCABC…).

Why does ABCABC… become “cubic”? Looking at a picture of fcc, one normally cannot see hexagonal layers. To make them visible, look at the cube along the body diagonal, the [111] direction. Then you can see that the (111) planes are arranged in a honeycomb-like pattern, and that they are stacked with a period of 3 layers as ABCABC…. In other words, ccp (cubic close-packed) and fcc (face-centered cubic) are names given to the same thing seen in different ways.
The energy difference between hcp and fcc is very small for many metals. So the structure can switch when the temperature or pressure is changed, and this is the origin of polymorphism.

9. Deriving the Ideal c/a = 1.633

The hexagonal unit cell of hcp has two lattice constants, a and c. If the spheres are perfectly rigid and in exact contact with the layers above and below, this ratio is fixed by geometry alone.

The 3 spheres of layer A and the 1 sphere of layer B sitting in the depression among them form a regular tetrahedron of edge a = 2r. The height h of a regular tetrahedron of edge a is the distance from the centroid of the base to the apex. The circumradius of the base (an equilateral triangle) is a/√3, so by the Pythagorean theorem

(11) h= a2− (a3)2 =a23

c spans two layers, A→B→A, so c = 2h. Therefore

(12) ca= 223 =1.63299…

This is the basic stance of this page The rigid-sphere model is not a model for explaining measurements. It is a baseline. When a measurement deviates from it, the deviation itself tells us about the nature of the bonding.
That the c/a of Zn is as much as 14 % larger than the ideal value means “strongly bonded within the layers but loosely bonded between them,” and indeed Zn shows strong anisotropy in thermal expansion, elastic modulus, and electrical conduction alike.
We will use this way of reading — reading the deviation from the model as information — once more later, in §12 (the radius-ratio rule).

10. Counting the Holes — Why 2N Tetrahedral and N Octahedral?

Even in the closest packing, 1 − 0.7405 = 0.2595 of the volume, that is, 25.95 %, is empty. This space is not scattered randomly; it has a well-defined shape and position.

Definition (holes in a close-packed structure) Tetrahedral hole: a gap surrounded by 4 spheres. 2N holes per N spheres.
Octahedral hole: a gap surrounded by 6 spheres. N holes per N spheres.

10.1 There are 2N tetrahedral holes

Between two adjacent layers, each sphere corresponds to one “upward-pointing” tetrahedron and one “downward-pointing” tetrahedron. So the number is twice the number of spheres.

Let us check this in the fcc unit cell. Since Z = 4, there should be 8 tetrahedral holes. Indeed, the coordinates of the tetrahedral holes are the 8 points of the (¼,¼,¼) type

(14,14,14) , (34,14,14) ,… (34,34,34)

and since all of them lie inside the cell, the share of each is 1. The total is 8, which agrees with 2N = 2×4 = 8.

10.2 There are N octahedral holes

In fcc, the octahedral holes are at the following two kinds of positions.

  • Body center (½,½,½): 1 × share 1 = 1
  • Midpoint of each edge: 12 × share ¼ = 3

The total is 4. Since Z = 4, this is indeed N.

An ionic crystal is a way of filling the holes Many ionic crystals can be understood as “a close-packed array of large anions with small cations placed in its holes.” Which holes you fill, and how many of them — that alone is enough to write down many different structures.
ccp of Cl− + all the octahedral holes → rock-salt type. ccp of S2− + half of the tetrahedral holes → zinc-blende type. ccp of Ca2+ + all the tetrahedral holes → fluorite type. ccp of O2− + 1/8 of the tetrahedral holes and 1/2 of the octahedral holes → spinel type. ccp of γ-iron + C in a very small fraction of the octahedral holes → austenite (steel).
Steel is hard because the carbon that has entered the octahedral holes hinders the motion of dislocations. The discussion of holes carries over directly to a discussion of the strength of materials.

11. Deriving the Critical Radius Ratios [The Core]

There is an upper limit to the size of a sphere that can be put into a hole. If the limit is exceeded, the surrounding spheres are pushed apart and the contacts of the host lattice can no longer be maintained. This upper limit is called the critical radius ratio.

The common pattern of the derivations Let D be the distance from the center of a coordination polyhedron to a vertex, and E the distance between adjacent vertices (both are geometric quantities measured on the same scale). When the anions (radius R) are just touching each other, E = 2R, and in that state the distance from the center to a vertex is D·(2R/E). The condition for a cation (radius r) to fit in exactly is R + r = D·(2R/E), that is,

(13) (rR)crit =DE/2−1

All that remains is to put in D and E for each polyhedron.

11.1 The octahedron (0.414) seen in a square cross section

Eq. (13) is convenient, but at first it is easier to be convinced by looking at a concrete case. Let us cut out the square cross section through an octahedral hole. Large spheres of radius R sit at the 4 corners of this square and touch one another along the sides. We place a small sphere of radius r at the center.

  • One side of the square = 2R, because two large spheres touch
  • Length of the diagonal = √2 × 2R = 2√2 R
  • Counting the same diagonal in terms of spheres = R + 2r + R = 2R + 2r

Setting the two equal gives

(14) 22R=2R+2r ⟹ rR=2−1=0.4142…

Fig. 4. Square cross section through an octahedral hole. Left: when r/R is exactly 0.414, the small sphere fits exactly while the large spheres remain in contact. Right: when it is smaller, the small sphere “rattles,” and the repulsion between the large spheres comes into play and makes the arrangement unstable.

11.2 The cube (0.732) and the tetrahedron (0.225)

For cubic coordination (CsCl type), the anions sit at the 8 vertices of a cube. If one edge is 2R (the anions touch along the edge), the body diagonal is 2√3 R, and this equals 2(r + R) when measured through the central cation, so

(15) 23R=2(r+R) ⟹ rR=3−1=0.7321…

For tetrahedral coordination, the distance from the center of a regular tetrahedron of edge a = 2R (the center of the circumscribed sphere) to a vertex is d = (√6/4)a = (√6/2)R. This equals R + r, so

(16) R+r=62R ⟹ rR=32−1 =0.2247…
Misconception 4 — Be careful about the meaning of “critical” r/R = 0.414 does not mean “the cation will not fit unless it is smaller than this.” What it correctly means is “below this value, the spheres of the host lattice come into contact with one another, and the cation rattles and becomes unstable.” Conversely, as long as the ratio is above it, the cation merely pushes the anions apart, and the structure is in fact often stable. That is why, among real structures, there are plenty of examples that adopt octahedral coordination even though r/R exceeds 0.414.

12. The Radius-Ratio Rule — How Well Does It Work?

Lining up the four boundaries derived in §11 gives a rule that predicts the coordination number from the radius ratio.

(17) 0.155≤r+r−<0.225:CN 3 , 0.225–0.414:CN 4 , 0.414–0.732:CN 6 , 0.732–1.0:CN 8
Misconception 5 — The most common mistake on exams When you calculate the radius ratio, make the assumed coordination number agree with the coordination number you read from the table. This is because an ionic radius has no meaning unless the coordination number is specified (Na+ is 0.99 Å for 4-coordination and 1.18 Å for 8-coordination). Strictly speaking, there is a circularity: you do not yet know the coordination number you want to determine, yet you use the radius for that coordination number. In practice, you make a provisional calculation with the 6-coordinate value to get a rough idea, and then recalculate with the value for the coordination number that comes out.

The hit rate of the radius-ratio rule is said to be around 60 %, depending on the literature. It is still worth using, because the way it fails tells you the reason.

Three reasons for the misses ① Covalency. Ions are not rigid spheres; they begin to share their electron clouds. The bonds acquire directionality.
② Polarization. Large, soft anions (I−, S2−) are deformed by electric fields (Fajans’ rules). This is why LiI is a miss.
③ Directionality of d electrons. The ligand field favors particular coordinations. Jahn–Teller distortions also occur.
KCl is a miss because K+ and Cl− are almost the same size, which puts the ratio just above the 0.732 boundary, and the difference in lattice energy is small. Compounds that sit “right at the boundary” in this way change structure easily under pressure (KCl becomes the CsCl type at high pressure).
Geometry → electrostatics → energy Explain as much as possible with geometry (the radius ratio), balance the charges with electrostatics (Pauling’s rules in the next section), and fill in the rest with energy. The tools for “filling in with energy” are the Born–Haber cycle and the lattice enthalpy, and beyond them lie ligand-field theory and first-principles calculations. Inorganic materials science is built on this three-layer structure.

13. Goldschmidt’s Tolerance Factor

The perovskite ABO3 is a structure with the large cation A (12-coordinate) at the corners of a cube, the small cation B (6-coordinate) at the body center, and O2− at each face center. Let us write down, from geometry, the condition under which this structure “fits just right.”

Let the lattice constant be a. In an ideal cubic perovskite,

  • B–O distance = a/2  → a = 2(rB + rO)
  • A–O distance = a/√2 → a = √2 (rA + rO)

The condition for these two to hold at the same time is

(18) 2(rB+rO) = 2(rA+rO)

This is the condition. So, as a quantity that measures whether this equation holds, we define

(19) t= rA+rO 2(rB+rO)

This is Goldschmidt’s tolerance factor. The numerator is “the actual A–O distance,” and the denominator is “the A–O distance geometrically required by the B–O distance,” so if they match exactly, t = 1.

Table 8. Branching of structures according to the tolerance factor

This one line explains ferroelectricity BaTiO3 is a ferroelectric because t exceeds 1. The Ba2+ (1.61 Å) at the A site is too large and expands the lattice, so Ti4+ has room to spare inside the TiO6 octahedron. It is more stable for Ti to move toward one of the oxygens and form a short, strong bond than to stay at the center, and this displacement produces the spontaneous polarization.
By comparison, SrTiO3 (Sr2+ is 1.44 Å) has t ≈ 1.00; Ti fits just right and so is not displaced, and at room temperature it is a cubic paraelectric.
Just replacing Ba with Sr at the A site turns a ferroelectric into a paraelectric. An atomic property, the ionic radius, determines a function through a single number, the tolerance factor.
Misconception 6 — t is not a cure-all The tolerance factor is an index of geometry only. Even for the same t, depending on the electron configuration of the B-site ion (whether it is d0 or not), how easily it is displaced changes greatly. That Ti4+ in BaTiO3 is d0 is essential, in the sense that it can be displaced and hybridize with the O 2p orbitals.
Also, although CaTiO3 has t = 0.966, close to 1, in reality the octahedra tilt and the structure is orthorhombic (Pnma). t is a tool for getting a rough idea, not a criterion for judgment.

14. Pauling’s Five Rules

Empirical rules on the structure of ionic crystals, put forward by Linus Pauling in 1929. Combining the radius-ratio rule (geometry) with electrostatic energy (physics), they serve as a practical tool for predicting structures.

Rule 1 (coordination polyhedron rule) Around each cation a polyhedron of anions forms; the cation–anion distance is the sum of the two radii, and the coordination number is determined by the radius ratio — a restatement of the radius-ratio rule of §12.
Rule 2 (electrostatic valence rule) — the most practical Define the electrostatic bond strength that a cation distributes to each of its bonds as s = z/CN. Then the sum of s over the bonds reaching each anion equals the absolute value of that anion’s charge.
(20) s=zCN , ∑isi =|zanion|

In short, the claim is “the charge balance must hold locally as well.” That the composition as a whole is electrically neutral goes without saying, but Rule 2 demands that the balance hold at the level of each individual anion.

14.1 Working it out by hand

(a) Rutile TiO2: Ti4+ is 6-coordinate, so s = 4/6 = 2/3. Each O2− is surrounded by 3 Ti.

∑isi= 3×23=2 =|−2|✓

(b) Perovskite SrTiO3: Ti4+ (6-coordinate) has s = 2/3 and each O has 2 Ti, Sr2+ (12-coordinate) has s = 2/12 = 1/6 and each O has 4 Sr.

∑isi= 2×23+ 4×16 =43+23 =2✓

(c) Spinel MgAl2O4: Mg2+ (4-coordinate) has s = 1/2 and each O has 1 Mg, Al3+ (6-coordinate) has s = 1/2 and each O has 3 Al.

∑isi= 1×12+ 3×12 =2✓
Rules 3, 4, and 5 Rule 3: Coordination polyhedra are stable in the order corner sharing > edge sharing > face sharing. This is because the more elements they share, the closer the central cations come to one another and the more they repel.
Rule 4: The larger the charge and the smaller the coordination number of a cation, the less willing it is to share polyhedra.
Rule 5 (parsimony rule): The number of essentially different kinds of constituents in a crystal tends to be as small as possible.

14.2 Checking Rule 3 with numbers

For regular octahedra, the distance between centers, with d the center-to-vertex distance, is 2d for corner sharing, √2 d for edge sharing, and (2/√3)d ≈ 1.155d for face sharing. With face sharing it shrinks to 58 % of the corner-sharing value. The larger the cation charge, the more this closer approach brings Coulomb repulsion into play.

All the BO6 octahedra of the perovskite are corner-sharing. That is why it is stable, and why there is such a variety of structures. Structures that contain face sharing (such as hexagonal perovskites), on the other hand, are limited to cases where the charge of the B site is small.

Where it does not match, there is information When Rule 2 does not hold, either (a) the structure was written down incorrectly (miscounting the coordination numbers is common), or (b) there is a defect there. In yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ), for example, part of the Zr4+ is replaced by Y3+, so the charge falls short, and oxygen vacancies make up the balance. Rule 2 is the starting point for estimating how many vacancies are needed.

16. Exercises and Solutions

Problem 1 Aluminum has the fcc structure, with lattice constant a = 4.05 Å. (1) Find the atomic radius r. (2) Give Z and the coordination number. (3) If an atom of just the limiting size were placed in an octahedral hole, what would its radius be?

Solution. (1) In fcc the atoms touch along the face diagonal. From √2 a = 4r, r = √2 × 4.05 / 4 = 1.432 ≈ 1.43 Å. (2) Z = 8×⅛ + 6×½ = 4, and the coordination number is 12. (3) The critical radius ratio of the octahedral hole is √2 − 1 = 0.414, so rhole = 0.414 × 1.432 = 0.593 Å.
Alternative solution: the octahedral hole of fcc is at the midpoint of an edge, and the distance from there to the nearest atom is a/2. Therefore rhole = a/2 − r = 2.025 − 1.432 = 0.593 Å. The two agree.

Problem 2 Derive the packing fraction √3π/8 of the bcc structure by following the three steps.

Solution. ① The atom at the body center touches the 8 corner atoms along the body diagonal. ② Along the body diagonal √3a there lie r + 2r + r = 4r, so √3a = 4r, that is, a = 4r/√3. ③ Substituting Z = 2 into Eq. (7) gives APF = 2·(4/3)πr3 / (4r/√3)3 = (8π/3)r3 · 3√3/(64r3) = √3π/8 ≈ 0.680. r has disappeared completely. If it does not disappear, you have made a mistake somewhere.

Problem 3 Show from Eq. (4) that the unit-cell volume of a hexagonal crystal is V = (√3/2)a2c. Then verify that the packing fraction of hcp with the ideal c/a is the same as that of fcc.

Solution. For a hexagonal crystal, α=β=90° and γ=120°, so cosα=cosβ=0 and cosγ=−½. The expression under the square root in Eq. (4) is 1 − 0 − 0 − ¼ + 0 = ¾, so √(3/4) = √3/2. With a=b, V = (√3/2)a2c.
In hcp, a = 2r and c = 2√(2/3)·a = 3.266r. Since a cell of (a,a,c) contains 2 atoms, V = 0.8660 × (2r)2 × 3.266r = 11.314r3, APF = 2×(4/3)πr3 / 11.314r3 = 8.378/11.314 = 0.7405. This agrees with fcc.

Problem 4 Point out the error in the following statement and restate it correctly. “An fcc unit cell has 4 lattice points, so the unit cell of the NaCl-type structure has 4 atoms.”

Solution. “The number of lattice points” has been confused with “the number of atoms.” The unit cell of the fcc lattice has 4 lattice points. The NaCl-type structure is obtained by placing at each of these lattice points a basis consisting of two atoms, Na+ and Cl−. The unit cell therefore contains 4×2 = 8 atoms (4 Na+ and 4 Cl−), and the number of formula units is Z = 4.

Problem 5 Calculate the radius ratio of MgO and find the predicted coordination number. Check whether it agrees with the actual structure (NaCl type).

Solution. From r(Mg2+, CN=6) = 0.72 Å and r(O2−, CN=6) = 1.40 Å, 0.72/1.40 = 0.514. By Eq. (17) this lies in the range 0.414–0.732, so the predicted coordination number is 6. Real MgO has the NaCl-type structure (6:6 coordination), so the prediction agrees.

Problem 6 Let the ionic radii be r(Ba2+,12)=1.61, r(Ti4+,6)=0.605, and r(O2−,6)=1.40 Å. (1) Find the tolerance factor t of BaTiO3. (2) What can be said from that value about the crystal system at room temperature? (3) Verify that Pauling’s Rule 2 is satisfied for cubic BaTiO3.

Solution. (1) From Eq. (19), t = (1.61+1.40)/[√2(0.605+1.40)] = 3.01/2.836 = 1.062.
(2) Since t > 1, Ti4+ has room to spare inside the octahedron. It is more stable when displaced from the center, so at room temperature the structure is tetragonal. The displacement produces a spontaneous polarization, so it is a ferroelectric.
(3) Each O2− is surrounded by 4 Ba2+ and 2 Ti4+. Since s(Ba) = 2/12 = 1/6 and s(Ti) = 4/6 = 2/3, Σs = 4×(1/6) + 2×(2/3) = 2/3 + 4/3 = 2 = |−2| ✓

Problem 7 A researcher synthesized a new oxide ABO3 and reported that it is “a cubic perovskite.” The 12-coordinate radius of A2+ is said to be 1.75 Å, and the 6-coordinate radius of B4+ 0.60 Å. Examine this report critically from the standpoint of the tolerance factor.

Solution. t = (1.75+1.40)/[√2(0.60+1.40)] = 3.15/2.828 = 1.11. This goes well beyond the region “t > 1.00” of Table 8, so the A-site ion is too large. It is quite likely to be unstable as a cubic perovskite, and it may instead adopt a tetragonal structure through displacement of B, or a hexagonal perovskite (a structure in which the BO6 octahedra share faces), or some other structure type.
Points to check: (a) whether the XRD pattern can really be indexed as cubic, and whether the peaks split on the high-angle side, (b) whether the coordination numbers of the ionic radii used are correct (the values for 12-coordinate A and 6-coordinate B?), (c) whether the A-site ion really has that valence.
However, t is an index of geometry only, so one cannot assert that “t is 1.11, therefore it is wrong.” The right way to use it is as grounds for asking for a remeasurement.

Problem 8 The c/a of Zn is 1.856, which is 14 % larger than the ideal value 1.633. What can be expected about the physical properties of Zn from this? Give two, with reasons.

Solution. That c/a is larger than ideal means that the distance to the layers above and below, which would be in contact for rigid spheres, has increased. That is, the bonding within the layers is strong and the bonding between the layers is relatively weak. The following can be expected from this (it is enough to give two).
• Anisotropy of thermal expansion: the thermal expansion coefficient is larger along the c axis, where the bonding is weak (in fact, αc of Zn is several times αa).
• Anisotropy of elastic moduli: the elastic modulus along the c axis is smaller than in the plane.
• Anisotropy of deformation: slip occurs readily on the basal plane (0001), and cleavage occurs easily.
• Anisotropy of electrical and thermal conduction: conduction is easier in the plane.
