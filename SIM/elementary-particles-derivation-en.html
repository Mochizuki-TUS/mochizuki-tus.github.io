Elementary Particles for Materials Scientists — From Hydrogen and Helium to Quarks and the Four Forces

— Atoms, nuclei, nucleons, and elementary particles, read through the hierarchy of “size” and “fluctuation.” Explanation and derivations for the companion simulator (elementary-particles-simulator-en.html)

In materials science and condensed-matter physics, most problems can be solved by treating an atom as “a sphere of radius about 1 Å,” an electron as “a point in an orbital,” and a nucleus as “a point charge of +Ze.” But once we start thinking about nuclear fusion, we run into questions: why does a nucleus not fly apart when the protons inside it should repel one another, is there any reason for neutrons to stick together, and would it not be more stable, after all, for a proton and an electron to combine into a neutron? These are simple but fundamental questions that the language of condensed-matter physics alone can no longer answer. This explanation answers such questions one by one, starting from the hydrogen atom and the helium atom.

The one backbone of the story is the view that “at every level, what determines the size is the quantum fluctuation of the constituents.” The size of an atom is set by the fluctuation of its electrons (the spread of the 1s orbital), that of a nucleus by the fluctuation of its nucleons, that of a nucleon by the fluctuations of quarks and gluons, while electrons and quarks themselves are “points” down to a resolution of 10−18 m. And the force that governs the fluctuation differs from level to level: the electromagnetic force for atoms, the nuclear force (the leftover of the strong force) for nuclei, the color force (the strong force itself) for nucleons, and the weak force, which converts protons and neutrons into each other. Gravity is only 10−36 times as strong as the electromagnetic force and becomes the leading player only at the scale of astronomical bodies.

1.2 “Size” and “fluctuation” are different concepts

Experimentally, the size of the electron is at most 2.8×10−19 m (electron–positron scattering at LEP [Bourilkov 2001]) and that of a quark at most 4.3×10−19 m (electron–proton scattering at HERA [ZEUS 2016]); the Standard Model treats both as point particles of zero size. On the other hand, the range over which that electron is found inside an atom (the amplitude of its fluctuation) is 10−10 m, and the range over which a quark is found inside a proton is 10−16 m. The “size of a particle” and “the range in which the particle is found” are entirely different quantities, differing by a factor of 103–108.

Nucleons and atoms have internal structure, so their size itself can be measured. The proton’s 0.84 fm is the rms radius of its charge distribution (from muonic-hydrogen spectroscopy [Antognini 2013]; 0.84075(64) fm in CODATA 2022), and the radius of an atom is the spread of its electron distribution. The size of a nucleon is the fluctuation of the quarks inside it, and the size of an atom is the fluctuation of the electrons inside it — that is, the “size” of a composite particle is the “fluctuation” of its constituents.

1.3 Why is the classical picture good enough in the world of materials?

In a crystal of metallic copper, against an atomic radius of 1.28 Å, the rms displacement of thermal vibration at room temperature is only about 0.08 Å (from the Debye–Waller factor of X-ray diffraction, B ≈ 0.55 Å², ⟨u²⟩ = B/8π²), and the zero-point motion makes up about 0.04 Å of it. The fluctuation is only 1/30 of the size — so atoms can be placed as “spheres,” and classical lattice models can be used. The reason is that atoms are 105 times heavier than electrons and the lattice confinement is loose on the Å scale, so the kinetic energy of Eq. (1) is only of the order of meV. The deeper we descend into the world of elementary particles, the more this ratio is reversed, until the fluctuation exceeds the size by a factor of 103–108 (the ledger in §8).

2. Atoms: Electromagnetism and Quantum Mechanics — Why Don’t Electrons Fall In, and Why Don’t They Become Neutrons?

2.1 Why the electron does not fall into the nucleus

In classical electromagnetism, an electron circling a nucleus loses energy by radiation and, in hydrogen, would fall into the nucleus in about 10−11 s. It does not fall because confining the electron to a region of radius r requires the kinetic energy of Eq. (1). The energy obtained by adding the Coulomb attraction,

2.2 Speed of the 1s electron: vrms = Zαc

Because the virial theorem ⟨T⟩ = −E holds for the Coulomb system, the kinetic energy of the 1s state is 13.6 Z² eV. Setting this equal to ½m⟨v²⟩ gives

(3) ⟨v2⟩=Zαc, α=e24πε0ℏc =1137.036, prms=Zαmc=Zℏa0

For hydrogen, v = 0.73% c = 2,190 km/s. The time scale of the fluctuation is a0/(αc) = ħ/Eh = 2.42×10−17 s (the atomic unit of time). In helium the two electrons screen each other’s nuclear charge; using the effective nuclear charge of the variational method, Z′ = 27/16 = 1.6875, one gets v = 1.23% c (the per-electron value from the exact ⟨T⟩ = 2.9037 hartree is 1.24% c). 2.3 Relativity shows up in heavy atoms: the color of gold and liquid mercury

Eq. (3) gives v/c = 0.58 for Z = 79 (gold) and 0.67 for Z = 92 (uranium). The point-nucleus 1s level of the Dirac equation is

(4) E1s=mc2 [1−(Zα)2−1] =−Z2Eh2 [1+(Zα)24+⋯]

and for gold the nonrelativistic −84.9 keV deepens to −93.6 keV, while the radius of the 1s orbital shrinks to √(1−(Zα)²) = 0.82 of its nonrelativistic value. This contraction propagates through the orthogonality condition up to the 6s orbital, lowering the d→s transition energy from 3.7 eV (ultraviolet) in silver (4d→5s) to 2.4 eV (blue) in gold (5d→6s) — which is why gold absorbs blue light and looks yellow. In mercury, the contracted 6s² becomes an inert closed shell, the metallic bonding weakens, and mercury is a liquid at room temperature (Pyykkö & Desclaux 1979). The common wisdom of condensed-matter physics that “heavy elements are relativistic” starts from an elementary-particle fact: the 1s electron is fluctuating at half the speed of light.

2.4 A proton and an electron do not combine into a neutron — the mass ledger

Classical electromagnetic intuition suggests that a positive proton and a negative electron combining into a neutral particle would be the most stable arrangement. But look at the particle masses:

(5) mn−mp−me =939.565−938.272−0.511 =+0.782 MeV>0

so the neutron is heavier than a proton plus an electron. Hence p + e− → n + νe (electron capture) cannot occur unless 0.782 MeV is supplied from outside, and conversely a free neutron decays as n → p + e− + ν̄e (lifetime 878.4 s, §5). There are two reasons for the stability of the hydrogen atom: (i) quantum mechanics (§2.1) stops the electromagnetic fall, and (ii) energy (Eq. (5)) forbids conversion into a neutron.

This 0.782 MeV is by no means a number to be taken for granted. The neutron–proton mass difference of 1.293 MeV (measured) is reproduced within the errors by the lattice calculation, which finds the net balance of +2.5 MeV from the d quark being heavier than the u quark and −1.0 MeV from the electromagnetic self-energy of the proton’s charge to be 1.51(16)(23) MeV (a first-principles lattice QCD+QED calculation by the BMW Collaboration [Borsanyi 2015], §4.6). If the difference were smaller than 0.511 MeV, hydrogen atoms would turn into neutrons by inverse β decay and neither chemistry nor life would exist — the same paper says as much.

So where is the condition for “combining” met? In dense matter where the electrons are degenerate, the Fermi energy of the electrons can pay this cost. For a free gas of protons and electrons, the condition is a kinetic energy EF ≥ 0.782 MeV, and

(6) pFc= (0.782+0.511)2−0.5112 MeV =1.19 MeV, ne=pF33π2ℏ3 =7.4×1036 m−3 ⟹ ρ≈2mune≈2×107 g/cm³

From the upper end of the central densities of white dwarfs (106–108 g/cm³) onward, protons and electrons really begin to “combine” (neutronization). For protons inside nuclei, higher densities (109–1011 g/cm³) are needed, and in the outer crust of a neutron star this electron capture turns nuclei one after another into neutron-rich ones. In other words, the intuition that “proton + electron → neutron is stable” is correct only in a world where gravity has squeezed the electrons up to 0.8 MeV.

2.5 Electrons cannot live inside the nucleus

Before the discovery of the neutron in 1932, the nucleus was thought to be “made of protons and electrons.” It is Eq. (1) that buried this idea. Confining an electron to the size of a nucleus (1–5 fm) gives a kinetic energy ħc/Δx = 40–200 MeV. On the other hand, the Coulomb attraction that the nucleus exerts on the electron is Ze²/(4πε0R), which is only 14 MeV even for Z = 50 and R = 5 fm, and the energy of the electrons that emerge in β decay is only about 1 MeV — a nucleus cannot confine an electron. So the neutral part inside the nucleus must be not an electron but a new particle about as heavy as the proton. That is the neutron (Chadwick 1932), and in the same year Yukawa began to work on the problem of “the force that binds protons and neutrons” (§3).

2.6 The visualization model: Nelson’s stochastic mechanics

The “motion” of electrons, nucleons, and quarks in the simulator is not a trajectory of quantum mechanics. It uses the diffusion process of Nelson’s stochastic mechanics [Nelson 1966], a random motion that preserves the probability density |ψ|²:

(7) dX= (v+u)dt +ℏmdW, u=ℏm∇ln|ψ|, v=ℏm∇S (ψ=|ψ|eiS)

The diffusion coefficient is ħ/2m, and the drift is the sum of the “current velocity” v and the “osmotic velocity” u; the stationary solution of the Fokker–Planck equation of this process is exactly ρ = |ψ|². For the 1s state (ψ real, v = 0), u = −(Zαc) r̂: it is no accident that the magnitude of the inward drift velocity equals Zαc, the same as vrms of Eq. (3). For a (real) wave function with v = 0, ⟨p²⟩ = ħ²∫|∇ψ|² d³r = m²⟨u²⟩ holds identically, so the osmotic velocity carries all of the kinetic energy. For 1s, in addition, the decay constant of |ψ| ∝ e−Zr/a0 is constant, so the magnitude of u is ħZ/ma0 = Zαc everywhere. For nucleons and quarks, the ground state of a harmonic oscillator (Gaussian ψ) is used, and the drift u = −(ħ/mb²) r reproduces a Gaussian distribution with rms radius b√(3/2). The distribution is exact, but a “single path” has no physical meaning — that is the convention of this visualization (§12).

3. Nuclei: The Nuclear Force (the Residual Strong Force) — How Can Repelling Protons Stay Together?

3.1 The Yukawa potential: the range of a force is set by the mass of its carrier

The carrier of the electromagnetic force (the photon) is massless, and the Coulomb potential reaches to infinity as 1/r. Hideki Yukawa (1935), noting that the nuclear force reaches only about 2 fm, supposed that its carrier has a mass. The equation for a field φ of mass m produced by a static point source gδ³(r) (the static limit of the Klein–Gordon equation) and its solution are

(8) (∇2−μ2)φ =−gδ3(r) ⟹ V(r)=− g24π e−μrr, μ=mcℏ, range1μ=ℏmc

For m → 0 it reverts to 1/r. From a range of 2 fm, Yukawa predicted m ≈ 100–200 MeV/c², and in 1947 the π meson (139.6 MeV) was found: ħ/mπc = 197.3/139.6 = 1.41 fm. Applying the same formula to the weak force, the mass of the carrier W, 80.4 GeV, gives a range of 2.5×10−18 m (§5). Range of a force = ħ/(carrier mass × c) — this single line explains half of the difference in the “apparent strengths” of the four forces.

3.3 Analogy with the van der Waals force: the nuclear force as a residual force

The “residual force” most familiar to materials scientists is the van der Waals force. Neutral atoms, although they carry no net charge, attract each other weakly through fluctuations of their internal charge distributions (induced dipoles), and the strength (10 meV) is far weaker than the bonds inside an atom (eV). The nuclear force has the same structure. Nucleons are color-neutral (the three colors mix to give “white”), yet they attract each other through fluctuations of the quarks and gluons inside — specifically, through the exchange of color-neutral mesons (π, σ, ρ, ω …) — and the strength (tens of MeV) is orders of magnitude weaker than the color force that confines quarks (GeV/fm). That is why the nuclear force is called “the leftover of the strong force” (the residual strong force). The difference is that, whereas the van der Waals force falls off as 1/r6, the nuclear force is cut off as e−r/1.4 fm because its carrier is massive.

3.5 Two neutrons and two protons do not bind, but a proton plus a neutron does

The intuition that “there is no reason for neutrons to stick together” is correct: the dineutron (nn) does not exist. The reason, however, is not the Coulomb force. For two identical fermions to be in a relative s wave (the configuration that makes the best use of the attraction), the Pauli principle requires the spins to be antiparallel (¹S₀, the spin singlet). A proton and a neutron, on the other hand, are distinguishable particles, so the spin-parallel ³S₁ (triplet) is also allowed, and there the tensor force of π exchange mixes in a ³D₁ component (probability ≈5%) and produces extra attraction. This is what decides the binding of the deuteron, and ¹S₀ does not have it. Comparing scattering lengths a (negative means unbound):

3.6 Why ⁴He is special, and why Coulomb wins in heavy nuclei

⁴He (the α particle) is bound by 7.07 MeV per nucleon, far more strongly than any other light nucleus (deuteron 1.11 MeV, ³He 2.57 MeV). The 1s orbital can be “fully occupied” by the four combinations of spin up/down × proton/neutron, so all four nucleons enter the configuration that makes the best use of the attraction. The fifth nucleon is pushed into the 1p orbital by the Pauli principle, so neither ⁵He nor ⁵Li is bound. For heavy nuclei, the semi-empirical mass formula

(10) B= aVA −aSA2/3 −aCZ2A1/3 −aA(N−Z)2A +δ, aV≈15.8, aS≈18.3, aC≈0.71, aA≈23.2 MeV

summarizes the story. The nuclear force has a short range, so it acts only as the volume term aVA, whereas the Coulomb term accumulates as Z². In this competition the binding per nucleon is greatest at iron and nickel (8.8 MeV) (the reason fusion releases energy; see the B/A curve in the nuclear fusion simulator (in Japanese)), and heavy nuclei require an excess of neutrons (N > Z) and, beyond Z ≈ 100, fall apart by spontaneous fission. The periodic table has an end because the electromagnetic force is a long-range force.

3.7 Why does a neutron inside ⁴He not decay?

A free neutron undergoes β decay in 15 minutes, but a neutron in ⁴He is stable forever. Turning n → p would convert ⁴He into ⁴Li, but ⁴Li is unstable against proton emission, and the energy required falls short by as much as 22.9 MeV. The difference in binding energies created by the nuclear force and the Pauli principle shuts out the weak force. Conversely, if the daughter nucleus is slightly more deeply bound, as in tritium (³H → ³He, Q = 18.6 keV, half-life 12.3 years), the decay does occur. Whether “a neutron inside a nucleus is stable” is decided not by the weak force but by the energy balance of the nucleus.

4. Nucleons: Quarks, Gluons, and Confinement — 99% of the Proton’s Mass Is Energy

4.1 Quarks and the charges of nucleons

Protons and neutrons are not elementary particles but composite particles with three valence quarks (Gell-Mann and Zweig 1964; confirmed by deep inelastic scattering at SLAC in 1968). There are six kinds (flavors) of quarks, and their charges are the fractions +2/3 or −1/3 of e:

The charges of nucleons come from quarks, and the difference between a proton and a neutron is only that one u has been swapped for one d — so when the weak force turns d ⇄ u, protons and neutrons convert into each other (§5). One convincing piece of evidence for the quark model is the magnetic moment: the model predicts μn/μp = −2/3, and the measured value is −1.913/2.793 = −0.685.

4.2 Color charge and gluons: the force of SU(3)

In addition to electric charge, quarks carry a “color charge” that takes three values (red, green, blue). When all three colors are present they make “white” (color-neutral), and a nucleon is a color singlet with one quark of each color (a meson is a quark plus an antiquark: color plus anticolor makes white). Gluons carry the color force, and there are 3×3 − 1 = 8 kinds of them, from the combinations of color and anticolor (in group-theoretic terms, the adjoint representation 8 of SU(3); see §7 of the Lie group page). Unlike photons, gluons carry color charge themselves, so gluons interact with each other. This is what separated the fates of the electromagnetic and strong forces: the field lines of the photon spread out and thin as 1/r², whereas the field lines of gluons attract one another and bundle into a thin tube (a string), transmitting a constant force independent of distance (§4.4).

The world of quarks inside a nucleon is a strongly coupled world where perturbation theory cannot be used, and lattice QCD is the only first-principles tool.

The quark–antiquark potential is well described by the Cornell form, from lattice QCD and the spectroscopy of heavy-quark systems (charmonium):

The force pulling quarks apart is constant, independent of distance, and its magnitude is the weight of 10 cars — pulling them apart by just 1 fm costs 0.9 GeV, the energy of six π mesons, so the string breaks on the way and a quark–antiquark pair (a meson) is created, and a lone quark never emerges. 4.5 The origin of the proton’s mass: only 1% comes from the Higgs

4.6 Why is the neutron 0.14% heavier than the proton?

As we saw in §2.4, this small difference (1.293 MeV) underpins the existence of the hydrogen atom. A first-principles calculation that incorporates QED into lattice QCD [Borsanyi et al. 2015] obtained

— showing that the difference is the net balance of two effects. Because d is heavier than u, the udd neutron becomes heavier, while the charged proton becomes heavier by the amount of its electromagnetic self-energy. If α were twice its present value, the QED term would be −2 MeV and the difference would shrink to nearly the electron mass, 0.511 MeV, leaving the hydrogen atom on the verge of stability; conversely, if the difference were too large, too many neutrons would be lost in Big Bang nucleosynthesis and heavy elements would hardly form — a textbook example of “cosmic fine-tuning.”

4.7 Fluctuation and size of quarks: more than 1,400 times

Inside the proton’s charge radius of 0.84 fm, the position of a valence quark fluctuates by about 0.6 fm rms (the rest comes from the π-meson cloud). On the other hand, the size of the quark itself is at most 4.3×10−19 m from electron–proton scattering at HERA [ZEUS 2016]: the fluctuation is more than 1,400 times the size. From Eq. (1) the momentum is p ≈ ħ/(0.5 fm) ≈ 400 MeV/c — exceeding the bare mass of 2–5 MeV/c² by orders of magnitude, so the quark moves at nearly the speed of light. Moreover, the content of the proton is not just “three quarks.” Gluons carry about half of the proton’s momentum (⟨x⟩g ≈ 0.4–0.5), and quark–antiquark pairs (sea quarks) are constantly created and annihilated. These are what the simulator’s nucleon view draws as small purple pairs. The time scale of the fluctuation is ħ/(pc) ≈ 1.6×10−24 s.

4.8 The neutron is a “positive core with a negative skin”

The charge of the neutron is 0, but the mean square radius of its charge distribution is negative: ⟨r²⟩n = −0.1155(17) fm² (PDG 2024). This means that there is positive charge on the inside and negative charge on the outside, and it is explained by both (i) the “π− cloud,” in which the neutron temporarily fluctuates into p + π−, and (ii) the spin-dependent color magnetic force pushing the d quarks outward. The magnetic radius is 0.86 fm, similar to the proton’s — even though the neutron is “neutral,” its inside is a whirl of charge and current. ▶ Simulator ①: nucleus → neutron

5. The Weak Force: β Decay, W and Z, and the Sun — “Weak” Because the Carrier Is Heavy

5.1 β decay changes the type of quark

The β decay of the neutron, n → p + e− + ν̄e, is, at the quark level, d → u + W−, W− → e− + ν̄e. The weak force is not a force that “sticks things together” but a force that changes the type (flavor) of particles, and of the four forces it is the only one that can change the type of a quark. Fermi (1934) formulated it as an interaction in which four fermions couple at a single point, and its strength is the Fermi constant GF = 1.1663787(6)×10−5 GeV−2. In 1983 the carriers W± (80.37 GeV) and Z0 (91.19 GeV) were discovered at CERN, and it became clear that the Fermi constant can be written in terms of the W mass and the coupling constant g:

(15) GF2= g28MW2, αW≡g24π =αsin2θW =1137.04×10.2313 ≈131.7

αW ≈ 1/32 is larger than the electromagnetic 1/137 — the coupling of the weak force itself is not weak. Substituting into Eq. (15) gives GF ≈ παW/(√2 MW²) ≈ 1.1×10−5 GeV−2, reproducing the measured value in both order of magnitude and coefficient.

5.2 Why is it “weak”? A range of 2.5×10−18 m

Applying Yukawa’s Eq. (8) to the W, the range is ħ/MWc = 197.3 MeV·fm / 80,369 MeV = 2.5×10−3 fm = 2.5×10−18 m. This is 1/340 of the size of the proton (0.84 fm) and 1/500 of the distance between nucleons inside a nucleus (1–2 fm). The Yukawa factor e−r/rW at r = 1 fm is e−400 ≈ 10−174 — between one nucleon and another, the weak force is literally zero, and it “shows its face” only at the instant d → u occurs inside a single nucleon. The effective strength at low energies is GFE² ∝ (E/MW)², which at the scale of the proton is GFmp² ≈ 10−5: this is the number for the weak force in the often-quoted “strong force 1, electromagnetic force 10−2, weak force 10−5–10−6, gravity 10−39.”

The slowness of the weak force shows up in lifetimes. A decay by the strong force (Δ → Nπ) takes 10−23 s, a decay by the electromagnetic force (π0 → γγ) 10−16 s, and decays by the weak force take 2.6×10−8 s for π+ and 878.4 s for the neutron (PDG 2024; half-life 10.2 minutes). The neutron is especially long-lived because Q = 0.782 MeV is small and the phase space scales as Q5 (Sargent’s rule). Indeed,

(16) 1τn= GF2|Vud|2 (1+3gA2) me5c4f(1+ΔR) 2π3ℏ7 ⟹ τn≈880 s

(Vud = 0.974 is an element of the Kobayashi–Maskawa matrix, gA = 1.275 is the axial coupling of the nucleon, f = 1.689 is the phase-space integral, and ΔR ≈ 3.9% is the radiative correction). In this way, the Fermi constant and the electron mass alone give almost the correct lifetime. The weak force is “weak” because GF is small, GF is small because the W is heavy — and the W is heavy because of the Higgs field (§5.4).

5.3 Why the Sun does not burn out in a hurry

The first step of the pp chain, the energy source of the Sun, is

(17) p+p→d+e++νe (Q=0.42 MeV), at the quark level u→d+W+, W+→e++νe

As we saw in §3.5, the diproton (pp) is not bound, so a deuteron cannot form unless, within the instant (10−22 s) in which the two protons are within reach of the nuclear force, a u → d occurs inside one of the protons and turns it into a neutron. The Coulomb barrier (500 keV against 1.3 keV at the center of the Sun, overcome by the tunnel effect) and the slowness of the weak force combine to make the reaction rate extremely small: the mean time before a given proton reacts at the center of the Sun is about 1010 years, and the heat generation density at the center is a mere 276 W/m³ — comparable to human metabolism or a compost heap. The Sun is bright only because its volume is enormous.

This is a blessing, not a defect. If the pp reaction proceeded by the strong force alone, the Sun would burn out in a few thousand years and there would have been no time for life to evolve. By contrast, fusion reactors choose D + T → ⁴He + n because this reaction does not need the weak force (the neutron is already inside the tritium) and its rate is as much as 1025 times that of pp (see the “Four Forces” and “Walls and Tunneling” tabs of the nuclear fusion simulator (in Japanese)).

5.4 Electroweak unification: above 100 GeV it merges with electromagnetism

In the Glashow–Weinberg–Salam theory, the electromagnetic and weak forces are four forces (W+, W−, Z0, photon) arising from a single SU(2)×U(1) gauge symmetry, and the coupling constants g, g′ are tied to the electric charge e = g sinθW through the mixing angle θW (sin²θW = 0.2313). The vacuum expectation value of the Higgs field, v = 246 GeV, gives the masses MW = gv/2 = 80 GeV and MZ = 91 GeV, and only the photon remains massless — and this is the whole reason why the electromagnetic force reaches to infinity while the weak force reaches only 10−18 m (the Higgs boson, 125.2 GeV, was discovered in 2012). When the collision energy exceeds 100 GeV (when the distance becomes shorter than 10−18 m), the Yukawa factor returns to 1 and the weak force acts with a strength comparable to the electromagnetic force. If you lower the distance to 10−18 m in tab ② of the simulator, you can see the curve of the weak force catch up with that of the electromagnetic force.

The weak force has one more feature that the other forces lack: it breaks mirror symmetry (parity) maximally and acts only on left-handed particles (the Lee–Yang prediction and Wu’s experiment, 1957). Neutrinos feel only the weak force, so they pass straight through the Earth and the Sun (solar neutrinos: Koshiba 2002; neutrino oscillation: Kajita 2015).

  • Radiocarbon dating: β decay of 14C → 14N (half-life 5,730 years).
  • Heat inside the Earth and radioactivity inside the body: β decay of 40K (half-life 1.25 billion years). About 4,000 Bq per human body, and one of the main sources of geothermal heat.
  • PET imaging: gamma rays from the annihilation of positrons emitted in β+ decay of 18F are imaged.
  • Tritium: β decay (12.3 years). The cause of the difficulty of handling it as a fusion fuel.
  • The speed at which the Sun burns (§5.3), and the process by which elements are made in supernovae (electron capture and neutrino emission).

6. The Size and Fluctuation of the Electron — A Point Below 10−18 m Trembles at 10−13 m

6.1 How is “size” measured?

The size of a particle is measured by scattering. If the charge distribution is extended, the scattering amplitude at momentum transfer q is multiplied by a form factor F(q²) = 1 − q²⟨r²⟩/6 + …, and when ħ/q becomes shorter than the size, deviations from a point charge become visible. The proton’s 0.84 fm is the actual size measured in this way (by electron scattering and muonic-hydrogen spectroscopy). For the electron, e+e− → e+e− scattering at LEP (√s ≈ 200 GeV) probed below 10−18 m and found no deviation, yielding an upper limit on the radius of 2.8×10−19 m (95% confidence level) [Bourilkov 2001]. For quarks, ep scattering at HERA gives 4.3×10−19 m [ZEUS 2016]. The anomalous magnetic moment of the electron, g−2, agrees with QED to 13 digits (g/2 = 1.001 159 652 180 59(13) [Fan et al. 2023]), and using a relation of composite models (Brodsky–Drell), the size of any structure is estimated to be below 10−22 m [Dehmelt 1988] — but this is a model-dependent estimate, not a measurement.

The classical electron radius often quoted as the “radius of the electron,” re = e²/(4πε0mc²) = 2.82 fm, is “the radius of a classical sphere whose electrostatic energy equals mc²,” and is not a size (it is 104 times larger than the actual upper limit). It only has meaning as the length scale that appears in the Thomson scattering cross section (8π/3)re².

6.2 The Compton wavelength: the limit to locating a single particle, and Zitterbewegung

So how finely can a “point” electron be localized? From the relativistic version of Eq. (1),

(18) Δx≲λ¯C=ℏmc =3.86×10−13 m ⟹ Δpc≳mc2 (electron–positron pairs are created)

If an electron is confined to less than the reduced Compton wavelength, the energy exceeds mc², electron–positron pairs are created from the vacuum, and one can no longer say “which one is the original electron.” This is the lower limit at which the position of the electron can be given meaning as a single particle.

The same thing appears in the Dirac equation. The eigenvalues of the Dirac velocity operator cα are only ±c, and the time evolution of the position operator of a free electron is

(19) x(t)= x0+c2pHt +(oscillating term), angular frequency2Hℏ≈2mc2ℏ =1.55×1021 s−1, amplitudeℏ2mc=λ¯C2

so even an electron at rest has its position trembling with an amplitude of ƛC/2 = 1.9×10−13 m, 1021 times per second (Zitterbewegung, Schrödinger 1930). This oscillation comes from the interference between positive-energy and negative-energy (positron) solutions; for a purely positive-energy wave packet it disappears, but instead the position operator itself is blurred over a range of ƛC (Newton–Wigner). Either way, the conclusion is the same: “an electron as a stationary point charge” does not exist on scales finer than 10−13 m. An observable trace is the Darwin term of the hydrogen atom. The correction, equivalent to averaging the Coulomb potential over a range of ƛC,

(20) HD= ℏ28m2c2 ∇2V =16⟨δr2⟩∇2V with ⟨δr2⟩=34λ¯C2, ΔE1s=Z4α4mc22 =0.72 meV(H)

raises only the s states and has been measured as part of the fine structure of hydrogen.

6.3 The electron shaken by the zero-point fluctuations of the vacuum: the Lamb shift

In QED the vacuum also has zero-point fluctuations of the electromagnetic field, and the electron is constantly shaken by them. In Welton’s (1948) intuitive calculation, one sums the equation of motion m d²δr/dt² = −eE(t) over all modes of the zero-point electric field to obtain

(21) ⟨δr2⟩= 2απ λ¯C2 lnmc2EB ⟹ δrms≈0.2λ¯C ≈8×10−14 m, ΔE2s=16⟨δr2⟩⟨∇2V⟩ ≈h×1 GHz

(EB ≈ 17 Ry is Bethe’s mean excitation energy, and the logarithmic factor is ≈ 8). When the electron is blurred over a range of 10−13 m, the potential it feels near the nucleus is averaged and the s levels become shallower — this is the intuitive explanation of the Lamb shift (measured 1057.8 MHz), which lifts the degeneracy of 2s1/2 and 2p1/2, and even this rough estimate gets the order of magnitude and the coefficient nearly right. If Zitterbewegung is “a property of the Dirac theory of a single electron,” Welton’s trembling is “a shaking from the medium called the vacuum,” and both are on the scale of 10−13 m.

The electron is a point of 10−18 m, yet its fluctuation creates the chemistry and the materials of the Å scale — the size of this gap (a factor of 108) is the number this explanation most wants to convey.

7.3 The ranking changes with distance

  • Å to m: the electromagnetic force (atoms, molecules, solids, life). Because matter is electrically neutral, it cancels out at long distances.

9. Forces Are Carried by “Particle Exchange” — Photon Exchange, the Yukawa Potential, and How It Differs from Exchange Energy

9.1 The electrostatic field is a cloud of virtual photons

In classical electromagnetism, a proton creates a Coulomb field E = e/4πε0r² in space, and an electron feels a force from that field. In quantum electrodynamics (QED), the electromagnetic field itself is quantized, and its quantum is the photon. The electrostatic field around a proton is a cloud of virtual photons that the proton constantly emits and absorbs, and when an electron is in that cloud, it absorbs a virtual photon emitted by the proton (or the other way around) — this is what is meant by “the force is carried by exchanging photons.” A real photon satisfies E = pc (mass 0), but a virtual photon need not satisfy this relation; instead, the uncertainty relation

(24) ΔE·Δt≳ℏ ⟹ time to borrow a carrier of mass m Δt≈ℏmc2, distance covered λ=cΔt=ℏmc

allows it to “borrow” energy and fly for a short time (the argument of Wick, 1938). Since the photon has mass 0, there is no lower bound on the energy that must be borrowed, and it reaches arbitrarily far (range ∞; the Coulomb force goes as 1/r²). The π meson must borrow 140 MeV, so it hardly reaches farther than 1.4 fm (the same conclusion as Yukawa’s argument in §3.1). The W boson borrows 80 GeV, so its range is 2.5×10−18 m. The formula of §3.1, “the mass of the carrier sets the range of the force,” is a mechanism common to all forces that exchange a carrier, including the electromagnetic force (mass 0 → infinite range). The color force alone is an exception: although the gluon has mass 0, the range does not become infinite, because the gluons interact with one another and bundle the lines of force into a thin string (§4.2), and that string breaks at ≈1.5 fm (§4.4). ▶ Simulator ④: photon exchange

9.2 The Fourier transform of the propagator gives the Coulomb potential

When “photon exchange” is turned into a calculation, the amplitude for the exchange of a single carrier with momentum ħq (the propagator) has the form 1/(q² + μ²), and the interaction energy between two particles is its Fourier transform:

(25) V~(q)= −g2q2+μ2 ⟹ V(r)= ∫d3q(2π)3 eiq·r V~(q) =−g24πe−μrr, μ=mcℏ

For μ → 0 (the photon), V = −g²/4πr: reading g² → e²/ε0, charges of opposite sign feel the Coulomb attraction −e²/4πε0r, and charges of the same sign feel repulsion. “How many photons are being exchanged?”: the electrostatic field contains innumerable virtual photons of small momentum (the infrared region), so their number cannot be defined. What is meaningful is the perturbative hierarchy: exchange of one photon (lowest order) gives the Coulomb force, while exchange of two or more photons or the momentary creation of electron–positron pairs (vacuum polarization) gives corrections that are each smaller by a factor of 1/137, and these corrections are what is measured as the Lamb shift of §6.3 and as g − 2. The classical “field” is the appearance of this innumerable collection of virtual photons taken as a whole.

9.3 Whether the force is attractive or repulsive is set by the spin of the carrier

Classically it is sometimes explained that “if two people throw balls to each other, they recoil away from each other (repulsion),” but attraction does not come out of this picture. Correctly, it is determined by the spin of the carrier and the sign of the charges: exchange of spin 1 (photon, gluon) gives repulsion between like charges and attraction between unlike charges, whereas exchange of spin 0 (σ meson) or spin 2 (graviton) gives attraction even between like charges (the overall sign of Eq. (25) changes with the spin of the carrier). That is why gravity, whose charges cannot cancel, is always attractive and adds up, while the electromagnetic force disappears once matter becomes neutral (§7.3). The π meson is a pseudoscalar (spin 0, negative parity), and the one-pion-exchange potential carries spin and isospin operators:

(26) Vπ(r)= f24πmπc23 (τ1·τ2) [(σ1·σ2)Y(x)+S12T(x)], Y=e−xx, x=mπcrℏ, f24π≈0.075

(S12 is the tensor operator, and T(x) = (1 + 3/x + 3/x²)Y. This is the form of the one-pion-exchange term of Argonne v18 [Wiringa et al. 1995].) The sign of the central part is set by (τ1·τ2)(σ1·σ2)/3: for the deuteron (T = 0, S = 1) it is (−3)(+1)/3 = −1, attractive; for np, nn, and pp in ¹S₀ (T = 1, S = 0) it is (+1)(−3)/3 = −1, also attractive; for T = 1, S = 1 it is a repulsion of +1/3, and for T = 0, S = 0 a strong repulsion of +3. That “the deuteron is bound only in the triplet,” as stated in §3.5, was the result of the tensor force S12T (which acts only in the triplet) being added to this central force.

9.4 Two meanings of “exchange” — exchanging particles and swapping identical particles

The “exchange” so far has meant (A) throwing carrier particles back and forth. The “exchange” in the “exchange energy” of many-electron theory is a different thing and refers to the operation of (B) swapping the coordinates of indistinguishable identical particles. The two-electron wave function must satisfy the Pauli antisymmetry Ψ(1,2) = −Ψ(2,1); if the spins are parallel, the spatial part is antisymmetric, that is, it vanishes at r1 = r2. Electrons of the same spin cannot approach one another — the expectation value of the Coulomb repulsion decreases by the amount of this “Fermi hole,” and that decrease is the exchange energy. The relation between the two “exchanges”: drawn as Feynman diagrams, both the Hartree term and the Fock term are the exchange of the same single photon. The difference is whether the labels of the two outgoing electron lines are swapped (the direct diagram and the exchange diagram). In other words, the exchange energy of (B) is the “swap term” that Pauli antisymmetry produces when the photon exchange of (A) is applied to “identical particles”; as the origin of the force, both are photon exchange. Note the sign as well: for the Coulomb repulsion the Fock term is negative (stabilizing), but that is because it “avoids the repulsion.” For an attractive interaction the sign of the exchange term would be reversed.

9.5 The “exchange force” of the nuclear force — a third kind of “exchange”

The answer to the question “does exchanging π mesons stabilize atomic nuclei?” is “yes, in the sense of (A).” It is the attractive potential produced by π exchange (−105 MeV from one-pion exchange for ⁴He in §3.4) that binds the nucleons, and this is a separate matter from the exchange energy of (B) due to swapping identical fermions (between nucleons too there is a Fock term of type (B), which accounts for part of the −136 MeV in nuclear Hartree–Fock calculations, but it is not the main actor). However, the nuclear force has the character of an “exchange force” in yet another sense. Before Yukawa, Heisenberg (1932) likened the force binding the neutron and the proton to the covalent bond of the hydrogen molecular ion H2+ (two protons bond by “exchanging” an electron), and formulated it as an “exchange force” in which the proton and the neutron exchange charge (Majorana 1933 used exchange of coordinates). That the name tags of the proton and the neutron are swapped by the exchange of a charged π meson (the nucleus view of simulator ①) is exactly this, and τ1·τ2 = 2Pτ − 1 (the isospin exchange operator) and σ1·σ2 = 2Pσ − 1 (the spin exchange operator) in Eq. (26) are the modern version of Heisenberg’s exchange force. Through the antisymmetry PrPσPτ = −1 this is tied to the exchange of coordinates Pr, and yields the property of the nuclear force that it is “attractive for pairs with even L, weak for odd L” (one of the reasons the nucleus saturates and has a constant density).

11. The Mechanics of a Vibrating String — From the Nambu–Goto Action to the Mass Formula and the Classification of Particles

“Is there an equation that describes the properties of a vibrating string?” — there is. A relativistic string is a field theory on a two-dimensional worldsheet (τ, σ), whose equation of motion is the wave equation, and which becomes infinitely many harmonic oscillators when quantized. If you know the |n⟩ and En = (n + ½)ħω of a harmonic oscillator, the spectrum of the string is just the sum of them. Here we proceed, within what can be followed at the master’s-course level, through action → wave equation → mode expansion → mass formula → classification of particles → hadron strings → minimum length (textbooks: Zwiebach [43], Polchinski [44], Ohta [45]). Below we write ħ = c = 1 and restore the units at the end.

11.1 The action and the equation of motion: a string minimizes the “area of its worldsheet”

Just as the action of a point particle is the length of its worldline, −m∫ds, the action of a string is the area of its worldsheet multiplied by the tension T (Nambu 1970, Goto 1971 [30]). Embedding the worldsheet in spacetime by Xμ(τ, σ) and writing Ẋ = ∂τX and X′ = ∂σX, we have

(32) SNG=−T ∫dτdσ (X˙·X′)2 −X˙2X′2 T=12πα′ , ℓs=α′

α′ is a constant called the “Regge slope,” which fixes the length ℓs of the string and its tension (as we will see in §11.5, α′ ≈ 0.9 GeV−2 for hadron strings and α′ ≈ ℓP² in superstring theory). The square root is awkward to handle, so we rewrite the action in the Polyakov form by introducing an auxiliary worldsheet metric hab. Using the freedom of choosing the worldsheet coordinates (reparametrization) and the freedom of Weyl transformations to fix hab = ηab (conformal gauge), the action becomes that of a free field, and

(33) SP=−T2 ∫dτdσ (X′2−X˙2) ⟹ ∂2Xμ∂τ2 − ∂2Xμ∂σ2 =0 , (X˙±X′)2=0

That is, each point of the string obeys the equation of a wave traveling at the speed of light, and as a remnant of the gauge fixing there are constraints (the Virasoro constraints). The constraints express the facts that “motion along the string is not physical (only transverse vibrations are physical)” and give the relation between energy and momentum (the mass formula). As for boundary conditions, a closed string is periodic, X(τ, σ + 2π) = X(τ, σ), and an open string has either X′ = 0 at the ends (Neumann: the ends move freely) or Xi = constant (Dirichlet: the ends are fixed on a D-brane). That the speed of waves on the string is the speed of light can also be seen from the tension T and the linear mass density T/c², as √(T/μ) = c.

11.4 Vibration patterns and the classification of particles: where do spin, charge, generations, and mass come from?

“The way a string vibrates determines the type of particle” — if we translate the metaphor familiar from documentaries into the language of superstring theory, it has four steps.

(1) Spin is determined by the type of vibration. The massless states of an open superstring are, in the NS sector (bosonic boundary conditions), ψi−1/2|0⟩ = a transverse vector (8 components), and in the R sector (fermionic boundary conditions), the ground state = a spinor (8 components). The former are gauge particles (spin 1) and the latter are fermions (spin ½). A closed string is the product of a left mover and a right mover, so (vector)⊗(vector) = 1 + 28 + 35 = dilaton + B field + graviton (spin 2), and (vector)⊗(spinor) = gravitino (spin 3/2). The ellipse that pulsates vertically and horizontally in the “Graviton” view of the simulator depicts the polarization of a symmetric traceless tensor (the + and × polarizations of gravitational waves).

(2) Charge is determined by where the ends of the string attach. The ends of an open string lie on D-branes (Polchinski 1995 [35]), and on a stack of N coincident branes each end has the Chan–Paton degree of freedom i, j = 1…N, “which brane of the stack it attaches to.” A string with both ends on the same stack is in the adjoint representation of U(N) (N² gauge particles: for 3 branes, 8 gluons + 1), and a string connecting stack a (Na branes) and stack b (Nb branes) is in the bifundamental representation (Na, N̄b):

(39) color stack (3 branes) × weak stack (2 branes)⟹ (3,2)= left-handed quarks weak stack (2 branes) × U(1) brane⟹ (1,2)= left-handed leptons and Higgs

Quarks are strings with ends on both the color stack and the weak stack, and leptons are strings with no end on the color stack — this is the picture of intersecting D-brane models (review by Blumenhagen–Cvetič–Langacker–Shiu [36]). The charges +2/3, −1/3, and −1 come from mixtures of the U(1)s (hypercharge).

(3) The number of generations is the number of times the branes intersect. When brane stacks a and b wind at angles around a six-dimensional compact space (three tori), chiral fermions are localized at the intersection points. The number of intersection points is a topological integer determined by the winding numbers (nr, mr)

(40) Iab= ∏r=13 (narmbr−marnbr) =3 (for the heterotic string on a Calabi–Yau) Ngen=|χ|2

Ibáñez–Marchesano–Rabadán (2001) [37] were the first to construct an intersecting-brane configuration that gives exactly three generations of SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1) and nothing else (no extra fermions). In the older construction that wraps a heterotic string on a Calabi–Yau manifold (Candelas–Horowitz–Strominger–Witten 1985 [38]), the number of generations is half the Euler number χ of the manifold.

(4) The mass is zero in the ground state, and geometry fixes the Yukawa couplings. A fermion living at a brane intersection is massless (N = 0). The hadron string is still alive today as the lines of force of QCD, and string theory (which later led to superstring theory) is what you get by shrinking it to 10−20 of its size and reinterpreting it in 1974 as a theory of gravity. Both satisfy the same formula J ≤ α′M² + 1; the only difference is the value of α′ — 0.9 GeV−2 = (0.19 fm)², or ℓP² = (1.6×10−35 m)².

11.6 The uncertainty relation of the string and the minimum length

For a point particle, by putting in momentum Δp one can see details down to Δx ≈ ħ/Δp without limit (§1.1). For a string, the energy put in stretches the string: in high-energy scattering the size of the string grows in proportion to α′E (Gross–Mende 1988 [47]), and together with Heisenberg’s term

(44) Δx≳ ℏΔp+ α′Δpℏ ⟹Δxmin=2α′=2ℓs (when Δp=ℏ/ℓs)

is the “uncertainty relation of the string” (Veneziano 1986, Amati–Ciafaloni–Veneziano 1989 [48], Konishi–Paffuti–Provero 1990 [49]). Yoneya (1989) [50] proposed a “spacetime uncertainty relation,” ΔTΔX ≳ α′, for the product of the uncertainties of time and space. T-duality says the same thing from another side. The mass formula of a closed string compactified on a circle of radius R is

(45) M2= (nR)2+ (wRα′)2+ 2α′(N+N~−2) Under R↔α′R,n↔w the formula is invariant

Here the momentum mode n (going around the circle) and the winding mode w (winding w times around the circle) are interchanged, and the space of radius R and the space of radius α′/R are physically identical. Therefore a geometry with a radius smaller than ℓs is merely another name for a geometry larger than ℓs. Even without assuming string theory, if the energy E is concentrated within Δx, the Schwarzschild radius 2GE/c⁴ exceeds Δx and a black hole forms, so

(46) Δx≥ℏcE and Δx≥2GEc4 ⟹Δx≥2ℓP , ℓP=ℏGc3= 1.6×10−35m

The two curves of the string (Eq. (44)) and of gravity (Eq. (46)) form the “floor” drawn in the plot on the right of the “Vibrating string” view of the simulator. Whichever road is taken, structure finer than 10−35 m cannot be measured in principle — this is the basis of the intuition that “space may not be continuous but may have a smallest graduation of about the Planck length.” String theory (spacetime arises from the vibrational state of the string called the graviton), matrix models (BFSS 1997 [51]: the spatial coordinates appear as eigenvalues of matrices), and loop quantum gravity (area is quantized in units of ℓP²) are different formulations of this intuition, and all of them await experimental tests.

Summary of this section: the “Schrödinger equation” of the string The equation of motion of the string is the wave equation, Eq. (33), on the worldsheet, and the mode expansion, Eq. (34), turns it into infinitely many harmonic oscillators (with frequencies n times the fundamental). What corresponds to En = (n + ½)ħω of a harmonic oscillator is the level N and the mass formulas, Eqs. (37) and (38), and the sum of the zero-point energies 1 + 2 + 3 + … = −1/12 fixes D = 26 (10 for the superstring). The massless vibrations (N = 0) are the photon, graviton, quarks, and leptons, and their differences lie in the type of vibration (spin), the brane on which the ends attach (charge), and the geometry (generations and mass). For hadrons the same Eq. (43) agrees with experiment at α′ = 0.9 GeV−2, and string theory was born from here.

12. Implementation Notes for the Simulator

  • Motion of the electron (tab ①): the Nelson diffusion of §2.6 is solved by the Euler–Maruyama method. In atomic units (length a0, time ħ/Eh = 24 as) the drift is −Z′r̂ and the diffusion coefficient is 1/2. The stationary distribution is exactly |ψ1s|² (e−2Z′r), and the blue cloud is 1,100 points sampled independently from this distribution. Helium is two 1s electrons with effective nuclear charge Z′ = 27/16 (electron correlation is ignored).
  • Nucleons and quarks: a Nelson process in the harmonic-oscillator ground state (drift −r/b²). Subtracting the center of mass of the N particles at every step makes the one-particle rms radius in the center-of-mass frame √(1 − 1/N)·√(3/2)·b, so b is chosen so that this matches the point-nucleon radius 1.45 fm of ⁴He, 1.97 fm of the deuteron, and 0.6 fm of the quark. Time is slowed down by a factor of 1022 to 1024.
  • π exchange and the p ⇄ n swap: a cartoon of Yukawa’s exchange force. The labels of the two nucleons that passed a π± are swapped, and for π0 they are not. The radius of the nucleon spheres, 0.84 fm, is the actual size (charge radius), and in ⁴He the nucleons look overlapped — this is the correct picture (the nuclear density of 0.16 nucleons/fm³ is about the same as the density inside a single nucleon).
  • Gluon exchange, color swapping, and sea quarks: a cartoon of the flow of color. The actual gluons form a color–anticolor octet, and the lifetime of a sea-quark pair, ≈ ħ/(2mqc²), is also just a guideline from the energy–time uncertainty. The Y-shaped string (the shape in which three lines of color force merge at a junction) is the shape of the baryon string confirmed by lattice QCD.
  • Animation of β decay: the range of the W (2.5×10−3 fm) is exaggerated by a factor of 60. In reality the W is born at a single point inside the nucleon and immediately decays into e + ν, and the electron and the neutrino fly off sharing Q = 0.782 MeV.
  • Curves of the four forces (tab ②): Eq. (23). The nuclear force is Malfliet–Tjon III (np, ³S₁), with the spin and isospin dependence ignored. The color force is the Cornell potential with αs = 0.3 fixed (the running coupling constant is ignored), drawn dashed beyond 1.5 fm, where the string breaks. The weak force is of Yukawa type, αW ħc e−r/rW/r, good only to the order of magnitude. Gravity is αG ħc/r.
  • Electron in vacuum: the Dirac trembling is drawn as circular motion with angular frequency 2mc²/ħ slowed down by a factor of about 1021, and the Welton trembling as a two-dimensional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with correlation time 0.12 s (rms 0.2 ƛC). The spreading of the wave packet is the exact solution of Eq. (22), and the Penning trap is a schematic of the superposition of the axial oscillation (0.2 µm) and the cyclotron motion (11 nm).
  • Ledger (tab ③): the values of the table in §8 laid out as they are on a logarithmic axis.
  • Photon exchange (tab ④): the picture in position space is a cartoon; carriers are emitted at constant intervals, and a carrier beyond the range λ = ħ/mc fades away as e−r/λ. The propagator and the potential are Eq. (25), and the coupling is α = 1/137 for all four pairs. The pair correlation function is g↑↑ of Eq. (27) (the Hartree–Fock free-electron gas, with correlation not included).

The orange arrows are projections of velocity vectors that follow a three-dimensional Gaussian distribution with standard deviation Δv = ħ/2mΔx in each component, and they are replaced smoothly by an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with a correlation time of 0.8 s.
