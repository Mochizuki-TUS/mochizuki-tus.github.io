How Do We Solve Damped and Forced Oscillations? — Wronskian, Differential Operator Method, and Green’s Function

— A mass attached to a spring feels air resistance and starts from x(0) = L, x′(0) = v0. What is it doing a few seconds later? (for first- and second-year undergraduates)

A mass m attached to a spring of spring constant k moves while subject to the air resistance −γdxdt that is proportional to its velocity. The equation of motion is mx¨+γx˙+kx=0. It is only a one-line equation, but the tools used to solve it — superposition, the Wronskian, the characteristic equation, the differential operator method, variation of parameters — can be reused as they are in almost every differential equation that appears from here on. Here we solve it all the way to the end, without skipping a single intermediate step.

The road ahead §1–§2 Why “finding two solutions” is enough (the solution space is two-dimensional; the Wronskian).
§3–§4 Why we may substitute eλt into the equation, and why a factor of t appears for a double root (the differential operator method).
§5–§6 Impose the initial conditions and complete the answers for the three cases.
§7 When we shake the mass from outside (forced oscillation). With the operator method, the particular solution comes out by “just substituting iω for D.”
§8 A formula that solves the problem for any external force (variation of parameters). What appears there is the Green’s function, and it is, as it stands, the gateway to linear response theory.

0. Setup — Writing Down the Equation of Motion

Two forces act on the mass: the restoring force of the spring −kx and the resistive force −γx˙ that opposes the velocity. Both carry a minus sign because they “pull back against the direction of stretching” and “act against the direction of motion.” Putting these into Newton’s equation of motion mx¨=F gives

(1) mx¨ +γx˙ +kx=0 , x(0)=L , x˙(0) =v0

Dividing by m, we introduce two symbols; the equations that follow become much easier to see through.

(2) x¨ +2βx˙ +ω02x=0 , ω0≡km , β≡γ2m

ω0 is the angular frequency when there is no resistance, β is the rate at which the amplitude decreases (it will appear later in the form e−βt). The ratio of these two

(3) ζ≡βω0 =γ2mk

is called the damping ratio. To state the conclusion first, this one number (more precisely, whether it is larger or smaller than 1) alone determines what the motion looks like. There is no need to worry about the three quantities m, k, γ separately.

Get into the habit of checking units γ is in [N·s/m] = [kg/s], β=γ/2m is in [1/s], ω0 is also in [1/s] (rad/s). Therefore ζ is dimensionless. Whenever a dimensionless quantity appears, it is usually the dividing line in the physics.

1. Getting the Big Picture in 30 Seconds

Eq. (2) says “the second derivative of x, the first derivative of x, and x itself add up to zero.” The only function that keeps its form when differentiated is the exponential function eλt. Substituting it, eλt becomes a common factor and can be taken out, giving

(4) (λ2+2βλ +ω02) eλt=0 ⟹ λ±= −β± β2−ω02

The differential equation has turned into an ordinary quadratic equation. From here on, the story splits into three cases depending only on the sign of the quantity under the square root.

Under the rootDamping ratioCharacteristic rootsMotionName
β2<ω02 ζ < 1 Two complex-conjugate roots Decays while oscillating Underdamping (damped oscillation)
β2=ω02 ζ = 1 Double root (only one) Returns without oscillating Critical damping
β2>ω02 ζ > 1 Two distinct real roots Returns slowly Overdamping
Do not stop here If all you know is “we substituted an exponential and it happened to work,” you will run into the wall that only one solution comes out when the roots coincide (for a second-order equation, a single arbitrary constant cannot satisfy two initial conditions). Why the substitution is legitimate, and where the missing solution comes from — we settle both in §2–§4.

2. Why “Finding Two Solutions” Is Enough

2.1 Linearity and superposition

Let us bundle the left-hand side into a single symbol.

ℒ[x]≡ mx¨ +γx˙ +kx

Since differentiation is a linear operation ((ax1 +bx2)′ =ax1′+ bx2′),

(5) ℒ[ax1 +bx2] =aℒ[x1] +bℒ[x2]

Therefore, if x1 and x2 are solutions of Eq. (1), then any sum of constant multiples of them is also a solution (the principle of superposition). Thanks to this one remark, all that is left is the job of finding the “building-block solutions.”

2.2 The solution space is exactly two-dimensional

Let us rewrite Eq. (1) as a first-order system. Setting v=x˙, we obtain

(6) ddt (xv) = ( 01 −ω02 −2β ) (xv)

Since the right-hand side is smooth in (x,v), the existence and uniqueness theorem for solutions applies. In other words, once a starting point (L,v0) is chosen, both the future and the past are determined uniquely. Conversely, the set of all solutions and the set of starting points ℝ2 are in one-to-one correspondence.

So the solution space is exactly two-dimensional. If you find two linearly independent solutions, they form a basis (a fundamental set of solutions), and every other solution can be written as a linear combination of them. This is the justification for “we have found two solutions, so there is no need to look any further.”

2.3 The Wronskian — a test for linear independence

Now, how can we check whether the two solutions we found, x1 and x2, are linearly independent? We want to know whether c1x1 +c2x2=0 holds for all t only when c1=c2=0, but checking this for each t separately is laborious. So let us line up this equation and the equation obtained by differentiating it.

( x1x2 x1′x2′ ) (c1 c2) = (00)

This has a solution other than c1=c2=0 only if the determinant is 0. This determinant is called the Wronskian.

(7) W(t)≡ | x1x2 x1′x2′ | =x1x2′ −x1′x2

W≠0 implies that they are linearly independent. The direction of this implication is easy to get backwards, so remember it together with the reason. If the two solutions are parallel (x2=cx1), then W=x1·cx1′ −x1′·cx1=0 — the terms always cancel. The contrapositive of “if parallel, then W = 0” is “if W ≠ 0, then independent.” However, this alone leaves the question of “at which t we should check.” The next result answers it.

2.4 Abel’s formula — the Wronskian shrinks exponentially

Let us differentiate W. The terms x1x2″ and x1′x2′ appear, but the latter terms cancel each other and vanish.

W′= x1x2″ −x1″x2

Since both are solutions of Eq. (2), we substitute x″= −2βx′ −ω02x. Then the ω02 terms also vanish neatly, giving

(8) W′= −2βW ⟹ W(t)= W(0) e−2βt =W(0) e−(γ/m)t

This is Abel’s formula. Four things can be read off immediately.

  • The exponential function is never zero, so if W is zero somewhere it is zero everywhere, and if it is nonzero somewhere it is nonzero everywhere. Therefore it suffices to check at the single point t = 0.
  • W can be found without knowing what the solutions are. It is determined solely by the coefficient γ/m of the equation.
  • W is, in the phase space (x,v), the area of the parallelogram spanned by the two solution vectors. That area shrinks as e−γt/m — this is the geometric meaning of “dissipation”.
  • In §2.3 we showed only one direction, “if W ≠ 0, then independent,” but for solutions of this equation, the converse also holds. This is because, if W(0)=0 then the two initial vectors (x(0), x′(0)) are parallel, and by the uniqueness in §2.2 the two solutions remain constant multiples of each other forever. In other words, for solutions we have W ≠ 0 ⟺ linearly independent.
When there is no friction (γ=0) Eq. (8) gives W= constant. The area in phase space is conserved — this is precisely Liouville’s theorem, a property that is the starting point of statistical mechanics. Once friction is introduced, that area shrinks exponentially. That “information is being lost” can be seen in a single line of equation. Check it in the figure below.

3. Why We May Substitute eλt into the Equation

Let us give a name to the operation of differentiation itself.

D≡ddt , ℒ=mD2+γD+k ≡f(D)

The benefit of this symbol shows up when it acts on eλt. Deλt=λeλt — that is, the exponential function is an eigenfunction of the differential operator, with eigenvalue λ. Since the same thing happens however many times we differentiate,

(9) f(D) eλt= f(λ) eλt

We simply substitute a number into the polynomial in the operator. Therefore, if we look for solutions of ℒ[x]=0 in the form eλt, the condition is f(λ)=0, that is, the characteristic equation of Eq. (4).

This trick works only for “constant coefficients” For example, when a coefficient contains t, as in x″+tx′=0, D and t do not commute (D(tx)= x+tx′). The reason we can factor it like a polynomial is that all the coefficients are constants.

4. The Differential Operator Method — Where the teλt of a Double Root Comes From

4.1 Factoring the operator

Just as f(λ)= m(λ−λ+) (λ−λ−), the operator itself can be factored in just the same way.

(10) m(D−λ+) (D−λ−) x=0

Expanding it out, we get m[D2 −(λ++λ−)D +λ+λ−], and if we use the relations between the roots and the coefficients λ++λ−= −γ/m, λ+λ−= k/m, we return to Eq. (1). The order of the factors can be exchanged without changing anything (they commute because the coefficients are constant).

4.2 Splitting into two stages and solving one first-order equation at a time

Setting u≡(D−λ−)x, Eq. (10) becomes (D−λ+)u=0. A first-order linear equation can always be solved by multiplying by an integrating factor. Multiplying by e−λt turns the left-hand side into a single derivative:

e−λt (u′−λu) =ddt (e−λtu)

Hence the solution of (D−λ+)u=0 is u=C1 eλ+t. Putting this back into the definition of u, we use the same trick once more.

(11) ddt (e−λ−tx) =C1 e(λ+−λ−)t

All that remains is to integrate the right-hand side. Here the story splits into exactly two cases.

  • When the two roots are different (λ+≠λ−): the right-hand side is a genuine exponential function, so it remains an exponential after integration. x=Aeλ+t +Beλ−t.
  • When there is a double root (λ+=λ−=λ): the exponent on the right-hand side vanishes, leaving a mere constant C1. Integrating a constant produces t, so e−λtx =C1t+C2, that is, x=(C1t +C2) eλt.
The real meaning of “for a double root, add teλt” is this: integrating a constant once made t appear, and nothing more. There is no need to memorize it as a formula. And since this gives us the two arbitrary constants we need, we can satisfy the two initial conditions properly.

4.3 Generalization — the shift theorem

D(eλtu) =eλt (D+λ)u applied repeatedly gives, for any polynomial f,

(12) f(D) [eλt u(t)] =eλt f(D+λ) u(t)

With this, the story of the double root is settled in an instant. In (D−λ)2x=0, putting x=eλtu gives eλtD2u=0, that is, u″=0, so u is a linear function. For an n-fold root, Dnu=0, and u is a polynomial of degree n−1. The same theorem also plays a major role in §7, when we obtain the particular solution of the forced oscillation.

5. Imposing the Initial Conditions and Completing the Answers

5.1 Underdamping (ζ < 1) — decaying while oscillating

β2<ω02 means that the quantity under the square root is negative, so

λ±= −β±iωd , ωd≡ ω02−β2

The general solution is Aeλ+t +Beλ−t, but x is a real quantity. Rewriting it with Euler’s formula eiθ=cosθ +isinθ and expressing the result with two real constants, we get

(13) x(t)= e−βt (C1cosωdt +C2sinωdt)

Now we impose the initial conditions. Setting t=0 gives C1=L. Differentiating and then setting t=0 gives −βC1 +ωdC2=v0, and hence

(14) x(t)= e−βt [Lcosωdt +v0+βLωd sinωdt]

Combining the trigonometric terms into one, we can see the envelope (the phase φ is determined by tanφ=v0+βLωdL).

(15) x(t)= Re−βt cos(ωdt−φ) , R=L2+ (v0+βLωd)2
This often appears on exams With resistance, the oscillation becomes “slower.” ωd= ω02−β2 <ω0, so the period becomes longer than without damping. However, if ζ = 0.1, then ωd/ω0 =1−0.01=0.995, which is hardly noticeable in an experiment. It matters only when the damping is strong.

5.2 Critical damping (ζ = 1) — the double-root case

For β=ω0, as we saw in §4.2, the fundamental solutions are e−βt and te−βt. Imposing the initial conditions gives

(16) x(t)= e−βt [L+ (v0+βL) t]

5.3 Overdamping (ζ > 1) — two real roots

Setting s≡β2−ω02, we have λ±= −β±s. Both are negative.

(17) x(t)= A+eλ+t +A−eλ−t , A±= ±v0−λ∓L λ+−λ−

Since |λ+| <|λ−|, after a while the term eλ−t dies out, and only the slower root remains. When γ is large, λ+≈ −k/γ, so the stronger the resistance, the slower the return.

Putting the three cases into a single picture in the complex plane In underdamping, λ±=−β±iωd, so |λ±|2=β2+ωd2=ω02. If we fix m and k and increase only γ from 0, the two roots slide down along a circle of radius ω0, and at ζ = 1 collide on the real axis at −ω0 (a double root), and from there they separate to the left and right along the real axis. The region off the circle is overdamping, and even there the product λ+λ−=ω02 does not change. You can move the roots in tab ② of the simulator.

5.4 The three cases are connected without a break

Splitting into cases makes it look like “three separate worlds,” but that is not so. All three combine into the following single formula.

(18) x(t)= e−βt [L C(t) +(v0+βL) S(t)]

Did you notice that the Wronskian row is the same in all three cases? This is because C(0)=1, S(0)=0, C′(0)=0, S′(0)=1 are the same in every case, so W(0)=1, and Abel’s formula (8) then fixes W=e−2βt (Abel’s formula knows nothing about the case distinction). Of course, if the basis is chosen differently, the constant in front changes — if we take x2= e−βt sinωdt as used in §8.2 (the convention without the factor 1/ωd), then W=ωd e−2βt.

Expanding S(t) in powers of ωd2, we get

— it approaches t. On the overdamped side too, sinhst/s →t. In other words, critical damping is connected as the limit from both sides. It is not that “a special formula is used only for a double root”: as a function of ωd2= ω02−β2, it is a single smooth formula from the start.

6. Reading the Decay in Numbers — Logarithmic Decrement and Q Factor

The underdamped answer, Eq. (15), contains two measurable quantities. They are how fast the amplitude decreases and with what period the mass goes back and forth.

After each round trip (period Td=2π/ωd), the envelope is multiplied by the factor e−βTd. The logarithm of the reciprocal of this factor (the ratio of adjacent peaks) is called the logarithmic decrement.

(19) Λ≡ln x(t) x(t+Td) =βTd= 2πβωd =2πζ 1−ζ2

This is the standard way to measure ζ in an experiment. Just by taking the logarithm of the ratio of the heights of two adjacent peaks, the value of γ is obtained.

Another quantity, used most often in both engineering and physics, is the Q factor.

(20) Q≡ω02β =mkγ =12ζ

Since the energy is the square of the amplitude, E∝e−2βt, the fraction lost in one period is 2βTd≈ 2π/Q. That is,

Q≈2π× stored energy energy lost in one period

— this is what the Q factor means. “Large Q = rings well = sounds for a long time.” As we will see in §7, the same Q also determines the sharpness of the resonance.

“Critical damping is the fastest” holds only under a condition If you require that it never overshoots, the fastest is exactly critical damping. This is because, among the non-oscillating solutions, the rate of decay e−|λ+|t is fastest at β=ω0. On the other hand, if the criterion is “settling to within 5 % of the original value is enough”, a slight overshoot is allowed, so a slight underdamping with ζ ≈ 0.7 gives the shortest time (about 0.6 times the time for critical damping). This is why ζ ≈ 0.7 is often chosen in control engineering. This value is very close to the one that appears in §7, the point where the resonance peak disappears, ζ=1/2, but it is a separate condition with a different origin (the ζ that minimizes the settling time shifts a little depending on the initial conditions and on how the “5 %” criterion is chosen). The settling time can be checked in tab ② of the simulator, and the resonance peak in tab ④ of the simulator. By either criterion, overdamping is slower than critical damping.

7. Forced Oscillation — Shaking the Mass from Outside

This time we put an external force on the right-hand side.

(21) mx¨ +γx˙ +kx= F0cosωt

The general solution of an inhomogeneous equation is the homogeneous solution (complementary function) + a particular solution. The homogeneous solution found in §5 can be used as it is, so all we need is to find a single particular solution.

7.1 Why use complex numbers?

cosωt= Reeiωt, so instead we solve

f(D)z= F0eiωt

Then we take the real part at the end (since ℒ has real coefficients, ℒ[Rez] =Reℒ[z], that is, taking the real part commutes with differentiation). With trigonometric functions we would have to compare the coefficients of the “cos terms” and the “sin terms” separately, but with exponentials the eigenfunction property of Eq. (9) can be used as it is. This is the one and only reason for going complex.

7.2 Just “substitute iω for D”

Let us use Eq. (9) as it is. Since f(D) eiωt= f(iω) eiωt, as long as f(iω)≠0, we have

(22) zp= 1f(D) F0eiωt = F0eiωt f(iω) = F0eiωt k−mω2+iγω

The differential equation has become a single complex division. The notation 1/f(D) (the inverse operator) means “the function that, when f(D) is applied to both sides, returns to the original,” and Eq. (9) guarantees this.

Writing the denominator in the polar form k−mω2+iγω =ρeiδ, we obtain zp= (F0/ρ) ei(ωt−δ). Taking the real part,

(23) xp(t)= A cos(ωt−δ) , A= F0/m (ω02−ω2)2 +(2βω)2 , tanδ= 2βω ω02−ω2
The phase lag δ runs from 0 to π Shaken slowly (ω≪ω0), δ≈0: the spring follows the hand (the spring dominates).
Exactly at resonance (ω=ω0), δ=π/2: this is because the real part of the denominator vanishes, and it is independent of the value of γ. Then the force and the velocity are in phase, and the energy is absorbed most efficiently.
Shaken fast (ω≫ω0), δ≈π: it moves opposite to the hand (inertia dominates).

7.3 The general solution — the transient always dies out

(24) x(t)= xh(t) ⏟transient solution + Acos(ωt−δ) ⏟steady-state solution

The initial conditions are carried by the transient solution. We set xh(0) =L−Acosδ, xh′(0) =v0−Aωsinδ and then simply put them into the formulas of §5.

For β>0, the real parts of both characteristic roots are negative (for underdamping, xh∝ e−βt), so the transient solution always dies out, whatever initial condition it starts from. The time constant is 1/β — but this is for ζ ≤ 1; in overdamping, the slow root of §5.3 λ+=−β+s determines it (when γ is large, λ+≈−k/γ, so the time constant actually becomes longer). In other words, after enough time has passed, the mass “forgets where it started” and oscillates only at the frequency ω of the external force. Thanks to this, we can say in an experiment that we are “measuring the steady state.”

7.4 Resonance — there is not just one “location of the peak”

Setting the derivative of A in Eq. (23) with respect to ω equal to 0, we find that the quantity in the denominator (ω02−ω2)2 +4β2ω2 must be minimal, which gives

(25) ωr= ω02−2β2 , Amax= F0γωd

If the quantity under the square root is not positive, there is no peak. The condition for it to be positive is ζ<1/2 ≈0.707. On the other hand, the velocity amplitude ωA and the peak of the absorbed power lie, independently of β, exactly at ω0.

(26) P¯(ω) =⟨Fx˙⟩ =12γω2A2 , P¯max= F022γ (ω=ω0)

Let us find the full width at half maximum of this peak (Lorentzian type). P¯ drops to half when |ω02−ω2| =2βω, and the difference between the two solutions is exactly

(27) Δω=2β= γm= ω0Q and A(ω0) A(0) =Q

Q simultaneously determines “how much the response is amplified” and “how narrow the peak is.” Reading a lifetime from the linewidth of a spectrum in spectroscopy, and reading a bandwidth from the Q of a circuit — all of it comes from this equation.

7.5 Resonance without friction — a double root appears again

If γ=0 and ω=ω0, then f(iω0)=0, so Eq. (22) cannot be used. This is because the external force has taken the same form as the homogeneous solution. Using the shift theorem of §4.3, the rule for this case also follows immediately. If f(λ)=0 and f′(λ)≠0, then

(28) 1f(D) eλt= teλt f′(λ)

Substituting f(λ)= mλ2+k, f′=2mλ, and λ=iω0, and taking the real part, we obtain

(29) xp(t)= F02mω0 t sinω0t

The amplitude grows in proportion to t without limit. Here again the same thing happens: “when they coincide, one extra t appears.”

If ω is shifted slightly from ω0, we get beats instead. For γ=0, x(0)= x˙(0)=0 the solution is

(30) x(t)= 2F0/m ω02−ω2 sin(ω0−ω)t2 sin(ω0+ω)t2

This is a fast oscillation at (ω0+ω)/2 wrapped in a slowly varying envelope at (ω0−ω)/2. In the limit ω→ω0, sinε≈ε reproduces Eq. (29) — resonance was a “beat with an infinitely long period” after all.

8. Solving for Any External Force — Variation of Parameters and the Green’s Function

What do we do when the external force is not cosωt, but something like “a single tap” or “a constant force applied from partway through”? Here the Wronskian appears once more, and in a decisive form. From here on we write the external force as f(t) — the same letter as the characteristic polynomial f(D) of §3, but a different thing (it corresponds to F0cosωt in Eq. (21)).

8.1 Variation of parameters

Suppose we have the fundamental solutions x1, x2 of the homogeneous equation. We look for a particular solution in a form where the constants are promoted to functions.

xp= u1(t)x1 +u2(t)x2

There are two unknown functions, but only one equation. Because of the surplus, we are free to impose one convenient condition of our own choosing. If we require u1′x1 +u2′x2=0 (with this choice, xp′ contains no u′ terms, so differentiating a second time gives no u″ either), and substitute into the original equation using ℒ[x1] =ℒ[x2]=0, what remains is

{ u1′x1 +u2′x2=0 u1′x1′ +u2′x2′ =f(t)/m

This is a plain system of simultaneous linear equations for u1′ and u2′. Solving by Cramer’s rule, the determinant of the coefficients is exactly the Wronskian W:

(31) u1′= − x2fmW , u2′= x1fmW

Integrating and substituting, we obtain a particular-solution formula that works for any external force.

(32) xp(t)= ∫0t x1(t′) x2(t) −x1(t) x2(t′) mW(t′) f(t′) dt′
The Wronskian is not merely “a tool for testing linear independence.” It makes its real appearance as the denominator of the formula for solving the inhomogeneous equation. W≠0 — that is, having two proper fundamental solutions in hand — is what matters here.

8.2 The Green’s function — “the response to a single tap”

We put the fundamental solutions for underdamping x1= e−βt cosωdt, x2= e−βt sinωdt and the Wronskian obtained from Abel’s formula W(t′)= ωd e−2βt′ into Eq. (32). The numerator combines via the addition theorem into e−β(t+t′) sinωd (t−t′) so

(33) xp(t)= ∫0t G(t−t′) f(t′) dt′ , G(τ)= e−βτ sinωdτ mωd (τ≥0)

We call this G the Green’s function (impulse response). Let us check three of its properties.

  • G(0)=0, G′(0)= 1/m — it is exactly the response when an impulse of 1 is given to a mass at rest (mΔv=1).
  • For τ<0, it is 0. It does not move before being tapped — this is causality.
  • It depends only on the time difference τ=t−t′. This is because the system does not depend on time (time-translation symmetry).

Eq. (33) is a convolution integral. It is the principle of superposition itself: “cut the external force into fine impulsive kicks, and add up the response G to each.”

8.3 Taking the Fourier transform brings us back to §7

A convolution becomes a plain multiplication under the Fourier transform. Transforming Eq. (33) with e−iωt (the convention is f∝eiωt) gives

(34) χ(ω)= ∫0∞ G(τ) e−iωτ dτ= 1k−mω2+iγω

— it is exactly the reciprocal of the denominator in Eq. (22). The language of time (the Green’s function) and the language of frequency (the response function) are two sides of the same coin. We call this χ the susceptibility or the response function.

Pole positions and causality The denominator of Eq. (34) vanishes at ω=±ωd+iβ. These are two points at a distance β from the real axis (in the upper half-plane in this convention). To evaluate the inverse transform ∫χeiωtdω for t<0, we close the contour in the lower half-plane, but there is not a single pole there, so the integral is 0 — “it does not move before being tapped” comes out automatically. Under the correspondence causality ⟺ the response function is analytic in a half-plane, the Kramers–Kronig relations (the real and imaginary parts determine each other) follow from here.
(Textbooks that use e−iωt place the poles in the lower half-plane. Either way, the content is the same.)

The absorbed power, Eq. (26), is proportional to −Imχ (in this convention Imχ<0. In textbooks that use e−iωt the sign is reversed, and the power is proportional to +Imχ) — the imaginary part is the dissipation, and the real part is the “storing response.” This structure is carried over exactly as it is into the linear response theory of the dielectric function, electrical conductivity, magnetic susceptibility, and so on. The damped oscillator is the smallest complete example of it.

9. The Same Equation Appears Everywhere

The equations so far were about “a spring and a weight,” but any system that oscillates slightly near a stable equilibrium and decays in proportion to the velocity takes exactly the same form, without exception. Only the names of the variables differ.

What to take away from this one problem ① A linear differential equation with constant coefficients becomes an algebraic problem just by substituting an exponential function. ② The extra factor of t for a double root appears because we integrated a constant; it is not a formula to be memorized. ③ The transient solution dies out, and the steady-state solution remains. So “the behavior after a long time” does not depend on the initial conditions. ④ For the response function χ(ω), the pole positions represent the damping, the distance from the real axis represents the linewidth, and analyticity in a half-plane represents causality. This viewpoint is, as it stands, the gateway to linear response theory.
