1. Introduction — What Is Sintering?

Ceramic parts are usually made from powder. A green body, made by packing powder into a die and compacting it, has particles that merely touch one another lightly, a relative density of only about 50–65 %, and crumbles when pressed with a finger. If this is heated at a temperature below the melting point, the particles stick together, the gaps (pores) shrink, and the body becomes a dense, strong solid — this is sintering.

Why does the body harden even though nothing has melted? And why does its volume shrink? This page answers these two questions. To give the answers first, the former is due to surface energy and the latter to mass transport from the grain boundaries.

In an actual process, however, there is one more important step before sintering. The sequence is “raw materials → calcination (solid-state reaction, formation of a solid solution) → shaping → sintering,” and calcination is the step in which two or more raw powders are mixed and heated to make a powder of the target compound or solid solution. This page also follows the order of the process: §2 first treats calcination (diffusion and solid-solution formation), and §3 onward treats sintering.

Notation a: particle radius. x: neck radius. ρ: radius of curvature of the neck surface (this page does not use this symbol for density; relative density is written Drel). γs: surface energy. γgb: grain-boundary energy. Ω: volume of one atom (one formula unit for a compound). Vm: molar volume. Dv, Db, Ds: self-diffusion coefficients for volume, grain boundary and surface. Dvac: diffusion coefficient of vacancies. Cv0: equilibrium concentration of vacancies beneath a flat surface. δ, δs: thickness of the grain-boundary and surface diffusion layers. η: viscosity. G: grain size. kB: Boltzmann constant. R: gas constant. (In §2, c is used for concentration and x for position or composition, and in §2.4 and §2.7 Ω is used for the interaction parameter. Each is noted within §2.)

2. Calcination and Solid-Solution Formation — Diffusion, Fick’s Laws, and Sintering Aids

The sintering treated from §3 onward is a story of “particles of the same substance sticking together.” Most actual ceramics, however, are first made into a powder of the target compound by mixing two or more raw powders, heating them, and letting them react. Barium titanate BaTiO3 is made from powders of BaCO3 and TiO2, and a Cr-doped alumina ceramic (a solid solution in which Cr3+ is dissolved in Al2O3) is made from powders of Al2O3 and Cr2O3, and so on. The mechanism by which different atoms mix is diffusion, which is the same mechanism that carries matter in the sintering step that follows. In this section we consider, in order, how atoms move (§2.2), how to express that in equations (§2.3, Fick’s laws), whether the mixed result becomes an ordered compound or a solid solution (§2.4), how to tell them apart by X-ray diffraction (§2.5), and how to fire substances that diffuse poorly (§2.6, sintering aids).

2.1 What is calcination?

Calcination is the step in which raw powders are heated at a temperature lower than that of sintering to turn them into a powder of the target compound. Two things mainly happen in calcination.

  • Thermal decomposition: carbonates, hydroxides, nitrates and the like decompose, release gas, and become oxides. Examples: CaCO3 → CaO + CO2, Mg(OH)2 → MgO + H2O.
  • Solid-state reaction: powders of solids react with one another and become a compound. Example: BaCO3 + TiO2 → BaTiO3 + CO2. In practice the reaction does not proceed in a single step like this; a route through intermediate products is known, in which BaTiO3 formed at the contacts reacts further with BaCO3 to form barium orthotitanate Ba2TiO4, and this reacts with the remaining TiO2 to become BaTiO3 (Refs. 26, 27).

A typical process is “weighing and mixing → calcination → grinding → shaping → sintering.” Calcination is separated from sintering so that the gas released by decomposition and the volume changes accompanying the reactions are finished before shaping, and the powder of uniform composition is ground fine before sintering (if gas release or volume change occurred during sintering, it would cause cracks and pores).

A solid-state reaction starts at the contacts between particles and proceeds by diffusion Even if powder A and powder B are mixed, they touch only at the contacts between particles. The reaction occurs there first, and once a layer of product forms at a contact, the reaction cannot proceed unless atoms (ions) diffuse through the product layer. The thicker the layer, the slower it gets, so the speed of the reaction is determined by the diffusion coefficient and the particle size (the Jander equation in §2.7). In the formation of spinel (MgAl2O4) and ferrite (MgFe2O4), it has been confirmed by marker experiments that the oxygen lattice in the product layer hardly moves and the cations diffuse through it in opposite directions (Carter, 1961, Ref. 29). For example, in MgO + Al2O3 → MgAl2O4, this is explained by a model in which three Mg2+ and two Al3+ move in opposite directions to preserve electrical neutrality (the Wagner mechanism, Ref. 28).

2.2 How atoms move — diffusion mechanisms and point defects

Atoms in a crystal vibrate about their lattice sites and occasionally hop to a neighboring site. There are mainly three ways of hopping.

Table 1. Diffusion mechanisms

The main path by which a substitutional atom (an A or B atom occupying a lattice site) moves is the vacancy mechanism. Therefore atoms cannot move if there are no vacancies. How many vacancies are there, then? To state the conclusion first, at a finite temperature vacancies always exist. We derive this from the configurational entropy.

Consider a crystal in which n of the N lattice sites are vacancies. Let ΔGf be the Gibbs energy required to create one vacancy (including the contribution of vibrational entropy). The vacancies can be placed in W = N!/[n!(N − n)!] ways, so the configurational entropy is Sconf = kB ln W. The change in the Gibbs energy of the crystal is

ΔG(n)=nΔGf −kBTln⁡ N!n!(N−n)!

Using Stirling’s formula ln M! ≈ M ln M − M, we obtain ln W ≈ N ln N − n ln n − (N − n) ln(N − n) (the first-order terms vanish because −N + n + (N − n) = 0). Differentiating this with respect to n gives

∂ln⁡W∂n =−ln⁡n−1+ln⁡(N−n)+1 =ln⁡N−nn

At equilibrium ΔG is a minimum, so ∂ΔG/∂n = ΔGf − kBT ln[(N − n)/n] = 0. Using n ≪ N, the site fraction of vacancies is

(1) cv=nN ≈nN−n =exp⁡(−ΔGfkBT)

Creating a vacancy costs energy (ΔGf > 0), but the gain in configurational entropy when a very small number of vacancies are introduced is ∂(kB ln W)/∂n = kB ln[(N − n)/n] → ∞ (n → 0), which always beats the energy cost. This is the reason why “the vacancy concentration never becomes zero at T > 0.” If we assume ΔGf = 1.0 eV, then at T = 1000 K we have kBT = 0.0862 eV, so cv ≈ exp(−11.6) ≈ 9×10−6, and at 300 K it is exp(−38.7) ≈ 2×10−17. As the temperature rises, the number of vacancies increases exponentially, and atoms move more easily. (Cv0, used later in §4, is the number of vacancies per unit volume, and the two are related by cv = Cv0Ω.)

In ionic crystals, the constraint of electrical neutrality applies If only cation vacancies were created in an ionic crystal of type MX (NaCl or MgO), the charge would become unbalanced, so cation vacancies and anion vacancies arise in pairs (Schottky defects). The product of the two concentrations is then fixed at exp(−ΔGS/kBT), and since the concentrations are equal, each is exp(−ΔGS/2kBT) (ΔGS is the formation energy of one pair). Also, when an impurity of different valence (for example Ca2+ in NaCl) is incorporated, a fixed number of vacancies is created to compensate the charge, independent of temperature. For this reason two regions appear: at low temperature the vacancy concentration is determined by the impurity (the extrinsic region), and at high temperature the thermal-equilibrium vacancies of the type in Eq. (1) take over (the intrinsic region).

2.3 Fick’s laws — putting diffusion into equations

Deriving the first law from atomic hops. Consider a simple cubic lattice (lattice constant a0) in which each atom hops Γ times per second, to any of the six directions with equal probability. Consider two adjacent atomic planes perpendicular to the x axis (at positions x and x + a0), and let the numbers of atoms per unit area be n1 = c(x)a0 and n2 = c(x + a0)a0 (c is the number of atoms per unit volume). The number hopping from plane 1 to plane 2 (in the +x direction) is Γn1/6 per second, and in the opposite direction Γn2/6, so the net flux is

J=Γ6(n1−n2) =Γa06[c(x)−c(x+a0)] ≈−Γa026∂c∂x

(In the last step we expanded c(x + a0) ≈ c(x) + a0 ∂c/∂x to first order.) This is Fick’s first law. Fick proposed this form in 1855 by analogy with the law of heat conduction (Ref. 10).

(2) J=−D∂c∂x , D=Γa026

The flow goes from the higher concentration to the lower, and its speed is proportional to the concentration gradient. Each atom merely hops in an arbitrary direction, but more atoms leave from the side of higher concentration, so on average a net flow arises. (For a two-dimensional square lattice there are four hopping directions, so D = Γa02/4, and for one dimension it is Γa02/2.)

Deriving the second law from the continuity equation. Consider a thin slab of cross-sectional area S and thickness Δx. During a time Δt, J(x)SΔt atoms enter through the left face and J(x + Δx)SΔt atoms leave through the right face. Atoms are neither created nor destroyed along the way (there is no reaction), so the increase in the number of atoms in the slab, Δc · SΔx, equals the difference:

(3) ΔcSΔx=[J(x)−J(x+Δx)]SΔt ⟹ ∂c∂t=−∂J∂x

(We divided both sides by SΔxΔt and took Δx, Δt → 0.) Substituting Eq. (2) and assuming that D does not depend on position gives Fick’s second law (the diffusion equation).

(4) ∂c∂t= D∂2c∂x2

Solution for a diffusion couple. Join pure substance A (x < 0) and pure substance B (x > 0) at x = 0 and keep them at a high temperature (a diffusion couple). The initial condition for the concentration c(x, t) of A atoms is c = c0 for x < 0 and c = 0 for x > 0. The sample is assumed to be long enough that the concentration at both ends does not change.

In this problem only D and t are available to set a length scale, and the only combination with the dimension of length is √(Dt). So we guess that the solution is a function c = f(η) of the similarity variable η = x/(2√(Dt)) alone, and substitute it. Since ∂η/∂t = −η/(2t) and ∂η/∂x = 1/(2√(Dt)), we have

∂c∂t=−η2tf′ , ∂2c∂x2=14Dtf″

Putting these into Eq. (4) gives −ηf′/(2t) = f″/(4t); t drops out, leaving an ordinary differential equation in η alone (this confirms that the guess was right).

f″=−2ηf′ ⟹ f′=C1e−η2 ⟹ f=C2+C1π2erf⁡η

(We integrated the first-order equation for f′, df′/f′ = −2η dη, and then integrated again using the error function erf η = (2/√π)∫0η e−u2 du.) The initial condition corresponds to f = c0 at η → −∞ and f = 0 at η → +∞ (because, as t → 0, points with x < 0 go to η → −∞ and points with x > 0 go to η → +∞). From erf(±∞) = ±1 we get C2 − C1√π/2 = c0 and C2 + C1√π/2 = 0. Solving these gives C2 = c0/2 and C1√π/2 = −c0/2. Therefore, using the complementary error function erfc = 1 − erf, we obtain

(5) c(x,t)= c02erfc⁡ (x2Dt)

Because erfc 0 = 1, the concentration at the interface (x = 0) is always c0/2, and the profile spreads sideways in proportion to √(Dt) as time passes. At x = √(Dt), η = 1/2 and c = (c0/2) erfc(0.5) ≈ 0.24 c0. That is, √(Dt) is a measure of “the distance over which atoms mix in during time t”, and this is called the diffusion length. Doubling the distance takes four times as long — the fact that “time is proportional to the square of the distance” is the reason why, in both calcination and sintering, the finer the powder, the faster the process. (In the language of random walks, ⟨x2⟩ = 2Dt in one dimension and ⟨r2⟩ = 6Dt in three dimensions.)

Temperature dependence. In the vacancy mechanism, for an atom to hop, ① there must be a vacancy next to it (probability ∝ cv, Eq. (1)), and ② it must cross the migration barrier ΔGm (probability ∝ exp(−ΔGm/kBT)). With the coordination number z and the atomic vibration frequency ν, Γ ≈ zν cv exp(−ΔGm/kBT), so D = Γa02/6 in Eq. (2) becomes

(6) D=D0exp⁡(−QRT) , Q=NA(ΔHf+ΔHm) , D0=zνa026 exp⁡ΔSf+ΔSmkB

This has the Arrhenius form (we split ΔG = ΔH − TΔS and put the entropy terms into the pre-exponential factor D0). The activation energy Q is the sum of “the enthalpy of creating a vacancy,” ΔHf, and “the enthalpy of hopping into a vacancy,” ΔHm (Ref. 21). In practice, an atom that has just hopped tends to return to the site it has just vacated, so the correlation factor f (a number smaller than 1; about 0.78 for self-diffusion in an fcc lattice, Ref. 19) enters as a multiplier. Also, in the extrinsic region described in the box of §2.2 the vacancy concentration does not depend on temperature, so Q consists only of the migration enthalpy, and the slope of the Arrhenius plot changes.

On the values of diffusion coefficients of oxides The diffusion coefficients of oxides vary by many orders of magnitude depending on the substance, the kind of diffusing ion (cation or oxygen), the temperature, the amount of impurities, and the atmosphere (oxygen partial pressure). Even for the same substance the measurements scatter widely, so this page does not quote specific values. Arrhenius plots of measured values for representative oxides are collected in Chapter 6 of Kingery et al., Introduction to Ceramics (Ref. 7). The values of D used in the exercises are assumed values for calculation practice.

Interdiffusion and the Kirkendall effect. Eq. (5) is the solution for the case in which A and B swap places at the same speed. In reality, the mobilities of A atoms and B atoms (the intrinsic diffusion coefficients DA, DB) differ. The coefficient that appears when the spreading of the concentration profile is expressed by Eq. (4) is called the interdiffusion coefficient D̃, and according to Darken’s analysis (1948, Ref. 18), D̃ = xBDA + xADB (xA, xB are mole fractions). D̃ usually varies with composition, in which case the profile deviates from the form of Eq. (5) (the Boltzmann–Matano method is a way to obtain D̃ from a measured concentration profile).

If DA ≠ DB, the number of atoms carried in one direction across the interface does not balance the number carried in the opposite direction. In the vacancy mechanism, vacancies flow opposite to the net flow of atoms, so atoms are depleted on the side where the fast-diffusing atoms are, and the lattice itself moves. Smigelskas and Kirkendall (1947, Ref. 11) wound fine molybdenum wires as markers around a rectangular bar of α-brass (Cu 70 %–Zn 30 %), electroplated copper over it, and heated it at 785 °C. Because Zn diffused outward faster than Cu, the distance between the markers shrank with time. This is called the Kirkendall effect, and it became strong evidence that diffusion proceeds by the vacancy mechanism (Ref. 25). When the excess vacancies on the side of the fast-diffusing atoms gather, voids (Kirkendall voids) may also form. For the lattice itself to move, vacancies must be able to be created and annihilated at dislocations, grain boundaries, surfaces and so on. The Darken equation above also assumes that vacancies are created and annihilated at such places, so that they are kept at their equilibrium concentration everywhere (the lattice can move). In tab ① of the simulator, where the number of vacancies is constant and the number of lattice sites does not change, this effect does not appear. Because the net flows of A and B are forced to balance, the simulator’s calculation shows, unlike the Darken equation, that the slow atoms limit the speed of mixing (Appendix A.2; it is known that the interdiffusion coefficient of a lattice model in which the number of vacancies is conserved cannot in general be expressed by a simple formula, Ref. 24).

2.4 Ordered compound or solid solution? The regular solution model

When A and B have mixed by diffusion, which state they finally settle into is decided by the competition between energy (which neighbors are favorable) and entropy (the number of ways of arranging them at random). We consider the simplest regular solution model.

A note on symbols Only in §2.4 and §2.7, Ω denotes the interaction parameter (an energy). It is a different quantity from the atomic volume Ω used later in §4 and §7. Also, in Darken’s equation of §2.3 and in §2.4 and §2.5, x denotes the mole fraction (composition) of B (in the diffusion equations of §2.3 it is a position, and in the later §6–§9 it is the neck radius).

Enthalpy of mixing. Suppose that (1 − x)N atoms of A and xN atoms of B are arranged on N lattice sites, and that each lattice site has z nearest-neighbor sites. There are zN/2 nearest-neighbor pairs in all. Let the energy of one pair be εAA for A–A, εBB for B–B, and εAB for A–B. Sign convention: bonding makes the system stable, so every ε is negative, and the smaller the value (the larger the absolute value), the stronger the bond. Assuming that A and B are arranged randomly, the probability that a given pair is A–A is (1 − x)2, that it is B–B is x2, and that it is A–B is 2x(1 − x) (the two cases A–B and B–A), so

Emix=zN2 [(1−x)2εAA+x2εBB +2x(1−x)εAB]

The energy before mixing (the state in which pure A and pure B exist separately) is (zN/2)[(1 − x)εAA + xεBB]. Taking the difference, and using (1 − x)2 − (1 − x) = −x(1 − x) and x2 − x = −x(1 − x), we get

ΔHmix=zN2x(1−x) [2εAB−εAA−εBB]

(Because the volume change in a solid is small, we regarded the energy change as the enthalpy change.) Defining the interaction parameter as

(7) Ω≡εAB−εAA+εBB2 , ΔHmix=zNΩx(1−x)

lets us write the result neatly. Ω < 0 means “an A–B pair is more stable than the average of A–A and B–B” (unlike atoms prefer to be neighbors), Ω > 0 means “like atoms prefer to gather together,” and Ω = 0 means “either is fine” (an ideal solution).

Entropy of mixing. The number of ways of arranging A and B on N lattice sites is W = N!/[(xN)!((1 − x)N)!]. Using Stirling’s formula as in §2.2, ln W ≈ N ln N − xN ln(xN) − (1 − x)N ln((1 − x)N) = −N[x ln x + (1 − x) ln(1 − x)], so per 1 mol (N = NA, NAkB = R),

(8) ΔSmix=−R[xln⁡x+(1−x)ln⁡(1−x)] ≥0

Therefore the Gibbs energy of mixing per mole is

(9) ΔGmix=ΔHmix−TΔSmix =zNAΩx(1−x) +RT[xln⁡x+(1−x)ln⁡(1−x)]

Fig. 2. Gibbs energy of mixing of a regular solution (Eq. (9)). ΔGmix/RT is plotted with W = zNAΩ. For W/RT > 2 the center rises, leaving two minima, and the Gibbs energy is lower if the system separates into two phases (phase separation).

Case Ω > 0: phase separation. Energy dislikes mixing, while entropy favors it. If the curve of ΔGmix has a concave-down part (∂2ΔGmix/∂x2 < 0), the system is more stable when it separates into two phases than as a uniform solid solution. Differentiating Eq. (9) twice gives ∂2ΔGmix/∂x2 = −2zNAΩ + RT/[x(1 − x)], and this first becomes negative at x = 1/2. Setting it equal to 0 there gives the upper temperature at which phase separation occurs (the critical temperature; the upper expression of Eq. (10) below, with R = NAkB). Below the critical temperature the components do not mix but separate into two phases (a miscibility gap), and above it the configurational entropy wins and a solid solution forms at all compositions. In other words, “if Ω > 0 they do not mix” is true only at low temperatures.

Case Ω < 0: ordered compounds and the order–disorder transition. Eq. (9) assumes that A and B are arranged randomly, so for Ω < 0 we get ΔGmix < 0 at all compositions, and ordering does not appear. If A–B neighbors are favorable, an ordered lattice (ordered phase) in which A and B alternate should in fact be even more favorable. We check this using a body-centered cubic (bcc) lattice of composition 1:1 as an example. A bcc lattice can be divided into two sublattices, α (corners) and β (body centers), that are nearest neighbors of each other (z = 8, and every nearest-neighbor pair is an α–β pair). We define the degree of order s so that the occupancy of the α sublattice by A is (1 + s)/2 and that of the β sublattice by A is (1 − s)/2. s = 0 is disorder (random), and s = 1 is perfect order (all α sites are A and all β sites are B; the CsCl type, B2 structure).

Among the zN/2 α–β pairs, the probability that a pair is A–B is ((1 + s)/2)2 + ((1 − s)/2)2 = (1 + s2)/2, and for A–A and B–B it is (1 + s)(1 − s)/4 = (1 − s2)/4 each. The energy is

E(s)=zN2 [1+s22εAB +1−s24(εAA+εBB)] =E(0)+zNΩ4s2

and for Ω < 0 the energy decreases the more the system orders (the larger s becomes). On the other hand, the entropy of the arrangements on each sublattice (N/2 lattice sites each) is, with p = (1 + s)/2, S(s) = −NkB[p ln p + (1 − p) ln(1 − p)], and for small s it can be expanded as S(s) ≈ NkB ln 2 − NkBs2/2 (obtained by expanding ln(1 ± s) up to s2). Hence G(s) − G(0) ≈ N(zΩ/4 + kBT/2)s2. If the quantity in parentheses is negative, that is, T < −zΩ/(2kB), then s ≠ 0 is more stable and an ordered phase appears (the lower expression of Eq. (10) below).

(10) Ω>0: Tc=zΩ2kB (phase separation below this temperature) Ω<0: Tc=z|Ω|2kB (ordered phase below this temperature, disordered solid solution above it)

At low temperatures the system becomes an ordered compound (an ordered lattice) in which A and B are arranged regularly, and at high temperatures the configurational entropy wins and it becomes a solid solution in which A and B are arranged randomly — this is the order–disorder transition. A typical example is β-brass (CuZn): at low temperatures Cu and Zn are arranged regularly in the CsCl type, and above about 740 K (about 470 °C; it varies a little with composition) it becomes a disordered bcc solid solution (Refs. 16, 17). (Eq. (10) is the result of the mean-field approximation (the Bragg–Williams approximation, Ref. 20), which looks only at the average of each site; because it ignores short-range order, it tends to overestimate Tc (Ref. 17). Also, some ionic-crystal compounds (in which cations and anions never exchange places) and strongly bonded intermetallic compounds such as NiAl melt or decompose before they become disordered.)

In summary Ω < 0 (unlike neighbors are favorable): an ordered compound at low temperatures, a disordered solid solution at high temperatures. Ω = 0: an ideal solution (random solid solution) at all compositions and all temperatures. Ω > 0 (like neighbors are favorable): phase separation at low temperatures, a solid solution above the critical temperature.
In every case the basic tendency is that at sufficiently high temperatures entropy wins and the system becomes disordered.

Conditions that favor solid solutions. The Ω of the regular solution model actually arises from the lattice strain due to differences in atomic size, differences in electronic structure, and so on. The Hume-Rothery rules are an empirical summary for substitutional solid solutions of metals: the more of the following are satisfied, the wider the range over which a solid solution forms easily: ① the difference in atomic diameter is within about 15 % (Hume-Rothery et al., 1934, Ref. 23), ② the crystal structures are the same, ③ the electronegativities are close, and ④ the valences are close. For ceramics (ionic crystals) as well, it has been summarized that a solid solution forms more easily the more the following hold: the difference in radius of the ions that replace each other is within about 15 %, the valences are the same (if they differ, defects to compensate the charge are needed), and the end members have the same crystal structure (a necessary condition for a complete solid solution) (Kingery et al., Chapter 4, Ref. 7). All of these are rules of thumb and not strict laws (as for Al2O3–Cr2O3 in Table 2, there are systems that form complete solid solutions at high temperatures even with a difference of around 15 %).

2.5 Telling them apart by X-ray diffraction — superlattice reflections and Vegard’s law

Whether the calcined powder has become an ordered compound, a disordered solid solution, or merely a mixture that has not reacted can be told apart by powder X-ray diffraction. The key is the structure factor (for the derivation see the section “Structure factor” of xrd-derivation-en.html, and for systematic absences see the section “Systematic absences” of the same page).

Take the B2-type (CsCl-type) AB of §2.4 as an example. In the unit cell, A is at (0, 0, 0) and B is at (1/2, 1/2, 1/2); with atomic scattering factors fA and fB, respectively, the structure factor Fhkl = Σj fj exp[2πi(huj + kvj + lwj)] becomes, from eπi(h+k+l) = (−1)h+k+l,

(11) Ordered phase: Fhkl=fA+(−1)h+k+lfB ={ fA+fB(h+k+l is even) fA−fB(h+k+l is odd) Disordered phase: Fhkl=f‾[1+(−1)h+k+l] ,f‾=fA+fB2

In the disordered phase every lattice site is occupied by an “average atom, half A and half B,” f̄, so the structure is the same as a body-centered cubic lattice and the reflections with h + k + l odd (100, 111, 210, etc.) vanish. In the ordered phase they appear with an intensity corresponding to fA − fB ≠ 0. These are called superlattice reflections (ordered-lattice reflections), and one can conclude that if there are superlattice reflections the material is an ordered compound, and if not it is a disordered solid solution. When the order is partial, the structure factor of a superlattice reflection is proportional to the degree of order s and its intensity is proportional to s2. However, if fA and fB are close (the atomic numbers are close), the superlattice reflections become very weak. CuZn (Cu is element 29 and Zn is element 30) is such an example: superlattice reflections are hard to detect with ordinary X-rays, and anomalous scattering with wavelengths near an absorption edge, neutron diffraction, and the like are used.

In a disordered solid solution, on the other hand, the average atomic size changes with composition, so the lattice constant changes continuously and the diffraction peaks shift gradually with composition. Empirically, the lattice constant is nearly proportional to composition (Vegard’s law, Ref. 13).

(12) ass(x)≈(1−x)aA+xaB

Vegard’s law is an approximation, and in real solid solutions it is not uncommon for the lattice constant to deviate upward or downward from the straight line (for example when the difference in ion or atom size is large; for an example that explains the deviation from the size difference, see Ref. 22). Even so, the fact that “the lattice constant moves continuously with composition” is strong evidence that a solid solution has formed. For an unreacted mixture, the peaks of the end members A and B appear separately at their own positions, so the two cases can be distinguished. For example, in a solid solution of MgO and NiO (lattice constants about 4.21 Å and about 4.18 Å, respectively), there is only one set of peaks, between those of the two.

2.6 Substances that diffuse poorly, and sintering aids

As in Eq. (6), the diffusion coefficient depends exponentially on the activation energy Q. Q contains the energy of creating a vacancy and the energy of moving an atom, and both are energies of breaking or distorting bonds, so there is a tendency that the stronger the bonds, the larger Q and the slower the diffusion at the same temperature. Substances with strong bonds also have high melting points, so this can be restated as “the higher the melting point, the slower the diffusion when compared at the same temperature” (when comparing substances with the same crystal structure and bonding type; Ref. 33).

If we therefore compare at the normalized temperature T/Tm, the temperature divided by the melting point Tm (K), there is an empirical rule that substances with the same crystal structure and the same bonding type have roughly the same diffusion coefficients (the Sherby and Simnad equation for metals, and Brown and Ashby, who organized metals and ceramics; Refs. 33, 34). The rule of thumb that solid-state reactions and sintering start to proceed visibly from around half the melting point (in K) (Tammann’s rule; for its relation to the creation of defects see Ref. 35) is based on the same idea. These are, however, empirical rules, and they may not hold between substances with different bonding types or crystal structures, or when the diffusing ions are of different kinds.

Misconception 1: “Heavy elements diffuse poorly” is not the main reason The pre-exponential factor D0 in Eq. (6) contains the atomic vibration frequency ν, and with spring constant ks and mass m, ν ∝ √(ks/m), so the heavier the atom, the fewer hopping attempts it makes. But the mass enters the pre-exponential factor only through a square root. Even if the mass doubles, ν becomes only 1/√2 ≈ 0.71 times as large. On the other hand, a difference of just 10 kJ mol−1 in Q changes the diffusion coefficient at 1500 K by a factor of exp(10000/(8.3145 × 1500)) ≈ 2.2. Experiments that compare the diffusion coefficients of isotopes (the isotope effect) have also confirmed that the difference due to mass is small. What mainly determines how hard it is for atoms to diffuse is the strength of the bonds (the activation energy), not the mass.

Strongly covalent ceramics. Silicon nitride Si3N4 and silicon carbide SiC are strongly covalent and their self-diffusion is very slow, so they are typical examples of substances that are hard to densify by themselves. Even if one tries to gain diffusion by raising the temperature, Si3N4 decomposes without melting (at about 1880 °C in 1 atm of nitrogen), and at high temperatures both tend to undergo coarsening by surface diffusion or evaporation–condensation (non-shrinking mechanisms, treated in detail in §9) before densification, so there is a limit (Riley, 2000; Greskovich and Rosolowski, 1976; Refs. 41, 39). So a small amount of an additive — a sintering aid — is added. There are broadly two ways it works.

  • Forming a low-melting liquid phase (liquid-phase sintering): if an additive that reacts with the raw material to form a eutectic liquid is added, a liquid phase forms at the grain boundaries at the sintering temperature. Diffusion in a liquid is orders of magnitude faster than in a solid, so densification proceeds through the three stages of liquid-phase sintering (rearrangement, dissolution–reprecipitation, and solid-state sintering; Table 6 in §12). “Adding an impurity that lowers the melting point promotes sintering” mainly means this (Refs. 48, 41).
  • Assisting sintering while remaining solid: without forming a liquid phase, it removes the surface oxide film or changes the properties of grain boundaries and surfaces so that the shrinking mechanisms work more easily (example: addition of B and C to SiC, Ref. 38).

2.7 Correspondence with simulator tab ① — lattice Monte Carlo and the Jander equation

The idea of the model. Tab ① of the simulator is a lattice Monte Carlo calculation in which A and B atoms are placed on a two-dimensional square lattice (48 × 28 lattice sites, width × height) together with a small number of vacancies. At the start the left half is pure substance A and the right half is pure substance B (a diffusion couple); because the temperature is finite, point defects (vacancies) are placed in advance on both sides. The fraction of vacancies is set by the temperature at the time of the reset, using the form of Eq. (1), cv = exp(−Ef/kBT). In one trial, one vacancy is chosen, one of its four neighboring lattice sites is chosen at random, and the vacancy attempts to swap places with the atom there (the vacancy mechanism, §2.2). Only nearest-neighbor bonds between atoms are considered, and as in §2.4 the pair energies of A–A, B–B, and A–B are written εAA, εBB, and εAB. If there are no vacancies, each A atom has z bonds, so the number of A–A pairs NAA and the number of A–B pairs NAB satisfy zNA = 2NAA + NAB (and likewise for B), and the total energy is

E=εAANAA+εBBNBB+εABNAB =z2(εAANA+εBBNB) +ΩNAB

which can be written in a form that changes only through Ω of Eq. (7) and the number of A–B pairs (the numbers of atoms NA and NB are constant). When there are vacancies (or sintering aid C), this relation does not hold exactly, but there are few vacancies in the simulator, so this form is used as it is. Therefore, writing the change in the number of A–B pairs caused by a swap as ΔNAB, we take ΔE = ΩΔNAB, and with Em the barrier for the moving atom to migrate, the probability of accepting a swap is

Paccept=exp⁡(−EmkBT) ×min[1,exp⁡(−ΔEkBT)]

(the second factor is the Metropolis method). A swap that lowers the energy is always accepted, and a swap that raises it is accepted with the probability given by the Boltzmann factor. The first factor is the same for the forward and reverse swaps, so detailed balance is preserved, and if the run is continued long enough the configuration approaches thermal equilibrium at temperature T. The first factor, exp(−Em/kBT), corresponds to the Arrhenius factor of Eq. (6): the lower the temperature and the higher the barrier, the harder it is for atoms to move. “Heavy elements and strongly bonded elements diffuse poorly” (§2.6) is expressed by making the barrier Em,B of B larger. Note, however, that Em,B represents the strength of bonding (heavy elements often have large ions and strong bonds, so it is used as a stand-in for that). The decrease in the attempt frequency due to mass (the vibration frequency in the pre-exponential factor, ν ∝ m−1/2) is not included in the model. Even if it were, it would act only through a square root, so the effect is small (Misconception 1 in §2.6). The sintering aid is represented by a schematic model in which Em is lowered only next to an atom C of the aid, so that atoms move more easily (the melting point is lowered locally, making atoms more mobile). It does not reproduce the chemistry of an actual liquid phase. The energy is an educational value in units of ε0 = kB × 1000 K (≈ 0.0862 eV, 8.31 kJ per mole), and the temperature is displayed in K as T = 1000 K × (kBT/ε0). To make the calculation fast, the migration barriers and so on are much smaller than real values (about 1 eV), so the temperature values are only a guide. The unit of time is the “MC step” (on average one trial per vacancy). The values of each parameter, how to read the screen, and the limits of the model are summarized in Appendix A.2.

  • Comparison with Fick’s law. When Ω = 0, the fraction of B averaged over each vertical column, cB(x), approaches the erfc form of Eq. (5) (the fluctuations are large because there are few vacancies). The simulator fits an erfc to this to read off the interdiffusion coefficient D̃, and confirms that the square of the diffusion length, D̃t, grows in proportion to the time t. The fact that mixing is faster when there are more vacancies (the higher the temperature at the time of the reset) corresponds to D ∝ cv in Eq. (6).
  • Ω < 0: regions in which A and B are arranged in a checkerboard pattern (an ordered lattice on the two-dimensional square lattice) appear at low temperatures and disappear at high temperatures (the two-dimensional version of the order–disorder transition of §2.4). Writing A as σ = +1 and B as σ = −1, a neighboring pair that is A–B gives (1 − σiσj)/2 = 1 and a like pair gives 0, so E = constant − (Ω/2)Σσiσj, which has the same form as an Ising model with J = Ω/2 when compared with H = −JΣσiσj (for Ω < 0 this is an antiferromagnet with J < 0, and the checkerboard corresponds to antiferromagnetic order). The square lattice can be divided into two sublattices, and reversing the sign of σ on every site of one sublattice turns J into −J, so the transition temperature is determined by |J| regardless of the sign. From the exact solution of the square-lattice Ising model (Onsager, Ref. 15), at composition 1:1 and with no vacancies the transition temperature is kBTc = 2|J|/ln(1 + √2) = |Ω|/ln(1 + √2) ≈ 1.13|Ω| (in the simulator’s units, for Ω = −1.5ε0, Tc ≈ 1.70 × 1000 K ≈ 1700 K). Putting z = 4 into Eq. (10) of the mean-field approximation gives 2|Ω|, which shows that the mean-field approximation overestimates Tc.
  • Ω > 0: below kBTc ≈ 1.13Ω (for Ω = +1.5ε0, Tc ≈ 1700 K), A and B do not mix completely and clusters of each remain (phase separation; in the Ising model, changing the sign of Ω gives the same Tc). In this case atoms can even gather from the lower-concentration side to the higher-concentration side (uphill diffusion). This is because the true driving force of diffusion is not the concentration gradient but the gradient of the chemical potential, and the simple Fick’s law of Eq. (2) is an approximation for nearly ideal cases such as Ω ≈ 0.

The Jander equation — how long does a powder reaction take? In an actual calcination, we consider a spherical particle of reactant A (radius r) that reacts while surrounded by the other reactant B (Jander, 1927, Ref. 14). Let the thickness of the product layer be y, and assume that the reaction rate is set by the speed at which B diffuses through the product layer (diffusion is the rate-limiting step). Approximation 1: when the layer is thin, regard it as a flat plate; by Fick’s first law (Eq. (2)) the flux through the layer is inversely proportional to the thickness of the layer, so

dydt=k′y ⟹ y2=2k′t

(y dy = k′ dt was integrated from y(0) = 0. k′ is a constant proportional to the diffusion coefficient and the concentration difference at the interface.) This is called the parabolic law. Approximation 2: the reacted fraction α is obtained from the volume of the core that has not yet reacted. The radius of the core is r − y, so

1−α=(4π/3)(r−y)3(4π/3)r3 ⟹ y=r[1−(1−α)1/3]

Substituting this into y2 = 2k′t and setting k = 2k′ gives the Jander equation.

(13) [1−(1−α)1/3]2 =ktr2

The time to reach the same degree of reaction α is t ∝ r2. If the particle size of the raw powder is halved, the calcination time needs to be only 1/4. Also, since k ∝ D, the temperature dependence is of the Arrhenius type of Eq. (6). However, the Jander equation applies the parabolic law of a flat plate to a sphere (Approximation 1) and ignores the difference in volume between product and reactant, so it fits well only in the early stage of the reaction (when α is small). There are also an improved equation that treats the spherical geometry correctly (the Ginstling–Brounshtein equation, Ref. 30) and an equation that also takes into account the volume difference between product and reactant (the Carter equation, Ref. 31) (for a review, see Ref. 32).

3. Driving Force — The Decrease of Surface Energy and Its Magnitude

A powder has an enormous surface area. A surface is a collection of “cut bonds,” so it carries an extra energy γs per unit area. When particles stick together, two surfaces (2γs) are replaced by one grain boundary (γgb). Since γgb is considerably smaller than 2γs (for metals, about 1/3 of γs), the total interfacial energy decreases. The driving force of sintering is this decrease in interfacial energy.

3.1 Estimating the magnitude

Consider the surface energy of 1 mol of powder (volume Vm) made of spherical particles of radius a. One sphere has surface area 4πa2 and volume (4/3)πa3, so the surface area per unit volume is 3/a. The volume of 1 mol is Vm, so the surface energy per mole is (since the energy of the grain boundaries that form is not subtracted, this is an estimate of the upper limit of the driving force)

(14) ΔGsurface≈ γs·3a·Vm

Al2O3 (Vm ≈ 25.6 cm3 mol−1 = 2.56×10−5 m3 mol−1, γs ≈ 1 J m−2) with a = 1 μm, for example, gives

(15) ΔGsurface≈ 1×310−6 ×2.56×10−5 ≈77J mol−1
Misconception 2: Having a driving force does not mean that “left alone, it will sinter” 77 J mol−1 is 3–4 orders of magnitude smaller than the enthalpy of a chemical reaction (several hundred kJ mol−1). (Compared per mole, it is even smaller than the thermal energy at room temperature, RT ≈ 2.5 kJ mol−1. However, as long as sintering lowers the free energy (ΔG < 0), the direction is fixed; whether it proceeds is not decided by comparing with RT.) Even though the direction of the driving force is fixed, diffusion, which moves the matter, is far too slow, so nothing happens at room temperature. That is why we must raise the temperature to make the diffusion coefficient D = D0 exp(−Q/RT) larger by orders of magnitude.
Also, Eq. (14) is proportional to 1/a. If the powder is made 10 times finer, the driving force becomes 10 times larger. This is one reason why nanoparticles sinter easily at low temperatures.

4. Curvature and Chemical Potential — Which Way Does Matter Flow?

We now know that the driving force is that “the surface as a whole wants to decrease.” But locally, where does matter move from and to? What decides this is the curvature of the surface.

4.1 Laplace pressure

A curved surface pushes inward (or pulls) because of surface tension. If the principal radii of curvature are r1 and r2, then

(16) Δp=γs (1r1+1r2)

Here the radius of curvature is taken to be positive if its center lies inside the solid (convex surface) and negative if it lies outside (concave surface). The surface of a sphere of radius a is convex, and Δp = 2γs/a > 0 (compression). On the other hand, the surface of the neck where two particles are joined is saddle-shaped: the principal radius of curvature is x in the circumferential direction (convex, positive) and a concave one (negative) of magnitude ρ in the constricted meridional direction. Putting these into Eq. (16) gives Δp = γs(1/x − 1/ρ); for x ≪ a, ρ is of order x2/a (x2/2a when the centers do not approach and x2/4a when they do; §6.1, §6.2), so ρ ≪ x and the second term dominates. Hence Δp ≈ −γs/ρ < 0 (from here on ρ denotes a positive magnitude); that is, the solid beneath the neck is pulled.

Where is ρ in the figure? Consider the cross section cut by a plane containing the axis joining the centers of the two particles (left panel of Fig. 3). The surface of the neck is a constriction that connects smoothly to the surfaces of the two particles, and when the curve of its cross section is approximated by a circular arc tangent to the two spheres, the radius of that circle is ρ. The center of this circle lies outside the solid (on the pore side), so the radius of curvature in this direction (the meridional direction along the axis) is r2 = −ρ (concave). The other principal direction is the circumferential direction going once around the axis, and its radius of curvature is the distance x from the axis to the neck surface (the center is inside the solid, convex). In the left panel of Fig. 3 and in tabs ② and ④ of the simulator, ρ is shown by the purple dashed circle and its radius line (Appendix A.3, A.5).

4.2 The difference in vacancy concentration

Atoms in a solid move by way of vacancies. Where a tensile stress σ acts, creating one vacancy lets the stress do work corresponding to the volume Ω, so the formation energy of a vacancy is lowered by σΩ. The equilibrium concentration of vacancies is therefore

(17) Cv=Cv0 exp⁡(σΩkBT) ≈Cv0 (1+σΩkBT)

(Under ordinary sintering conditions σΩ/kBT is smaller than 1, so we expanded to first order. When the neck is extremely thin (ρ of a few nm or less), however, the approximation becomes rough.) Beneath the neck σ ≈ γs/ρ (tensile), so there is an excess of vacancies compared with beneath a flat surface.

(18) ΔCv= Cv0γsΩkBTρ
Matter and vacancies flow in opposite directions Vacancies diffuse from beneath the neck (where they are in excess) to the particle surface or the grain boundary (places with few vacancies, or places that can absorb vacancies). If vacancies flow one way, atoms flow the opposite way. Therefore atoms gather toward the neck, and the neck thickens.
In terms of the chemical potential, atoms on a convex surface have a high μ and atoms on a concave surface (the neck) have a low μ. Matter flows from where μ is high to where it is low — just as water flows from a high place to a low place.
The question is where those atoms come from. This is the subject of §9.

6. Geometry of the Two-Sphere Model

The standard tool for treating the initial stage is the model in which two spheres of radius a are in contact. Let the radius of the neck (the circle of the junction) be x, and find the geometric quantities in the approximation x ≪ a.

6.1 When the centers do not approach (the source of matter is the surface)

This is the case where the center-to-center distance of the two spheres stays at 2a and matter piles up on the neck and thickens it. Approximate the surface of the neck by a circular arc of radius ρ. This arc is tangent to the surfaces of the two spheres and passes through the rim of the neck circle. By the Pythagorean theorem, (a + ρ)2 = a2 + (x + ρ)2; expanding gives

(19) 2aρ=x2+2xρ ⟹ ρ=x22(a−x) ≈x22a

The area A of the neck surface is the circumference 2πx times the arc length ≈ πρ, so A ≈ 2π2xρ = π2x3/a, and the volume piled up on the neck is V ≈ πx4/(2a) (all to lowest order in x ≪ a). The upper figures in tabs ② and ④ of the simulator draw the circle exactly as given by the form of Eq. (19) before the approximation, ρ = x2/2(a − x).

6.2 When the centers approach (the source of matter is the grain boundary)

When matter is removed from the grain boundary and moved to the neck, the two spheres sink into each other. Suppose that each center approaches the contact plane by h (half of the center-to-center distance becomes a − h). To express h and ρ in terms of x, we proceed in two steps: first consider a simple geometry, then correct its flaw.

(i) The geometry of simply cutting off two spheres. If we just cut off the overlapping part at the contact plane, the radius of the cross section of the sphere cut at the contact plane is x, so

x2=a2−(a−h)2 =2ah−h2≈2ah ⟹ h≈x22a

However, with this geometry the constriction of the neck does not become round. As in §6.1, consider a circle of radius ρ that is tangent to the surfaces of the two spheres and passes through the point at distance x from the axis. The center of the circle lies on the contact plane at distance x + ρ from the axis, and the center of the sphere lies at distance a − h from the contact plane, so the tangency condition is

(a−h)2+(x+ρ)2 =(a+ρ)2

Expanding both sides gives a2 − 2ah + h2 + x2 + 2xρ + ρ2 = a2 + 2aρ + ρ2; the terms a2 and ρ2 cancel, leaving 2(a − x)ρ = x2 − 2ah + h2, that is,

ρ=x2−2ah+h22(a−x) ≈x22a−h

(Setting h = 0 returns Eq. (19).) Substituting the exact relation for the cut-off, x2 = 2ah − h2, makes the numerator 0, so ρ = 0. In other words, the surfaces of the two spheres meet at the contact plane to form a corner, and the corner of the neck becomes sharp, ρ → 0. Then the Laplace pressure of Eq. (16) would become arbitrarily large, which is not realistic. Moreover, the material of the cut-off part (the overlapping lens) simply vanishes without going anywhere, so the amount of matter is not conserved either.

(ii) Attaching the cut-off material to the outside of the neck. In reality, the matter that leaves the grain boundary attaches to the surface of the neck and rounds off the constriction. We determine h from this balance of matter, working to lowest order in x ≪ a.

  • Volume removed from the grain boundary. For each sphere this is a spherical cap of height h protruding beyond the contact plane, whose volume is πh2(3a − h)/3 ≈ πah2. For the two spheres together, Vout ≈ 2πah2.
  • Volume attached to the neck. To lowest order, the surface of the neck can be regarded as a cylindrical surface at distance x from the axis (the deviation due to the rounding of the constriction is of order ρ, a small higher-order quantity). At height z above the contact plane, the distance rs of the sphere’s surface from the axis is rs2 = a2 − (a − h − z)2 ≈ 2a(h + z). Let z1 be the height at which this equals x2; then z1 = x2/2a − h. For 0 ≤ z ≤ z1, matter is attached in the ring-shaped region inside the cylinder and outside the sphere, whose cross-sectional area is π(x2 − rs2) ≈ π[x2 − 2a(h + z)] = 2πa(z1 − z). Adding both sides of the contact plane, Vin ≈ 2∫0z1 2πa(z1 − z) dz = 2πaz12.
  • Rounding of the constriction. The lowest-order form of the tangency condition above, ρ ≈ x2/2a − h, is exactly z1. That is, ρ ≈ z1 (the height over which the surface of the sphere extends out to distance x from the axis becomes the radius of the circle of the constriction). For h = 0, Vin = 2πa(x2/2a)2 = πx4/2a, which agrees with V in §6.1.

From conservation of matter, Vin = Vout, we get 2πaz12 = 2πah2, that is, z1 = h. Putting in z1 = x2/2a − h gives x2/2a − h = h, so

(20) h≈x24a , ρ≈z1=h≈x24a

The approach of the centers is half of that for simple cutting off, and the radius ρ of the rounded constriction is correspondingly of the same order as h. The treatment based on the two-sphere model of initial-stage sintering (Kingery and Berg, 1955; Coble, 1958; Refs. 36, 50) also widely uses the form ρ ≈ x2/4a and linear shrinkage ΔL/L0 ≈ x2/4a2 when the centers approach (the coefficients depend on how the approximation is made in each reference). The estimate above is a lowest-order approximation, but even when the volume of the neck bounded by the circular arc is integrated accurately and numerically and the conservation of matter is solved, h/(x2/a) is 0.253 at x/a = 0.1 and 0.261 at 0.3, and approaches 1/4 as x/a → 0.

The center-to-center distance shrinks from 2a to 2(a − h), so the linear shrinkage is

(21) ΔLL0= 2h2a= ha≈ x24a2

If the shrinkage is isotropic, the volume shrinkage is 1 − (1 − ΔL/L0)3 (≈ 3ΔL/L0 if ΔL/L0 is small). Numerical example. For x/a = 0.30, h = 0.09a/4 = 0.0225a, the linear shrinkage is 2.25 %, and the volume shrinkage is 1 − (1 − 0.0225)3 = 1 − 0.9340 = 6.60 %. 8.1 Herring’s scaling law — how much faster does a finer powder sinter?

In Eq. (27), the time to reach the same x/a (to reach geometrically similar states) is t ∝ am. Therefore, comparing powders of particle sizes a1 and a2,

(28) t2t1 =(a2a1)m

This is called Herring’s scaling law (1950). If grain boundary diffusion (m = 4) dominates, then going from a particle size of 1 μm to 0.2 μm reduces the required time to (0.2)4 = 1/625. It is because of this exponent that making the raw material finer is so effective for sintering. The same reason explains why nanopowders sometimes sinter at temperatures hundreds of degrees lower.

9. Shrinking and Non-Shrinking Mechanisms [The Crux]

The last column of Table 5 — whether the sample shrinks or not — is the most important part of this page. The criterion is just one: where do the atoms that pile up on the neck come from?

9.1 When the source of matter is the particle surface

In surface diffusion, evaporation–condensation, and volume diffusion from the surface, atoms are taken from the convex surface of the particle (or just beneath it) and carried to the neck. The surface of the particle is shaved off a little, and the neck thickens by that amount — matter is merely moving along the surface. The positions of the centers of the two particles do not change at all.

Therefore, no matter how thick the neck grows, the distance between particles does not shrink, and the volume of the pores hardly changes. The strength goes up (because the neck thickens) but the density does not. This is the real cause of the “it won’t densify” state that one often meets in experiments.

9.2 When the source of matter is the grain boundary

In grain boundary diffusion and volume diffusion from the grain boundary, atoms are removed from the grain boundary where the two particles are in contact and carried to the surface of the neck. If one layer of atoms is removed from the grain boundary plane, the two particles approach each other by that thickness. The center-to-center distance shrinks — that is, the body shrinks and densifies. This is the h in Eqs. (20) and (21).

The rate and the exponents of shrinkage. We check that the exponents do not change even when the coefficient changes in the geometry of §6.2 (ρ ≈ x2/4a). We use the same estimate as in §7 (diffusion distance ρ, cross-sectional area A) for volume diffusion from the grain boundary. The neck surface area is A ≈ 2π2xρ = π2x3/2a, and the volume carried from the grain boundary and attached to the neck is Vout ≈ 2πah2 = πx4/8a from §6.2, so dV/dt = (πx3/2a)(dx/dt) and A/ρ2 = (π2x3/2a)·(16a2/x4) = 8π2a/x. Restated in terms of vacancies The vacancies that become excessive beneath the neck (Eq. (18)) must be annihilated somewhere.
If the vacancies are annihilated at the particle surface — that is, atoms are supplied from the surface — the particle is merely shaved and the particles do not approach.
If the vacancies are annihilated at the grain boundary — the grain boundary is a “sink” that can absorb vacancies — the atomic planes of the grain boundary are eaten away one at a time, so the particles approach.
Densification means letting vacancies be absorbed and annihilated at grain boundaries, and bringing the centers of the particles closer together by that amount. This is why §11 says that “once a pore leaves the grain boundary, it can no longer be eliminated”: the pore has lost this sink.

Thinking about many particles. In a green body, each particle is in contact with many surrounding particles, and the gaps between particles are the pores. If the source of matter is the particle surface, no particle’s center moves, so even as the necks thicken and the particle shapes become rounded and connected, neither the outer shape of the green body nor the total pore volume changes. If the source of matter is the grain boundary, the centers approach one another at every contact, so the whole green body shrinks and the pores become smaller. Pores that were at first connected to one another and open to the outside (open pores) are eventually pinched off here and there and become isolated pores surrounded by particles (closed pores) (the final stage in Table 4). Tab ④ of the simulator draws this difference qualitatively with many circles arranged in two dimensions (Appendix A.5).

Misconception 5: It is not true that “sintering at a high temperature will densify the sample by any mechanism” The activation energy of surface diffusion is generally small, so at low temperatures surface diffusion acts first. This thickens the neck, but at the same time it relaxes the curvature of the neck (makes ρ larger) and uses up the driving force. As a result, when the temperature is raised slowly from a low temperature, much of the driving force is used up before shrinkage begins, and the sample may even become harder to densify. This is one of the reasons why rapid heating (such as SPS) is advantageous.
Note that the “low temperature” here refers to surface diffusion (non-shrinking) during heating. The “lowering the temperature” in §11.2 and §11.3 refers to suppressing grain growth (grain boundary migration) at the holding temperature after densification has begun. Both rest on the same idea: not letting the driving force be used by processes other than densification.

10. Competition Between Densification and Grain Growth

Once the intermediate stage begins, the pores form a tubular network along the grain edges, and the two-sphere model can no longer be used. Coble (1961) regarded the grains as tetrakaidecahedra (14-faced polyhedra) and built a model in which cylindrical pores along the edges shrink by grain boundary diffusion (or volume diffusion). Showing only the result, the rate of change of the relative density Drel takes the form

(29) dDreldt ∝DbδγsΩ kBTG4 (grain boundary diffusion) , ∝DvγsΩ kBTG3 (volume diffusion)

(G is the grain size and δ is the thickness of the grain boundary). The values m = 4 and 3 agree with the particle-size exponents of §8.

Here lies the greatest difficulty of sintering The rate of densification is proportional to G−4. At high temperatures, however, grain growth proceeds at the same time as densification. If the grains double in size, the rate of densification drops to 2−4 = 1/16.
Grain growth is the phenomenon in which grain boundaries move, driven by their curvature, and while pores are attached to the grain boundaries the pores act as a brake (pinning). Therefore in the intermediate stage, depending on how the pores move, growth is slow, close to G3 − G03 ∝ t or G4 − G04 ∝ t, but when the pores decrease and the brake is released, growth accelerates toward the G2 − G02 ∝ t type, which is governed by the motion of the grain boundary itself.
Densification is dragged down by grain growth, and grain growth is braked by the pores — this tug-of-war between the two decides the success or failure of the final stage.

11. Pores Left Behind, and Two-Step Sintering

11.1 A pore can be eliminated as long as it sits on a grain boundary

As we saw in §9.2, densification means letting vacancies be absorbed into grain boundaries, which act as sinks. As long as a pore is on a grain boundary (especially at an edge or corner where three grains meet), the vacancies leaving the pore can be absorbed by the nearby grain boundary and annihilated (the grain boundary acts as a sink).

If grain growth is too fast, however, the moving grain boundary passes by and leaves the pore behind. When the migration speed of the grain boundary exceeds the speed at which a pore can keep up with it (determined by the mobility of the pore itself), the pore breaks away from the grain boundary and is trapped inside the grain (Brook, 1969).

A pore left inside a grain can no longer be eliminated The vacancies of a pore inside a grain have to travel a long way to the grain boundary by volume diffusion. That distance is about the grain size, and the grains keep growing. In a practical time, the pore hardly disappears.
As a result, the relative density levels off at about 90–95 %, and further firing only makes the grains larger. This is fatal for translucent ceramics (which require a relative density of 99.9 % or higher).

11.2 Why does “lowering the temperature” work? — The difference in activation energies

Densification and grain growth are both thermally activated processes, so their rates are proportional to exp(−Qd/RT) and exp(−Qg/RT), respectively. When the temperature is lowered from T1 to T2 (< T1), the rate becomes

(30) k(T2)k(T1) =exp⁡[−QR (1T2−1T1)]

times its original value. The larger Q is, the larger the drop when the temperature is lowered. In some materials the activation energy of grain growth is larger than that of densification (the simulator assumes this), and in that case, lowering the temperature slows grain growth down more. Densification can then be made relatively favorable.

Checking with numbers With Qd = 400 kJ mol−1 and Qg = 560 kJ mol−1 (the values in the simulator), lowering the temperature from 1650 K to 1500 K gives 1/T2 − 1/T1 = 6.06×10−5 K−1. Densification: exp(−48,110 × 6.06×10−5) ≈ 0.054 times, grain growth: exp(−67,350 × 6.06×10−5) ≈ 0.017 times. Grain growth is suppressed about 3.2 times more strongly.

11.3 Two-step sintering

Pushing this idea further gives the two-step sintering of Chen and Wang (2000). The sample is first heated to a high temperature T1 to bring the relative density to about 75 % (a state in which the pores are still pinned to the grain boundaries), and then immediately cooled to a slightly lower temperature T2 and held for a long time. At T2, grain boundary migration almost stops while densification by grain boundary diffusion continues, so densification can be completed with almost no grain growth. It was reported that nanocrystalline Y2O3 could be densified while keeping its grain size at about 60 nm.

Misconception 6: The mechanism of two-step sintering is not only the “difference in Q” The Arrhenius argument of §11.2 is a simplification to get an intuition. Chen and Wang attribute the stopping of grain growth at T2 to the suppression of the motion of triple junctions of the grain boundaries (the lines where three grain boundaries meet). The details of “why it works” are still under research, and the conditions under which it works differ from material to material.

15. Exercises and Solutions

Problem 1 Suppose the diffusion coefficient of a cation in a certain oxide is D = 1.0×10−16 m2 s−1 at 1200 °C. (a) Find the diffusion distance √(Dt) in a 1-hour calcination. (b) How many hours does it take for the diffusion distance to reach 5 μm? (c) From this result, explain the meaning of mixing the raw powders well and making them fine before calcination.

Solution. (a) With t = 3600 s, √(Dt) = √(1.0×10−16 × 3600) = √(3.6×10−13) ≈ 6.0×10−7 m = 0.6 μm. (b) t = x2/D = (5×10−6)2/(1.0×10−16) = 2.5×105 s ≈ 69 hours. When the distance becomes about 8 times larger, the time becomes about 70 times longer (time ∝ distance squared). (c) In 1 hour atoms can mix in less than 1 μm (0.6 μm in (a)), so if the particles of the raw powder are large, or if mixing is insufficient and the same powder forms lumps of several μm or more, the reaction is not finished in a realistic time, and unreacted raw material and compositional unevenness remain. By making the particles fine and bringing the different powders close together, the required diffusion distance itself can be made shorter.

Problem 2 A certain solid-state reaction follows the Jander equation (13), and it took 8 hours to reach a fraction reacted α = 0.3 with a raw powder of particle radius 5 μm. (a) How many hours does it take to reach α = 0.3 at the same temperature if the particle radius is 2.5 μm? (b) What happens if the radius is kept at 5 μm but the temperature is chosen so that the diffusion coefficient becomes 4 times larger?

Solution. (a) For the same α, the left-hand side of Eq. (13) is the same, so kt/r2 is constant and t ∝ r2. t = 8 × (2.5/5)2 = 2 hours. (b) Since k ∝ D, t ∝ 1/D. t = 8/4 = 2 hours. Halving the particle size has the same effect as making the diffusion coefficient 4 times larger. (We regarded α = 0.3 as lying in the early stage of the reaction, where the Jander equation fits well.)

Problem 3 For a 1:1 alloy AB on a bcc lattice (z = 8), consider the cases in which the nearest-neighbor pair energies are (a) εAA = −0.50 eV, εBB = −0.40 eV, εAB = −0.47 eV, and (b) εAA = −0.50 eV, εBB = −0.40 eV, εAB = −0.43 eV. In each case, find Ω of Eq. (7) and judge whether what forms at low temperature is an ordered compound or two separated phases. Also find Tc from Eq. (10) for each case and state what forms above it (kB = 8.617×10−5 eV K−1).

Solution. (εAA + εBB)/2 = −0.45 eV. (a) Ω = −0.47 − (−0.45) = −0.02 eV < 0. Since A–B neighbors are favorable, at low temperature A and B line up alternately to form an ordered compound (CsCl type). Tc = z|Ω|/(2kB) = 8 × 0.02/(2 × 8.617×10−5) ≈ 9.3×102 K. Above this it becomes a disordered solid solution (bcc). (b) Ω = −0.43 − (−0.45) = +0.02 eV > 0. Since like neighbors are favorable, at low temperature the alloy separates into two phases, an A-rich phase and a B-rich phase. Tc = zΩ/(2kB) ≈ 9.3×102 K, and above this the configurational entropy wins and a solid solution forms at all compositions. (Both are mean-field values, and the actual transition temperature is usually somewhat lower. If the compound of (a) is examined by X-ray diffraction, superlattice reflections with h + k + l odd appear below Tc (Eq. (11)).)

Problem 4 For a powder of ZrO2 (Vm = 21.7 cm3 mol−1, γs = 1 J m−2) with particle radius a = 0.1 μm, estimate the driving force of sintering with Eq. (14). Also explain why sintering does not proceed at room temperature even though there is a driving force.

Solution. ΔG ≈ 1 × 3/(10−7) × 2.17×10−5 ≈ 6.5×102 J mol−1. This is smaller than RT = 8.3145 × 298 ≈ 2.5×103 J mol−1 at room temperature, and the ratio becomes smaller still when compared with RT ≈ 1.2×104 J mol−1 at the sintering temperature (1500 K), so the magnitude relative to RT cannot explain why sintering does not proceed at room temperature. The size of the driving force and the speed are separate matters, and the essential reason it does not proceed at room temperature is that the diffusion coefficient is extremely small. In D = D0exp(−Q/RT), Q is several hundred kJ mol−1, and at room temperature atoms can hardly move.

Problem 5 In sintering dominated by grain boundary diffusion, by what factor does the time to reach the same x/a change when the particle size of the raw powder is reduced from 1 μm to 0.1 μm? What about the case dominated by volume diffusion?

Solution. From Herring’s scaling law (Eq. (28)), t2/t1 = (a2/a1)m. For grain boundary diffusion (m = 4), (0.1)4 = 10−4 times (one ten-thousandth). For volume diffusion (m = 3), (0.1)3 = 10−3 times.

Problem 6 In the two-sphere model the neck radius has reached x/a = 0.2. What is the linear shrinkage when the source of matter is the grain boundary, and when it is the surface?

Solution. For the grain boundary, from Eq. (21), ΔL/L0 ≈ (0.2)2/4 = 0.01 (1 %). For the surface, the centers do not approach, so it is 0 %. (If one uses the geometry in which the sphere is simply cut off, h ≈ x2/2a, one gets 2 %, but in this geometry the corner of the neck becomes sharp (ρ → 0) and the amount of matter is not conserved either (“Misconception 3” in §6.2). In any case it is proportional to (x/a)2.)

Problem 7 In the initial-stage sintering of a certain powder, the time dependence of the neck radius was plotted on log–log axes and the slope was 1/5. Can the dominant mechanism be identified from this information alone? If not, what should be measured in addition?

Solution. It cannot be identified. A slope of 1/5 (n = 5) suggests volume diffusion, but in the arrangement of Table 5, n = 5 whether the source of matter is the surface or the grain boundary. Measure the shrinkage (dimensional change or density) at the same time, and judge it to be volume diffusion from the grain boundary if the sample shrinks, and volume diffusion from the surface if it does not shrink. Furthermore, changing the temperature to obtain the activation energy and comparing it with literature values of the volume diffusion coefficient gives supporting evidence.

Problem 8 In the intermediate stage the grain size became 3 times larger. If the Coble model with grain boundary diffusion as the rate-limiting step applies, by what factor does the rate of densification change?

Solution. From Eq. (29), the rate of densification ∝ G−4, so 3−4 = 1/81. This shows how strongly grain growth hinders densification.

Problem 9 The relative density of a certain sintered body leveled off at 95 %, and holding it longer only made the grain size larger. Explain what is happening and give three countermeasures.

Solution. Grain growth was too fast, the moving grain boundaries left the pores behind, and the pores were trapped inside the grains (§11.1). The pores inside the grains were cut off from the grain boundaries, which are the sinks for vacancies, so they do not disappear in a practical time.
Countermeasures (any three will do): ① lower the firing temperature (when the Q of grain growth is larger than that of densification), ② add a grain-growth inhibitor (for example, MgO added to Al2O3), ③ use two-step sintering, ④ speed up densification by pressure-assisted sintering (HP, HIP, SPS), ⑤ make the raw powder finer and more uniform so that densification is finished before grain growth begins.

Problem 10 Let Qd = 400 kJ mol−1 and Qg = 560 kJ mol−1. By what factors do the rates of densification and grain growth change when the firing temperature is lowered from 1650 K to 1500 K (ignore the T dependence of the pre-exponential factor)? From this result, explain the meaning of firing for a long time at a low temperature.

Solution. 1/1500 − 1/1650 = 6.06×10−5 K−1. From Qd/R = 4.00×105/8.3145 ≈ 4.81×104 K, densification becomes exp(−2.92) ≈ 0.054 times as fast. From Qg/R ≈ 6.74×104 K, grain growth becomes exp(−4.08) ≈ 0.017 times as fast.
Both become slower, but grain growth is suppressed about 3.2 times more strongly. Therefore, firing for a long time at a low temperature makes it possible to reduce the grain growth before the same density is reached, and the trapping of pores is easier to avoid. The price is time (and the energy that goes with it).

Things to try (the numbers are one example of a calculation with the random seed fixed).

  • Becoming a solid solution (Ω = 0). The concentration profile fits erfc well (residual about 0.05), and D̃t grows in proportion to t. Under the default conditions D̃ ≈ 1.7×10−3 a2/MC step, and after 20,000 MC steps the diffusion distance is about 6a. This value agrees well with the value obtained by multiplying about 3.6×10−3, which is the two-dimensional Γa2/4 (§2.3) computed from the number of jumps per atom Γ, by the correlation factor f ≈ 0.467 of the vacancy mechanism on a square lattice (an atom that has just jumped tends to return to its original place, §2.3, Ref. 19; this value agrees with 1/(π − 1) = 0.4669).
  • Becoming ordered compound AB (Ω = −1.5ε0). T = 800 K is lower than Tc ≈ 1700 K, so checkerboard domains grow near the interface (η ≈ 0.5 after 150,000 MC steps). If you then raise T to 2400 K, the checkerboard breaks up, η drops to about 0.2, and the verdict becomes “Solid solution (A–B neighbors favored)” (the order–disorder transition of §2.4; even above the transition temperature short-range order remains, R ≈ 1.2–1.3). Ordering makes atoms harder to move by the amount of the energy gain, so mixing is much slower than for Ω = 0.
  • No mixing (Ω = +1.5ε0). The interface stays sharp, and even after 150,000 MC steps the diffusion distance does not reach 1a (about 0.7a; phase separation). If T is made well above Tc ≈ 1700 K (for example, 2400 K), the atoms mix while keeping a tendency for like atoms to cluster (“for Ω > 0 the atoms do not mix” in §2.4 refers to low temperatures).
  • Raise the temperature to mix, then lower it. If you raise T (for example, to 2400 K) to mix A and B thoroughly and then lower T (for example, to 800 K), the sample becomes almost single-phase. It becomes ordered compound AB for Ω < 0 and a solid solution for Ω ≈ 0. This is because at high temperature the configurational entropy wins and the atoms mix, while at low temperature the system settles into the phase determined by Ω at that moment (§2.4).
  • Making B a heavy, strongly bonded element (Em,B = 1.6ε0). The number of jumps of B falls to about 1/6, and the overall mixing speed also drops (after 20,000 MC steps D̃ is half or less; about 0.46 times for the default seed, and about 0.4 times on average over 8 seeds). Because the number of vacancies is limited, the hard-to-move B obstructs the movement of vacancies, and the slower atoms end up determining the overall speed. This appears to be the opposite of Darken’s equation in §2.3, in which the fast-moving atoms govern interdiffusion, but the premises are different. Darken’s equation applies when vacancies are created and annihilated at dislocations and grain boundaries so that the equilibrium concentration is maintained and the lattice can move. In ①, where the number of vacancies is constant, the flows of A and B have no choice but to balance, so in the calculation one sees a tendency toward the side where the slower atom determines the speed (for example, the form DADB/(xADA + xBDB)) (it is known that the interdiffusion coefficient of such a lattice model cannot in general be expressed by a simple formula, Ref. 24). If the sintering aid C is added here, B can also move near C, so the mixing speed recovers somewhat (D̃t becomes larger by about 30–50 %; however, the scatter depending on the random seed is large, and sometimes it is slower instead).
Limitations of the model of ① (calcination and solid-solution formation) ① The Kirkendall effect does not appear. The number of lattice sites and the number of vacancies are both constant, and there are no places where vacancies are created or annihilated (dislocations, grain boundaries, surfaces), so the number of B atoms that entered the left half and the number of A atoms that left the left half always balance, apart from small deviations in the numbers of vacancies and C on the left and right. Even if Em,B is made large so that DA ≠ DB, the numbers of A and B that crossed the interface are almost the same, and the movement of the lattice itself (§2.3) cannot be represented. What can be seen is only that the slow B holds down the overall mixing speed, and this has different premises from Darken’s equation (§2.3) for the case where vacancies are kept at the equilibrium concentration.
② The compound layer does not become sharp. Since only nearest-neighbor bonds are considered, even a single B atom sitting alone in A has four A–B bonds, just like a B in the checkerboard, and the energy gain is the same. Therefore B dissolves widely into A, and the checkerboard (compound AB) forms where the composition has become about 1:1. To reproduce at the atomic scale how a layer of product of definite composition grows sharply sandwiched between the reactants, as in a real solid-state reaction (the Jander model in the lower figure), interactions with second neighbors and the like are needed.
③ It is a two-dimensional, single-species lattice. It does not represent how, as in an oxide, the cations and anions occupy different sublattices and the cations exchange places within the oxygen framework (the Wagner mechanism of §2.1).
④ The values are educational. The energies (in units of ε0), the temperature, Ef and k are not values of any specific substance (the barriers are smaller than real ones, so the temperature T on the screen is only a guide), and the vacancy fraction is rounded to 0.5–3 % for visibility. The sintering aid is represented as an atom that merely lowers the barrier and is energetically neutral. The lattice Monte Carlo above and the Jander equation below are separate calculations that show the same phenomenon at two scales, and their numbers are not connected to each other.

A.3 ② Driving force of sintering

It draws the two-sphere model, in which two spheres of radius a are joined by a neck (radius x, orange line). The green arrows are the flow of matter, directed from the convex particle surface (high chemical potential μ) to the concave neck (low μ). A curved surface presses the inside (compression) if it is convex and pulls the inside (tension) if it is concave. This is the equation in the controls, the Laplace pressure Δp = γs(1/r1 + 1/r2), the same as Eq. (16) of the main text. The reasoning that the chemical potential is high at a convex surface and low at a concave surface, because the material under a convex surface is compressed and that under a concave surface is pulled, is explained in §4 (especially in the box “Matter and vacancies flow in opposite directions”).

Display of ρ. The purple dashed circles above and below the neck are circles that follow the constriction of the neck (circles tangent to the surfaces of the two spheres), and the purple line drawn from the center of each circle to the surface of the neck is the radius ρ. ρ measures how sharply the constriction bends in a cross section containing the axis of the two particles (the meridional radius of curvature); since the center of the circle lies outside the solid it is concave, and in the Laplace pressure equation it enters as a negative value, r2 = −ρ. The other principal radius of curvature lies in the circumferential direction around the axis and is the neck radius itself, r1 = x (convex, positive). These two notes are at the lower left of the figure. For x ≪ a we have ρ ≪ x, so Δp = γs(1/x − 1/ρ) < 0, and the material beneath the neck is pulled (the box “Where is ρ in the figure?” in §4.1). For example, at x/a = 0.30 the surface area decreases by about 1.8 %, and at x/a = 0.60 by about 4.5 %. How the area is calculated is summarized in A.9.

The thicker the neck, the smaller the surface area and the lower the free energy of the system — this is the driving force of sintering (§3). If you change only a while keeping x/a fixed, the area changes in proportion to a2, but the fractional decrease does not change. Even so, the finer the powder the larger the driving force (proportional to 1/a in Eq. (14)), because the number of particles in the same volume of 1 mol increases in proportion to 1/a3, and the surface area of the whole powder therefore increases in proportion to 1/a.

However, the driving force itself is small: about 77 J mol−1 for an Al2O3 powder with a = 1 μm (Eq. (15)). This is 3–4 orders of magnitude smaller than the enthalpy of a chemical reaction (several hundred kJ mol−1), so the temperature must be raised to make diffusion fast (“Misconception 2” in §3). And for densification, matter must come “from the grain boundary”. This leads to ④ next.

A.4 ③ Neck growth and the exponent n

When you choose mass-transport mechanisms in the controls (the six in Table 5), it draws the neck-growth curve x/a versus time t for each on log–log axes. At first three are selected: surface diffusion, grain boundary diffusion, and volume diffusion from the grain boundary. The marks “shrinks” and “no shrinkage” beside the mechanism names indicate the difference in the source of matter (④), and the colors match Fig. 4 of the main text. The equation in the controls is Eq. (27) of §8, (x/a)n = F(T) t/am, which becomes a straight line of slope 1/n on log–log axes.

  • The smaller n is, the steeper the slope 1/n, and the faster the curve rises with time.
  • In this figure the coefficient F(T) is set to the same value for all mechanisms, so what is being compared is only the shape of the time dependence. Which mechanism is actually faster is determined by the temperature and the respective diffusion coefficients (the “main factor in F(T)” of Table 5). In general, surface diffusion has a small activation energy and so acts first at low temperatures (“Misconception 5” in §9), while grain boundary diffusion and volume diffusion take over at higher temperatures. Evaporation–condensation acts for substances with a high vapor pressure, and viscous flow acts in glasses and liquid-phase sintering.
  • Changing a of “Particle size” (0.2–4.0 μm) shifts the curves sideways. This is because the time to reach the same x/a is proportional to am, which is Herring’s scaling law of §8.1. If a is changed from 1 μm to 0.2 μm, grain boundary diffusion (m = 4) becomes 54 = 625 times faster. This is why making the raw material finer works so well for sintering.
  • If you move t of “Choose the time” (101–104, arbitrary units), the table below the figure shows, for the selected mechanisms, n, m, the source of matter, whether the sample shrinks, the slope 1/n, and the model value of x/a at that time. Where x/a would be 0.8 or more, “—” is displayed and the curve is not drawn either. However, the two-sphere model is usable only up to x/a ≲ 0.3 (A.5).

If you measure the time dependence of x/a experimentally on log–log axes and read n from the slope, you can narrow down the candidate mechanisms. This is the classical method for investigating sintering mechanisms. However, volume diffusion gives n = 5 whether the source of matter is the surface or the grain boundary, so n alone cannot settle the mechanism, and you judge it together with whether the sample shrinks (④) and with the activation energy (“Misconception 4” in §8, Problem 7).

A.5 ④ Shrinking and non-shrinking mechanisms

The upper and lower two-particle figures are two-sphere models with exactly the same neck radius x. The upper one is the case where the source of matter is the particle surface (surface diffusion, evaporation–condensation, volume diffusion from the surface), and the center-to-center distance stays 2a. The lower one is the case where the source of matter is the grain boundary (grain boundary diffusion, volume diffusion from the grain boundary); as the atoms on the grain boundary (red line) move to the neck, the two particles sink into each other by h each, and the center-to-center distance shrinks to 2(a − h). There is only one way to tell them apart: where the matter comes from (§9, Fig. 4). As long as matter is supplied from the surface, the particles do not approach however thick the neck grows, and the pores remain. The strength rises but the density does not — this is the situation often met in experiments.

The “one-line comment” box changes in three ways depending on the result.

  • When the relative density exceeds 99 %: this means that densification was finished before grain growth ran away, with the initial grain size kept small. Translucent ceramics need still more, 99.9 % or higher (§11.1), and to pack them that far one borrows the techniques of ⑦.
  • When pores are left behind: the grain size has exceeded Gc (4 μm in this model), and the moving grain boundary has left the pores behind. A pore inside a grain loses the escape route of the grain boundary, so it cannot be removed in a practical time by lattice diffusion alone. Firing longer only makes the grains larger (§11.1). Lower the temperature or reduce the initial grain size, and look at the countermeasures in ⑥.
  • When densification is still under way: it will proceed if you lengthen the holding time. Raising the temperature makes it proceed faster at first, but grain growth becomes faster still, so the final density can actually drop (under the default conditions, the density after 600 min is about 98 % at 1450 K but about 96 % at 1550 K). This tug-of-war is the essence of sintering, and ⑥ looks at it in detail.

The activation energy of densification is taken as Qd = 400 kJ mol−1 and that of grain growth as Qg = 560 kJ mol−1. Because Q is larger for grain growth, lowering the temperature suppresses grain growth more strongly (the numerical example of §11.2 and Problem 10). This is one of the reasons why countermeasure ② of ⑥ (lowering the temperature) and the two-step sintering of §11.3 work. However, the mechanism of two-step sintering cannot be explained by the difference in Q alone (“Misconception 6” in §11.3).

A pore can be eliminated as long as it sits on a grain boundary If a pore is on a grain boundary, the vacancies that leave the pore can be absorbed by the nearby grain boundary (a sink for vacancies) and annihilated. But if grain growth is fast, the moving grain boundary passes by and leaves the pore behind. A pore trapped inside a grain is far from the grain boundaries that would absorb its vacancies, so it can hardly be eliminated (“Restated in terms of vacancies” in §9.2, §11.1). In the simulator, Brook’s condition (the grain boundary moves faster than the pores can follow) is simplified to whether the grain size has exceeded Gc.

Controls. For a value s, it raises the breakaway grain size to Gc × s and divides the grain-growth coefficient by s. When a small amount of MgO is added to Al2O3 (in Coble’s patent, 1/8–1/4 wt % was said to be preferable, Ref. 4), the motion of the grain boundaries slows (Ref. 43) and pores are less likely to be left behind. Translucent alumina (the arc tube of a high-pressure sodium lamp) was realized by this discovery (Coble’s patent, 1962, Ref. 4) (Table 6). Even under the default conditions, breakaway no longer occurs if you raise the slider to 1.5.

When pores have been left behind, the “one-line comment” gives four countermeasures. ① Add a grain growth inhibitor to raise Gc; ② lower the temperature and make use of the larger Q of grain growth (§11.2); ③ reduce the initial grain size so that densification finishes sooner; ④ speed up densification by pressure-assisted sintering (⑦). This is the same content as the solution to Problem 9 in the main text. Conversely, if the relative density exceeds 99 % with the pores still attached to the grain boundaries, the density has been raised fully before grain growth begins (or while the grain boundaries are kept slow by an inhibitor), and this is exactly the aim when making translucent ceramics. If neither is the case, lengthen the holding time and look for the point at which the grain size exceeds Gc and the density levels off.

A.9 Calculation models and cautions

All the figures and numbers of the simulator are calculated on the spot by the JavaScript of the page; they are not curves prepared in advance. However, models of this kind are “for understanding trends” and do not quantitatively predict the sintering of real materials. The calculation in each tab is as follows.

  • Lattice Monte Carlo and the Jander equation in ①. It uses the acceptance probability of §2.7, and the random numbers are pseudo-random numbers with a fixed seed. The values and the way the verdict is made are as in Table A2 and A.2. In each frame it advances the calculation by as many MC steps as the “Speed” setting, and each time recomputes and draws the concentration profile, the erfc fit, and the order parameter. For the Jander equation it draws α = 1 − (1 − √(kt)/r)3, which is Eq. (13) solved for α (α = 1 if √(kt) ≥ r).
  • Surface area in ②. The neck surface is regarded as the surface obtained by rotating around the axis an arc of radius ρ = x2/2(a − x) that is tangent to the two spheres (the center-to-center distance is 2a). From the surface area 8πa2 of the two spheres, the area of the two spherical caps inside the points of tangency with the arc, 2 × 2πaρ·a/(a + ρ), is subtracted, and the area of the neck surface, 2 × 2πρ[(x + ρ)θ0 − ρa/(a + ρ)] (θ0 = arctan[a/(x + ρ)]), is added. A ≈ 2π2xρ in §6.1 is the lowest-order approximation of this for x ≪ a.
  • Neck growth in ③. Based on the arrangement of the two-sphere model by Kingery et al. (n and m in Table 5), it is computed as (x/a)n = 10−5 t/am (a in μm, t in arbitrary units). The coefficient 10−5 is common to all mechanisms and has no physical meaning (A.4).
  • Shrinkage in ④. It uses h = x2/4a and ΔL/L0 = x2/4a2 of Eqs. (20) and (21) (a = 1). The ρ in the figure is the radius of the circle tangent to the two spheres, with half the center-to-center distance taken as a − h, ρ = (x2 − 2ah + h2)/2(a − x), which is x2/2(a − x) for h = 0, and (x2/2 + h2)/2(a − x) ≈ x2/4(a − x) for h = x2/4a (§6.2, A.5). The figure in ② is drawn by the same function with h = 0, and the magnification is fixed in px per 1 μm (A.3).
  • Schematic of many particles in ④. Solid and pores are separated by the sign of a function that joins each particle’s “distance from the edge of the circle” with a smooth minimum, and painted pixel by pixel. The scaling factor of the radius is tabulated by numerically solving the area condition (constant area fraction for the surface source, constant solid area for the grain-boundary source) at each point of p in steps of 0.05, and linearly interpolated in between. Open and closed pores are counted by pixel connectivity (4 neighbors: up, down, left, right) (A.5).
  • Densification and grain growth in ⑤, ⑥ and ⑦. Eq. (31) of §13 is used as it is. The parameters (Qd, Qg, Gc = 4 μm, φ = 0.03, ψ = 1/[1 + 17.6(1 − Drel)]) and the control of the numerical integration step are also as written in §13.
