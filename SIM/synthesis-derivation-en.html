For the four ways of making inorganic materials (solid-state synthesis, coprecipitation as a solution route, chemical vapor deposition (CVD), and physical vapor deposition (PVD)), it summarizes in what state the raw materials mix, along what path the matter moves, and where the reaction and deposition take place, together with the reaction equations, formulas, units and sources. The backbone of this page The synthesis methods are easier to survey if you compare them not by “whether a liquid is used” but by the length scale over which the raw materials mix and the place where the reaction and deposition occur. Solid-state synthesis makes grains of µm size react at their contacts, so the time required is proportional to the square of the diffusion distance r (§2). Solution synthesis (coprecipitation) mixes the ions in solution before precipitating them, so the diffusion distance inside the precursor is short and a low temperature is enough (§3). In the vapor-phase methods, the raw material is turned into a gas (CVD) or a flow of atoms (PVD), carried to the substrate and made into a film on its surface. In CVD the chemical reaction on the surface plays the leading role, and in PVD the sticking of atoms does (§4, §5).

1. Introduction — How to Compare the Four Methods

There are many ways to make ceramic powders and thin films, but textbooks usually divide them into “solid-state synthesis,” “solution synthesis” and “vapor-phase methods.” What matters here is that the criterion for the division is not “whether a liquid is used.” Ethanol is used in the wet mixing of solid-state synthesis, but ethanol is a dispersion medium that disperses the powder and breaks up lumps; it does not dissolve the raw materials. In solution synthesis (coprecipitation), the raw materials are dissolved in water, mixed in the form of ions, and then the precursor is precipitated in the solution.

2. Solid-State Synthesis — Wet Mixing, Drying, Calcination and Diffusion-Controlled Reaction

2.1 The procedure and the purpose of each step

  1. Weighing: weigh out the raw powders so as to give the composition of the target material (the feed ratio).
  2. Wet mixing: put the powders and ethanol in a mortar and grind them together. Lumps of particles are broken up, the two kinds of particles intermingle, and the particles also become finer. No reaction occurs at this stage (the target compound is not formed the instant the powders are mixed).
  3. Drying: transfer the mixture to weighing paper and hold it in a 60 °C drying oven for 30 minutes or more to evaporate the ethanol (the YBCO procedure in lab manual §6.1; drying with an infrared lamp is also possible). The ethanol does not take part in the reaction and leaves entirely. The arrangement of the mixed particles remains as it was.
  4. Calcination: heat in an electric furnace. The reaction starts at the contacts between particles, and once a layer of product has formed, the raw materials can meet only by diffusing through that layer. The carbonate decomposes and CO₂ escapes. The atmosphere is air (student experiments are done in air; in research, a tube furnace with flowing N₂ or a glove box filled with Ar is sometimes used. Prof. Mochizuki’s instruction (2026-09-25)).

After this, in actual synthesis the powder is reground, pressed into shape and sintered (for the difference in purpose between calcination and sintering, see Sintering ① and YBCO synthesis). Tab ① of this simulator deals only with the part up to calcination, “mixing the powders and letting them react.”

2.2 The reaction equation and the mass balance

BaCO3+6Fe2O3→BaFe12O19+CO2↑ (1)

The molar masses (calculated from the 2021 IUPAC atomic weights) are BaCO₃ 197.335, Fe₂O₃ 159.687, BaFe₁₂O₁₉ 1111.448 and CO₂ 44.009 g/mol. The left-hand side, 197.335 + 6 × 159.687 = 1155.457 g, and the right-hand side, 1111.448 + 44.009 = 1155.457 g, agree, and when the reaction goes to completion in the stoichiometric ratio (Fe/Ba = 12), the theoretical mass loss is 44.009 / 1155.457 = 3.81 %. For a total mass of 5.00 g, the weighed amounts are 0.854 g of BaCO₃ and 4.146 g of Fe₂O₃.

If the feed ratio R = Fe/Ba deviates, the reaction stops when the limiting raw material is used up, and the surplus raw material remains. In the simulator, α is the fraction of BaCO₃ that has reacted, and the remaining raw materials, the products and the CO₂ released are calculated from the conservation of the elements. For R ≥ 12 only Eq. (1) proceeds (nBaM = αnBa), and the surplus Fe₂O₃ remains. For R < 12, the surplus Ba is assumed to form a secondary phase through BaCO₃ + Fe₂O₃ → BaFe₂O₄ + CO₂, and from the conservation of Ba (x + y = αnBa) and of Fe (12x + 2y = αRnBa) the amounts of BaFe₁₂O₁₉ and BaFe₂O₄ are allotted as x = α(R − 2)nBa/10 and y = α(12 − R)nBa/10 (BaFe₂O₄ is 313.013 g/mol). In either case the CO₂ is αnBa. For example, when a total of 5.00 g reacts completely, 0.146 g of BaFe₂O₄ remains at R = 11 and 0.314 g of BaFe₂O₄ at R = 10 (BaFe₁₂O₁₉ 4.465 g, mass loss 4.42 %), and 0.323 g of α-Fe₂O₃ remains at R = 13. In the actual pathway BaFe₂O₄ forms first as an intermediate phase and then reacts with Fe₂O₃ to give BaFe₁₂O₁₉, but this intermediate stage is omitted (only the Ba left over is counted as a secondary phase at the end). For this reason the release of CO₂ (the mass loss) is also treated schematically as proportional to α; in reality most of the CO₂ escapes at the stage where BaFe₂O₄ forms (700–800 °C), and the mass loss proceeds faster than α.

However, “exactly the stoichiometric 12 is the best” is not necessarily true. Even in solid-state synthesis, α-Fe₂O₃ tends to remain at Fe/Ba ≥ 12, and an optimum near 11.7 has been reported, and at Fe/Ba < 11 BaFe₂O₄ is said to coexist below 1000 °C (the review in Ref. 20 and the papers cited there). For this reason the simulator labels a slight Ba excess at Fe/Ba = 11–12 as “a composition that is also used in practice (the surplus remains as a small amount of secondary phase),” and for the failure-example presets it uses Fe/Ba = 10 (a large excess of Ba, leaving the secondary phase BaFe₂O₄) and 13 (α-Fe₂O₃ remains). The feed ratios of Fe/Ba = 10–11.5 often used in the coprecipitation of ② are thought to compensate for the Ba lost in washing (§3.2; the reasoning includes inference; provisional). In solid-state synthesis no Ba is lost, so the surplus Ba becomes the secondary phase as it is.

2.3 Degree of reaction under diffusion control — the Jander equation and the Ginstling–Brounshtein equation

Suppose a spherical particle of radius r (here Fe₂O₃) is surrounded by the other raw material (BaCO₃). Once a product layer has formed at the contact, the raw material (Ba²⁺, O²⁻, etc.) must diffuse through the product layer for the reaction to continue. If the thickness of the layer is y, the flux through the layer is proportional to 1/y, so approximating the layer as a flat plate gives dy/dt = D/y, that is, y² = 2Dt (the parabolic law). Substituting the relation between the degree of reaction α (the fraction of the volume that has reacted) and the layer thickness, y = r[1 − (1 − α)1/3], gives the Jander equation (Ref. 1).

[1−(1−α)1/3]2=kt (2)

The Jander equation treats the layer as a flat plate, so it stops fitting as the reaction proceeds. Solving the steady-state diffusion in a spherical shell correctly gives the Ginstling–Brounshtein (GB) equation (Ref. 2). The simulator uses the GB equation by default and lets you choose the Jander equation for comparison.

1−2α3−(1−α)2/3=kt,k=2Dreff2,D=D0exp(−EaRT) (3)

The left-hand side becomes 1/3 at α = 1, so the reaction is complete at kt ≥ 1/3. Eq. (3) has two key points. (i) The time the reaction takes is proportional to reff²: halving the particle size makes it 4 times faster. This is the reason for “making the powder fine and mixing it well.” (ii) The temperature acts exponentially, through the Arrhenius equation for the diffusion coefficient D. The simulator uses D0 = 1.56 × 10−3 cm²/s and Ea = 250 kJ/mol. These are calibrated values (estimates) chosen in the barium ferrite model of the Virtual Laboratory (in preparation) so that “a coprecipitated precursor of radius 0.3 µm reaches α = 0.90 at 900 °C for 2 h”; they are not measured diffusion coefficients. A check in Node.js gives α = 0.900 at 900 °C for 2 h with r = 0.3 µm and h = 1, which reproduces the calibration condition.

3. Solution Synthesis (Coprecipitation) — Mixing of Ions, Supersaturation and Precipitation

3.1 In what sense is this a “solution” method?

In coprecipitation, solution B, in which the salts of Fe and Ba are dissolved in water, is added to solution A of the base (NaOH and Na₂CO₃). In solution, Fe³⁺ and Ba²⁺ are mixed as ions, so at the moment of precipitation the two lie side by side at a distance of nm to 0.1 µm. The length scale of mixing differs by one to two orders of magnitude from that of solid-state synthesis, in which µm-sized particles are brought together at their contacts. However, in the calculation of calcination in ②, the radius of the agglomerated particles (about 0.32 µm as the standard), not the distance over which the ions mix, is used as the particle radius r in the GB equation (§3.5, §3.6). The diffusion distance reff shown in the readout is therefore 0.33 µm for the solution route and 1.36 µm for the standard solid-state case, so the difference in the model is about a factor of 4. Note also that the precipitate immediately after coprecipitation is not BaFe₁₂O₁₉. The Fe side is amorphous hydrous iron oxide (ferrihydrite) and the Ba side is BaCO₃, and the target crystalline phase forms only on calcination (Refs. 5, 6). Tab ② of this simulator shows only the key points of dropwise addition, settling, decantation washing with warm water, and collection; the whole process, including the number of washes and the pH, drying and calcination, is treated in Coprecipitation of barium ferrite.

3.2 Feed amounts and procedure (lab manual §8.1)

Solution A: 12.5 g of NaOH (312.5 mmol) and 3.15 g of Na₂CO₃ (29.72 mmol) in 100 mL of distilled water. Solution B: 10.14 g of FeCl₃·6H₂O (37.52 mmol) and 0.835 g of BaCl₂·2H₂O (3.419 mmol) in 50 mL of distilled water. The feed ratio is Fe/Ba = 10.97, which has 9 % more Ba than the stoichiometric 12 (of the 3.419 mmol of Ba, about 0.29 mmol is the amount exceeding stoichiometry). In coprecipitation it is customary to make the Fe/Ba of the starting solution smaller than 12, and many examples of 10–11.5 have been reported (Refs. 5, 20). The reason is thought to be to make up for the tendency of Ba²⁺ to be lost with the supernatant during washing and to avoid leaving surplus Fe as α-Fe₂O₃ (the loss of Ba includes inference from the literature and from chemistry; provisional). Ba that is still left over after washing may remain as a small amount of a secondary phase such as BaFe₂O₄ after calcination. Tab ② shows the washing steps, but the change in the composition of the precipitate caused by washing is not calculated, so the Fe/Ba of the solid stays at the feed ratio of 10.97, and this caveat is displayed in the one-line comment on the screen. In the calculation for calcination too, as when Fe/Ba is made smaller than 12 in ①, the portion in which the surplus Ba becomes BaFe₂O₄ is subtracted (§2.2, §3.6). These masses and amounts of water are the values in §8.1 of the 2025 edition of the lab manual for Materials Science Experiments (Inorganic Materials) of the former Tokyo Institute of Technology (they also agree with the values in the Virtual Laboratory).

In the lab course, solution B is added little by little to solution A while A is stirred well. In the lab video, B is poured along a glass rod in several portions, and the mixture is stirred with the glass rod in between. After the precipitate has settled, the supernatant is removed with a Komagome pipette until about 125 mL of liquid remains, warm water at about 60 °C from a hot plate is added up to about 300 mL, and the mixture is stirred and left to stand (the warm water is added little by little with a 20 mL Komagome pipette in lab manual §8.1, and poured directly from a 500 mL beaker in the lab video; the figure in the simulator follows the video). This is repeated until the pH of the supernatant (pH test paper) reaches about 7 (decantation washing), followed by suction filtration and drying at 105 °C (lab manual §8.1). Calcination is at 925 °C for 2 h (§8.2), and the default of ② in the simulator was also set to this condition. Tab ② of the simulator draws the repeated washing as a single washing motion and does not calculate the number of washes or the change in pH (these are treated in tab ③ of the coprecipitation simulator).

3.3 pH and the amount of precipitate — charge balance and solubility products

The dropwise addition is divided into 60 steps, and, assuming that the solution reaches equilibrium after each addition, the pH and the amount of precipitate are calculated. The pH is not an input; it is determined by the charge balance from the amounts of reagents added.

[Na+]+2[Ba2+]+qFe[Fe3+]+[H+]=[Cl−]+[OH−]+[HCO3−]+2[CO32−] (5)

The amounts of Fe³⁺ and Ba²⁺ that can remain dissolved are determined by the solubility products. qFe = 3 − Kh/(Kh + [H⁺]) is the average charge that includes the first hydrolysis of Fe³⁺ (Kh = 6.3 × 10−3), and the dissolved carbonate is partitioned into H₂CO₃*, HCO₃⁻ and CO₃²⁻ with Ka1 = 4.45 × 10−7 and Ka2 = 4.69 × 10−11.

[Fe3+][OH−]3≤Ksp,Fe(OH)3,[Ba2+][CO32−]≤Ksp,BaCO3 (6)

The values used are Ksp(Fe(OH)₃) = 2.79 × 10−39 and Ksp(BaCO₃) = 2.58 × 10−9 (CRC Handbook, 25 °C, Ref. 4). The Virtual Laboratory model uses 4 × 10−36 and 8.1 × 10−9, respectively (the given conditions of the problem in lab manual §8.1), so there is a difference of three orders of magnitude for Fe(OH)₃. At high pH, almost all the Fe precipitates in either case, so the result is hardly affected, but it matters, through √Ksp, for the dissolution of BaCO₃ during washing (an item to confirm in §7). The amount of dissolved BaCO₃ is found by solving the quadratic equation b·α2(CT − BT + b) = Ksp, where b is the concentration of dissolved Ba (CT and BT are the total concentrations of carbonate and Ba, and α2 is the fraction present as CO₃²⁻). Carbonate that has become H₂CO₃* in an acidic solution is assumed to escape from the solution as CO₂ (a schematic of an open system).

3.4 Supersaturation and nucleation

Whether a precipitate forms is determined by the supersaturation ratio S (the ratio of the ion activity product, IAP, to the solubility product). If S > 1, the solid is the more stable state, nuclei form, and a precipitate is born. If S ≤ 1, nothing precipitates.

S=IAPKsp,ΔG*∝γ3(kTlnS)2 (7)

In classical nucleation theory, the nucleation barrier ΔG* decreases in inverse proportion to (ln S)² (γ is the interfacial energy). The larger the supersaturation, the more nuclei form at once, and the finer the particles become. The “log₁₀ S at the moment of addition” shown by the simulator is a value calculated on the assumption that each added portion of liquid mixes throughout the whole liquid before it precipitates. In the standard direction (B into A), log₁₀ S ≈ 37 for Fe(OH)₃ and ≈ 4.5 for BaCO₃, and both precipitate at the same time. In the reverse direction (A into B), the solution is acidic at first, so log₁₀ S of Fe(OH)₃ is only on the order of a few and that of BaCO₃ is negative (undersaturated); Fe precipitates first, and Ba precipitates only after the base is in excess. This “offset in the timing of precipitation” is expressed by the simultaneity hsync = 1 − ⟨|FFe − FBa|⟩ (F is the fraction of the final amount of precipitate that has precipitated up to that point) (an index of our own). The rate of nucleation itself (the kinetics of precipitation) is not calculated.

3.5 Stirring strength, pouring speed and particle size (estimated equations)

For how the stirring strength (in rpm equivalent) and the pouring speed act, the estimated equations of the Virtual Laboratory model were used as they are: the score for stirring strength s = log₁₀(1 + rpm/60)/log₁₀ 11, the score for pouring speed q = 1 − log₁₀(speed/3 mL min⁻¹)/1.6 (0 to 1), the mixing coefficient (0.45 + 0.35s + 0.30q)/1.10, and the radius of the agglomerated particles 0.30 µm/(0.55 + 0.45sq) (2.6 times larger for the reverse direction; hereafter called the “agglomerate size,” but it is used as the particle radius r in the GB equation). The homogeneity is the product of hsync and the mixing coefficient. None of them is an equation fitted to measurements; each is an estimate that shows a trend. In the lab course the mixture is stirred with a glass rod, so the rpm is used as a guide (a condition for comparison) converted to the rotation speed of a stirrer, in order to compare how well the mixing goes.

The degree of reaction when the collected precursor is calcined is calculated with the GB equation of §2.3, with the particle radius r taken as the agglomerate size and the homogeneity as the value above. The dehydration of the hydrous iron oxide and the pathway through BaFe₂O₄ are omitted, so this is a comparison for seeing “how large a difference the diffusion distance alone makes”. However, α is the progress of the reaction between the raw materials present in the solid, so the fraction of Fe that can become BaFe₁₂O₁₉ has an upper limit set by the Fe/Ba (R) in the solid. The way of counting is the same as in ① (§2.2): for R ≥ 12 it is 12/R (Ba is insufficient and the remaining Fe is α-Fe₂O₃), and for R < 12 it is 12(R − 2)/(10R) (the Fe taken up by the surplus Ba forming BaFe₂O₄ is subtracted). The bar graph and the readout on the screen show “the fraction of Fe that becomes BaFe₁₂O₁₉ = α × upper limit.” For Na₂CO₃ 0.2 g (Fe/Ba of the solid = 19.9) the upper limit is 60 %, and even with α = 0.89 at 925 °C for 2 h it stays at 54 %, the rest becoming α-Fe₂O₃. Without NaOH, Ba does not precipitate, so the upper limit is 0 and no BaFe₁₂O₁₉ forms even on calcination.

4. CVD — Surface-Reaction-Limited and Transport-Limited Growth (the Grove Model)

4.1 The reaction and the apparatus

SiH4(g)→Si(s)+2H2(g) (8)

The main example is a polycrystalline Si film by low-pressure CVD (LPCVD). Wafers are stood in a row in a quartz reaction tube and the whole is heated by a heater outside the tube (a hot-wall furnace), while SiH₄ is flowed at a low pressure of about 0.2–1 Torr. Typical conditions are 575–650 °C and a growth rate of about 10–100 nm/min (Ref. 8). The by-product H₂ and the unreacted SiH₄ are exhausted and treated in an abatement system (SiH₄ ignites spontaneously in air). Changing the source system produces various films by the same mechanism (Table 5).

4.2 The Grove model — setting two fluxes equal

Near the surface of the substrate there is a boundary layer in which the gas flow is slow. The source material is carried across the boundary layer by diffusion (flux J1) and reacts on the surface to become a film (flux J2). Let the gas-phase concentration be Cg, the surface concentration Cs, the mass-transfer coefficient hg, and the rate constant of the surface reaction ks (Refs. 7, 8). Then

J1=hg(Cg−Cs),J2=ksCs⇒Cs=hghg+ksCg,G=CgNfhgkshg+ks (9)

Nf is the atomic number density of the film (5.0 × 1028 m−3 for Si). At low temperature ks ≪ hg, so that G ≈ ksCg/Nf (surface-reaction-limited): the growth rate depends exponentially on temperature and gives a straight line in an Arrhenius plot. The surface concentration is almost the same as in the gas phase, so the film is deposited with the same thickness on every wafer. At high temperature hg ≪ ks, so that G ≈ hgCg/Nf (transport-limited): the dependence on temperature is weak, and the film thickness tends to become uneven depending on how the gas flows. In LPCVD the pressure is low, so the gas-phase diffusion coefficient (∝ 1/p) is large and the surface-reaction-limited regime extends toward the high-temperature side. This is one of the reasons for reducing the pressure.

4.3 The rate equation of the simulator — three resistances in series

We rewrite Eq. (9) in terms of partial pressure, add how much of the flowing source is consumed (the supply of the source), and express the growth rate G (nm/s) as three resistances in series. The first two (surface reaction and transport) can be derived from the Grove model, but the form in which the third term, the supply term c p, is added as a resistance in series is a schematic constructed as a rough guide to the effect of the source being depleted (it cannot be derived from the Grove model).

G=pSiH41ks+1hg+cpSiH4,ks=4.3×107exp(−133.4kJ mol−1RT) (10)

Strictly, if the source is consumed along the flow by a first-order reaction, the film thickness thins exponentially toward the downstream end, and the conversion is 1 − exp(−G0c) (G0 is the local rate p/(1/ks + 1/hg) determined by the partial pressure at the inlet). The series form underestimates the depletion compared with this (example: at 750 °C, 0.3 Torr and 5 sccm, X = 0.49 for the series form and 0.61 for the plug-flow equation). Here the series form was used as a rough guide. Also, when the conditions are changed partway through deposition, what is deposited under the conditions at each moment is accumulated, so the thickness and structure (§4.4) of the film already deposited do not change afterward.

Mass balance: the number of Si atoms that became film is equal to the number of SiH₄ molecules consumed, and twice as many H₂ molecules are produced. 4.4 Film structure and plasma CVD

Si films made by LPCVD are said to be amorphous below about 580 °C and polycrystalline (columnar grains) at 600 °C and above. The simulator only displays this boundary as a rough guide, and it has not been checked against the numerical values in the original source (§6). Thermal CVD drives the reaction with heat, whereas plasma CVD (PECVD) creates highly reactive species by electron collisions and can therefore deposit films at lower temperatures (Ref. 16). ALD (atomic layer deposition) is a relative of CVD that repeats self-limiting surface reactions, half a cycle at a time.

5. PVD — Vacuum Evaporation, Sputtering and the Structure Zone Model

5.1 Vacuum evaporation — vapor pressure and the Hertz–Knudsen equation

When a material is heated in a vacuum, atoms leave the surface thermally. The evaporation flux per unit area and unit time is given by the Hertz–Knudsen equation (Ref. 10).

J=αvp*−p2πmkBT[m−2 s−1] (11)

p* is the saturated vapor pressure, and αv is the evaporation coefficient (taken as 1 for a clean surface). The vapor pressure of Al is interpolated segment by segment as straight lines in log p versus 1/T from the CRC Handbook table (the temperatures at which it reaches 1, 10, 100 and 1000 Pa are 1482, 1632, 1817 and 2054 K; Ref. 4), and outside the table range (below 1482 K and above 2054 K) it is extrapolated from the end segment and labeled “extrapolated.” Check: at 1482 K (1 Pa), J = 1.32 × 1022 m−2 s−1.

Atoms leaving a small planar evaporation source are emitted in proportion to cos φ, where φ is the angle from the normal to the surface (the cosine law). Consider a point at a distance x from the center on a flat substrate at a distance h directly above the evaporation source. The distance is given by r² = h² + x², and the cosines of the emission angle and the incidence angle are both h/r, so

d(x)d0=cosφcosθ/r21/h2=h4(h2+x2)2=[1+(x/h)2]−2 (12)

The deposition rate at the center is J Ae/(πh²) multiplied by the atomic volume Ω = M/(ρNA) (of the total flux J Ae that a planar source of area Ae emits into the half-space, the fraction falling on a unit area directly above is 1/(πh²)). The source area Ae = 1 cm² is provisional. Check: at 1482 K and h = 0.3 m the rate is 0.077 nm/s (4.6 nm/min), and at 1600 K it is 28 nm/min. Moving the substrate farther away makes the film more uniform, but the deposition rate falls as 1/h².

5.2 Mean free path — why a high vacuum is needed

λ=kBT2πd2p (13)

For a molecular diameter d = 0.36 nm and 300 K, the mean free path is 7.2 mm at 1 Pa, 0.72 m at 10−2 Pa and 72 m at 10−4 Pa (the same order as the rule of thumb λ ≈ 5 mm·Pa/p in the TU Wien lectures, Ref. 10). For evaporation, the fraction of flying atoms that reach the substrate without ever hitting the residual gas is taken as e−h/λ (an approximation that uses the gas–gas λ also for metal atoms). The simulator also displays the ratio of the number of residual-gas molecules striking the substrate (Eq. (11) with p* taken as the residual-gas pressure and m as 28 u) to the number of metal atoms. Even at 10−4 Pa the ratio is about 0.1, which is a cause of O₂ and H₂O being incorporated into the film and oxidizing or contaminating it (the sticking probability is not included).

5.3 Sputtering — knocking out atoms by momentum transfer

When Ar⁺ ions produced in an Ar glow discharge strike a target to which a negative voltage is applied, a chain of collisions knocks atoms out of the surface (they come from a depth of about 1 nm, and most of them are neutral atoms). The number of atoms knocked out per ion is called the sputtering yield Y; above a threshold (10–30 eV) it increases with the energy of the ion (Refs. 10, 14, 15). The simulator uses representative values for 600 eV Ar⁺ (Cu 2.3, Ti 0.6, Al 1.2; these come from reference tables of equipment vendors, with no original source cited and an uncertainty of ±20–30 %, Ref. 17), and makes the approximation that the yield is proportional to the energy in excess of the 25 eV threshold, Y(E) = Y600(E − 25 eV)/(575 eV) (the 25 eV threshold is provisional). The ion energy is assumed to be the discharge voltage × the elementary charge. The rate at which the target is eroded is given by

x˙=YjMeNAρ (14)

where j is the ion current density. Because Y for metals is around 1, the rates differ less from one material to another than in evaporation, which makes sputtering suitable for alloy and multicomponent films. The flux reaching the center of the substrate is obtained by multiplying by the on-axis value Rt²/(Rt² + h²) for a disk-shaped source of radius Rt = 25 mm (provisional).

Sputtering is carried out in Ar at 0.1 to a few Pa, so the flying atoms collide with Ar. Eq. (13) is the value for thermally moving gas molecules colliding with each other, and the √2 comes from the relative velocity. When a fast atom of several eV or more travels through nearly stationary Ar, the √2 does not appear, and the collision cross section also becomes smaller as the energy increases, so the mean free path is longer than that given by Eq. (13). In the simulator we use λsp = kBT/(0.5 πd²p) (an approximation that drops the √2 and takes the cross section as half that for gas–gas collisions; the coefficient 0.5 is provisional), which gives 41 mm at 0.5 Pa. This is of the same order as the mean free path of Cu at 0.5 Pa, about 40–110 mm, given by a Monte Carlo calculation that uses energy-dependent cross sections (Zhu et al. 2022, Ref. 19). We take the mean number of collisions as n = h/λsp, the fraction of energy remaining after one collision as f = 1 − 2M1M2/(M1 + M2)² (the hard-sphere average; 0.53 for Cu and Ar), the mean energy at ejection as E0 = 10 eV (a provisional value chosen from the representative range of 5–40 eV), and, assuming that the number of collisions follows a Poisson distribution with mean n, we estimate the mean energy on arrival as E0 Σk P(k) fk = E0 exp[−n(1 − f)] (the lower limit is the thermal energy of the gas, 0.026 eV). According to this expression, the distance over which Cu cools (thermalizes) to 0.1 eV is about 40 cm at 0.5 Pa (p·d ≈ 20 Pa·cm). At 0.5 Pa and 60 mm there are 1.5 collisions on average, and 77 % of the atoms collide at least once (in the Poisson distribution P(0) = 0.23, P(1) = 0.34, P(2) = 0.25); since each collision costs 47 % of the energy on average, the mean energy on arrival is about half the energy at ejection (≈ 5 eV). Even so, this is an order of magnitude larger than for evaporated atoms (about 0.3 eV), and this bombardment makes the film dense (Zone T). At 4 Pa the atoms are thermalized and arrive obliquely (Zone 1). The fraction η that reaches the substrate is a schematic model: the sum of the part that arrives without colliding, e−h/λ, and the scattered part, (1 − e−h/λ) × 0.5 × min(1, 3λ/h). The 0.5 is the approximate fraction of the atoms, whose directions have been randomized by scattering, that head toward the substrate, and min(1, 3λ/h) expresses that when the pressure is high and the transport becomes diffusive, the atoms are less likely to arrive (both are provisional). As the pressure is raised, the deposition rate falls gradually (85 → 60 → 12 nm/min for 0.1 Pa → 0.5 Pa → 4 Pa), and the atoms arrive obliquely and with lower energy. The lower limit of the pressure at which a magnetron can sustain the discharge is taken as 0.1 Pa (provisional); below that, the simulator reports “no discharge.”

5.4 The structure zone model — the microstructure of the film changes with substrate temperature and pressure

Movchan and Demchishin organized the microstructure of evaporated films by the ratio of the substrate temperature to the melting point of the film material, Ts/Tm (Ref. 13), and Thornton extended this to sputtered films by adding an axis for the Ar pressure (Ref. 12). In washing, the numbers of Na⁺, Cl⁻, OH⁻ and CO₃²⁻ in the supernatant and of the Fe³⁺ and Ba²⁺ left in solution are reduced to show the dilution (schematic).
  • Graphs: the left graph shows the amount added and the pH (upper) and the supersaturation ratio at the moment of addition, log₁₀ S (lower; Fe(OH)₃ and BaCO₃). The right graph shows side by side, for the precursor of the solution route and for the powder of ①, the fractions of Fe and Ba precipitated and the Fe/Ba of the solid, and the fraction of Fe that becomes BaFe₁₂O₁₉ when calcined at the same temperature and time (an upper limit determined by the degree of reaction α × the Fe/Ba of the solid (§3.6); the upper limit is the red dashed line).
  • Controls: “Add dropwise” → “Let settle” → “Wash with warm water (decant)” → “Filter, dry and collect.” If you collect without washing, the simulator states that washing was skipped and that the mass counts only the precipitate. During washing (including while paused), the “Add dropwise” and “Let settle” buttons and the collection button cannot be pressed. The default for calcination is 925 °C and 2 h (lab manual §8.2).
  • Note on the feed ratio: the standard Fe/Ba = 10.97 has 9 % more Ba than stoichiometry. In the one-line comment after collection, the simulator shows the reason (provisional) for the Ba excess often used in coprecipitation, and that the surplus Ba remains as a little secondary phase after calcination (§3.2).
  • Comparisons to try in class: (1) Standard (B into A) versus A (reverse direction): in both, all of the material precipitates, but in the reverse direction Fe precipitates first and Ba afterward, and part of the carbonate (8.9 mmol) escapes as CO₂ (homogeneity 0.96 → 0.76, radius of the agglomerated particles 0.32 → 0.83 µm). (2) Calcination of the solution route versus the solid-state route (at 925 °C and 2 h, α = 0.97 and 0.34, and the fractions of Fe that become BaFe₁₂O₁₉ are 95 % and 34 %; at 900 °C and 2 h, α = 0.85 and 0.27).
  • Failure examples: In B (Na₂CO₃ 0.2 g), 45 % of the Ba does not precipitate and the Fe/Ba of the solid shifts to 19.9, so even after calcination at most 60 % of the Fe can become BaFe₁₂O₁₉ (54 % when α = 0.89 at 925 °C and 2 h). In C (no NaOH), half of the Fe stays dissolved and Ba does not precipitate, so no BaFe₁₂O₁₉ forms even after calcination (the filtrate is acidic and Fe³⁺ and Ba²⁺ remain). In D (no stirring, 60 mL/min) the homogeneity decreases.

A.4 ③ Vapor route: CVD (chemical vapor deposition)

  • Apparatus diagram: cylinders of SiH₄ and N₂, flow meters, a quartz reaction tube surrounded by a heater with six wafers (the darkness of the color shows the film thickness; farther downstream the source is more dilute and the film becomes thinner), a pressure gauge, and a pump with an abatement system. The reaction equation is shown at the top. The film thickness of each wafer is the value built up from the upstream and downstream contributions in each frame of deposition, and the average of the six wafers is the “film thickness” in the readouts (an average over the area, which agrees with the mass balance) (§4.3).
  • Interior diagram: a cross section near the substrate. The upper part is the gas phase (the number of SiH₄ molecules is a rough guide to the partial pressure); below the dotted line is the boundary layer, in which the molecules are placed so that the SiH₄ concentration falls linearly from Cg to Cs. Shown are SiH₄ adsorbed on the surface, H₂ desorbing and rising (the number is a rough guide to the growth rate), and the Si film (polycrystalline with columnar grain boundaries, amorphous with none). The film is drawn as layers piled up from the bottom in the order in which they were deposited, and the microstructure of each layer is determined by the temperature at which that layer was deposited (moving the temperature slider afterward does not change the film already deposited; the readouts also show the “structure of the film deposited so far” and the “structure of the film to be deposited” separately). The rate-limiting step and Cs/Cg are shown at upper right.
  • Graphs: the left graph is an Arrhenius plot (surface reaction only, transport only, the supply limit, and the total; the green band is the temperature range over which the Gieske equation was obtained). The right graph shows the film thickness against the deposition time.
  • Comparisons to try in class: (1) Raise the temperature 560 → 620 → 700 °C: the rate increases along the straight line of the Arrhenius plot (surface-reaction-limited). (2) C (750 °C, 10 Torr, SiH₄ 5 %): transport-limited, so raising the temperature does not make it faster. (3) Compare total pressures of 0.3 Torr and 10 Torr and see that the temperature at which the line bends becomes lower.
  • Failure examples: A (560 °C) is slow and amorphous; in D (flow rate 5 sccm), 49 % of the source is consumed and the downstream wafers become thin (in 30 min, 786 nm on average, 1039 nm upstream and 533 nm downstream).
  • Stacking layers: after depositing at A (560 °C), if you select B (800 °C) and press “Deposit more,” a polycrystalline layer is stacked on top of the amorphous layer.

A.5 ④ Vapor route: PVD (physical vapor deposition)

  • Apparatus diagram: vacuum chamber, pump, pressure gauge, substrate. In evaporation, atoms fly straight from the Al in a W boat (its color changes with temperature); in sputtering, Ar⁺ ions from the plasma above the target enter the target, and the knocked-out atoms fly while colliding with Ar (the frequency of collisions is matched to the ratio of λ to the distance).
  • Interior diagram: the upper part shows the mechanism of ejection (evaporation: thermal evaporation from the surface of molten Al; sputtering: collision of Ar⁺ → chain of collisions (yellow) → atoms at the surface fly out). The lower part is a cross section of the film, drawn with the microstructure that corresponds to the structure zone (Zone 1, T, 2, 3); the higher the pressure, the more obliquely the arriving atoms come. If you change the conditions and keep depositing, the (film thickness, structure zone) of each layer is recorded and the layers are piled up from the bottom (earlier layers are not redrawn).
  • Graphs: the left graph shows the film-thickness distribution over a substrate 100 mm in diameter (1 at the center; the film deposited so far is the sum of the distributions of the individual frames, and the dashed line is what will be deposited from now on under the current conditions). The right graph is the structure zone diagram (for sputtering, Ts/Tm and the Ar pressure; for evaporation, Ts/Tm only) with the current point and the previous point.
  • Comparisons to try in class: (1) The difference in the mechanism of ejection between evaporation and sputtering (thermal evaporation at 0.28 eV versus knock-out at 5–40 eV). (2) Ar pressures of 0.5 Pa and 4 Pa in sputtering: deposition rate 60 → 12 nm/min, mean arrival energy 5.0 → 0.04 eV (thermalized at 4 Pa), structure zone Zone T → Zone 1. (3) Cu and Ti: the difference in yield (1.50 and 0.39). (4) F (substrate at 900 K): columnar grains of Zone 2.
  • Failure examples: A (evaporation at 10−1 Pa): the fraction arriving is 1.5 %, and the residual gas strikes the substrate 6500 times as often as the metal. B (source at 1350 K): the film grows at only 0.4 nm/min. E (Ar at 0.05 Pa): there is no discharge, so pressing “Deposit a film” does not advance the time, and the simulator reports that deposition is not possible.
