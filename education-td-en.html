Ideal Gas — Building the Equation of State (PV = nRT) from Molecules in a Box

TD 01

Choose a gas, temperature, pressure, and volume, then press “Run” and the molecules begin flying around in the box. In ①, the pressure calculated solely by summing impulses on the walls, without using nRT/V, nearly matches the ideal-gas value. Even after you press “Make all speeds equal,” collisions alone restore a Maxwell distribution. In ②, choose a fixed wall or a piston and heat the gas. Compare the heat-input and work bars to see that the extra heat needed with the piston corresponds to expansion work (CP−CV=R).

Carnot Cycle — How Heat Produces Work, and the Upper Limit on Efficiency

TD 02

Set the hot and cold reservoir temperatures, working gas, and expansion ratio, then run the cycle. In ①, points travel around two diagrams in step with the piston. The enclosed area of the P–V diagram gives the work per cycle, while the T–S diagram forms a rectangle. In ②, follow the heat and work for each stage in a table and verify that the efficiency η=1− TCTH depends only on the two reservoir temperatures and does not change when the working gas is replaced.

Run the engine Piston, reservoirs, P–V diagram, T–S diagram, and energy flow Follow the four stages numerically Heat and work balances, and η = 1 − TC/TH Read the derivations of the efficiency limit and entropy Explanation (for second- and third-year undergraduates)

Joule–Thomson Effect — Why Throttling Can Cool a Gas, and How Air Conditioners Work

TD 03

Choose one of eight gases, set its inlet temperature and pressure drop, and pass it through an insulated restriction. In ①, because an ideal gas shows no temperature change, calculate with a van der Waals real gas and confirm that this constant-enthalpy process cools the gas inside the inversion curve on the T–p diagram, while He and H₂ lie outside the curve at room temperature and warm up. In ②, use the same throttling process as an air conditioner's expansion valve, choose among six refrigerants, and examine p–h diagrams and COP values for cooling and heating.

Entropy of Mixing — Why Do Gases Mix Spontaneously When a Partition Is Removed?

TD 04

Place different gases on each side at equal temperature and pressure, then remove the partition. In ①, the entropy obtained by counting the molecules as they mix approaches ΔS=−nRΣxlnx. With identical gases on both sides, only the color-based counting increases, while the thermodynamic ΔS is zero: Gibbs' paradox. In ②, verify that the direction of mixing coincides with the direction in which the Helmholtz free energy F decreases (at constant temperature and volume) or the Gibbs free energy G decreases (at constant temperature and pressure).

Remove the partition Molecular mixing and a counting-based measurement of ΔS View it through free energy For an isolated system: S; at constant volume: F; at constant pressure: G Read the derivations of the entropy of mixing and free energy Explanation (for second- and third-year undergraduates)

Gibbs Free Energy and Phases — Where Do Ice, Water, and Water Vapor Trade Places?

TD 05

Vary the external pressure from 1 Pa to 10 bar. In ①, plot G=H−TS against temperature for ice, water, and water vapor. The crossings where the lowest curve changes determine the melting and boiling points. Below the triple-point pressure, the liquid curve is never the lowest at any temperature, so ice sublimates directly. Follow the competition in which lower H wins at low temperature and larger S wins at high temperature. In ②, collect the crossings at different pressures to construct the phase diagram.
