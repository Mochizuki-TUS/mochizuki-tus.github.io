Educational Resources

Advancing the nation through the promotion of science

In both science and engineering, thinking deeply about a subject—especially in research—often requires a thorough understanding of the fundamentals. As stated in the founding spirit of Tokyo University of Science, spreading a deeper understanding of fundamental science can help both science and engineering flourish and, in turn, contribute to the nation's development. Subjects such as quantum mechanics may seem difficult at first, but simple model calculations offer a way to see, enjoy, and become familiar with them. I believe this experience is essential to deepening understanding. I hope these resources will support learners far beyond our research group and university.

These resources turn lecture topics that are difficult to grasp from equations alone into hands-on explorations in your browser. Change a parameter, and the results are calculated and redrawn immediately. No installation is needed, and they also work on smartphones.

Confucius said: “Learning without reflection leaves one confused; reflection without learning leaves one in peril.”

The simulators on this page, their explanation pages, and the advanced textbooks High School Mathematics (AD 01) and University Mathematics (AD 02) are in English. The other advanced textbooks and the scanned handwritten lecture notes are currently available in Japanese only.

Single-Slit Experiment — Where Do Particles Go After Passing Through a Slit?

QM 01

Change the slit width and the particle mass and speed, then launch particles individually or 10,000 at a time to accumulate their landing positions. Watch the histogram approach the theoretical Fraunhofer diffraction curve |ψ(x)|2 . Keeping the display range fixed while increasing the mass from an electron to a baseball makes the distribution collapse to a single point, approaching the classical picture.

Double-Slit Experiment — Interference Fringes, One Particle at a Time

QM 02

Ehrenfest's Theorem — A Trajectory Becomes Blurred as Mass Decreases

QM 05

Reduce the mass from a baseball to an electron and then to an even lighter hypothetical particle. Set the launch speed and angle. In a uniform gravitational field, the mean position follows the same classical parabola regardless of mass; only the surrounding spread σ(t) changes. As the mass decreases, the trajectory disappears into a cloud and the landing positions become more scattered.

Carnot Cycle — How Heat Produces Work, and the Upper Limit on Efficiency

TD 02

Set the hot and cold reservoir temperatures, working gas, and expansion ratio, then run the cycle. In ①, points travel around two diagrams in step with the piston. From Molecular Orbitals to Bands — Lining Up Atoms Turns Energy Levels into a Band

SS 01

In ①, increase the number of atoms N from 2 to 60 and click energy levels to see the shapes of their orbitals. The levels crowd into a fixed energy range and form a band. In ②–③, close the chain into a ring: all allowed k values lie on one dispersion curve. As more atoms are added, the points become denser and the distribution concentrated near the band edges approaches the theoretical curve. In ④, alternate atoms or bonds to open a gap, splitting a half-filled band into a full band and an empty band to produce an insulator. In ⑤, choose an orbital from s to f and the phase pattern, reciprocal-lattice points, and band diagram respond together; differences in the way the orbitals overlap determine the bandwidths and splittings.

X-Ray Diffraction — From Bragg's Condition to Peak Positions Set by the Bravais Lattice

IM 01

In ①, select crystal planes in CsCl, SrTiO₃, or NaCl and see waves from adjacent planes reinforce at 2dsinθ=λ, while the atomic layers within one period determine peak intensity. In ②, draw the reciprocal lattice and Ewald sphere to confirm that the Laue condition says the same thing as Bragg's condition. In ③, draw the structure factor as a sum of arrows in the complex plane and follow the systematic absences, in which reflections cancel and vanish, as in face-centered cubic lattices. In ④, keep the lattice parameters fixed and change the contents of the unit cell to verify that the Bravais lattice alone sets the possible peak positions in 2θ. In ⑤, calculate powder XRD patterns for 27 real crystal structures. In ⑥, estimate crystallite sizes from peak widths using the Scherrer equation.

Sintering — How Powder Particles Stick Together and Become Dense

IM 02

In ①, see calcination, the step before sintering, in which two powders in contact mix by atomic diffusion to form an ordered compound or a solid solution. From ② onward, turn to sintering itself and confirm that its driving force is the reduction of surface energy. In ③, compare neck growth in a two-sphere model on log–log plots: the exponent n in (x/a)n∝t is 7 for surface diffusion, 6 for grain-boundary diffusion, 5 for volume diffusion, and 3 for evaporation–condensation. In ④, see that whether matter comes from the particle surface or the grain boundary determines whether shrinkage occurs. In ⑤–⑥, plot densification and see it stop when growing grains leave pores behind. In ⑦, compare how effective each of four approaches is: pressure, a liquid phase, powder design, and additives.

① Calcination and solid-solution formation Two powders mix by diffusion; Fick's law and the Jander equation ② Driving force for sintering Reduced surface energy; neck curvature and material transport ③ Neck growth and the exponent n Log–log plots for each mechanism; effects of particle size ④ Mechanisms with and without shrinkage Surface or grain boundary as the source of matter; open and closed pores ⑤ Densification curves Relative density versus time and temperature; trapped pores ⑥ Competition between densification and grain growth Microstructure after firing; effects of grain-growth inhibitors ⑦ Four approaches to assist sintering Pressure, liquid phases, powder design, and additives Read the derivations from diffusion during calcination to the driving force, neck growth, and competition between densification and grain growth Explanation (for second- and third-year undergraduates)

Binary Phase Diagrams — How Does the Microstructure Change as You Move Composition and Temperature?

IM 03

Choose an isomorphous, eutectic, or peritectic system. In ①, drag across the phase diagram to read the phases, microstructure, and phase fractions calculated by the lever rule in two-phase regions. In ②, use Gibbs free-energy curves and their common tangent to trace the equilibrium compositions and construct the phase diagram. In ③, cool a melt and compare equilibrium cooling with Scheil-type solidification without solid-state diffusion to see microsegregation and nonequilibrium eutectics in schematic microstructure diagrams. In ④, compare glass formation on rapid quenching with crystallization on slow cooling. In ⑤, use schematic atomic arrangements to see how a glass or solid solution that is homogeneous at high temperature separates into two phases by spinodal decomposition on cooling.

Taylor Expansion — How Far Does Increasing the Order Improve the Approximation?

MA 01

In ①, choose among ten functions with different convergence behavior and increase the order to see how far the accurate range extends and where it stops. In ②, move the evaluation point and numerically locate the c whose existence is all that the theorem asserts. Compare the actual error with the Lagrange bound and see how singularities in the complex plane determine the radius of convergence. In ③, use a simple pendulum and a Lennard-Jones potential to explore why an expansion requires specifying what the small quantity is small relative to.

The Method of Lagrange Multipliers — Finding the Lowest Point Under a Constraint

MA 02

In ①, use the two coefficients of the molecular orbital Ψ=cAψA +cBψB as coordinates. Go once around the ellipse drawn by the normalization constraint and find the lowest-energy point. In ②, vary the multiplier ε to see that a nontrivial (nonvanishing) wave-function solution appears only at those values of ε for which the determinant vanishes. In ③, use presets or sliders to separate αA and αB, and follow the shift from covalent to ionic bonding in the energy levels and wave function.

Determinants — Connecting Linear Algebra to Area and Volume

MA 03

Expectation Values — From Dice to |ψ|²

MA 05

In ①, label the outcomes by dividing 0–6 into n equal intervals, then increase the number of faces from 6 to 1,200. Each outcome's probability tends to zero, but the probability density, obtained by dividing by the interval width, stays constant. In ②, choose a two-peaked distribution to see that the expectation value lies at neither peak. In ③, increase the subdivisions to 200 and watch the discrete sum approach ⟨x⟩=∫x |ψ |2 dx. In ④, vary the mixture and make measurements: only the two energy levels appear as outcomes, while their average lies between them.

Fourier Series — Building Shapes by Adding Waves

MA 06

In ①, choose among six waveforms and increase N to 60. The broad shape emerges first, while Gibbs oscillations persist near discontinuities. In ②, see orthogonality as cancellation of the signed area under the product of two basis functions. In ③, evolve an initial profile with the heat and wave equations: each mode evolves independently, and finer modes decay faster in heat conduction. In ④, choose a metal and vary the electron density to explore Thomas–Fermi screening. Damped and Forced Oscillations — Solving Second-Order Linear Differential Equations

Increase the damping γ in ① to move from underdamped through critically damped to overdamped motion.
