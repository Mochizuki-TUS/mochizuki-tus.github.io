Gaussian Processes and Bayesian Optimization — Prediction Bands and the Next Point to Measure

From the points already measured, predict the value and its uncertainty at unmeasured conditions with a Gaussian process, then use that prediction to try Bayesian optimization, which decides “where to measure next.” With synthetic data for teaching purposes (dopant amount and ionic conductivity), check the difference between the latent-function band and the observation band, the relation between the length scale ℓ and the log marginal likelihood, the points chosen by the acquisition functions (EI, UCB, PI), and how to compare against random search, comparing the formulas with the numbers.

The vertical solid line marks the current ℓ, and ◆ marks the maximum of the curve.

Procedure and What to Observe

  1. In tab ①, press “Place the example 8 points.” What to look for: near the observation points, the latent-function band ±2σ* (filled) becomes narrow and widens between 6.4 and 8.2 mol%, where there are no observations. The observation band ±2√(σ*2 + σ2) (dashed) never becomes narrower than ±2σ = ±0.1, even right on an observation point.
  2. Move the “query point x*” from 6.4, which is on an observation point, to 7.0, which has none (keep the example 8 points). What to look for: in “Putting the current numbers into the formula,” for σ*2 = σf2 − vTv, the subtracted term vTv decreases from 0.638 to 0.556 and σ* increases from 0.050 to 0.290. Next, set ℓ to 0.2. At x* = 7.0, vTv ≈ 0, so σ*2 returns to the prior variance σf2 = 0.64 and μ* returns to near the mean function m = −4.469 (−4.459) (far from the data, the prediction simply reverts to the mean function).
  3. Set the length scale ℓ to 0.2 and then to 5. What to look for: ℓ is small, the mean drops back to m between observation points and the band balloons; when it is large, the two peaks cannot be represented and the fit is poor. In the lower figure, the data-fit term goes down as ℓ is increased, while the complexity-penalty term goes up (the penalty gets lighter).
  4. Press “Grid search: maximize ℓ” and “Grid search: maximize ℓ, σf, σ.” What to look for: the ℓ that maximizes the log marginal likelihood, and the values of the three terms there. With little data, the maximum is flat and the selected value cannot be trusted.
  5. Display “Samples from the prior” and “Samples from the posterior,” and vary ℓ. What to look for: how fine the waves of the prior samples are is determined by ℓ. Near the observation points the posterior samples pass close to the observed values; farther away they scatter like the prior samples.
  6. In tab ②, press “Observe next point” repeatedly (default: EI, ξ = 0, 3 initial points). What to look for: the acquisition function (lower figure) becomes large both “where the predictive mean is high” and “where the band is wide,” and the observations gather near the global maximum (about 7.0 mol%) rather than the local maximum (about 2.2 mol%). Also look at the history table and the simple regret.
  7. In tab ②, switch to “Minimize −y,” press “Restart,” and advance the same number of steps. What to look for: minimization uses LCB and an EI adapted to the sign convention, but since this merely flips the sign of the objective function, with the same seed the sequence of observed points matches that of maximization exactly.
  8. In tab ③, press “Run” (default: 200 repetitions, budget 20). Then press “BO loses (ℓ = 10 fixed, B = 30)” and run it. What to look for: the median and interquartile range (band) of the simple regret. By default, the median for Bayesian optimization is nearly an order of magnitude smaller than that for random search. If the length scale is fixed at 10 mol%, the median becomes larger than that of random search (BO loses).

With the keyboard, move to buttons and sliders with Tab, press with Enter (or Space), and move sliders with the arrow keys. Tabs can be switched with ←→. Clicking the figure in tab ① can also be done with “Dopant amount to observe x” and “Observe at this x” in the right-hand column.

Formulas and Algorithms Computed

For the Gaussian process f ~ 𝒢𝒫(m, k), the covariance function is the RBF (Gaussian) kernel, with length scale ℓ and signal variance σf2. The mean function m is a constant.

k(x,x′)=σf2exp(−(x−x′)22ℓ2),yn=f(xn)+εn,εn∼𝒩(0,σ2)

From N observations y and Ky ≡ K + σ2I (K ∈ ℝN×N is the Gram matrix), at the test input x*, the latent function f* has the predictive distribution 𝒩(f* | μ*, σ*2), and the observation y* has the predictive variance

μ*=m+k*TKy−1(y−m1),σ*2=k(x*,x*)−k*TKy−1k*Var[y*]=σ*2+σ2lnp(y∣θ)=−12(y−m1)TKy−1(y−m1)⏟data fit−12ln|Ky|⏟complexity penalty−N2ln2π⏟constant

Here, k* = (k(x1, x*), …, k(xN, x*))T, θ = (ℓ, σf, σ). No matrix inverse is formed. The Cholesky decomposition Ky = LLT is computed only once, and forward and backward substitution give a = LT\(L\(y − m1)) and v = L\k*; then μ* = m + k*Ta, σ*2 = k(x*, x*) − vTv, and ln |Ky| = 2 Σi ln Lii (the same procedure as Algorithm 2.1 in the textbook by Rasmussen and Williams). If the decomposition fails, a small value (jitter) is added to the diagonal and the decomposition is retried; the amount added is shown on screen. If rounding makes σ*2 negative, it is clipped to 0 and the number clipped is shown.

In Bayesian optimization (tabs ② and ③), taking the best observed value fbest as the reference, the acquisition function is evaluated on a grid of 401 points (spacing 0.025 mol%) and the best point is observed next. z = (μ* − fbest − ξ)/σ*, and Φ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard Gaussian distribution.

EI(x)=𝔼[max(f*−fbest−ξ,0)]=(μ*−fbest−ξ)Φ(z)+σ*𝒩(z∣0,1)σ*→0impliesEI→max(μ*−fbest−ξ,0)PI(x)=Φ(z),UCB(x)=μ*+κσ*

fbest can be switched between “the best observed value” and “the best posterior mean μ*(xn) over the observed points.” The simple regret is rn = ymax − y(x̂n), where x̂n is the point with the largest observed value up to evaluation n (the recommended point), y(x̂n) is the true function value at that point, ymax = −3.400 (at x = 7.000 mol%) is the true maximum value.

Data and Random-Number Conditions

  • All observed values are synthetic data for teaching purposes (Data C, “Dopant Amount and Ionic Conductivity”); they are not measured values of a real material. The dopant amount x ∈ [0, 10] mol%, y = log10(σion / S cm−1) (dimensionless), the true function y(x) = −5.0 + 1.0 exp[−(x − 2.2)2/(2·0.72)] + 1.6 exp[−(x − 7.0)2/(2·1.12)], the observed value = true value + ε, ε ~ 𝒩(0, 0.052) (independent). The local maximum is at x ≈ 2.200 mol% (y ≈ −4.000), and the global maximum is at x ≈ 7.000 mol% (y ≈ −3.400).
  • The noise standard deviation of the synthetic data, 0.05, is fixed. The slider for σ sets the noise standard deviation assumed by the model and does not affect the synthetic data.
  • The dopant amounts of the “example 8 points” in tab ① are 0.4, 1.6, 2.8, 4.0, 5.2, 6.4, 8.2, and 9.4 mol% (fixed); only the observation noise is random.
  • Random numbers come from a seeded generator (mulberry32). The default seed is 20261112 in all of ①, ②, and ③. In ①, if you change the seed, the existing observation points keep their x values unchanged and the observed values are redrawn with new random numbers. In ②, changing the seed restarts from the beginning (the initial points’ x are uniform random numbers, and the observation noise is drawn from the same random sequence at each observation). In ③, for repetition r (counted from 0), Bayesian optimization uses seed + r, random search uses seed + 100000 + r, and the observation noise of the grid uses seed + 200000 + r. The samples from the prior and posterior use fixed vectors of standard Gaussian random numbers generated with seed + 1 + (number of redraws), so when you vary ℓ and the like, the samples change shape continuously.
  • Defaults: ℓ = 1.00 mol%, σf = 0.80, σ = 0.050, the mean function m is the mean of the observed values ȳ (−4.5 when there are no observations). Defaults in ②: maximization, EI (ξ = 0), fbest is the best observed value, 3 initial points, budget 20. Defaults in ③: budget 20, 200 repetitions, EI (ξ = 0), ℓ = 1.00 mol% fixed.
  • Limits: at most 40 observation points in ①, a budget of at most 40 in ② and ③, and at most 200 repetitions in ③. The grid search in ① uses ℓ 41 points × σf 13 points × σ 21 points (11193 combinations). When “Choose ℓ by marginal likelihood each time” is used in ② and ③, the grid is ℓ ∈ [0.2, 5] mol% with 25 points. The computation in ③ is divided so that each frame takes at most 12 ms, and it can be stopped partway.

Simplifications

  • The input is one-dimensional (the dopant amount only). Real materials searches have several inputs, such as composition, temperature, and time, and the kernel length scale also differs from input to input.
  • The mean function is a constant. By default, the mean of the observed values ȳ is used, and the log marginal likelihood is computed treating ȳ as a known constant (ȳ itself is determined from the data, so this is slightly optimistic compared with the exact marginal likelihood).
  • The acquisition function is maximized on a grid of 401 points (in practice, it is usual to run a continuous optimization from many starting points). If several points have the same value, the one with the smaller x is chosen.
  • The hyperparameters are either fixed or set only by maximizing the log marginal likelihood with a grid search (gradient methods and prior distributions are not used).
  • The recommended point is “the point with the largest observed value.” Because the observations are noisy, a point that happened to receive favorable noise may be recommended, and the simple regret can temporarily increase even as evaluations accumulate.
  • For the equally spaced grid, for each number of evaluations n, the simple regret is computed as if the grid that would be placed for a budget of n, xk = 10(k − ½)/n mol% (k = 1, …, n) had been measured.

Conditions Under Which Results Become Unstable

  • If the model noise σ is small and ℓ is large, Ky becomes almost singular. The same happens when the same x is observed many times. The Cholesky decomposition continues by adding jitter, and the amount added is shown in the readout.
  • σ is made too small, the predictive mean picks up even the noise and swings widely between observation points (because noisy observations are treated as “exact values”).
  • With few observations, the maximum of the log marginal likelihood becomes flat, or ℓ attains its maximum at the edge of the range. Do not trust the grid-search result as it is; look at the shape of the lower figure.
  • fbest is set to the best observed value, a point hit by large noise becomes the reference, and EI and PI become small, so the search tends to stall.
  • ℓ is made long and σ is made small, σ* becomes small everywhere, and EI and PI can become so small (to 0) that double precision cannot represent them. Then no point can be expected to give an improvement, so the left end of the grid is chosen among the points with the same value (a note is shown in the figure). This is an example of the surrogate model believing “I already know everything” and the search stalling.
  • If NaN or infinity appears in the computation, the computation is stopped and the reason is shown.

Short Questions to Answer After Operating

  1. In tab ①, after pressing “Place the example 8 points” (default seed), at x* = 7.0 mol%, what are σ* and √(σ*2 + σ2)? What about exactly on an observation point (x* = 6.4)? Why does the observation band never become narrower than ±2σ?
    Sample answers

    With the defaults (ℓ = 1.00 mol%, σf = 0.80, σ = 0.050, m = ȳ = −4.469), at x* = 7.0 we get σ* = 0.290 and √(σ*2 + σ2) = 0.294, so the two bands have almost the same width (the point is far from the observation points, σ* ≫ σ). On an observation point, x* = 6.4, σ* narrows to 0.0499, but √(σ*2 + σ2) = 0.0706. The variance of the next observed value is σ*2 + σ2 ≥ σ2, so no matter how accurately f is known, the part due to observation noise cannot be predicted.

  2. What does the formula for EI become when σ* → 0? Why is EI almost 0 on an observation point?
    Sample answers

    z = (μ* − fbest − ξ)/σ* goes to +∞ if the improvement is positive and to −∞ if it is negative, so Φ(z) → 1 or 0, 𝒩(z | 0, 1) → 0, and EI → max(μ* − fbest − ξ, 0) (the improvement of a value known with certainty). On an observation point, σ* is small and μ* is near the observed value at that point, which is at most fbest, so almost no expected improvement remains.

  3. In tab ②, if you run maximization of y and minimization of −y with the same seed and the same number of steps, what happens to the sequence of observation points? Why?
    Sample answers

    They match exactly. Flipping the sign of the objective function flips the sign of the posterior mean (μ* is linear in y) and leaves the posterior variance unchanged. fbest also flips sign, so the improvement in minimization fbest − μ* − ξ has the same value as the improvement in maximization; EI and PI take the same values, and LCB is UCB with the sign flipped.

  4. With the defaults of tab ③ (budget 20, 200 repetitions), what are the medians of the simple regret at evaluation 20 for Bayesian optimization and for random search? What happens in “BO loses”? Why does it lose?
    Sample answers

    With seed 20261112 and 200 repetitions, the median [interquartile range] of the simple regret at evaluation 20 is 0.0037 [0.00041, 0.010] for Bayesian optimization and 0.031 [0.0053, 0.088] for random search (0.041 for the equally spaced grid). Comparing paired repetitions, Bayesian optimization was smaller in 81.5 % of them, so it does not always win. In “BO loses” (ℓ = 10 mol% fixed, budget 30), the medians are 0.049 [0.012, 0.13] for Bayesian optimization and 0.019 [0.0032, 0.054] for random search, so Bayesian optimization loses (the fraction of repetitions in which Bayesian optimization was smaller is 36.5 %). If ℓ is too long compared with the peak widths (0.7 mol% and 1.1 mol%), the surrogate model is too smooth to represent the peak positions correctly and, moreover, underestimates σ*, so it measures only near the wrong place and stops exploring. If the surrogate model is wrong, the search is wrong too (in tab ①, when ℓ = 5 is applied to the example 8 points, the log marginal likelihood is −230, far smaller than that for ℓ = 1 (−6.5), which shows that the data do not support this ℓ.)
