Probability Distributions — Shape, Interval Probability, and Behavior of the Sample Mean

Draw the shapes of the binomial, Poisson, Gaussian, Cauchy (Lorentz form), Gamma, Beta, Student’s t, and Weibull distributions, and obtain the probability of an interval from the cumulative distribution function. Check that some distributions have no mean or variance, and that even with the same full width at half maximum the Gaussian and Cauchy distributions have completely different tails. In ②, you will see that as the sample grows, the sample mean of the Gaussian distribution settles down while that of the Cauchy distribution does not (the median does settle).

Probability density function (probability mass function for a discrete distribution) and interval probability

The shaded area is the probability P(x1 ≤ x ≤ x2) that the variable falls in the interval. The value is obtained from the difference of the cumulative distribution function below, F(x2) − F(x1).

Cumulative distribution function F(x) = P(X ≤ x). The length of the vertical arrow is F(x2) − F(x1), that is, the shaded area in the figure above. For a discrete distribution F is a staircase, and the interval includes its end integers.

Procedure and what to observe

  1. Open ① with its defaults (Gaussian distribution 𝒩(x | 0, 12), interval [−1, 1]). What to see: the shaded area P(−1 ≤ x ≤ 1) = Φ(1) − Φ(−1) = 0.8413 − 0.1587 = 0.6827. Press “± 2 standard deviations” in the interval controls to get 0.9545, and check the mean, the variance, and the FWHM 2√(2 ln 2) σ = 2.355 in the readout.
  2. Press “Gaussian and Cauchy (match the FWHM)” under “Comparison examples”, and turn on “Logarithmic vertical axis” and “Wide horizontal axis (see the tails)”. What to see: even with the same peak width, the tail of the Cauchy distribution decreases only as 1/x2, while the Gaussian falls off steeply. P(−1 ≤ x ≤ 1) is 0.6827 for the Gaussian and 0.4482 for the Cauchy. The readout says, with a reason, that the mean and variance of the Cauchy distribution “do not exist”.
  3. Set the distribution to the Cauchy distribution and draw “1000 samples”. What to see: the histogram matches the shape of the density, yet the sample mean is 5.33 (seed 20261112), far from the center 0, and the sample variance is as large as 1.1×105. How many points fall outside the range. The median is −0.048, close to the center.
  4. Press “t (ν) and Gaussian” under “Comparison examples” and move the degrees-of-freedom ν slider from 1 to 1000. What to see: at ν = 1 the shape is the same as the Cauchy distribution, and for large ν it approaches the Gaussian distribution. The mean exists only for ν > 1 and the variance only for ν > 2 (variance 3 at ν = 3, and 1.071 at ν = 30).
  5. Open ② (default: n up to 104, 5 paths, seed 20261112). What to see: the sample mean of the Gaussian distribution on the left shrinks into the band ±σ/√n, whereas the sample mean of the Cauchy distribution on the right occasionally jumps by a large amount, and even at n = 104 the five values are scattered: 0.769, −1.83, 0.118, 1.35, −0.910 (2 of the 5 paths have |x̄ − x0| > γ).
  6. In ②, set “Quantity to draw” to “Median”. What to see: even for the Cauchy distribution the median settles down and enters the band ±πγ/(2√n) (±0.0157 at n = 104). Pressing “Start over with the same seed” does not change the curves.

With the keyboard, Tab moves to buttons and sliders, Enter (or Space) presses a button, and the arrow keys move a slider (Page Up / Page Down move it in larger steps). The tabs can be switched with ← and →.

Formulas and algorithms computed

The interval probability is the integral of the probability density function p(x), computed as a difference of the cumulative distribution function F. For a discrete distribution it is a sum that includes the end integers.

P(x1≤x≤x2)=∫x1x2p(x)dx=F(x2)−F(x1),discrete:∑k=⌈x1⌉⌊x2⌋p(k)

The density (mass) function of each distribution, and its mean and variance (only when they exist):

Binomial:p(x)=(Nx)μx(1−μ)N−x,𝔼[x]=Nμ,Var[x]=Nμ(1−μ) Poisson:p(x)=λxe−λx!,𝔼[x]=Var[x]=λ Gaussian:𝒩(x∣μ,σ2)=12πσ2exp(−(x−μ)22σ2),F(x)=Φ(x−μσ),FWHM22ln2σ Cauchy:p(x)=1πγ(x−x0)2+γ2,F(x)=12+1πarctanx−x0γ,FWHM2γ Gamma:Gam(x∣a,b)=baxa−1e−bxΓ(a),𝔼[x]=ab,Var[x]=ab2(b is the rate) Beta:Beta(x∣a,b)=Γ(a+b)Γ(a)Γ(b)xa−1(1−x)b−1,𝔼[x]=aa+b,Var[x]=ab(a+b)2(a+b+1) Student’s t:p(x)=Γ((ν+1)/2)Γ(ν/2)νπs[1+(x−μ)2νs2]−(ν+1)/2,𝔼[x]=μ(ν>1),Var[x]=s2νν−2(ν>2) Weibull:F(s)=1−exp[−(s/s0)m],𝔼[s]=s0Γ(1+1/m)

The mean of the Cauchy distribution does not exist because the integral truncated at R on both sides of x0 diverges as R → ∞ (the principal value is x0, but the definition of the expectation, ∫|x| p(x) dx < ∞, is not satisfied).

∫x0−Rx0+R|x−x0|p(x)dx=γπln(1+R2γ2),∫x0−Rx0+R(x−x0)2p(x)dx=2γπ[R−γarctanRγ]

For the sample mean in ②, x̄n = (x1 + ⋯ + xn)/n, if the variance σ2 is finite, then Var[x̄n] = σ2/n, and the probability of falling in the band ±σ/√n is about 68 % for large n. For the Cauchy distribution, from the characteristic function 𝔼[eikx] = eix0k − γ|k|,

𝔼[eikx¯n]=(eix0k/n−γ|k|/n)n=eix0k−γ|k|⇒x¯n∼Cauchy(x0,γ)

so the probability that |x̄n − x0| > γ remains 1/2 regardless of n. The median, on the other hand (if the density at the median p(m) > 0), shrinks approximately with standard deviation 1/(2p(m)√n): σ√(π/2)/√n for the Gaussian and πγ/(2√n) for the Cauchy.

Numerical computation: the cumulative distribution functions are computed with the regularized incomplete beta and gamma functions (continued fractions), the error function, and arctan (shared library MLCore). The “numerical-integration check” on the screen is a value obtained by tanh–sinh (double-exponential) numerical integration of the density over small intervals separated by quantiles, and is meant to be compared with the difference of the cumulative distribution function. The sample mean in ② uses compensated (Kahan) summation, and the median is a running median using two heaps.

Data and random-number conditions

  • The samples in ① and the paths in ② are synthetic data for teaching purposes drawn from the chosen distribution (not measured values of a real material). Quantities are dimensionless (the s of the Weibull distribution can be read as a strength in the same unit as the scale s0).
  • The random numbers come from a seeded generator (mulberry32). The default seed is 20261112 for both ① and ②. Gaussian random numbers are made with the Box–Muller method, Cauchy random numbers with tan(π(u − 1/2)), and t random numbers with z/√(g/ν) (z is a standard Gaussian and g is χ2 with ν degrees of freedom).
  • The sample in ① is redrawn from the same seed each time a parameter is changed (with the same seed it is made from the same sequence of uniform random numbers).
  • The random numbers of path j in ② are made from the seed and j, so the initial part of each path is the same even if you change the upper limit of n or the number of paths. Starting over with the same seed gives the same curves.
  • Limits: 10000 samples in ①; in ②, n ≤ 105 and up to 20 paths (at most 2×106 samples × 2 distributions). The computation in ② proceeds in 12 ms slices and can be stopped midway. The curves are recorded at points equally spaced in log n (151, 180, and 197 points when the upper limit of n is 103, 104, and 105).

Simplifications

  • The parameter ranges are restricted (for example, μ ∈ [−5, 5] and σ ∈ [0.1, 5] for the Gaussian, and N ≤ 200 for the binomial). The degrees of freedom of the t distribution are chosen from 17 values between 0.5 and 1000.
  • The histogram in ① counts only inside the plotted range; the number outside the range is given in the readout.
  • In ②, the location is fixed at 0 and the scale (σ of the Gaussian, γ of the Cauchy, s of the t) is changed with one common slider. The FWHM of the Gaussian and Cauchy is not matched (the scale parameters are matched).
  • The median band ±1/(2p(m)√n) is an approximation for large n.

Conditions under which results become unstable

  • For samples from the Cauchy distribution or from a t distribution with ν ≤ 2, very large values (105 or more) occasionally appear, and the sample mean and sample variance jump greatly. This is not a computational error but a property of the distribution.
  • For Gamma (a < 1), Beta (a < 1 or b < 1), and Weibull (m < 1), the density diverges at the edge of the domain (the area is finite). In the figure, the part above the frame is cut off. The numerical integration over an interval where the density diverges at the edge may be off by about 10−8, because double precision limits how close one can get to the edge.
  • With a logarithmic vertical axis, very small tail probabilities (below about 10−300) are rounded to 0 and the line breaks.
  • If NaN or infinity appears, the computation is stopped and the reason is displayed.

Short questions to answer after operating

  1. For the Gaussian distribution 𝒩(x | 0, 1) and the Cauchy distribution with matched FWHM (γ = √(2 ln 2) ≈ 1.177), what are P(−1 ≤ x ≤ 1) and P(|x| > 3), respectively?
    Sample answers

    P(−1 ≤ x ≤ 1) is 0.6827 for the Gaussian and (2/π) arctan(1/1.177) = 0.4482 for the Cauchy. P(|x| > 3) is 0.0027 for the Gaussian against 0.2381 for the Cauchy, about 88 times larger (for |x| > 5, 5.7×10−7 and 0.147). Even with the same peak width, the Cauchy distribution keeps much of its probability in the tails. Fitting a spectral line shape with a Gaussian misses the intensity in these tails.

  2. Explain, using the partial-integral values in the readout, why the mean of the Cauchy distribution (γ = 1) does not exist.
    Sample answers

    Truncating ∫|x − x0| p(x) dx at ±R gives (γ/π) ln(1 + R2/γ2), which is 1.47, 2.93, 4.40, and 8.80 for R = 10, 100, 1000, and 106; it keeps growing by about 1.47 each time R is multiplied by 10 (logarithmic divergence). Because p(x) decreases only as 1/x2, the integral of x p(x) ~ 1/x does not converge.

  3. With the defaults of ② (n ≤ 104, 5 paths, seed 20261112, scale 1), how widely are the sample means at n = 104 scattered for the Gaussian and for the Cauchy? How does this correspond to theory?
    Sample answers

    The Gaussian values are −0.0035, 0.0006, −0.0087, 0.0075, 0.0137, which stay within about the theoretical standard deviation σ/√n = 0.01. The Cauchy values are 0.769, −1.83, 0.118, 1.35, −0.910, and 2 of the 5 paths have |x̄| > γ = 1 (at n = 103 there is even a path with the large value 5.33). x̄n follows Cauchy(x0, γ) regardless of n, and the probability of |x̄n − x0| > γ is always 1/2. The medians of the same samples are 0.0057, 0.0022, 0.0052, 0.0005, 0.0036, which lie within the range πγ/(2√n) = 0.0157.

  4. For the t distribution with ν = 1, 2, 3, 30, what happens to the mean and the variance?
    Sample answers

    ν = 1: neither the mean nor the variance exists (same as the Cauchy distribution). ν = 2: the mean μ exists but the variance diverges. ν = 3: variance s2ν/(ν − 2) = 3. ν = 30: 30/28 = 1.071, approaching the Gaussian distribution (variance 1).
