Latent Variables — k-means, the EM Algorithm, and PCA

Advance k-means, which splits unlabeled data into “similar” groups, and the EM algorithm for a Gaussian mixture one step at a time, and check that the responsibilities, means, covariances, and log-likelihood change exactly as the formulas say. In the second half, use PCA (principal component analysis) to check numerically that the direction of maximum projected variance is an eigenvector of the covariance matrix, that the reconstruction error equals the sum of the discarded eigenvalues, and that principal components do not coincide with the physical component spectra.

Procedure and what to observe

  1. With ① at its defaults (data “Overlapping,” K = 2, Gaussian-mixture EM), press “E step.” What to see: the marker shapes and color depths change, and the readout shows the responsibilities γnk of the representative points A, B, and C. The responsibility of point B, which lies between the two clusters, is between 0 and 1. Each row sums to 1.
  2. Then press “M step.” What to see: the means and ellipses move, and the log-likelihood ln p(X | θ) increases (see the plot below and the before and after values in “Putting the current numbers into the formula”). Check πk = Nk/N numerically.
  3. Press “Run to convergence.” What to see: the log-likelihood never decreases from one iteration to the next, increases, and eventually levels off.
  4. Set the method to “k-means,” choose the data “Elongated,” and press “Run to convergence”; then switch to Gaussian-mixture EM and do the same. What to see: the k-means boundary (the edge between the pale colored regions) is the perpendicular bisector of the two means, and it cuts across the elongated clusters. The Gaussian mixture splits along the clusters by using the orientation of the ellipses. The vertical axis of the lower plot is the objective J for k-means and the log-likelihood for EM, which are different quantities.
  5. With the data “Separated” (K = 3), set the k-means initial-value seed to 8 and press “Run to convergence.” What to see: it stops at a local minimum (J ≈ 411.6) in which one component takes charge of two clusters and one cluster is split between two components. With seed 1, J ≈ 83.45 (dependence on the initial values).
  6. Choose the data “Degenerate example,” turn off “Use variance floor,” and press “Run to convergence.” What to see: component 2 shrinks onto the isolated point at the upper right, the log-likelihood keeps rising, and the run stops with a stated reason once the variance falls below the stopping threshold. When you turn “Use variance floor” back on (the run restarts from the beginning with the same seed), it converges to a finite value. The gray dashed line in the lower plot is the previous trajectory.
  7. In ②, choose “2D point cloud” and move the angle φ. Press “Align to principal axis.” What to see: the angle at which uTSu is largest is the angle of the first principal axis, and there uTSu = λ1 and the reconstruction error = λ2.
  8. Set “Scale factor c for the unit of x2” to 10 or 0.1, and then turn on “Standardize.” What to see: merely changing the unit changes the direction of the principal axis. After standardization the eigenvalues are 1 ± r (r is the correlation coefficient) and no longer depend on the scale factor.
  9. In ②, choose “Spectra.” What to see: with 2 components there are two large eigenvalues, and with 3 components there are three; the rest line up at the height of the top of the noise-level band. The loadings differ in shape from the pure-component spectra and also take negative values. When you change “Number of principal components k used for reconstruction,” the mean reconstruction error matches the sum of the discarded eigenvalues.

Formulas and algorithms computed

k-means: rnk ∈ {0, 1} indicates whether point n is assigned to component k.

J=∑n=1N∑k=1Krnk‖xn−μk‖2

E (assignment): assign each point to the component with the nearest mean. M (update): set μk to the mean of the points assigned to it. Neither half step increases J. The algorithm has converged when the assignments no longer change.

The EM algorithm for the Gaussian mixture:

p(x)=∑k=1Kπk𝒩(x∣μk,Σk),γnk=πk𝒩(xn∣μk,Σk)∑jπj𝒩(xn∣μj,Σj)Nk=∑nγnk,μk=1Nk∑nγnkxn,πk=NkNΣk=1Nk∑nγnk(xn−μk)(xn−μk)T(using the new μk)lnp(X∣θ)=∑n=1Nln∑k=1Kπk𝒩(xn∣μk,Σk)

The E step computes the responsibilities γnk, and the M step computes πk, μk, and Σk. The responsibilities and the log-likelihood are computed with a log-sum-exp of the values ln πk + ln 𝒩 to avoid overflow. ln 𝒩 is obtained from the Cholesky decomposition of the 2×2 covariance matrix, and no inverse matrix is formed. The log-likelihood never decreases from one iteration to the next (the ELBO and KL decomposition in Section 17.7 of the text).

Variance floor: when “Use variance floor” is on, the eigenvalues λ of Σk obtained in the M step are replaced by max(λ, c) (the eigenvectors are unchanged). With the mean held fixed, this is the covariance matrix that maximizes the likelihood under the constraint “eigenvalues ≥ c,” so the log-likelihood does not decrease in this case either. When it is off, an update whose smallest eigenvalue falls below 10−10 is not accepted; the run stops and the reason is displayed.

PCA: take the singular value decomposition of the matrix X (N rows, D columns) from which the data mean x̄ has been subtracted. Variance is defined with 1/N (the eigenvalues differ by a factor of N/(N − 1) from the convention of dividing by N − 1).

S=1N∑n=1N(xn−x¯)(xn−x¯)T,Su=λuX=USsvVT,λi=si2N,tni=viT(xn−x¯)x^n=x¯+∑i=1ktnivi,1N∑n=1N‖xn−x^n‖2=∑i>kλiTwo dimensions: u=(cosφ,sinφ)T,uTSu=S11cos2φ+2S12cosφsinφ+S22sin2φ

All eigenvalues and loadings are computed from the SVD (one-sided Jacobi method). The covariance matrix S is formed only to show uTSu on the two-dimensional screen.

Data and random-number conditions

  • The points in ① are synthetic data for teaching purposes (two dimensionless features, for example two standardized measurements). Each cluster is generated as x = m + R(θ)(s1z1, s2z2)T, with z1, z2 ~ 𝒩(0, 1) (independent; R(θ) is the rotation by angle θ).
    • Separated: three clusters (1/3 each), m = (−2.0, −1.2), (2.0, −1.0), (0.0, 2.0), s1 = s2 = 0.55.
    • Overlapping: m = (−1.0, −0.4), s = (0.9, 0.6), θ = 20° (55%) and m = (1.2, 0.6), s = (0.7, 0.7) (45%).
    • Elongated: m = (−0.7, 0.7) and (0.7, −0.7), s = (1.7, 0.22), θ = 45° (50% each).
    • Different sizes: m = (−0.6, 0.0), s1 = s2 = 1.25 (80%) and m = (1.6, 0.4), s1 = s2 = 0.22 (20%).
    • Degenerate example: m = (0, 0), s = (0.8, 0.6), θ = 30° for one cluster (N − 1 points), plus one isolated point (3.6, 3.0).
  • Initial values: K data points are chosen without repetition as the means (the first K after shuffling with the initial-value seed), the covariance matrices are (tr Sdata/2) I, and the mixing coefficients are 1/K. k-means and EM start from the same initial means. In the degenerate example, the initial mean of the last component is placed on the isolated point (initial values that cause degeneracy are chosen on purpose).
  • The representative points A, B, and C are the data points nearest to three positions fixed for each data set (cluster centers, the gap between clusters, and so on).
  • The two-dimensional point cloud in ② is also synthetic data for teaching purposes: m = (3.0, 1.5), s = (1.4, 0.45), θ = 35°. The units are “the unit of measurement 1” and “the unit of measurement 2,” and the scale factor c corresponds to changing the unit of measurement 2 (x2 → c x2).
  • The spectra in ② are also synthetic data for teaching purposes (Raman-like; not a real material). They have 200 points at Raman shifts ν = 200–1100 cm−1. Each pure component is a sum of Gaussian peaks sk(ν) = Σ h exp[−(ν − ν0)2/(2w2)]: component 1 has (ν0, w, h) = (320, 18, 1.0) and (610, 25, 0.55), component 2 has (480, 20, 0.9) and (820, 30, 0.7), and component 3 has (700, 35, 0.8) and (1000, 22, 0.5) (ν0 and w are in cm−1, and h is in arbitrary units). Spectrum n is xn(ν) = Σk In fnk sk(ν) + σ ε, with overall intensity In ~ U(0.7, 1.3), mixing ratios (fn1, …) ~ Dirichlet(1, …, 1), and ε ~ 𝒩(0, 1) (independent).
  • Random numbers come from a seeded generator (mulberry32). The default seeds are 20261113 for the data in ①, 1 for the initial values in ①, and 20261113 for the point cloud and spectra in ②. Starting over with the same seed gives the same result.
  • Upper limits: the data in ① has 10–300 points, K is 1–6, and there are at most 1000 iterations. EM is regarded as converged when the increase in the log-likelihood falls to 10−10×(|ln p| + 1) or less. The point cloud in ② has 2–300 points.

Simplifications

  • ① is two-dimensional only. The hand calculation in Section 17.6 of the text is one-dimensional, but the formulas are the same.
  • The initial values are only a random choice of data points (refinements such as k-means++ are not used). The practical method of building the EM initial values from a k-means result is not used either.
  • The number of components K is chosen by hand. For selection by BIC and similar criteria, see Section 17.10 of the text.
  • The synthetic spectra in ② contain no baseline (background) or peak shifts. The noise is Gaussian and independent of the wavenumber.

Conditions under which results become unstable

  • Without a variance floor, a component may take charge of only one point (or points on a single line), its covariance matrix becomes nearly singular, and the log-likelihood grows without bound (degeneracy). This simulator does not accept an update whose smallest eigenvalue falls below 10−10; it stops and displays the reason.
  • A component that takes almost no points (Nk ≈ 0) can appear (when K is large). In that case its mean and covariance keep their previous values, and a notice appears in the readout. A k-means component to which no point is assigned is treated the same way.
  • Both the EM algorithm and k-means can stop at a local optimum. Changing the initial-value seed changes the result.
  • In PCA, when the eigenvalues are nearly equal (a nearly circular point cloud), the direction of the principal axis wobbles with noise. With only 2 data points the second eigenvalue is 0.

Short questions to answer after operating

  1. From the default state you pressed “E step” and “M step” once each. Did the log-likelihood increase? What is π1 + π2 after the M step?
    Sample answers

    The log-likelihood increases from −483.67 to −410.19 (default data of 150 points, data seed 20261113, initial-value seed 1). Since πk = Nk/N and the responsibilities of each point sum to 1, ΣkNk = N, and therefore π1 + π2 = 1.

  2. Why does k-means fail to split the clusters correctly for the data “Elongated”? Why can the Gaussian mixture split them?
    Sample answers

    k-means assigns each point to the nearest mean (in Euclidean distance), so the boundary is the perpendicular bisector (a straight line) of the two means, and it implicitly assumes that every component is a round cluster of the same size. For an elongated cluster, a far-away point along the cluster’s direction ends up closer to the mean of the neighboring cluster. The Gaussian mixture measures the Mahalanobis distance with the covariance matrix of each component, so it can represent the orientation and elongation of a cluster.

  3. Why does the log-likelihood keep rising when the variance floor is not used? Does that mean the model has become “good”?
    Sample answers

    If you place a mean at a single point xn and let the variance σ2 approach 0, the density at that point, 𝒩(xn | xn, σ2I) = 1/(2πσ2), grows without bound, while the other points are explained by another component, so the log-likelihood has no upper limit. This does not represent the structure of the data; it is a pathological solution of maximum likelihood estimation. It is prevented by a variance floor, regularization, or MAP estimation. The smaller the floor c, the larger the log-likelihood at convergence.

  4. In the 2D mode of ②, how does the angle of the first principal axis change when the scale factor c goes from 1 to 10? What happens with standardization?
    Sample answers

    For the default data (100 points, seed 20261113), the angle is about 36.0° for c = 1, about 85.0° for c = 10 (nearly the direction of x2), and about 3.2° for c = 0.1 (nearly the direction of x1). This is because the variance of x2 is multiplied by c2 and dominates the total variance. Standardization turns the analysis into PCA of the correlation matrix; in two dimensions the principal axis is always 45° or 135°, the eigenvalues are 1 ± r (here r = 0.697, so 1.697 and 0.303), and they do not depend on the scale factor c. Because the result depends on the choice of units, consider standardization for quantities whose units are not comparable.
