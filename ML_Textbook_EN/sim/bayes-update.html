Bayesian Update — The Prior Becomes the Posterior with Each Observation

For a success rate μ or a mean μ, check how the knowledge held before seeing data (the prior) changes each time one observation is added (the posterior). Compare formulas and numbers to confirm the difference among maximum likelihood, MAP estimation, and the posterior mean; that the influence of the prior fades as data accumulate; that updating one observation at a time gives the same result as updating all at once; and that the predictive uncertainty has two components.

Procedure and what to observe

  1. In ①, set the “Prior type” to “Uniform Beta(1,1)” and press “Add 1 success” three times. What to look for: the difference among the maximum-likelihood estimate m/N = 1, MAP = 1, and the posterior mean = 4/5 = 0.8. MAP is the point of maximum posterior density, whereas the posterior mean is the center of mass of the distribution, so for Beta(4, 1), which keeps some probability on the side μ < 1, only the posterior mean falls below 1. In the formulas, MAP (a+m−1)/(a+b+N−2) adds a − 1 and b − 1 (0 pseudo-counts for the uniform prior), while the posterior mean (a+m)/(a+b+N) adds a and b (1 success and 1 failure for the uniform prior; Laplace’s rule of succession).
  2. Press “Add 1 failure”, then cancel it with “Undo last”. What to look for: a single failure makes the posterior density at the right end (μ = 1) equal to 0 and lowers MAP from 1 to 0.75.
  3. After “Clear data”, set the prior to “Strong Beta(20,20)” and press “Draw 10” several times. What to look for: the maximum-likelihood estimate moves toward the true μ (default 0.70), while the posterior mean is pulled back toward 0.5. In the lower figure, as N grows the posterior mean approaches the maximum-likelihood estimate and the credible interval narrows (the data weight w = N/(a+b+N) approaches 1).
  4. Press “Compare sequential and batch updates”. What to look for: the last entry of the parameter sequence updated one observation at a time agrees with the batch Beta(a+m, b+l). The density obtained by applying Bayes’ theorem numerically one observation at a time on a grid also agrees with the analytical batch posterior to within rounding error.
  5. In ②, press “Observe 10” several times. What to look for: the posterior becomes narrower (σN decreases), but the width of the predictive distribution never becomes narrower than σ. In the lower figure and the breakdown bar, only σN2 decreases.
  6. In ②, reduce the prior standard deviation σ0 to 0.1 and move the prior mean μ0 away from the true value. What to look for: a strong prior (small σ0) holds the posterior mean near μ0 while there are few data.

With the keyboard, Tab moves to buttons and sliders, Enter (or Space) presses a button, and the arrow keys move a slider. Tabs can be switched with ← and →.

Bayes’ theorem is applied to the success rate μ (viewed as a function of μ, the evidence p(𝒟) in the denominator is a normalizing constant).

The 95% credible interval runs from the 2.5% point to the 97.5% point of the posterior (an equal-tailed interval). The regularized incomplete beta function Ix(a, b) is computed by a continued fraction, and its inverse is found with Newton’s method and bisection.

When updating one observation at a time, the previous posterior is used as the next prior. A success just adds 1 to a and a failure adds 1 to b, so the result is the same as the batch result regardless of order. For the numerical check on screen, ln μ or ln(1 − μ) is added one observation at a time to the log density on a grid of μ (2001 points), and the finally normalized density is compared with the analytical batch solution.

For the mean of a Gaussian in ② (noise variance σ2 known, prior 𝒩(μ | μ0, σ02)), completing the square gives

1σN2=1σ02+Nσ2,μN=σN2(μ0σ02+Nx¯σ2)p(x∣𝒟)=𝒩(x∣μN,σ2+σN2)

Of the predictive variance, σ2 comes from noise (aleatoric uncertainty) and σN2 comes from not knowing μ (epistemic uncertainty).

Data and random-number conditions

  • The observations from “Draw from true μ” are synthetic data for teaching purposes: x = 1 (success, e.g., a sample passes) is drawn with probability μtrue and x = 0 (failure) with probability 1 − μtrue, independently. The default is μtrue = 0.70. There are no units.
  • The observations in ② are also synthetic data for teaching purposes: x = μtrue + σ ε, ε ~ 𝒩(0, 1) (independent). The defaults are μtrue = 1.5, σ = 1, and prior μ0 = 0, σ0 = 2. The quantities are dimensionless (for example, a measured value expressed as a deviation from a reference value).
  • Random numbers come from a seeded generator (mulberry32). The default seed is 20261112 for both ① and ②. Starting over with the same seed gives the same sequence of observations. Any seed operation (start over, change, reset to default) clears all observations and draws again from the beginning.
  • Upper limits: up to 2000 observations in ①, and 1000 in ②.

Simplifications

  • In ①, the observations are assumed independent and the success rate μ constant (close to the case of samples from the same lot under identical conditions). In a real process, μ may change over time.
  • In ②, the noise variance σ2 is assumed known. If σ2 is also unknown, the posterior predictive distribution is a Student’s t distribution (Section 4.9 of the text, Chapter 3, Section 3.9).
  • The credible interval is an equal-tailed interval (not a highest-density interval).
  • The prior parameters are restricted to a, b ∈ [0.5, 30], σ0 ∈ [0.1, 3], and σ ∈ [0.2, 3].

Conditions under which results become unstable

  • When a + m < 1 or b + l < 1, the posterior density diverges at an endpoint and the MAP formula cannot be used (the screen shows “no interior maximum”). When one of them is exactly 1, the endpoint (μ = 0 or 1) is the maximum and the formula value also coincides with that endpoint. With the uniform Beta(1,1) left as is (a+m = b+l = 1), the maximum is not a single point (the screen shows “not unique”).
  • For N = 0, the maximum-likelihood estimate m/N is undefined (0/0).
  • With many observations (N ≳ 1000), the posterior becomes very narrow and the vertical axis of the figure stretches a great deal. The numerical computation is done in logarithms, so no overflow occurs.
  • If the true μ is set near 0 or 1, long runs with only successes (or only failures) appear, and the maximum-likelihood estimate sticks to 0 or 1 (the posterior mean does not).

Short questions to answer after operating

  1. Under the uniform prior Beta(1,1), 3 successes were observed in 3 trials. What are the maximum-likelihood estimate, MAP, and posterior mean? Why does only the posterior mean differ?
    Sample answer

    Maximum likelihood 3/3 = 1; MAP (1+3−1)/(1+1+3−2) = 3/3 = 1; posterior mean (1+3)/(1+1+3) = 4/5 = 0.8. Under a uniform prior the prior density is constant, so the point of maximum posterior density (MAP) coincides with the point of maximum likelihood. The posterior mean is the center of mass of the whole distribution; since probability remains on the side μ < 1, it is smaller than 1. It is also the probability p(x=1 | 𝒟) that the next trial succeeds.

  2. With the priors Beta(20,20) and Beta(1,1), the same observations (7 successes, 3 failures) are entered. What is the posterior mean in each case? What happens if the observations are multiplied by 100 (700 successes, 300 failures)?
    Sample answer

    N = 10: 27/50 = 0.54 for Beta(20,20) and 8/12 ≈ 0.667 for Beta(1,1). N = 1000: 720/1040 ≈ 0.692 and 701/1002 ≈ 0.700 (the maximum-likelihood estimate is 0.7 in both cases). The data weight w = N/(a+b+N) rises from 10/50 = 0.2 to 1000/1040 ≈ 0.96, and the influence of the prior fades.

  3. Why do the result of updating one observation at a time and the result of updating all at once agree? What happens if the order of observations is changed?
    Sample answer

    If the observations are independent, the likelihood is the product of the individual likelihoods, and multiplying the posterior after k observations by the likelihood of the (k+1)-th and normalizing gives the same result as normalizing the full product. In the Beta–Bernoulli model a success just adds 1 to a and a failure adds 1 to b, so even if the order is changed the final result is Beta(a+m, b+l) (the intermediate steps differ).

  4. In ② with σ = 1 and σ0 = 1, what are σN2 and the predictive variance for N = 0, 1, 9, and 99? However many observations are added, below what value can the predictive variance never fall?
    Sample answer

    σN2 = 1/(1+N) , so 1, 0.5, 0.1, 0.01. The predictive variance 1 + σN2 is 2, 1.5, 1.1, 1.01, and never falls below the noise variance σ2 = 1. Only the component that comes from lack of knowledge can be reduced by adding data.
