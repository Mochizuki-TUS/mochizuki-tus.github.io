Neural Network — Forward Propagation, Backpropagation, and Training

With a small network, follow one step at a time, with numbers, the forward pass that computes the output from the input, backpropagation that distributes the gradient of the loss from the output side, and the update that moves the weights along the gradient. Next, change the number of hidden units, the activation function, the learning rate, the minibatch, and the weight decay to observe how training proceeds and the “basis functions that move to fit the data,” and finally check that the backpropagation gradient agrees with the finite-difference approximation.

Procedure and What to Observe

  1. In ①, press “Forward.” What to look at: a(1) = (0.45, 0.8), z(1) = (0.421899, 0.664037), a(2) = −0.0421378, ŷ = 0.489467, L = 0.714438. Although y = 1, ŷ is below 0.5, so the loss is a little larger than ln 2 ≈ 0.693.
  2. Press “Backward.” What to look at: the output error δ(2) = ŷ − y = −0.510533 is multiplied by the weights W(2) = (1.0, −1.0) and the tanh slope 1 − z2 and distributed to the hidden layer, giving δ(1) = (−0.419659, 0.285416). The sign of δ for hidden unit 2 is reversed because W(2)2 is negative.
  3. Press “Update weights.” What to look at: each weight moves by −η∂L/∂w, and the loss falls from 0.714438 to 0.343888. If you repeat from “Forward” again, the loss falls further.
  4. In ①, set the learning rate η to 5 and do the same. Next set y = 0. What to look at: when η is large, a single update overshoots. Changing y flips the sign of δ(2), and the signs of all gradients reverse.
  5. In ②, press “Run” with the task left at “Regression (Data A),” and change the number of hidden units H to 1, 3, and 8. What to look at: in the lower figure, the hidden-unit outputs zj(s) are “moving basis functions” whose positions and slopes change as training proceeds. Switching to “Contribution” shows that their superposition is the prediction curve above. Data A is smooth, so with the default seed the training RMSE after 2000 epochs is 0.945, 0.927, and 0.919 % for H = 1, 3, and 8, about the noise standard deviation of 1 %, and the validation RMSE (1.24, 1.24, 1.22 %) hardly changes either. Increasing H increases the shapes that can be represented, but this data does not need them.
  6. In ②, set the task to “Concentric circles” and train for about 2000 epochs (default H = 3). What to look at: the training loss keeps falling (0.0153 at 2000 epochs), but the validation loss reaches its minimum of 0.104 at epoch 806 and then starts to rise, reaching 0.360 at 2000 epochs (overfitting). Stopping at the epoch of minimum validation loss (◆) is early stopping. With H = 4 and the weight decay λ set to 0, 10−3, and 10−2, the validation loss at 2000 epochs is 0.342, 0.218, and 0.308; when λ is too large, the training loss also stops falling (0.0129, 0.0488, 0.214).
  7. In ②, set the learning rate near its maximum and the minibatch to 1. What to look at: the loss oscillates, and when it diverges the computation stops and the reason is displayed. Set “Initial value” to “All 0” and run. What to look at: with tanh the weights stay at 0 and do not move, and the prediction is a constant (the mean of the training data). With sigmoid the weights do move, but every hidden unit keeps the same value, so increasing H does not increase the shapes that can be represented (symmetry).
  8. In ③, vary ε from 10−12 to 10−1. What to look at: the error of the central difference is smallest (about 10−12) near ε ≈ 10−5; if ε is made too small the error grows because of rounding error, and if it is made too large the error grows because of truncation error (a V shape). On the large-ε side, the central-difference error grows with slope 2 and the forward difference with slope 1.

Formulas and Algorithm

A network with one hidden layer and one output. Input x ∈ ℝD, number of hidden units H, W(1) ∈ ℝH×D, W(2) ∈ ℝ1×H, activation function h(·) (tanh, ReLU, sigmoid).

a(1)=W(1)x+b(1),z(1)=h(a(1)),a(2)=W(2)z(1)+b(2)y^=σ(a(2))=11+e−a(2)(classification),y^=a(2)(regression)

The loss for one data point is the cross-entropy for classification (y ∈ {0, 1}) and half the squared error for regression. To avoid ln 0, the classification loss computes ln(1 + ea) in a form that does not overflow.

ℓ=−[ylny^+(1−y)ln(1−y^)]=ln(1+ea(2))−ya(2),ℓ=12(y^−y)2L=1|ℬ|∑n∈ℬℓn+λ2(‖W(1)‖2+‖W(2)‖2)

ℬ is the minibatch (a set of data indices) and λ is the weight-decay coefficient (not applied to the biases). Backpropagation finds δ ≡ ∂ℓ/∂a in order from the output side.

δ(2)=y^−y,δ(1)=h′(a(1))⊙(W(2)Tδ(2))∂ℓ∂W(l)=δ(l)z(l−1)T,∂ℓ∂b(l)=δ(l),z(0)=xh′(a)=1−tanh2a(tanh),σ(a)[1−σ(a)](sigmoid),1(a>0),0(a≤0)(ReLU)

The minibatch gradient is the mean of the per-sample gradients plus λW. The update is gradient descent w ← w − η ∂L/∂w (η is the learning rate). At the start of each epoch the order of the training data is shuffled randomly, and the weights are updated for each minibatch of size B (with “All,” one update per epoch).

In the gradient check in ③, from the loss with each parameter w alone moved by ±ε

∂L∂w≈L(w+ε)−L(w−ε)2ε(central difference; error O(ε2)),L(w+ε)−L(w)ε(forward difference; error O(ε))

we form these and compare them with the backpropagation value. The per-component relative error is |gbackprop − gdiff| / max(|gbackprop|, |gdiff|, 10−8), and the relative error as a vector is ‖gbackprop − gdiff‖ / max(‖gbackprop‖, ‖gdiff‖).

Data and Random-Number Conditions

  • ① uses one data point (default x = (1.0, 0.5), y = 1) and the same weights as Chapter 15, Section 15.3 of the main text: W(1) = ((0.5, −0.3), (0.8, 0.2)) (the first row is hidden unit 1), b(1) = (0.1, −0.1), W(2) = (1.0, −1.0), b(2) = 0.2, and learning rate η = 0.5. No random numbers are used.
  • The regression in ② uses synthetic data for teaching purposes, “Data A (firing temperature and relative density)”: s ~ U(−1, 1), firing temperature T = 1300 °C + 200 °C × s, relative density y = g(s) + ε, g(s) = 88 + 8s − 5s2 + 2 sin 3s (%), ε ~ 𝒩(0, 1.02) (%). The number of training data points is 5 to 50 (default 20). Training uses the targets standardized by the training mean ȳ and standard deviation sy, (y − ȳ)/sy, and the figure is drawn after converting back to % (the loss is in standardized units). This is not data of a real material.
  • The classification in ② also uses synthetic data for teaching purposes (dimensionless 2D points, with y ∈ {0, 1} in equal halves). Concentric circles: add 𝒩(0, 0.12) to a radius of 1.0 (y = 0) or 0.5 (y = 1), with the angle uniform. Two moons: y = 0 is (cos t, sin t) and y = 1 is (1 − cos t, 0.5 − sin t), t ~ U(0, π); subtract (0.5, 0.25) and add 𝒩(0, 0.12) to each coordinate. XOR: centers (±0.5, ±0.5), the quadrants with x1x2 > 0 are y = 1, and 𝒩(0, 0.22) is added to each coordinate. The number of training data points is 40 to 200 (default 100).
  • The validation data are a separate 200 points from the same distribution as the training data (a separate random-number stream). Changing the number of training data points does not change the validation data.
  • The random numbers come from a seeded generator (mulberry32). The “data seed” (default 20261112) determines the training and validation data, and the “random-number seed” (default 20261112) determines the initial weights and the minibatch order. Starting over with the same seed gives the same training history.
  • Initial values: for tanh and sigmoid, the Xavier (Glorot) normal distribution (variance 2/(fanin + fanout)); for ReLU, the He normal distribution (variance 2/fanin); the output layer uses Xavier; biases are 0. “All 0” can also be selected.
  • Computational limits: 16 hidden units and 5000 epochs. The ε figure in ③ has 34 points from 10−12 to 10−1.

Simplifications

  • There is one hidden layer and one output (multiclass softmax is not treated).
  • The only optimizer is plain (stochastic) gradient descent. Momentum, Adam, learning-rate decay, and dropout are not used.
  • The training mean and standard deviation are used to standardize the regression targets (no information from the validation data is used).
  • The numbers in the figure of ① are rounded to 4 significant digits for display (the computation is in double precision; the table has 6 digits).

Conditions Under Which Results Become Unstable

  • When the learning rate is large, the loss oscillates and diverges. When the loss becomes NaN or infinite, or exceeds 106, training stops and the reason is displayed. The smaller the minibatch, the larger the scatter of the gradient, and the more easily training becomes unstable even at the same learning rate.
  • When sigmoid is used in the hidden layer and the input weights grow large, h′ ≤ 1/4 becomes small and training slows down (the entrance to vanishing gradients). With ReLU, a unit that stays at a ≤ 0 has gradient 0 and no longer moves.
  • If the training data in classification can be separated perfectly, then without weight decay the weights keep growing and the training loss keeps approaching 0. The validation loss may rise (overfitting).
  • In the gradient check, if ε is made too small, the difference of L(w ± ε) is buried in rounding error (about 10−16 × L). For components whose gradient is inherently close to 0, the per-component relative error looks large (judge by the relative error as a vector). For an ε that straddles the ReLU kink a = 0, the difference is not the correct derivative.
  • If the initial values are all 0, then with tanh and ReLU, z(1) = h(0) = 0 gives ∂L/∂W(2) = 0, and W(2) = 0 gives δ(1) = 0, so none of the weights move (only b(2) moves, and the prediction is a constant). With sigmoid, W(1) moves, but every hidden unit keeps the same value.

Short Questions to Answer After Operating

  1. With the default values in ①, why are the signs of δ(1) for hidden units 1 and 2 opposite? Also, why is ∂L/∂W(1)12 exactly half of ∂L/∂W(1)11?
    Sample answers

    δ(1)j = (1 − zj2) W(2)j δ(2), and 1 − z2 > 0 and δ(2) = −0.510533 are common to both, so the sign is determined by W(2)j = +1.0, −1.0 (−0.419659 and +0.285416). ∂L/∂W(1)ji = δ(1)j xi, and x2/x1 = 0.5/1.0 (−0.209829 = 0.5 × (−0.419659)).

  2. In ①, one update changes the loss from 0.714438 to 0.343888. When η is made very small, what determines how much the loss decreases?
    Sample answers

    By the first-order Taylor expansion, L(w − ηg) ≈ L − η‖g‖2, so the decrease is proportional to the square of the gradient magnitude, ‖g‖2 = 1.00151 (over all 9 parameters). The actual decrease at η = 0.5, 0.370550, is smaller than 0.5 × 1.00151 ≈ 0.501, so it deviates from the first-order approximation (η = 0.5 cannot be called “small”).

  3. In ② with concentric circles, why does the validation loss rise while the training loss keeps falling? Where is it good to stop training?
    Sample answers

    Once the training data can be separated perfectly, the training loss keeps falling as the weights are enlarged to raise the confidence even for training points near the boundary, but if a validation point is misclassified with high confidence, the cross-entropy becomes large. Either stop at the epoch of minimum validation loss (◆ in the figure) (early stopping), or use the weight decay λ to keep the weights from growing.

  4. In ③, when ε is made smaller, why does the error decrease up to a point and then increase beyond it?
    Sample answers

    The truncation error of the central difference is about (ε2/6)|L‴| and decreases with ε, but L(w + ε) and L(w − ε) each carry a rounding error of about 10−16 × L, and dividing it by 2ε makes the rounding error grow in proportion to 1/ε. The error is smallest near ε ≈ 10−5, where the two balance.
