Sequence Models — Hidden Markov Models and the Kalman Filter

Whether a machine is “normal” or “degraded” (the hidden state) cannot be seen directly; only sensor values (observations) arrive at each time step. From the sequence of observations, estimate the hidden states. Check with the numbers plugged into the formulas: the difference between filtering by the forward algorithm (uses the observations up to that time) and smoothing γt that also uses the backward algorithm (uses the observations at all times); the fact that the most probable state sequence (Viterbi algorithm) can differ from the sequence of the most probable state at each time; and that a continuous hidden state leads to the Kalman filter.

Procedure and what to observe

  1. In ①, look at the figure with the defaults (discrete three-valued, π1 = 0.95, A11 = 0.97, A22 = 0.95, separation s = 0.7, T = 100; the true state is degraded from time 69 to 86). What to look at: the dashed line (filtering) jumps around with each new observation and, even in a normal interval, wrongly exceeds 0.5 when “High” continues. The solid line (smoothing γt) also uses later observations, so it is smooth and catches the start of the degradation early. In the readout the accuracy is 0.90 for filtering, 0.99 for smoothing, and 0.98 for Viterbi.
  2. Press “Advance time” or repeat “Step 1 time step.” What to look at: in “Putting the current numbers into the formula,” the “prediction” (the previous filtering probability multiplied by the transition probabilities Aij and summed) is multiplied by the emission probability and normalized, which gives the filtering probability at that time.
  3. Set the separation s to 0, then to 1. What to look at: at s = 0 the observations say nothing about the state, so γt becomes the prior probability determined by the transitions alone, and the accuracy drops. As s increases, the estimate approaches the true state.
  4. Switch to “Gaussian” and change the degraded mean Δ. What to look at: when Δ is small (the two distributions overlap) the estimate becomes ambiguous, and when Δ is 3 or more it is almost always right.
  5. Switch to “Hand calculation (T = 3).” What to look at: α3(1) + α3(2) and the sum of the probabilities p(x1:3, z1:3) of the 8 state sequences give the same value p(x1:3) = 0.036280. γt agrees with the value marginalized from the full enumeration.
  6. In hand-calculation mode, press “Mismatch example (Low, High, Mid).” What to look at: the Viterbi path is Normal → Degraded → Degraded, yet the sequence of the state with the largest γt at each time is Normal → Degraded → Normal. Compare the probabilities of those two sequences in the full-enumeration table.
  7. In ②, turn on “Insert missing observations.” What to look at: at times with no observation only the prediction is made, so the band (±2√Pt) keeps widening and then narrows abruptly when observations return. In the lower figure, Kt = 0 and Pt grows by q at every step.
  8. In ②, uncheck “Use the same q, r as the data” and make the filter’s q smaller or larger. What to look at: assuming a small q makes the estimate sluggish (K∞ is small), and assuming a large q makes it swayed by the observation noise. The fraction of times the true value falls inside the ±2√Pt band (the coverage) deviates from 95 %.

Formulas and algorithms computed

Let the hidden state be zt ∈ {1, 2} (1 = normal, 2 = degraded), the initial distribution πi = p(z1 = i), the transition matrix Aij = p(zt = j | zt−1 = i) (from i to j; each row sums to 1), and the emission probability Bjv = p(xt = v | zt = j). Then the joint distribution is

p(x1:T,z1:T)=πz1∏t=2TAzt−1zt∏t=1TBztxt

The forward algorithm (αt(j) = p(x1:t, zt = j)) and the backward algorithm (βt(j) = p(xt+1:T | zt = j)):

α1(j)=πjBjx1,αt(j)=Bjxt∑iαt−1(i)Aij,p(x1:T)=∑jαT(j)βT(i)=1,βt(i)=∑jAijBjxt+1βt+1(j)γt(j)=p(zt=j∣x1:T)=αt(j)βt(j)p(x1:T),p(zt=j∣x1:t)=αt(j)∑kαt(k)(filtering)

αt(j) is a product of probabilities, so for large T it shrinks exponentially and eventually rounds to 0 in floating-point numbers (in this simulator’s test it rounded to 0 at T = 2000). We therefore use the following scaling. At each time we divide by the normalizing coefficient ct = p(xt | x1:t−1) and compute the normalized α̂t(j) = p(zt = j | x1:t) in sequence.

ct=∑jBjxt∑iα^t−1(i)Aij,α^t(j)=Bjxt∑iα^t−1(i)Aijct,lnp(x1:T)=∑t=1Tlnctβ^T(i)=1,β^t(i)=1ct+1∑jAijBjxt+1β^t+1(j),γt(j)=α^t(j)β^t(j)

Inside the computation, a version that obtains the same quantities in logarithms (updating ln αt with log-sum-exp) is also computed in parallel, and the readout shows the difference between the two log-likelihoods (the size of the rounding error). For Gaussian observations, the density 𝒩(xt | μj, σ2) is used in place of Bjxt, and at each time we divide by the larger of the two densities before computing (the divided amount is added back to the log-likelihood).

The Viterbi algorithm takes a maximum instead of a sum. ωt(j) is “the joint probability of the most probable state sequence among those ending in state j at time t,” and ψt(j) is the previous state at that point (the back pointer). The computation is done in logarithms.

ω1(j)=πjBjx1,ωt(j)=Bjxtmaxiωt−1(i)Aij,ψt(j)=argmaxiωt−1(i)Aij

The Kalman filter in ② is the forward algorithm for the case in which a continuous hidden state zt follows a random walk zt = zt−1 + wt (wt ~ 𝒩(0, q)) and the observation is xt = zt + vt (vt ~ 𝒩(0, r)). Because the distributions remain Gaussian throughout, only the mean and the variance need to be updated (the update is the same computation as “Bayesian estimation of the mean of a Gaussian distribution” in Chapter 4).

Prediction:μt∣t−1=μt−1,Pt∣t−1=Pt−1+q(for t=1: μ0, P0)Gain:Kt=Pt∣t−1Pt∣t−1+rUpdate:μt=μt∣t−1+Kt(xt−μt∣t−1),Pt=(1−Kt)Pt∣t−1=Pt∣t−1rPt∣t−1+rSteady state:P∞−=q+q2+4qr2,K∞=P∞−P∞−+r,P∞=K∞r

μt and Pt are the mean and variance of p(zt | x1:t) = 𝒩(zt | μt, Pt), and μt|t−1 and Pt|t−1 are the mean and variance of the prediction from the observations up to one step earlier. At a time when the observation is missing, no update is made: μt = μt|t−1 and Pt = Pt|t−1. The variance update is computed in the subtraction-free form Pt|t−1r/(Pt|t−1 + r), so it cannot become negative through rounding.

Data and random-number conditions

  • The sequence in ① is synthetic data for teaching purposes (not a record of a real machine). State 1 = normal, 2 = degraded. z1 ~ π, zt | zt−1 = i ~ (Ai1, Ai2). A12 = 1 − A11, A21 = 1 − A22. The defaults are π = (0.95, 0.05), A11 = 0.97, A22 = 0.95, T = 100.
  • Discrete three-valued observation (the sensor value divided into three levels, Low, Mid, High; v = 1, 2, 3): with separation s ∈ [0, 1], B1· = ((1 + s)/3, 1/3, (1 − s)/3) (normal tends to give “Low”) and B2· = ((1 − s)/3, 1/3, (1 + s)/3) (degraded tends to give “High”). Default s = 0.7.
  • Gaussian observation: xt = μzt + σ εt, εt ~ 𝒩(0, 1), μ1 = 0, μ2 = Δ, σ = 1. The sensor values are dimensionless (the mean in the normal state is taken as 0 and the standard deviation of the scatter as 1). Default Δ = 1.5.
  • For estimation, the same π, A, B (or μj, σ) used to generate the data are used.
  • The sequence in ② is also synthetic data for teaching purposes: z1 = μ0 + √P0 ε1, zt = zt−1 + √q εt, xt = zt + √r ηt (εt and ηt ~ 𝒩(0, 1) are independent). For example, a quantity (dimensionless) representing the machine’s state drifts slowly, and the sensor returns that value with noise added. The defaults are q = 0.2, r = 1.0, μ0 = 0, P0 = 1, T = 100. Observations are missing for times t in (0.3T, 0.45T] and (0.7T, 0.8T] (for T = 100, 31–45 and 71–80). Inserting missing observations does not change the values of the observations themselves.
  • Random numbers come from a seeded generator (mulberry32). The default seed is 20261112 for both ① and ②. Starting over with the same seed gives the same sequence. In ①, changing a parameter regenerates the sequence with the same seed (the random-number stream is the same, but the transition and emission probabilities change, so the states and observations change). When only T is changed, a shorter sequence is the same as the beginning of a longer one.
  • Upper limits: the sequence length T is 10–200. The hand-calculation mode uses T = 3 (full enumeration of 23 = 8 sequences).

Simplifications

  • There are only two states, and the transition and emission probabilities are constant in time. Real degradation often only progresses and never reverses (A21 = 0), but here a return to normal, for example through repair, is also allowed (setting A22 = 1 gives a model with no return).
  • The parameters π, A, B are assumed known. The Baum–Welch algorithm (EM algorithm) for learning them from data is not covered (it is introduced in Section 22.4 of the text).
  • The standard deviation of the Gaussian observation is fixed at the common value σ = 1 for both states.
  • The Kalman filter is limited to a one-dimensional random walk (state-transition coefficient 1), and backward smoothing (RTS smoothing) is not covered. The initial mean and variance are fixed at μ0 = 0 and P0 = 1.

Conditions under which results become unstable

  • The unnormalized αt(j) shrinks exponentially with T and rounds to 0 at T of a few hundred to a few thousand. This simulator computes with the scaled version (and the log version), so there is no loss of significance even at T = 200.
  • When a zero-probability transition (Aij = 0) and a zero-probability emission (B1,High = B2,Low = 0 when s = 1) coincide, the probability of the observation sequence itself can become 0 (this can be reproduced in the hand-calculation mode). In that case the computation stops and the reason is displayed.
  • At s = 0 or Δ = 0 the observations say nothing about the state, so the estimate is determined by the transitions alone. When states tie in probability, the Viterbi path is not unique, and this simulator picks the state with the smaller index (normal).
  • If the Kalman filter assumes a q that is far smaller than the true value, the estimate variance Pt becomes too small and the true value keeps falling outside the band (the filter stops trusting the observations).

Short questions to answer after operating

  1. In the hand-calculation mode defaults (π = (0.6, 0.4), A11 = 0.7, A22 = 0.6, observations Low, Mid, High), compute α1(1), α2(1), and p(x1:3) by hand.
    Sample answers

    α1(1) = 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.30000, α1(2) = 0.4 × 0.1 = 0.040000. α2(1) = B1,Mid(0.3 × 0.7 + 0.04 × 0.4) = 0.4 × 0.226 = 0.090400. Similarly α2(2) = 0.3 × (0.3 × 0.3 + 0.04 × 0.6) = 0.034200, α3(1) = 0.1 × (0.0904 × 0.7 + 0.0342 × 0.4) = 0.0076960, and α3(2) = 0.6 × (0.0904 × 0.3 + 0.0342 × 0.6) = 0.028584. p(x1:3) = 0.0076960 + 0.028584 = 0.036280. This agrees with the sum over the 8 enumerated sequences.

  2. In the “Mismatch example (Low, High, Mid),” why does the sequence of the state with the largest γt at each time (Normal, Degraded, Normal) differ from the Viterbi path (Normal, Degraded, Degraded)?
    Sample answers

    γ3 = (0.54627, 0.45373) is the probability summed over (marginalized over) both the sequences in which z2 is normal and those in which it is degraded. The last observation “Mid” is hard to distinguish, with B1,Mid = 0.4 and B2,Mid = 0.3, and once the sequences passing through z2 = normal are included, z3 = normal becomes slightly more probable. On the other hand, the single most probable sequence, Normal, Degraded, Degraded (probability 0.0097200), is more probable than Normal, Degraded, Normal (0.0086400) because the probability of staying degraded, A22 = 0.6, is larger than the probability of returning from degraded, A21 = 0.4. “The most probable state at each time” and “the most probable state sequence” are different questions.

  3. With the defaults in ①, what are the accuracies of filtering and smoothing? Why does smoothing tend to be higher? Which of the two can be used in real-time monitoring?
    Sample answers

    With the defaults (seed 20261112), filtering is 0.90, smoothing (γ max) is 0.99, and Viterbi is 0.98. Smoothing also uses observations after time t, so it can correct, after the fact, a judgment made just after the state switched. However, it cannot be computed until the later observations arrive, so in real-time monitoring, where a decision must be made at that time, only filtering can be used (smoothing can be used when analyzing a record afterwards).

  4. With the defaults in ② (q = 0.2, r = 1, P0 = 1), find K1, K2, and K∞. How does Pt change over an interval in which the observations are missing?
    Sample answers

    K1 = 1/(1 + 1) = 0.5, P1 = 0.5. P2|1 = 0.5 + 0.2 = 0.7, K2 = 0.7/1.7 = 0.41176. The steady-state values are P∞− = (0.2 + √(0.04 + 0.8))/2 = 0.55826 and K∞ = 0.55826/1.55826 = 0.35826. When observations are missing no update is possible, and Pt = Pt−1 + q grows by 0.2 at every step (by 3.0 over 15 steps), and the gain at the time observations return becomes large.
