Regression and Overfitting — How Predictions Change with Degree, Regularization, and the Number of Data Points

Fit polynomial and Gaussian-basis linear models to synthetic data on firing temperature and relative density (Data A), made for teaching purposes. Check against the equations in the text, using the numbers on the screen, that the training error keeps falling as the degree rises while the validation error starts to grow partway (overfitting); that regularization λ suppresses this; how bias and variance trade off behind it; and that in Bayesian linear regression the predictive uncertainty splits into two parts, one from noise and one from lack of knowledge.

Procedure and What to Observe

  1. In ①, keep the defaults (N = 10, noise 1.0 %, no regularization, seed 20261112) and change the degree d to 0, 1, 3, and 9. What to see: the training RMSE keeps falling, 6.10 → 1.60 → 0.855 % → almost 0 (about 10−13), whereas the validation RMSE goes 6.48 → 1.95 → 1.23 → 14.8 %, reaching its minimum at d = 3 and worsening sharply at d = 9 (the U shape in the figure below). At d = 9 the largest absolute value of the coefficients is 3160 (3.16×103).
  2. With d = 9, uncheck “No regularization” and move ln λ from −10 to 0. What to see: at ln λ = −5 the validation RMSE falls to 1.31 % and the coefficients shrink to about ten or so. If λ is made too large (ln λ = 0), the curve becomes flat and both training and validation get worse.
  3. Increase the number of training data points N from 10 to 50 (d = 9, no regularization). What to see: even with the same degree, the validation RMSE falls from 14.8 % (N = 10) to 5.25 % (N = 20) to 1.13 % (N = 50), and overfitting becomes less conspicuous.
  4. In ②, keep the horizontal axis as “ln λ” and wait for the computation with the defaults d = 9 and N = 15. What to see: the variance is large when λ is small and the bias is large when λ is large, so their sum (plus the noise) has a minimum partway. In the upper figure, the bundle of gray fits widens as λ is made smaller.
  5. In ③, press “1 point from Data A” several times, then move the point of interest s* to a place with no data (for example 1.3). What to see: the lack-of-knowledge band ±2(φTSNφ)1/2 is narrow near the data and becomes wide far from it. The observation band never becomes narrower than the noise band ±2σ, anywhere.
  6. In ③, set the basis to “Gaussian basis” and set s* to 1.4. What to see: where there are no basis functions, φ ≈ 0 and even the lack-of-knowledge band becomes narrow (it looks as if the model were confident although there is no data; this is a limit of the choice of basis).

Equations and Algorithms Computed

The input is the standardized firing temperature T (°C), s = (T − 1300)/200 ∈ [−1, 1]. The basis of the degree-d polynomial consists of M = d + 1 functions φj(s) = sj (j = 0, …, d), and the design matrix is Φ ∈ ℝN×M, Φnj = snj. The target value is the relative density minus the fixed baseline 88 %, yn − 88 %, and the curve on the screen is displayed with 88 % added back (the baseline is not computed from the data).

The ridge regression in ① minimizes the following loss (λ = 0 is the least-squares method).

L(𝒘)=12‖𝒚−𝚽𝒘‖2+λ2‖𝒘‖2,(𝚽T𝚽+λ𝑰)𝒘=𝚽T𝒚

The normal equations are not formed. For λ = 0 the Householder method is used to QR-decompose Φ, and for λ > 0 the vertically stacked matrix [Φ; √λ I], and w is obtained by back substitution with the triangular matrix (the least-squares solution of [Φ; √λ I] coincides with the ridge solution). When the number of coefficients M = d + 1 exceeds the number of data points N and λ = 0, there are infinitely many solutions with zero training error, so the minimum-norm solution from the singular value decomposition Φ = U diag(si) VT is shown, and the screen says so. The RMSE is

RMSE=1N∑n=1N(yn−f(sn;𝒘))2

where the training RMSE is computed on the training data and the validation RMSE on 200 points drawn separately from the same distribution.

Bias–variance decomposition in ②: denote the fit to the l-th data set (l = 1, …, L) by f(l)(s) and its mean by f̄(s) = (1/L) Σl=1L f(l)(s), and take the average ⟨·⟩s over s with the uniform distribution on [−1, 1] (midpoint rule, 200 points).

𝔼[(y−f^(s))2]=⟨(f¯(s)−g(s))2⟩s⏟(bias)2+⟨1L∑l=1L(f(l)(s)−f¯(s))2⟩s⏟variance+σ2⏟noise

The cross term 2⟨(f̄ − g)(f(l) − f̄)⟩ vanishes when averaged over l, and since the noise ε is independent of the fit, its cross terms also have zero expectation. When sweeping λ, the singular value decomposition of Φ is done only once for each data set, and the solutions for all λ are made by w(λ) = Σi vi si/(si2 + λ) uiTy (they agree with the QR solutions within rounding error). The mean and variance are updated one set at a time by Welford’s method, and the computation is divided into chunks of at most 12 ms per frame.

Bayesian linear regression in ③: the prior is p(w) = 𝒩(w | 0, σw2I) and the noise variance is σ2. The posterior is 𝒩(w | mN, SN), and the predictive distribution is as follows.

𝑺N−1=σw−2𝑰+σ−2𝚽T𝚽,𝒎N=σ−2𝑺N𝚽T𝒚p(y*∣s*,𝒟)=𝒩(y*∣𝒎NT𝝓*,σ2⏟noise+𝝓*T𝑺N𝝓*⏟lack of knowledge)

The lack-of-knowledge band is the predictive mean ± 2(φ*TSNφ*)1/2, and the observation band is ± 2(σ2 + φ*TSNφ*)1/2. SN is not formed; SN−1 = RTR is obtained from the QR decomposition of the vertically stacked matrix [Φ/σ; I/σw], and φTSNφ = ‖R−Tφ‖2 is computed by triangular substitution. A sample from the posterior is w = mN + R−1z (z ~ 𝒩(0, I)). The MAP estimate mN coincides with the ridge regression solution for λ = σ2/σw2 (the screen shows the difference).

The Gaussian basis is φj(s) = exp[−(s − cj)2/(2r2)], with nine centers cj = −1, −0.75, …, 1 and width r = 0.25 (equal to the spacing of the centers). No constant basis function is included.

Data and Random-Number Conditions

  • Synthetic data for teaching purposes (Data A, “firing temperature and relative density”; not data of a real material): s is drawn from the uniform distribution on [−1, 1], y = g(s) + ε, g(s) = 88 + 8s − 5s2 + 2 sin 3s (relative density, %), ε ~ 𝒩(0, σ2). The firing temperature is T = 1300 + 200s (°C), which corresponds to 1100–1500 °C. The default noise standard deviation is 1.0 % (it can be varied from 0 to 3 % in ① and ②; the points “drawn from Data A” in ③ are fixed at 1.0 %).
  • Points are drawn one at a time (in the order s → ε), so with the same seed the earlier points do not change when N is increased. The 200 validation points are drawn from a separate random-number sequence with seed + 1000003.
  • The random numbers come from a seeded generator (mulberry32). The default seed is 20261112 in ①, ②, and ③. Starting over with the same seed gives the same data. The posterior samples in ③ use a fixed z made from the random-number sequence with seed + 7, so the samples change continuously as data are added.
  • Limits: 5–50 training points, degree 0–12, 10–200 data sets, 100 observation points in ③, and 10 posterior samples.

Simplifications

  • The regression is done on the target value minus the fixed 88 %, and the same penalty (λ/2)‖w‖2 or the same prior 𝒩(0, σw2I) is applied to all components of w (including the constant term w0). As λ is increased, the fit approaches the horizontal line at 88 %. In practice the constant term is often left unpenalized, in which case the results change slightly.
  • The bias and variance in ② are estimated from a finite number L of sets, and bias2 contains an estimation error of about variance/L. The average over s is taken with the uniform distribution on [−1, 1] (the same distribution as the training data).
  • In ③, the noise variance σ2 and the prior σw are taken as given and are not estimated from the data (for example by the evidence maximization of Chapter 8).
  • The polynomial basis consists of monomials sj. Orthogonal polynomials would improve the condition number at high degree, but here the correspondence with the equations in the text is given priority.

Conditions under Which Results Become Unstable

  • Without regularization and with a high degree, the condition number of Φ becomes about 104–108 and the coefficients become orders of magnitude larger. Since the system is solved by QR decomposition, the computation itself is stable, but the curve changes greatly with small changes in the data (the variance in ②).
  • With d + 1 > N and λ = 0, the solution is not unique (the minimum-norm solution is shown). In ② this condition can make the variance exceed 1010, which is cut off at the top edge of the figure.
  • In ③, if σ is made small and σw large, the predictive mean passes almost through the observation points, and with a high polynomial degree it swings widely outside the data. If the computation gives NaN or infinity, it is stopped and the reason is shown.
  • With the Gaussian basis in ③, φ ≈ 0 away from the centers of the basis functions, and the lack-of-knowledge band approaches 0. That the uncertainty looks small although there is no data comes from the model’s assumption (the placement of the basis), and does not indicate the reliability of extrapolation.

Short Questions to Answer After Operating

  1. With the defaults of ① (N = 10, seed 20261112, no regularization), setting d = 9 makes the training RMSE almost 0. Why? What is the validation RMSE at this point, and how does it compare with d = 3?
    Sample answer

    There are d + 1 = 10 coefficients and 10 training points, so Φ is a nonsingular 10×10 matrix and there is exactly one polynomial satisfying Φw = y (interpolation through all points). The validation RMSE is 14.8 %, more than an order of magnitude worse than the 1.23 % at d = 3. The curve swings widely between the training points and has memorized even the noise.

  2. With d = 9, how do the validation RMSE and the largest absolute value of the coefficients change for ln λ = −10, −5, 0? Why does it get worse when λ is made too large?
    Sample answer

    The validation RMSE goes 2.40 → 1.31 → 2.43 %, and the largest absolute value of the coefficients goes 75.0 → 12.8 → 5.89. The penalty (λ/2)‖w‖2 keeps the coefficients small and so suppresses the swings, but if it is too large the curve can no longer express even the curvature of the true function (the bias increases), and the training RMSE also rises to 1.68 %.

  3. With the defaults of ② (d = 9, N = 15, L = 100), around which ln λ is bias2 + variance + noise smallest? Which component is larger to the left and to the right of that point?
    Sample answer

    Around ln λ ≈ −3 (bias2 ≈ 0.16, variance ≈ 0.73, noise 1.00, total ≈ 1.89 %2). To the left (small λ) the variance is larger, and to the right (large λ) bias2 is larger.

  4. In ③, why does the width of the observation band never become narrower than ±2σ, however many observation points are added? What happens to the width of the lack-of-knowledge band?
    Sample answer

    Of the predictive variance of the observed value, σ2 + φTSNφ, the term σ2 is the measurement noise and does not decrease with more data. Since σ−2φφT is added to SN−1 with each observation, φTSNφ becomes small where there are data, and the lack-of-knowledge band approaches 0.
