Approximate Inference and MCMC — Handling Intractable Integrals with Approximations and Random Numbers

This page checks two methods that work without knowing the normalization constant Z = ∫ p̃(x) dx, using a one-dimensional target distribution. In Tab ①, the Laplace approximation and the Gaussians that minimize the KL divergence in its two directions are overlaid, so that you can see which way each approximation is distorted and check numerically that the ELBO is a lower bound on ln Z whose gap is KL(q‖p). In Tab ②, you run Metropolis–Hastings chains and read the proposal width, acceptance rate, autocorrelation, effective sample size, and R̂, and you confirm that for a double well with a high barrier the chains “look settled yet have not converged.”

Procedure and what to observe

  1. In Tab ①, start with the default (skewed unimodal Beta(3, 9)). What to observe: the Laplace approximation is centered at the mode x* = 0.2, and its variance is 1/Λ = 1/62.5 = 0.016. The true mean is 0.25 and the true variance is 0.01442. The Laplace approximation misses the long right tail and puts probability 0.0569 on the left side x < 0 (where p = 0), so KL(q‖p) is ∞.
  2. Set the target to “Bimodal” and switch “Initial value for KL(q‖p) minimization” among “Near the left peak,” “Near the right peak,” and “Wide, from the center.” What to observe: the q that minimizes KL(q‖p) falls into one of three local solutions depending on the starting point: the left peak, the right peak, or a wide shape covering both (for the default w = 0.6, d = 6, KL(q‖p) = 0.507, 0.911, 0.846). The q that minimizes KL(p‖q) is centered in the low-probability region between the two peaks and covers both.
  3. Switch “q to put into the formula” and read “Putting the current numbers into the formula” below. What to observe: ELBO + KL(q‖p) gives the same ln Z for every q. In the ELBO bar figure, the gap from ln Z (the hatched band) is KL(q‖p).
  4. Turn on “Overlay a manually moved q” and move m and s. What to observe: the closer q is to p, the closer the ELBO gets to ln Z. KL(q‖p) and KL(p‖q) are in general different values.
  5. In Tab ②, with the default (double well b = 2, s = 0.3, four chains), press “Advance 1000 steps” twice. What to observe: the histogram approaches the true density; also read the acceptance rate, τint, the effective sample size, and R̂.
  6. Set the proposal width s to 0.01 and to 10, and start over with the same seed. What to observe: for too small an s, the acceptance rate is high but the trace moves slowly and τint is large. For too large an s, the acceptance rate is low and the trace becomes flat steps. In both cases the effective sample size is small.
  7. Press “Barrier example (b = 12)” (s = 0.3, four chains, spread initial values, 2000 steps with the default seed). What to observe: each of the four chains stays in the well it started in; each chain’s trace looks settled and τint is small, yet R̂ is large, about 9.9. Setting burn-in to 50 % changes nothing. If you set the initial values to “All at the same point,” R̂ ≈ 1.00, but the mean stays at −0.98, far from the true value 0.
  8. Open “Build the Metropolis transition matrix for a three-state discrete distribution” below Tab ②. What to observe: for p̃ = (2, 3, 5), each row of the transition matrix sums to 1, piTij = pjTji (detailed balance), and pT = p (stationary distribution).

With the keyboard, Tab moves to buttons and sliders, Enter (or Space) presses a button, and the arrow keys move a slider. The tabs can be switched with ← and →.

Formulas and algorithms computed

The target is given by an unnormalized density p̃(x), and p(x) = p̃(x)/Z. This corresponds to the posterior distribution of Chapter 20, p(θ | 𝒟) = p(𝒟, θ)/p(𝒟) (x ↔ θ, p̃ ↔ p(𝒟, θ), Z ↔ p(𝒟)).

Skewed unimodal: p~(x)=xa−1(1−x)b−1(0<x<1),Z=B(a,b)Bimodal: p~(x)=we−(x+d/2)2/2+(1−w)e−(x−d/2)2/2,Z=2πDouble well: p~(x)=exp[−b(x2−1)2],b=EbkBT

The Laplace approximation expands ln p̃ to second order around the chosen mode x*. The approximation of the marginal likelihood Z (p(𝒟) in Chapter 20) also follows from this expansion. The curvature is written Λ (the same letter as in Chapter 20; it is the precision of the Gaussian).

q(x)=𝒩(x∣x*,Λ−1),Λ=−d2dx2lnp~(x)|x=x*lnZ≈lnp~(x*)+12ln2πΛ

The KL divergence has different values and different properties depending on its direction (the unit is the nat).

KL(q‖p)=∫q(x)lnq(x)p(x)dx,KL(p‖q)=∫p(x)lnp(x)q(x)dxlnZ=ELBO(q)+KL(q‖p),ELBO(q)=𝔼q[lnp~(x)]−𝔼q[lnq(x)]≤lnZ

The Gaussian q = 𝒩(x | m, s2) that minimizes KL(p‖q) has m = 𝔼p[x] and s2 = Varp[x] (matching the mean and variance). The q that minimizes KL(q‖p) (= the q that maximizes the ELBO) has no closed form, so it is searched for numerically over (m, ln s) with the Nelder–Mead method. If the objective function has several local minima, the destination depends on the initial value.

Metropolis–Hastings algorithm: from the current point x, make a proposal x′ = x + sε (ε ~ 𝒩(0, 1)); if a uniform random number u ∈ [0, 1) satisfies u < min(1, r), accept (x ← x′); otherwise reject (x stays, and the same value is counted once more in the sample). Because the proposal is symmetric, q(x | x′) = q(x′ | x) cancels, and Z cancels too, so it is not needed.

r=p~(x′)q(x∣x′)p~(x)q(x′∣x)=p~(x′)p~(x),acceptance probability min(1,r)

The autocorrelation function ρk is computed for the samples x1, …, xS of a chain (after burn-in) with the standard estimator whose denominator is divided by S (computed by FFT; the tests confirm that it agrees with the direct sum). The integrated autocorrelation time τint (the same symbol as in Chapter 21; a subscript is added because τ alone would be confused with the temperature in Chapters 26 and 27) and the effective sample size are

τint=1+2∑k=1kcρk,Seff=Sτint

Truncation rule: the upper limit kc of the sum is determined by Sokal’s automatic window. Add terms for k = 1, 2, …, and let kc be the first k such that k ≥ 5 τint(k) (τint(k) is the partial sum up to that k). If the condition is not met even after summing up to half the series length, “series too short” is displayed, and τint and the effective sample size may be underestimated. The effective sample size over all chains is the sum over the chains. The Monte Carlo standard error (MCSE) of the mean is √(variance / effective sample size).

Definition of R̂: this is split-R̂ (the form in Gelman et al., Bayesian Data Analysis, 3rd ed., Section 11.4; no rank normalization). Split each chain after burn-in into a first half and a second half to get M = 2 × (number of chains) sequences, let n be their length, W the mean of the within-chain variances, and B the spread of the chain means. Then

B=nM−1∑j=1M(x¯j−x¯)2,R^=n−1nW+BnW

R̂ is displayed only when there are two or more chains. It is close to 1 if the chains look at the same distribution and becomes large if each chain looks at a different region. As a rule of thumb, suspect non-convergence if it exceeds 1.01 (the older rule of thumb was 1.1). However, R̂ ≈ 1 is only a necessary condition for convergence.

In the three-state example, the transition matrix Tij = Qij min(1, p̃jQji / (p̃iQij)) (j ≠ i), Tii = 1 − Σj≠i Tij is built from the proposal Qij = q(j | i) (1/2 for each of the other two states). Rows are the “current state,” columns are the “next state,” and each row sums to 1.

Data and random-number conditions

  • The target distributions are teaching examples given by formulas, not measured data. x is dimensionless. Defaults: in Tab ①, the skewed unimodal Beta(3, 9); the bimodal target has w = 0.6, d = 6 (standard deviation 1 for each component); the double well has b = 3. In Tab ②, the double well has b = 2.
  • The normalization constant Z, the mean, the variance, the KL, and the ELBO are numerical integrals on a grid (Simpson’s rule with 4000 intervals). For Beta, the end-point singularity is weakened by the change of variable x = u3(10 − 15u + 6u2). Expectations over q are computed on a grid of the mean of q ± 12 s (2000 intervals).
  • Tab ① uses no random numbers. The random numbers in Tab ② come from a seeded generator (mulberry32). The default seed is 20261112. Chain k (k = 0, 1, 2, 3) uses the generator with seed + 7919 k, so the sequence of chain 1 is the same even if the number of chains is changed. At each step, one ε (Gaussian random number) and one u (uniform random number) are drawn. Starting over with the same seed gives the same sequence.
  • Initial values: “Spread” uses the 5 %, 95 %, 30 %, and 70 % points of the true distribution (chains 1–4); “All at the same point” uses the 5 % point for all four chains. The initial values are not counted as samples.
  • Limits: one chain has at most 20000 steps, and there are at most four chains. The proposal width s is 0.01–10, a and b are 1.5–20, w is 0.05–0.95, d is 0–8, and the barrier b is 0.25–15.

Simplifications

  • The target is one-dimensional and limited to distributions that can be normalized by numerical integration on a grid. In real inference, Z cannot be computed, which is why approximations and MCMC are used. Here, Z is deliberately computed by numerical integration so that the quality of the approximations can be checked.
  • The family of approximations is only the one-dimensional Gaussian. For a Beta target, a Gaussian that puts probability outside 0 < x < 1 gives KL(q‖p) = ∞, so the minimization of KL(q‖p) and the ELBO bars use the distribution obtained by truncating q to 0 < x < 1 and renormalizing (the table says “truncated”). KL(p‖q) and the Laplace approximation are untruncated Gaussians.
  • The MH proposal is only the Gaussian random walk (there is no Gibbs sampling, replica exchange, or Hamiltonian Monte Carlo). R̂ is the split-R̂ without rank normalization.

Conditions under which results become unstable

  • The minimization of KL(q‖p) has local solutions, and the result depends on the initial value (this is itself an object of observation). If the Nelder–Mead method does not converge in 4000 iterations, the table says so.
  • When a or b of the Beta is close to 1.5, the slope of the density near the end becomes steep, and the Laplace approximation is far off.
  • If the MH chain is short, the truncation of τint reaches half the series length (“series too short”), and τint, the effective sample size, and R̂ are unreliable. If a proposal falls outside the support (x ≤ 0 or x ≥ 1 for Beta), then p̃ = 0 and it is always rejected.
  • If NaN or infinity appears during the computation, it stops and displays the reason.

Short questions to answer after operating

  1. What are the mean and variance of the Laplace approximation of Beta(3, 9)? Compared with the true mean and variance, in which direction is the approximation distorted?
    Sample answers

    The mode is x* = (a − 1)/(a + b − 2) = 2/10 = 0.2, and Λ = 2/0.22 + 8/0.82 = 50 + 12.5 = 62.5, so the mean is 0.2 and the variance is 1/62.5 = 0.016. The true mean is 3/12 = 0.25 and the variance is 27/1872 ≈ 0.01442. Because the Laplace approximation is centered at the mode, it misses the weight of the long right tail and underestimates the mean. It also puts probability 0.0569 on the left side x < 0. KL(p‖q) is 0.12884, which is larger than that of the Gaussian with matched mean and variance (0.048117).

  2. For the bimodal target, where are the q that minimizes KL(q‖p) and the q that minimizes KL(p‖q) placed? Why?
    Sample answers

    KL(q‖p) is an expectation under q, so if q puts probability where p is almost 0, ln(q/p) becomes large and is penalized heavily. The q therefore moves toward one peak, and its variance is also smaller (by default, the left peak: m = −2.988, s = 1.019). KL(p‖q) is an expectation under p, so it is penalized where p has probability but q is close to 0, and q spreads to cover both peaks (m = −0.6, s2 = 9.64; its center lies in the low-probability valley).

  3. What is the difference between the ELBO and ln Z? What does maximizing the ELBO amount to?
    Sample answers

    The difference is KL(q‖p) ≥ 0 (ln Z = ELBO + KL(q‖p)). Since ln Z does not depend on q, maximizing the ELBO is the same as minimizing KL(q‖p). For Beta(3, 9), ln Z = ln B(3, 9) = −6.2046, and the ELBO of the (truncated) q that minimizes KL(q‖p) is −6.2437; the difference 0.039178 is KL(q‖p).

  4. In the barrier example (b = 12), the trace of each chain looks settled and τint is small. Why can we not say the chains have converged? How does R̂ capture this?
    Sample answers

    Every chain sees only the well it started in and does not know the probability (one half) of the other well. Within a well the chain mixes well, so τint is small (about 4) and does not change even when burn-in is discarded. R̂ compares the between-chain spread B with the within-chain variance W; because the chain means split into −0.98 and +0.98, it gives a large value of about 9.9. However, if all four chains start from the same well, R̂ ≈ 1 while the overall mean is −0.98, and even R̂ does not notice.
