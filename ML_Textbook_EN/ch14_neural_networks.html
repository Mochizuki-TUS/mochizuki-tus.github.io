Chapter 14Representations in Neural Networks — Learning the Basis Functions

The question this chapter answers
  • No matter how many linear transformations we stack, the result can do nothing more than a single linear transformation. So what must we insert between the stages to be able to represent curved decision boundaries and curves?
  • In the linear regression of Chapter 8, a person chose the centers and widths of the Gaussian basis functions in advance. What, in terms of equations, does it mean to learn the basis functions themselves from data?
  • There is a theorem that “a sufficiently large neural network can approximate any continuous function,” and yet learning sometimes fails. Why?

When we fit a curve to the data on firing temperature and relative density (data A, used in Chapters 6 and 8), in Chapter 8 a person chose “polynomials” or “Gaussian basis functions with equally spaced centers,” and only their weights were determined by least squares. Choosing the shape of the basis functions was the person’s job. With one input, equal spacing is enough, but for materials data with eight descriptors, placing five centers along each axis gives $5^{8}=390625$ basis functions. That is unusable.

A neural network is a model that determines even the location, direction, and slope of these basis functions from data, as weights. A single component (a neuron) is simple: it “forms a weighted sum and passes it through a nonlinear function.” But when we line neurons up to form a layer and stack layers, we can represent curved boundaries and complicated curves. In this chapter we build up, in order, the neuron and the activation function, layers and the shapes of matrices, why nonlinearity is necessary (a hand calculation for XOR), the view of the network as a model that learns basis functions, and the output layer and the loss (the fact that, for every combination, the error at the output becomes $\hat{\bm{y}}-\bm{y}$). In the second half we treat the meaning and the limits of the universal approximation theorem, the number of parameters and the symmetries of weight space, and the idea of a neural network potential, which represents the total energy as a sum of per-atom energies. The computation of the gradients of the weights (backpropagation) is treated in the next chapter, Chapter 15; this chapter concentrates on “what can be represented.”

What you will learn in this chapter
  • Write the neuron equations $a=\bm{w}^{\tp}\bm{x}+b$ and $z=h(a)$, and explain that logistic regression is a single neuron.
  • Write the formulas and derivatives of sigmoid, tanh, and ReLU, and state the characteristics of each.
  • Tabulate the shapes (dimensions) of the weight matrix, bias, and activation of each layer, and show that for a minibatch the computation becomes a matrix product.
  • Prove that if the activation function is the identity map, then stacking any number of layers gives a single linear map, and verify by hand the weights that solve XOR with two hidden units.
  • Contrast the outputs of the hidden layer, viewed as “basis functions that move to fit the data,” with the fixed basis functions of Chapter 8.
  • Derive the output layer and loss for regression, binary classification, and multiclass classification from maximum likelihood estimation, and derive that the error at the output is $\hat{\bm{y}}-\bm{y}$ in every case.
  • State the universal approximation theorem, and explain with examples that “what can be represented,” “what can be found by learning,” and “what generalizes” are separate problems.
  • Count the parameters of a fully connected layer, and explain what the symmetries of weight space (permutation, sign flip, and scaling) mean.

14.1 The Neuron — Weighted Sum and Activation

14.1.1 What We Want to Do

Suppose we want to predict, from the normalized firing temperature $x_1$ and the normalized dopant amount $x_2$, “whether the sintered body becomes sufficiently dense.” The simplest approach is to multiply the two inputs by weights, add them, and judge that the body “densifies” if the value is large. The value of a weighted sum can become arbitrarily large or small, so it cannot be read as a probability as it stands. We therefore pass the sum through a function that squeezes it between 0 and 1. This single unit of computation, “form a weighted sum and pass it through a function,” is called a neuron (also called a unit) (Figure 14.1).

14.1.2 Definitions and Notation

Definition 14.1: Neuron

For an input $\bm{x}=(x_1,\dots,x_D)^{\tp}\in\R^{D}$, using a weight $\bm{w}\in\R^{D}$ and a bias $b\in\R$, the unit that computes

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} a&=\bm{w}^{\tp}\bm{x}+b\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^{D}w_ix_i+b,\\ z&=h(a) \end{aligned} \label{eq:14-neuron} \end{equation}

is called a neuron. We call $a\in\R$ the activation (the weighted sum itself), $h:\R\to\R$ the activation function, and $z=h(a)$ the output of the neuron.

Notation: Symbols Used in This Chapter

The symbol $z$ is used for the output of a neuron (hidden unit). In Chapters 17 and 18 the same $z$ is used for a latent variable, but it is a different object. We write the number of hidden units as $H$ (distinct from the entropy $H[p]$ of Chapter 5). The sigmoid function is always written with its argument, as $\sigma(a)$. The same letter $\sigma$ is also used for the standard deviation of Gaussian noise, but where both appear in Section 14.6, we distinguish them in the text.

If we take $h$ to be the sigmoid function $\sigma(a)=1/(1+e^{-a})$, the output lies between 0 and 1 and can be read as $p(y=1\mid\bm{x})$. This is exactly the logistic regression of Chapter 9, Section 9.3. In other words, logistic regression is “a single neuron.” Likewise, if we take $h$ to be the identity map $h(a)=a$, we obtain linear regression on the inputs (the case of Chapter 8 with basis functions $\phi_i(\bm{x})=x_i$).

The decision boundary lies where $\sigma(a)=1/2$, that is, where $a=\bm{w}^{\tp}\bm{x}+b=0$. This is a hyperplane in $\R^{D}$ (a line when $D=2$), and $\bm{w}$ gives the direction of its normal. The only classification a single neuron can perform is “which side of the hyperplane.” This constraint leads to the XOR problem of Section 14.4.

Example 14.1: Computing the probability of densification with a single neuron

Consider a neuron with weight $\bm{w}=(2,\,-1)^{\tp}$, bias $b=-0.5$, and sigmoid activation function (the numbers are provisional values for illustration). The inputs are the normalized firing temperature $x_1$ and the normalized dopant amount $x_2$ (both dimensionless). (1) Find the activation $a$ and the output $z$ for $\bm{x}=(1,\,0.5)^{\tp}$ and for $\bm{x}=(0,\,1)^{\tp}$. (2) Write the equation of the decision boundary.

Solution. (1) For $\bm{x}=(1,0.5)^{\tp}$, $a=2\times1+(-1)\times0.5+(-0.5)$ $=2-0.5-0.5=1.0$, and $z=\sigma(1.0)=1/(1+e^{-1})$ $=1/1.367879=0.731$. For $\bm{x}=(0,1)^{\tp}$, $a=0-1-0.5=-1.5$, and $z=1/(1+e^{1.5})=1/(1+4.481689)=0.182$.

(2) Setting $a=0$ gives $2x_1-x_2-0.5=0$, that is, the line $x_2=2x_1-0.5$. Below this line ($x_2\lt 2x_1-0.5$) we have $z\gt 1/2$. The signs of the weights express the provisional relationship that raising the temperature (increasing $x_1$) makes densification easier, while increasing the dopant amount makes it harder.

14.2 Activation Functions — Sigmoid, Tanh, and ReLU

14.2.1 Three representative functions

An activation function is the component that gives a neuron its “bend.” The following three are used most often (Figure 14.2).

Definition 14.2: Sigmoid, tanh, and ReLU

\begin{align} \sigma(a)&=\frac{1}{1+e^{-a}}, \notag\\ \sigma'(a)&=\sigma(a)\bigl(1-\sigma(a)\bigr) \label{eq:14-sigmoid} \end{align} \begin{align} \tanh a&=\frac{e^{a}-e^{-a}}{e^{a}+e^{-a}}, \notag\\ (\tanh a)'&=1-\tanh^{2}a \label{eq:14-tanh} \end{align} \begin{align} \relu(a)&=\max(0,a), \notag\\ \relu'(a)&=\begin{cases}1 & a\gt0\\ 0 & a\lt0\end{cases} \label{eq:14-relu} \end{align}

ReLU (rectified linear unit, a function that passes only the positive part) is not differentiable at $a=0$, but in actual computation we adopt the convention $\relu'(0)=0$.

Derivation: The derivatives of sigmoid and tanh

Sigmoid: differentiate $\sigma(a)=(1+e^{-a})^{-1}$ by the chain rule.

\begin{align*} \sigma'(a)&=-(1+e^{-a})^{-2}\cdot(-e^{-a})\\ &=\frac{e^{-a}}{(1+e^{-a})^{2}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-a}}\cdot\frac{e^{-a}}{1+e^{-a}}\\ &=\sigma(a)\bigl(1-\sigma(a)\bigr). \end{align*}

In the last equality we used $\dfrac{e^{-a}}{1+e^{-a}}=\dfrac{(1+e^{-a})-1}{1+e^{-a}}$ $=1-\sigma(a)$.

Tanh: in the quotient rule, let the numerator be $u=e^{a}-e^{-a}$ and the denominator $v=e^{a}+e^{-a}$. Then $u'=v$ and $v'=u$, so

$$ \begin{aligned} (\tanh a)'&=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^{2}}=\frac{v^{2}-u^{2}}{v^{2}}\\ &=1-\Bigl(\frac{u}{v}\Bigr)^{2}=1-\tanh^{2}a . \end{aligned} $$

In both cases the derivative can be obtained from the value of the function itself. This property is useful in the backpropagation of Chapter 15, where $h'$ is computed directly from the $z$ already obtained in forward propagation.

∎

14.2.2 The character of each function

  • Sigmoid: Its output lies in $(0,1)$, so it can be read as a probability. It suits the output layer (binary classification). When used in a hidden layer, $\sigma'(a)$ becomes almost 0 where $\abs{a}$ is large (saturation), and the maximum of the derivative is also small, $1/4$. This is the entrance to the vanishing gradient of Chapter 15, Section 15.7.
  • Tanh: Its output lies in $(-1,1)$ and is symmetric about 0 (an odd function, $\tanh(-a)=-\tanh a$). The relation $\tanh a=2\sigma(2a)-1$ holds (Exercise 14.2), so it is merely a translated and scaled sigmoid, but because its slope at the origin is 1, it is often easier to train than the sigmoid in hidden layers. It also saturates at both ends.
  • ReLU: It is cheap to compute, and for $a\gt0$ its derivative is 1, so it does not saturate. It is widely used in deep networks. On the other hand, for $a\lt0$ the derivative is 0, so a unit that stays at $a\lt0$ throughout training never moves again (a “dead” unit). The output of a network built from ReLUs is a piecewise linear function. We use this property in Sections 14.7 and 14.10.

In addition, GELU (Gaussian error linear unit) is often used in the Transformer (Chapter 25) and elsewhere. Let $\Phi(a)$ be the cumulative distribution function of the standard Gaussian distribution (the light-face $\Phi$, distinct from the bold $\bm{\Phi}$ of the design matrix). Then

\begin{equation} \mathrm{GELU}(a)=a\,\Phi(a) \label{eq:14-gelu} \end{equation}

is the definition (Hendrycks & Gimpel). When $a$ is large and positive, $\Phi(a)\approx1$ and $a$ passes through unchanged; when it is large and negative, $\Phi(a)\approx0$ and the output is 0. It is a form in which the kink of ReLU is smoothed, and for $a\lt0$ the value is slightly negative (the minimum is about $-0.17$ at $a\approx-0.75$; see the dotted line in Figure 14.2(a)). In this chapter we only introduce its name and shape.

Why are so many activation functions monotone?

The sigmoid, tanh, and ReLU listed here are all increasing functions (GELU decreases slightly in part of the negative side, but it is nearly monotone). Monotonicity is not essential, but it keeps the readability of “the larger the weighted sum, the larger the output” and does not make the loss landscape extremely complicated, so it is preferred. Conversely, if an activation function such as a polynomial is used, the property described in Section 14.4 below, that “stacking layers increases the functions that can be represented,” is limited (a composition of polynomials is again a polynomial, and for networks whose degree is bounded the set of representable functions does not grow; this is the result of Leshno et al. in Section 14.7).

Example 14.2: Values of the derivatives and saturation

(1) Find $\sigma(a)$, $\sigma'(a)$, $\tanh a$, $(\tanh a)'$, and $\relu'(a)$ at $a=0$ and $a=2$. (2) Verify numerically that $\tanh 2=2\sigma(4)-1$. (3) Find the values at $a=-2$ from the results of (1), without computing them afresh.

Solution. (1) $a=0$: $\sigma(0)=1/2$, $\sigma'(0)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=0.25$, $\tanh0=0$, $(\tanh)'(0)=1$, $\relu'(0)=0$ (by the convention). $a=2$: $\sigma(2)=1/(1+e^{-2})=1/1.135335=0.880797$, $\sigma'(2)=0.880797\times0.119203=0.104994$. $\tanh2=0.964028$, $(\tanh)'(2)=1-0.964028^{2}$ $=1-0.929350=0.070651$. $\relu'(2)=1$. Merely by increasing $a$ from 0 to 2, the slope of the sigmoid drops from $0.25$ to $0.105$ and the slope of tanh drops from 1 to $0.071$ (saturation), whereas the slope of ReLU remains 1.

(2) $\sigma(4)=1/(1+e^{-4})=1/1.018316=0.982014$, and $2\times0.982014-1=0.964028$, which agrees with $\tanh2$.

(3) From $\sigma(-a)=1-\sigma(a)$ we get $\sigma(-2)=1-0.880797=0.119203$. $\sigma'(-2)=\sigma(-2)\{1-\sigma(-2)\}$ $=0.119203\times0.880797$ $=0.104994$ ($\sigma'$ is an even function). $\tanh(-2)=-0.964028$ (an odd function), $(\tanh)'(-2)=0.070651$ (an even function). $\relu'(-2)=0$.

14.3 Layers and the Shapes of Matrices

14.3.1 Arranging neurons into a layer

If we line up $D_1$ neurons that receive the same input $\bm{x}$, we can write the $D_1$ activations together using a matrix $\bm{W}^{(1)}$ whose rows are the $D_1$ weight vectors. If the output $\bm{z}^{(1)}$ is used as the input of the next layer, we obtain a network of stacked layers. A network in which the computation flows in one direction from input to output, and in which neurons within the same layer are not connected to each other, is called a feed-forward network, or a multilayer perceptron (MLP). The layers between the input and the output are called hidden layers, and their neurons are called hidden units.

Definition 14.3: Fully connected feed-forward network

Number the layers $l=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{layer}}$ ($n_{\mathrm{layer}}$ is the number of layers; many books write $L$ for the number of layers, but in this book $L$ is used for the loss), let $D_l$ be the number of units in layer $l$, and let the input dimension be $D_0=D$. Starting from $\bm{z}^{(0)}=\bm{x}$, compute, in the order $l=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{layer}}$,

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \bm{a}^{(l)}&=\bm{W}^{(l)}\bm{z}^{(l-1)}+\bm{b}^{(l)},\\ \bm{z}^{(l)}&=h_l\bigl(\bm{a}^{(l)}\bigr) \end{aligned} \label{eq:14-layer} \end{equation}

We call $\bm{W}^{(l)}\in\R^{D_l\times D_{l-1}}$ the weight matrix, $\bm{b}^{(l)}\in\R^{D_l}$ the bias, and $\bm{a}^{(l)},\bm{z}^{(l)}\in\R^{D_l}$ the activation and the output of layer $l$. The function $h_l$ acts componentwise (the $j$th component of $\bm{z}^{(l)}$ is $h_l(a^{(l)}_j)$). The output of the last layer, $\hat{\bm{y}}=\bm{z}^{(n_{\mathrm{layer}})}$, is the prediction of the network.

$W^{(l)}_{ji}$ is the weight “from unit $i$ of layer $l-1$ to unit $j$ of layer $l$,” so the row index is the destination and the column index is the source.

Following this computation in order from input to output is called forward propagation. The activation function may differ from layer to layer: hidden layers use tanh or ReLU, and the output layer uses the identity, sigmoid, or softmax according to the task (Section 14.6). In this book the hidden-layer activation function is often simply written $h$. The way of counting layers differs from book to book; in this book the number of layers is the number of weight matrices, $n_{\mathrm{layer}}$ (a network with one hidden layer has $n_{\mathrm{layer}}=2$).

14.3.3 Minibatches turn vectors into matrices

In training, we compute $B$ data points together (a minibatch; Chapter 7, Section 7.5). If we form the matrix $\bm{Z}^{(0)}=(\bm{x}_1,\dots,\bm{x}_B)\in\R^{D_0\times B}$ by arranging the $B$ inputs in columns, then Eq. \eqref{eq:14-layer} for all $B$ points together can be written as

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \bm{A}^{(l)}&=\bm{W}^{(l)}\bm{Z}^{(l-1)}+\bm{b}^{(l)}\bm{1}_B^{\tp},\\ \bm{Z}^{(l)}&=h\bigl(\bm{A}^{(l)}\bigr) \end{aligned} \label{eq:14-batch} \end{equation}

Here $\bm{A}^{(l)},\bm{Z}^{(l)}\in\R^{D_l\times B}$, and the $n$th column is $\bm{a}^{(l)}$, $\bm{z}^{(l)}$ for the $n$th data point. $\bm{1}_B\in\R^{B}$ is the vector all of whose components are 1, and $\bm{b}^{(l)}\bm{1}_B^{\tp}$ is the $D_l\times B$ matrix in which the same bias is lined up for $B$ columns. The computation of one layer is a single product of a $D_l\times D_{l-1}$ matrix and a $D_{l-1}\times B$ matrix ($D_lD_{l-1}B$ multiplications). Since matrix multiplication is one of the operations that a computer can execute fastest, the computation of a neural network is carried out in this batched form.

Notation: The convention of arranging data in rows

Many programming libraries, and Chapters 24–26 of this book (attention and the Transformer), use the matrix $\bm{X}\in\R^{B\times D}$ with the data arranged in rows, and multiply the weights from the right: $\bm{A}=\bm{X}\bm{W}^{\tp}+\bm{1}_B\bm{b}^{\tp}$. This is the transpose of the whole of Eq. \eqref{eq:14-batch}, and the content is the same. Which convention is in use can be told from the shapes of the matrices.

Example 14.3: Forward propagation of a 2–3–1 network (a minibatch of two)

Let the hidden-layer activation function be ReLU, let the output layer be the identity map, and let

$$ \bm{W}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0.5&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad \bm{b}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0.5\\0\end{pmatrix}, $$ $$ \bm{W}^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&0.5\end{pmatrix},\quad b^{(2)}=0.1 $$

Arrange the two inputs $\bm{x}_1=(2,1)^{\tp}$ and $\bm{x}_2=(0,1)^{\tp}$ in columns, and find $\bm{A}^{(1)}$, $\bm{Z}^{(1)}$, and $\bm{A}^{(2)}$ by Eq. \eqref{eq:14-batch}.

Solution. We have $\bm{Z}^{(0)}=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\in\R^{2\times2}$. Each component of $\bm{W}^{(1)}\bm{Z}^{(0)}$ is the dot product of a “row of $\bm{W}^{(1)}$” and a “column of $\bm{Z}^{(0)}$,” so

$$ \begin{aligned} \bm{W}^{(1)}\bm{Z}^{(0)}&=\begin{pmatrix}1\cdot2+0\cdot1 & 1\cdot0+0\cdot1\\ 0.5\cdot2-1\cdot1 & 0.5\cdot0-1\cdot1\\ -1\cdot2+1\cdot1 & -1\cdot0+1\cdot1\end{pmatrix}\\ &=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} $$

Adding the bias to each column gives $\bm{A}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0.5&-0.5\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$. ReLU sets negative components to 0, so $\bm{Z}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0.5&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. The output is

$$ \begin{aligned} \bm{A}^{(2)}&=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&0.5\end{pmatrix}\bm{Z}^{(1)}+0.1\,\bm{1}_2^{\tp}\\ &=\begin{pmatrix}2-1+0+0.1 & 0+0+0.5+0.1\end{pmatrix}\\ &=\begin{pmatrix}1.1&0.6\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned} $$

The shapes are $\bm{A}^{(1)},\bm{Z}^{(1)}\in\R^{3\times2}$ and $\bm{A}^{(2)}\in\R^{1\times2}$, and the columns correspond to the individual data points. For $\bm{x}_1$, hidden unit 3 is “switched off” (output 0), and for $\bm{x}_2$, hidden units 1 and 2 are. The fact that the set of active units changes with the input is the mechanism by which a ReLU network builds a piecewise linear function.

14.4 Why Nonlinearity Is Necessary — Stacked Linear Maps and XOR

14.4.1 Stacking linear layers, however many, gives a single layer

One is tempted to think that stacking layers makes a model stronger. However, if the activation function is the identity map ($h(a)=a$), stacking any number of layers adds nothing.

Theorem 14.1: A composition of linear layers is a single linear map (affine map)

If the activation function of every layer is the identity map, the output of an $n_{\mathrm{layer}}$-layer network can be written as $\hat{\bm{y}}=\bm{W}\bm{x}+\bm{b}$, using some matrix $\bm{W}\in\R^{D_{n_{\mathrm{layer}}}\times D_0}$ and some vector $\bm{b}\in\R^{D_{n_{\mathrm{layer}}}}$.

Proof

We use mathematical induction on the number of layers. It suffices to show that the output $\bm{z}^{(k)}$ up through $k$ layers ($k=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{layer}}$) can be written as an affine map of $\bm{x}$. For $k=1$, we have $\bm{z}^{(1)}=\bm{W}^{(1)}\bm{x}+\bm{b}^{(1)}$, so the claim holds. Suppose that the output up through $k-1$ layers can be written as $\bm{z}^{(k-1)}=\bm{W}'\bm{x}+\bm{b}'$ (with $\bm{W}'\in\R^{D_{k-1}\times D_0}$ and $\bm{b}'\in\R^{D_{k-1}}$). Since the $k$th layer is also the identity map,

\begin{align} \bm{z}^{(k)}&=\bm{W}^{(k)}\bm{z}^{(k-1)}+\bm{b}^{(k)} \notag\\ &=\bm{W}^{(k)}\bigl(\bm{W}'\bm{x}+\bm{b}'\bigr) \notag\\ &\quad+\bm{b}^{(k)} \notag\\ &=\bigl(\bm{W}^{(k)}\bm{W}'\bigr)\bm{x} \notag\\ &\quad+\bigl(\bm{W}^{(k)}\bm{b}'+\bm{b}^{(k)}\bigr) \label{eq:14-collapse} \end{align}

Here $\bm{W}^{(k)}\bm{W}'$ is the product of a $D_k\times D_{k-1}$ matrix and a $D_{k-1}\times D_0$ matrix, hence a $D_k\times D_0$ matrix, and $\bm{W}^{(k)}\bm{b}'+\bm{b}^{(k)}\in\R^{D_k}$. Therefore the claim also holds for $k$ layers. Setting $k=n_{\mathrm{layer}}$ and letting $\bm{W}=\bm{W}^{(n_{\mathrm{layer}})}\bm{W}'$ and $\bm{b}=\bm{W}^{(n_{\mathrm{layer}})}\bm{b}'+\bm{b}^{(n_{\mathrm{layer}})}$, we obtain $\hat{\bm{y}}=\bm{z}^{(n_{\mathrm{layer}})}=\bm{W}\bm{x}+\bm{b}$.

∎

A matrix product is a composition of linear maps (University Mathematics, Chapter 11, Section 11.4). No matter how many linear maps we compose, the result is a linear map, so stacking layers yields the same set of functions as a single layer. In fact, if an intermediate layer is narrow, the rank of the composed matrix $\bm{W}$ is limited by that width, and what can be represented may even shrink (Exercise 14.8). Only by inserting nonlinear activation functions does composition produce “new functions.”

14.4.2 A single neuron cannot separate XOR

The smallest example in which nonlinearity is necessary is XOR (exclusive OR). For an input $(x_1,x_2)\in\{0,1\}^{2}$, let $y=1$ when the two values differ and $y=0$ when they are the same:

$$ \begin{aligned} &(0,0)\mapsto0,\qquad (1,0)\mapsto1,\\ &(0,1)\mapsto1,\qquad (1,1)\mapsto0 . \end{aligned} $$

The two points at opposite corners of the square belong to the same class, so a single straight line cannot separate them. We confirm this with equations. If a single neuron (logistic regression) could separate them, then $f(\bm{x})=w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b$ would have to be positive at the points with $y=1$ and negative at the points with $y=0$:

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} b&\lt0, & w_1+b&\gt0,\\ w_2+b&\gt0, & w_1+w_2+b&\lt0 . \end{aligned} \label{eq:14-xor-ineq} \end{equation}

Adding the second and third gives $w_1+w_2+2b\gt0$. Adding the first and fourth gives $w_1+w_2+2b\lt0$. Both cannot hold at the same time, so this is a contradiction. Therefore no $w_1,w_2,b$ separate XOR correctly. The fact that XOR cannot be handled by a one-layer perceptron became widely known through the book by Minsky & Papert (1969).

14.4.3 Solving XOR with two hidden units

Inserting one hidden layer lets us solve XOR. The idea is to “map the inputs to an arrangement that a straight line can separate, and then separate them.” Here we use two ReLU hidden units:

  • Hidden unit 1: $z_1=\relu(x_1-x_2)$ (positive only when $x_1$ is larger than $x_2$)
  • Hidden unit 2: $z_2=\relu(x_2-x_1)$ (positive only when $x_2$ is larger than $x_1$)

14.5 Models That Learn Basis Functions

14.5.1 Fixed bases and moving bases

In the linear regression of Chapter 8, Section 8.1, we fixed the basis functions $\phi_j(\bm{x})$ ($j=1,\dots,M$) in advance, and the model

\begin{equation} f(\bm{x};\bm{w})=\sum_{j=1}^{M}w_j\phi_j(\bm{x})+w_0 \label{eq:14-fixed-basis} \end{equation}

learned only the weights $w_j$. For example, the centers $\mu_j$ and the width $s$ of Gaussian basis functions $\phi_j(x)=\exp\{-(x-\mu_j)^{2}/(2s^{2})\}$ are chosen by a person. Since $f$ is linear in $\bm{w}$, minimizing the squared error can be solved as a system of linear equations, the normal equations, and the loss was convex in $\bm{w}$ (Chapter 7, Section 7.4).

If we take a network with one hidden layer and make the output layer the identity map, the output is

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} f(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})={}&\sum_{j=1}^{H}W^{(2)}_j\,h\bigl(\bm{w}_j^{\tp}\bm{x}+b^{(1)}_j\bigr)\\ &+b^{(2)} \end{aligned} \label{eq:14-nn-basis} \end{equation}

Here $\bm{w}_j^{\tp}$ is the $j$th row of $\bm{W}^{(1)}$, and $\bm{\theta}$ collects all the weights and biases. Placing this next to Eq. \eqref{eq:14-fixed-basis}, we see that $h(\bm{w}_j^{\tp}\bm{x}+b^{(1)}_j)$ plays the role of the basis function $\phi_j(\bm{x})$, $W^{(2)}_j$ plays the role of its coefficient $w_j$, and $b^{(2)}$ plays the role of $w_0$. The difference is that $\bm{w}_j$ and $b^{(1)}_j$ inside the basis function are also learned. A neural network can be read as a linear model with “basis functions that move to fit the data.”

14.5.2 A single tanh unit is a “step with a location and a width”

When the input is one-dimensional ($x=s$), a tanh hidden unit $z_j(s)=\tanh(w_js+b_j)$ ($w_j\ne0$) is a step that changes smoothly from $-1$ to $+1$ (from $+1$ to $-1$ if $w_j\lt0$) around the point where $a=w_js+b_j=0$,

$$ s_j^{\ast}=-\frac{b_j}{w_j} $$

The slope at the center is $w_j$, and the width of the transition on which $\abs{a}\le1$ is $2/\abs{w_j}$. In other words, learning the bias $b_j$ and the weight $w_j$ amounts to learning the location and sharpness of the step. If the input is $D$-dimensional, $z_j(\bm{x})=\tanh(\bm{w}_j^{\tp}\bm{x}+b_j)$ is a “ridge”-like function (a ridge function) that changes only in the direction of the normal $\bm{w}_j$, across the hyperplane $\bm{w}_j^{\tp}\bm{x}+b_j=0$. Since the direction of $\bm{w}_j$ is also learned, the data decide which direction of change is picked up.

14.6 The Output Layer and the Loss — The Error Is Always ŷ − y

14.6.1 Choosing the output layer for the task

The hidden layers can be built in the same way whatever the task, but the activation function and the loss of the output layer are determined by the task. All of them come from the same idea: “read the output as the parameters of a probability distribution, and take the negative log-likelihood of the data under that distribution as the loss” (maximum likelihood estimation (MLE); Chapter 4, Section 4.2). Below, we write the activation of the last layer as $\bm{a}=\bm{a}^{(n_{\mathrm{layer}})}$, the output as $\hat{\bm{y}}$, and the loss of one data point as $\ell$, and define the error of the output layer as

$$ \bm{\delta}^{(n_{\mathrm{layer}})}\equiv\pdiff{\ell}{\bm{a}^{(n_{\mathrm{layer}})}}\quad(\text{component }\delta_k=\partial\ell/\partial a_k) $$

. In Chapter 15 we distribute this $\bm{\delta}$ in order from the output toward the input.

14.6.2 Regression: the identity map and the sum of squares

Suppose the target value is $y=f(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})+\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon\sim\Normal(0,\sigma^{2})$ (here $\sigma$ is the noise standard deviation, not the sigmoid). Then the negative log-likelihood of one data point is

$$ \begin{aligned} -\ln\Normal(y\mid\hat{y},\sigma^{2})&=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(\hat{y}-y)^{2}+\frac12\ln(2\pi\sigma^{2}),\\ \hat{y}&=f(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}) \end{aligned} $$

. If $\sigma^{2}$ is fixed, only the first term depends on $\bm{\theta}$, and apart from the positive constant $1/\sigma^{2}$ we obtain $\ell=\tfrac12(\hat{y}-y)^{2}$. The output layer is taken to be the identity map $\hat{y}=a$ (the target value can take any real value, so we do not restrict the range).

\begin{equation} \delta=\pdiff{\ell}{a}=a-y=\hat{y}-y . \label{eq:14-delta-reg} \end{equation}

14.6.3 Binary classification: sigmoid and cross-entropy

We model $y\in\{0,1\}$ by the Bernoulli distribution $p(y\mid\bm{x})=\hat{y}^{\,y}(1-\hat{y})^{1-y}$. The negative log-likelihood is

$$ \ell=-\ln p(y\mid\bm{x})=-y\ln\hat{y}-(1-y)\ln(1-\hat{y}) $$

, which is the cross-entropy (Chapter 5, Section 5.5). Since $\hat{y}$ must lie between 0 and 1, we take the output layer to be $\hat{y}=\sigma(a)$. Differentiating with respect to $a$:

\begin{align} \pdiff{\ell}{a}&=\Bigl(-\frac{y}{\hat{y}}+\frac{1-y}{1-\hat{y}}\Bigr)\diff{\hat{y}}{a} \notag\\ &=\Bigl(-\frac{y}{\hat{y}}+\frac{1-y}{1-\hat{y}}\Bigr)\hat{y}(1-\hat{y}) \notag\\ &=-y(1-\hat{y})+(1-y)\hat{y} \notag\\ &=\hat{y}-y . \label{eq:14-delta-bin} \end{align}

In the second line we used $\sigma'(a)=\sigma(a)\{1-\sigma(a)\}$ from Eq. \eqref{eq:14-sigmoid}. The expansion in the third and fourth lines is $-y+y\hat{y}+\hat{y}-y\hat{y}=\hat{y}-y$. The derivative of the sigmoid, $\hat{y}(1-\hat{y})$, cancels exactly against the denominators of the cross-entropy.

In numerical computation, to avoid $\hat{y}$ being rounded to 0 or 1 and giving $\ln0$, we use the form of $\ell$ written directly in terms of $a$, $\ell=\ln(1+e^{a})-ya$ (when $a$ is large, rewrite $\ln(1+e^{a})$ as $a+\ln(1+e^{-a})$ to prevent overflow).

14.6.4 Multiclass classification: softmax and cross-entropy

We represent the label of $K$ classes by a one-hot vector $\bm{y}\in\{0,1\}^{K}$ (only the component of the correct class is 1). The output layer is the softmax

$$ \hat{y}_k=\softmax(\bm{a})_k=\frac{e^{a_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{a_j}}\qquad(k=1,\dots,K) $$

, so that $\hat{y}_k\gt0$ and $\sum_k\hat{y}_k=1$ are satisfied. The negative log-likelihood of the categorical distribution is $\ell=-\sum_k y_k\ln\hat{y}_k$. Since $\ln\hat{y}_k=a_k-\ln\sum_j e^{a_j}$, differentiating with respect to $a_m$ gives

$$ \pdiff{\ln\hat{y}_k}{a_m}=\delta_{km}-\frac{e^{a_m}}{\sum_j e^{a_j}}=\delta_{km}-\hat{y}_m $$

($\delta_{km}$ is the Kronecker delta: 1 if $k=m$ and 0 otherwise; it is distinct from the error $\bm{\delta}$). Therefore

\begin{align} \pdiff{\ell}{a_m}&=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}y_k\bigl(\delta_{km}-\hat{y}_m\bigr) \notag\\ &=-y_m+\hat{y}_m\sum_{k=1}^{K}y_k \notag\\ &=\hat{y}_m-y_m \label{eq:14-delta-soft} \end{align}

. In the last step we used the one-hot property $\sum_k y_k=1$. To compute the softmax, we subtract $\max_j a_j$ from all components before exponentiating (the log-sum-exp trick; Chapter 9, Section 9.5), which prevents overflow.

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \bm{\delta}^{(n_{\mathrm{layer}})}&=\pdiff{\ell}{\bm{a}^{(n_{\mathrm{layer}})}}\\ &=\hat{\bm{y}}-\bm{y} \end{aligned} \label{eq:14-delta} \end{equation}

(This holds for each of identity + half the sum of squares, sigmoid + cross-entropy, and softmax + cross-entropy.)

In the three combinations, the error of the output layer takes the same form, “prediction − correct answer”. This is not a coincidence: each is a combination in which the natural parameter of an exponential family (Chapter 3, Section 3.11) is taken to be $a$ (the canonical link function). Conversely, if we combine the sigmoid output with the squared error, we get $\delta=(\hat{y}-y)\hat{y}(1-\hat{y})$, and the error becomes small when the prediction is far off and saturated (Exercise 14.3).

14.6.5 Conventions for the minibatch loss and weight decay

In Chapters 14 and 15 of this book and in Simulator S05, we write the loss of a minibatch $\mathcal{B}$ (a set of data indices, of size $B=\abs{\mathcal{B}}$) as a mean, and add weight decay (the regularization of Chapter 6, Section 6.6):

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} L(\bm{\theta})={}&\frac{1}{B}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{B}}\ell_n\\ &+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{n_{\mathrm{layer}}}\norm{\bm{W}^{(l)}}^{2} \end{aligned} \label{eq:14-loss-mean} \end{equation}

Here $\ell_n$ is the loss of the $n$th data point, and $\norm{\bm{W}^{(l)}}^{2}$ is the sum of the squares of all components of the matrix. Weight decay is not applied to the biases (a bias is merely a translation of the output, and there is no reason to make it small). In Chapters 8 and 9 we wrote the loss as a sum, so even for the same $\lambda$ the effect differs by a factor of $B$ (or $N$ if all the data are used). When comparing values of $\lambda$ in the literature or in software, we must check whether the loss is a mean or a sum. Writing the loss as a mean has the advantage that the appropriate magnitude of the learning rate $\eta$ depends little on the number of data points (Chapter 7, Section 7.5).

14.7 The Universal Approximation Theorem — What Can Be Represented, What Can Be Found, and What GeneralizesDetailed Route

14.7.1 Statement of the theorem

How far does the class of functions a network can represent extend when we insert just one hidden layer? The answer is: “if we increase the number of hidden units enough, any continuous function can be approximated to arbitrary accuracy.”

Theorem 14.2: Universal approximation theorem (one hidden layer)

Let $K\subset\R^{D}$ be a bounded closed set and $g:K\to\R$ a continuous function. Suppose the activation function $h$ is continuous and not a polynomial (sigmoid, tanh, and ReLU satisfy this). Then for any $\varepsilon\gt0$ there exist a number of hidden units $H$ and weights $\bm{w}_j\in\R^{D}$, $b_j,\,v_j,\,c\in\R$ such that, setting

$$ f_H(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{H}v_j\,h\bigl(\bm{w}_j^{\tp}\bm{x}+b_j\bigr)+c $$

we have

\begin{equation} \sup_{\bm{x}\in K}\,\abs{g(\bm{x})-f_H(\bm{x})}\lt\varepsilon \label{eq:14-uat} \end{equation}

.

For sigmoid-type activation functions, Cybenko (1989) (for continuous functions that approach 0 as $a\to-\infty$ and 1 as $a\to+\infty$) and Hornik, Stinchcombe & White (1989) (for monotone nondecreasing functions that change from 0 to 1) proved the result independently in the same year. Leshno et al. (1993) showed that, for locally bounded, piecewise continuous activation functions, a network with one hidden layer can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary accuracy (uniformly on a bounded closed set) if and only if the activation function is not a polynomial (assuming the bias $b_j$ is included). Theorem 14.2 is a special case of this. $f_H$ is exactly the network of Eq. \eqref{eq:14-nn-basis} with one hidden layer and an identity output.

14.7.2 In one dimension, ReLU piecewise linear functions can build the approximation

The proof for general $D$ uses tools from functional analysis, so this book does not cover it; but when the input is one-dimensional and ReLU is used, we can construct the approximating network explicitly.

Derivation: Piecewise linear interpolation with ReLU and an estimate of the error

Divide the interval $[s_0,s_K]$ into $K$ equal parts, and build the piecewise linear function that connects the values $g(s_k)$ at the nodes $s_k=s_0+kh_s$ (with $h_s=(s_K-s_0)/K$). Let $m_k=\{g(s_k)-g(s_{k-1})\}/h_s$ denote the slope on the $k$th interval $[s_{k-1},s_k]$.

(i) Write the piecewise linear function as a sum of ReLUs. Since $\relu(s-s_k)$ is 0 for $s\le s_k$ and a function of slope 1 for $s\ge s_k$, if we set

$$ \begin{aligned} f_K(s)={}&g(s_0)+m_1\,\relu(s-s_0)\\ &+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}(m_{k+1}-m_k)\,\relu(s-s_k) \end{aligned} $$

then the slope on the interval $[s_{k-1},s_k]$ is $m_1+(m_2-m_1)+\dots+(m_k-m_{k-1})=m_k$. Together with $f_K(s_0)=g(s_0)$, this shows that $f_K$ is a piecewise linear function that agrees with $g$ at the nodes. There are $K$ hidden units (weights $w_k=1$, biases $b_k=-s_k$); the output-layer weights are $v_1=m_1$ and $v_{k+1}=m_{k+1}-m_k$, and the bias is $c=g(s_0)$.

(ii) Estimate the error. Suppose $g$ is twice differentiable and $\abs{g''}\le M_2$. Within the interval $[s_{k-1},s_k]$, $e(s)=g(s)-f_K(s)$ is 0 at both endpoints, and $e''=g''$ ($f_K$ is a linear function within the interval). By the formula for the error of linear interpolation (a standard result obtained from Taylor's theorem),

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \abs{e(s)}&=\frac{\abs{g''(\xi)}}{2}\\ &\quad\times(s-s_{k-1})\\ &\quad\times(s_k-s)\\ &\le\frac{M_2}{2}\cdot\frac{h_s^{2}}{4}\\ &=\frac{M_2h_s^{2}}{8} \end{aligned} \label{eq:14-pl-err} \end{equation}

($\xi$ is some point in the interval; the maximum of $(s-s_{k-1})(s_k-s)$ is $h_s^{2}/4$, attained at the midpoint of the interval). Since $h_s=(s_K-s_0)/K$, the error decreases in proportion to $1/K^{2}$, and it can be made as small as we like by taking $K$ large. Even if $g$ is merely continuous, uniform continuity allows us to make the maximum error arbitrarily small by taking $K$ large.

∎

For the true function of data A, $g(s)=88+8s-5s^{2}+2\sin3s$, we have $g''(s)=-10-18\sin3s$, and its maximum on $[-1,1]$ is $M_2=28$ (at $s=\pi/6$, where $\sin3s=1$). Dividing $[-1,1]$ into $K$ equal parts gives $h_s=2/K$, so the upper limit in Eq. \eqref{eq:14-pl-err} is $28\cdot(2/K)^{2}/8=14/K^{2}$ (%). In Figure 14.6, the actual maximum error decreases along this upper limit. For comparison, we also drew an approximation of $g$ by steps, using sigmoids made steep enough to act as steps (step functions). With steps, the error decreases only as $1/K$. Even for the same statement “can be approximated,” the number of units required differs greatly depending on the activation function and on the smoothness of the function to be approximated.

For the network $f(s)=c+v_1\relu(s+1)+v_2\relu(s)$ that represents the true function $g(s)$ of data A by a piecewise linear function with nodes $s=-1,\,0,\,1$ ($K=2$), find $c$, $v_1$, and $v_2$. Compare $f$ and $g$ at $s=0.5$ (1400 °C), and compare the error with the upper limit in Eq. \eqref{eq:14-pl-err}.

Solution. The values at the nodes are $g(-1)=88-8-5+2\sin(-3)$ $=75-0.282240=74.717760$, $g(0)=88$, and $g(1)=88+8-5+2\sin3=91.282240$ (%). The slopes are $m_1=(88-74.717760)/1=13.282240$ and $m_2=(91.282240-88)/1=3.282240$. Hence $c=g(-1)=74.7178$, $v_1=m_1=13.2822$, and $v_2=m_2-m_1=-10.0000$, so that

$$ \begin{aligned} f(s)={}&74.7178+13.2822\,\relu(s+1)\\ &-10.0000\,\relu(s). \end{aligned} $$

At $s=0.5$, $f(0.5)=74.7178+13.2822\times1.5$ $-10.0000\times0.5$ $=74.7178+19.9234-5.0000$ $=89.6411$. The true value is $g(0.5)=88+4-1.25+2\sin1.5$ $=90.75+1.994990=92.7450$, so the error is $3.10\ \%$. The upper limit, with $h_s=1$ and $M_2=28$, is $28/8=3.5\ \%$, so the error lies within the upper limit. This is also the maximum error on $[-1,1]$ ($3.10\ \%$ at $s\approx0.505$).

14.7.3 What the theorem does not say

The universal approximation theorem says only that approximating weights exist. It does not say the following three things.

  1. The number of units required: How large $H$ must be depends on $g$ and $D$. When the input dimension is high, there are functions that require exponentially many units with one hidden layer. For example, there are known $D$-dimensional (nearly spherically symmetric) functions that a small network with two hidden layers can represent, but that a network with one hidden layer cannot approximate to a fixed accuracy unless its width is made exponentially large in $D$ (Eldan & Shamir 2016; this holds for most activation functions, including sigmoid and ReLU). Also for ReLU and similar networks, there are functions that deeper networks can represent with few units, whereas shallower networks require a number of units exponential in the number of layers (Telgarsky 2016). The existence of such families of functions that can be represented with few units by going deeper is one of the reasons for using deep networks (whether the function behind real data belongs to such a family is, however, a separate question).
  2. Whether learning can find it: Even if there are weights satisfying Eq. \eqref{eq:14-uat}, gradient descent is not guaranteed to reach them. The loss is nonconvex, with local solutions and flat regions. In the XOR example of Simulator 14.1, although the structure is capable of representing it ($H=2$), the accuracy stayed at 75 % with the default seed. This is because the two learned hidden units mapped two clusters with different labels to the same point in the hidden layer, so that the output layer could no longer distinguish them.
  3. Whether it generalizes: The theorem discusses approximation on the assumption that the true function $g$ is known. What we actually have in hand is only a finite amount of data containing noise, and being able to fit the training data does not guarantee predicting unknown data correctly (Chapter 6). The greater the expressive power, the greater the danger of overfitting, in which even the noise is memorized.

To summarize, being able to represent (expressive power), being able to find by learning (optimization), and predicting unknown data correctly (generalization) are separate problems. The universal approximation theorem answers only the first. The second is treated in Chapter 7 and Chapter 15, and the third in Chapter 6 and Chapter 15, Section 15.8.

14.8 Counting Parameters and the Symmetries of Weight SpaceDetailed Route

14.8.1 The number of parameters of a fully connected layer

The weight matrix $\bm{W}^{(l)}\in\R^{D_l\times D_{l-1}}$ of layer $l$ has $D_lD_{l-1}$ components, and the bias $\bm{b}^{(l)}\in\R^{D_l}$ has $D_l$ components. Therefore

\begin{align} (\text{layer }l\text{ })&=D_l\,(D_{l-1}+1), \notag\\ (\text{total})&=\sum_{l=1}^{n_{\mathrm{layer}}}D_l\,(D_{l-1}+1) \label{eq:14-nparam} \end{align}

The “$+1$” accounts for the bias; if we think of it as appending a component that is always 1 to the input, the bias can be regarded as part of the weights. A 2–3–1 network has $3\cdot3+1\cdot4=13$ parameters (Table 14.1). If we double the width $D_l$, the parameters of the layers before and after it roughly double each, and if we double the widths on both sides, they become four times as many.

14.8.2 More than one set of weights represents the same function

Consider the network of Eq. \eqref{eq:14-nn-basis} with one hidden layer and $H$ hidden units.

  • Permutation: for hidden units $j$ and $k$, if we swap the incoming weights $(\bm{w}_j,b_j)$ and the outgoing weight $W^{(2)}_j$ as a whole, only the order of the sum changes and the output is the same. There are $H!$ ways to permute.
  • Sign flip (for odd functions such as tanh): since $\tanh(-a)=-\tanh a$, the quantity $W^{(2)}_j\tanh(\bm{w}_j^{\tp}\bm{x}+b_j)$ does not change under $(\bm{w}_j,b_j,W^{(2)}_j)\to(-\bm{w}_j,-b_j,-W^{(2)}_j)$. We can choose for each unit whether or not to flip, so there are $2^{H}$ ways.
  • Scale (ReLU): ReLU is not an odd function, so there is no sign-flip symmetry. Instead, for $c\gt0$ we have $\relu(ca)=c\,\relu(a)$, so the output does not change under $(\bm{w}_j,b_j,W^{(2)}_j)\to(c\bm{w}_j,cb_j,W^{(2)}_j/c)$. This is a continuous (infinite) family of symmetries.

In a tanh network with one hidden layer, at least $H!\,2^{H}$ sets of weights represent the same function as a given set. If there are several hidden layers, we multiply this number layer by layer.

What the symmetries mean

(1) If the loss $L(\bm{\theta})$ has a local minimum, every point obtained from it by a symmetry is also a local minimum with the same value. The loss landscape has many valleys of the same shape lined up, so it cannot be convex (if two points of a convex function were both minima, the whole line segment between them would also have to consist of minima, but at the midpoint of two permuted points the loss is in general larger). (2) The values of the weights themselves have no fixed meaning such as “hidden unit 1 is in charge of temperature.” If we train again, the numbering and the signs are exchanged. Care is needed when one tries to interpret individual weights physically. (3) If we set all the initial values equal (for example, all 0), training proceeds with the permutation symmetry preserved, and the hidden units keep the same values without ever becoming distinguishable. This is the reason initialization is discussed in Chapter 15, Section 15.6.

Example 14.8: Counting the parameters of a network for composition descriptors

Consider a network (8–32–32–1) that takes 8 descriptors as input, has two hidden layers (32 units each, tanh), and has one output. (1) Find the number of parameters. (2) How many sets of weights represent the same function through hidden-unit permutations and sign flips alone? (3) When there are 200 training data points, what can be said about the relation between the number of parameters and the number of data points?

Solution. (1) By Eq. \eqref{eq:14-nparam}, the first layer has $32\times(8+1)=288$, the second layer $32\times(32+1)=1056$, and the third layer $1\times(32+1)=33$. The total is $288+1056+33=1377$ parameters.

(2) For one hidden layer, $32!\times2^{32}$ $\approx2.63\times10^{35}\times4.29\times10^{9}$ $\approx1.13\times10^{45}$ ways. Since the two layers can be chosen independently, there are $(1.13\times10^{45})^{2}\approx1.28\times10^{90}$ ways.

(3) The parameters (1377) outnumber the data points (200). This is a situation in which, for linear regression, the solution would not be uniquely determined, and the network is also likely to reproduce the training data almost perfectly. Weight decay (Eq. \eqref{eq:14-loss-mean}), early stopping, and checking with validation data (Chapter 6; Chapter 15, Section 15.8) are indispensable. However, because of the symmetries, the “effective degrees of freedom that can be moved freely” are fewer than the number of parameters, and generalization cannot be judged from the ratio of the number of parameters to the number of data points alone.

14.9 Connections to Materials Engineering and Condensed Matter Physics — Regression from Descriptors and Per-Atom EnergiesDetailed Route

14.9.1 Nonlinear regression from descriptors to physical properties

Application: Predicting physical properties from composition descriptors

From the composition of a material we compute numerical quantities such as the mean atomic mass, the mean electronegativity, the ratio of ionic radii, and the number of valence electrons (descriptors), take them as the input $\bm{x}\in\R^{D}$, and predict a physical property $y$ such as the band gap or the formation energy. This problem setting is fundamental in the analysis of materials data. Physical properties are often nonlinear functions of the descriptors, and combinations of descriptors (for example, “the electronegativity difference is large and, at the same time, the ratio of ionic radii is small”) often matter. A hidden unit in Eq. \eqref{eq:14-nn-basis} builds the weighted sum of the descriptors $\bm{w}_j^{\tp}\bm{x}+b_j$, a “new combination of descriptors,” and uses its nonlinear response as a basis function. The difference from regression with fixed basis functions (Chapter 8) is that a person does not have to decide which combinations matter.

On the other hand, as we saw in Example 14.8, with several tens to several hundreds of experimental data points the number of parameters of a network quickly reaches several thousand. When data are scarce, it is better to try linear regression or Bayesian linear regression (Chapter 8) and Gaussian process regression (Chapter 12) first. These give solutions that are found stably and can also provide the uncertainty of the prediction. Networks show their real strength when the data are sufficiently abundant, as with thousands to millions of data points generated by first-principles calculations.

14.9.2 Expressing the total energy as a sum of per-atom energies

A function that returns the energy from the arrangement (coordinates) of the atoms is called the potential energy surface. The force acting on an atom is its negative gradient, $\bm{F}_i=-\partial E/\partial\bm{r}_i$, and once this is known we can carry out molecular dynamics calculations that follow the motion of the atoms numerically. Computing the energy with first-principles calculations (calculations that obtain the state of the electrons from the equations of quantum mechanics) is highly accurate, but the computation time grows rapidly as the number of atoms increases. Therefore machine-learning potentials, which learn a function for the energy from data generated by first-principles calculations and then compute it cheaply and accurately, are being studied.

Behler & Parrinello (2007) proposed representing this function with a neural network. The idea is as follows (Figure 14.7).

  1. Split the total energy into a sum of per-atom energies: \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} E&=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{at}}}E_i,\\ E_i&=\mathrm{NN}_{\mathrm{el}(i)}\bigl(\bm{G}_i\bigr) \end{aligned} \label{eq:14-bp} \end{equation} Here $N_{\mathrm{at}}$ is the number of atoms, $\mathrm{el}(i)$ is the element of atom $i$, and $\mathrm{NN}_{\mathrm{el}}$ is a network prepared for each element (atoms of the same element share the same weights).
  2. The input $\bm{G}_i$ of each atom is a descriptor of the environment around atom $i$ (inside a cutoff radius), built only from interatomic distances and angles. For example, the simplest form, which uses only distances, is $$ G_i=\sum_{j\ne i}\exp\!\left(-\eta_G\,r_{ij}^{2}\right)f_{\mathrm{c}}(r_{ij}) $$ ($r_{ij}$ is the distance between atoms $i,j$, $\eta_G$ is a constant that determines the width, and $f_{\mathrm{c}}$ is a function that vanishes at the cutoff radius). Functions of this kind are called symmetry functions (Behler 2011).

With this form, the physically required properties are satisfied automatically.

  • Invariance under permutation: even if we relabel atoms of the same element, only the order of the sum in Eq. \eqref{eq:14-bp} changes and $E$ is the same.
  • Invariance under translation and rotation: since $\bm{G}_i$ is determined only by distances and angles, $E$ is the same when the whole system is translated or rotated.
  • Extensivity: if we compute two systems far apart together, the energy is the sum. The same network can be used for structures with different numbers of atoms.

If we feed the vector that lists all the atomic coordinates directly into one large network, none of these properties holds (the labeling of the atoms alone produces $N_{\mathrm{at}}!$ different inputs, and the dimension of the input changes when the number of atoms changes). The general theory of building the symmetries of a structure into a model is treated in Chapter 16, Section 16.7, and machine-learning potentials are discussed in detail in Chapter 27, Section 27.7. The force $\bm{F}_i=-\partial E/\partial\bm{r}_i$ is obtained by differentiating through the network and the descriptors, and the backpropagation of Chapter 15 can be used as it is for this computation.

Example 14.9: Computing the energy of a toy model of three atoms

In a system consisting of a single element, let the descriptor be $G_i=\sum_{j\ne i}\exp\{-(r_{ij}/r_0)^{2}\}$ ($r_0=2.5$ Å, no cutoff) and let the atomic network, with one hidden unit, be $E_i=0.5\tanh(2G_i-1)-3.0$ (eV) (the numbers are provisional values for illustration and do not represent any real material). (1) Place the atoms on a straight line at $x=0,\ 2.5,\ 5.0$ Å, and find $G_i$, $E_i$, and $E$. (2) Verify that $E$ does not change when the atoms are relabeled or when the whole system is rotated.

Solution. (1) The distances are $r_{12}=r_{23}=2.5$ Å and $r_{13}=5.0$ Å. The values of $r/r_0$ are 1 and 2, so $e^{-1}=0.367879$ and $e^{-4}=0.018316$. For the end atoms 1 and 3, $G=e^{-1}+e^{-4}=0.386195$, and for the central atom 2, $G=2e^{-1}=0.735759$.

End atoms: $2G-1=-0.227610$, $\tanh(-0.227610)=-0.223757$, $E=0.5\times(-0.223757)-3.0=-3.111879$ eV. Central atom: $2G-1=0.471518$, $\tanh0.471518=0.439425$, $E=-2.780288$ eV. The total is $E=2\times(-3.111879)+(-2.780288)$ $=-9.004047$ eV.

(2) Relabeling: if we place atom 1 at $x=5.0$, atom 2 at $x=0$, and atom 3 at $x=2.5$ Å, the central atom becomes number 3 and the list changes to $(E_1,E_2,E_3)$ $=(-3.111879,\,-3.111879,\,-2.780288)$, but the sum is the same, $-9.004047$ eV. Rotation: even if we rearrange the three atoms along the $y$ axis (for example, $(1,1,1)$, $(1,3.5,1)$, $(1,6.0,1)$ Å), the distances do not change, so $G_i$ and $E$ are the same. Because the input is built from distances alone and the per-atom outputs are combined by a sum, these invariances are guaranteed by design.

14.10 Common Misconceptions and Limits of ApplicabilityDetailed Route

Caution: A neural network is not a model of the brain

The names “neuron” and “network” come from inspiration drawn from nerve cells. However, what we treated in this chapter is a family of mathematical functions built from weighted sums and compositions with nonlinear functions; it does not reproduce the electrical activity of real nerve cells or the mechanisms of learning in the brain. Do not read properties such as “thinks like a brain” or “understands” into the name. Discuss properties starting from Eq. \eqref{eq:14-layer} and the training procedure.

Caution: Deeper and larger does not always mean better

Adding layers or units increases the functions that can be represented, but it also increases the number of parameters, and with little data the model overfits more easily (Chapter 6). In data A of Simulator 14.2, increasing $H$ from 3 to 8 hardly changed the validation error. Making the network deeper also makes training itself harder (vanishing gradients; Chapter 15, Section 15.7). Choose the size of the model according to its performance on validation data and to the quantity and quality of the data.

Caution: Predictions outside the training range (extrapolation) are not guaranteed

The universal approximation theorem merely states an approximation within a bounded range $K$. Outside the range, the shape of the network determines the prediction. A ReLU network is piecewise linear, so outside the range it becomes a straight line that simply extends the outermost line segment. A tanh network has its hidden units saturate outside the range, and the prediction approaches a constant. Neither knows about physical constraints (for example, that the relative density does not exceed 100 %) (Example 14.10).

Caution: Do not read physical meaning into individual weights

By the symmetry of Section 14.8, there are innumerable sets of weights that give the same prediction, and each time we train again, both the numbering and the signs of the hidden units are interchanged. It is risky to conclude that “because the weight of hidden unit 2 is large, this descriptor is important.” When we want to know which inputs matter, we use quantities that are unchanged by the symmetry, such as examining how the prediction changes when the input is varied slightly (for example, the derivative of the prediction with respect to the input).

Caution: “Universal” does not mean “can learn anything”

As we saw in Section 14.7, the universal approximation theorem says only that weights that approximate exist. The number of units required, whether training can find them, and whether the model generalizes from limited data are separate questions. As in the XOR problem of Simulator 14.1, even for a small problem training sometimes fails to find weights that can represent the function.

Example 14.10: Extrapolation of ReLU and tanh networks

(1) For the ReLU network of Example 14.7, $f(s)=74.7178+13.2822\,\relu(s+1)$ $-10.0000\,\relu(s)$, find the predictions at $s=2,\ 3,\ 4$ ($T=1700,\ 1900,\ 2100\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$), outside the training range. (2) The tanh network of Figure 14.5 ($H=3$, after 2000 epochs) has weights $\bm{W}^{(1)}=(-1.2417,\ 1.5103,\ -0.2983)^{\tp}$, $\bm{b}^{(1)}=(-0.8601,\ 0.2723,\ -0.1143)^{\tp}$, $\bm{W}^{(2)}=(-1.3520,\ 0.5462,\ 0.4132)$, $b^{(2)}=-0.5512$, and standardization constants $\bar{y}=85.138\ \%$, $s_y=7.152\ \%$. Find the limits of the prediction as $s\to+\infty$ and as $s\to-\infty$.

Solution. (1) For $s\gt0$ both ReLUs are active, and $f(s)=74.7178+13.2822(s+1)-10.0000s$ $=88.0000+3.2822s$ is a straight line (the extension of the line segment on the last interval $[0,1]$). $f(2)=94.56\ \%$, $f(3)=97.85\ \%$, $f(4)=101.13\ \%$. At $T=2100\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ the relative density exceeds 100 %, a value that is physically impossible.

(2) As $s\to+\infty$, $\tanh(w_js+b_j)\to\sgn(w_j)$, so $\bm{z}\to(-1,\,+1,\,-1)$. We have $W^{(2)}\bm{z}=1.3520+0.5462-0.4132=1.4851$; adding $b^{(2)}$ gives $0.9339$, and the prediction is $85.138+7.152\times0.9339=91.82\ \%$. As $s\to-\infty$, $\bm{z}\to(+1,-1,+1)$, $W^{(2)}\bm{z}=-1.4851$; adding $b^{(2)}$ gives $-2.0363$, and the prediction is $85.138-7.152\times2.0363=70.58\ \%$. The tanh network approaches a constant value outside the range (at $s=2$ it is 92.93 %). That ReLU and tanh behave completely differently under extrapolation shows that extrapolation is determined not by the data but by the form of the model. To trust predictions outside the data, we need either a model that incorporates physical constraints or a model that can report uncertainty (Chapter 12).

14.11.1 Summary

  • Neuron: pass the weighted sum $a=\bm{w}^{\tp}\bm{x}+b$ through an activation function to get $z=h(a)$. A single sigmoid neuron is logistic regression, and its decision boundary is a hyperplane.
  • Activation functions: sigmoid ($\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\le1/4$), tanh ($1-\tanh^{2}$, $\tanh a=2\sigma(2a)-1$), ReLU (the derivative is 0 or 1). GELU is a smoothed version of ReLU.
  • Layers: $\bm{a}^{(l)}=\bm{W}^{(l)}\bm{z}^{(l-1)}+\bm{b}^{(l)}$, $\bm{W}^{(l)}\in\R^{D_l\times D_{l-1}}$. With a minibatch this becomes the matrix product $\bm{W}^{(l)}\bm{Z}^{(l-1)}$. The number of parameters is $\sum_lD_l(D_{l-1}+1)$.
  • Nonlinearity: no matter how many layers of the identity map we stack, the result is a single linear map. XOR cannot be separated by one neuron, but it can be separated if a hidden layer maps it to a “separable configuration.”
  • Moving basis: a network with one hidden layer is a linear model that learns even the location, direction, and sharpness of the basis $h(\bm{w}_j^{\tp}\bm{x}+b_j)$. In exchange, the loss becomes nonconvex.
  • Output layer and loss: identity + sum of squares/2, sigmoid + cross-entropy, and softmax + cross-entropy all follow from maximum likelihood estimation, and in every case the error at the output is $\hat{\bm{y}}-\bm{y}$. In Chapters 14 and 15 of this book we write the loss as the average over a minibatch, and weight decay is not applied to the biases.
  • Universal approximation and symmetry: one hidden layer can approximate a continuous function to any accuracy, but being representable, being findable by training, and generalizing are separate matters. There are at least $H!\,2^{H}$ sets of weights representing the same function (tanh).
  • Connections to materials: nonlinear regression from descriptors to physical properties, and machine-learning potentials based on the sum of per-atom energies $E=\sum_iE_i(\bm{G}_i)$. When data are scarce, the methods of Chapters 8 and 12 come first.

Consider a 2–2–1 network with a tanh hidden layer and a sigmoid output, and let $\bm{W}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $\bm{b}^{(1)}=(0,\,-0.5)^{\tp}$, $\bm{W}^{(2)}=(2,\,-1)$, $b^{(2)}=-1$. (1) For the input $\bm{x}=(0.5,\,1.0)^{\tp}$, find $\bm{a}^{(1)}$, $\bm{z}^{(1)}$, $a^{(2)}$, and $\hat{y}$. (2) Find the cross-entropy loss and $\delta^{(2)}$ for $y=1$. (3) What is $\hat{y}$ if the hidden layer is replaced by ReLU? (4) Count the number of parameters of this network.

Hint: $\tanh1.5\approx0.905$, $\tanh1\approx0.762$. For (2), use Eq. \eqref{eq:14-delta-bin}.

→ Solution

Exercise 14.2: The relation between tanh and sigmoid (derivation)

(1) Show that $\tanh a=2\sigma(2a)-1$. (2) Show that the network with a tanh hidden layer $f(\bm{x})=\sum_jW^{(2)}_j\tanh(\bm{w}_j^{\tp}\bm{x}+b_j)+b^{(2)}$ can be represented exactly by a network with a sigmoid hidden layer after changing the weights and biases, and write the formulas for the change. (3) From this result, does the “set of representable functions” change depending on whether the hidden layer is sigmoid or tanh? What about ease of learning?

Hint: For (1), write the right-hand side in terms of $e^{-2a}$ and put it over a common denominator. For (2), substitute (1) and collect the coefficient of $\sigma$ and the constant.

→ Solution

Exercise 14.3: What goes wrong if squared error is used with a sigmoid output? (derivation and computation)

Consider the case where the squared error $\ell_{\mathrm{SE}}=\tfrac12(\hat{y}-y)^{2}$ is used with the output $\hat{y}=\sigma(a)$. (1) Find $\partial\ell_{\mathrm{SE}}/\partial a$. (2) For $y=1$ and $a=-5$ (a prediction that is far off), compare numerically $\delta=\hat{y}-y$ in the cross-entropy case with $\partial\ell_{\mathrm{SE}}/\partial a$ in the squared-error case. (3) State the effect of this difference on learning.

Hint: $\sigma(-5)=1/(1+e^{5})$.

→ Solution

Exercise 14.4: One hidden unit cannot separate XOR (derivation)

Consider the 2–1–1 network $\hat{y}=\sigma\bigl(v\,h(\bm{w}^{\tp}\bm{x}+b)+c\bigr)$. Suppose $h$ is monotone nondecreasing (sigmoid, tanh, ReLU, and so on). (1) Show that $\{\bm{x}:\hat{y}\gt1/2\}$ can be written as one of the conditions “$u\gt t$,” “$u\ge t$,” “$u\lt t$,” “$u\le t$” on $u=\bm{w}^{\tp}\bm{x}+b$ (or as the set of all $\bm{x}$, or the empty set). (2) From this, show that the four XOR points cannot be separated correctly. (3) Relate this to the result of step 2 of Simulator 14.1 (accuracy 73 %).

Hint: $\hat{y}\gt1/2\iff vh(u)+c\gt0$. Split into cases by the sign of $v$ and use the monotonicity of $h$. (2) is the same argument as Eq. \eqref{eq:14-xor-ineq}.

→ Solution

Exercise 14.5: Representing the band gap of a mixed crystal with ReLU (computation, materials context)

Consider data B (the band gap of a hypothetical mixed crystal A$_{1-x}$B$_x$C; the true function of the synthetic data for teaching purposes) $E_g(x)=(1-x)\,1.20+x\,2.40-0.80\,x(1-x)$ eV ($0\le x\le1$). (1) Find $E_g(0)$, $E_g(0.5)$, and $E_g(1)$. (2) Represent the piecewise linear function through these three points by a network of the form $f(x)=c+v_1\relu(x)+v_2\relu(x-0.5)$. (3) Find the maximum error of the piecewise linear function and the composition at which it occurs, and compare it with the noise standard deviation of 0.03 eV of data B. (4) How many hidden units are needed to bring the maximum error of an equally spaced piecewise linear function down to 0.01 eV or less? (5) State why, for this problem, linear regression with the basis $\{1,\,x,\,x(1-x)\}$ of Chapter 8 is better suited than a neural network.

Hint: For (3) and (4), use Eq. \eqref{eq:14-pl-err}. $E_g''(x)$ is a constant.

→ Solution

Exercise 14.6: Properties of the sum of per-atom energies (computation, materials context)

Use the toy model of Example 14.9 ($G_i=\sum_{j\ne i}\exp\{-(r_{ij}/r_0)^{2}\}$, $r_0=2.5$ Å, $E_i=0.5\tanh(2G_i-1)-3.0$ eV). (1) Find the energy of two atoms (a dimer) at a distance of 2.5 Å. (2) Find the energy of a system of four atoms obtained by placing two such dimers on a straight line at $x=0,\ 2.5,\ 20,\ 22.5$ Å, and estimate how much it differs from twice the result of (1). (3) If we feed the vector of the 30 coordinates of 10 atoms directly into a single network, how many different inputs represent the same structure? List two problems with this approach.

Hint: In (2), estimate the magnitude of the contribution from distant atoms (for the nearest pair, $\exp\{-(17.5/2.5)^{2}\}$).

→ Solution

Exercise 14.7: Symmetries of the weight space (discussion and computation)

(1) For a tanh network with three hidden units (one input, one output), how many sets of weights represent the same function through permutations and sign flips? (2) For $f(s)=1.5\tanh(0.7s+0.2)$ $-0.8\tanh(-1.3s+0.4)+0.1$, write the weights with the sign of hidden unit 1 flipped, and verify that the value does not change at $s=0.37$. (3) Show by an example that the sign-flip symmetry does not hold for ReLU. Show, using $f(s)=2\relu(s-0.5)$ as an example, the symmetry that holds instead.

Hint: For (3), compare $\relu(-a)$ and $-\relu(a)$ at $a=1$. The scale symmetry is $\relu(ca)=c\,\relu(a)$ ($c\gt0$).

→ Solution

Exercise 14.8: Linear networks and narrow layers (computation and derivation)

(1) For a 2–3–1 network whose activation functions are all the identity map, let $\bm{W}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$, $\bm{b}^{(1)}=(1,0,-1)^{\tp}$, $\bm{W}^{(2)}=(1,\,-1,\,2)$, $b^{(2)}=0.5$. Using Eq. \eqref{eq:14-collapse}, find the single-layer $\bm{W}$ and $\bm{b}$ that do the same job. (2) For a 3–1–3 network whose activation functions are the identity map (the width of the middle layer is 1), show that the composed $3\times3$ matrix $\bm{W}^{(2)}\bm{W}^{(1)}$ has rank at most 1. (3) (2) is an example in which “stacking layers reduces what can be represented.” What can this structure, which passes the input through a narrow layer and restores it, be used for?

Hint: For (2), use the fact that $\bm{W}^{(2)}\bm{W}^{(1)}$ is the product of a $3\times1$ matrix and a $1\times3$ matrix. For (3), see Chapter 18 (PCA and autoencoders).

→ Solution
