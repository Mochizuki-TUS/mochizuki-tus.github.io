Chapter 6Second Quantization

In Chapter 5 we introduced the Slater determinant as the starting point for many-electron wave functions, and for a two-electron system we derived the Hartree–Fock (HF) energy and the HF equations by fully expanding $\langle\Phi|\hat{H}|\Phi\rangle$ term by term. But recall that calculation. With only two electrons, merely expanding the bra and ket determinants produced 16 terms, and we had to check one by one that most of them vanish because of the orthogonality of the orbitals. As the number of electrons $N$ grows, the number of terms in the expansion explodes in proportion to $(N!)^2$, so this method is utterly impractical for an $N$-electron system. Yet the way in which the terms vanish is the same pattern every time. It is therefore wiser to sort out this combinatorics of antisymmetrization just once, translate it into algebraic rules for operators, and from then on calculate using only those rules (the anticommutation relations). This is the subject of this chapter, second quantization. The tools developed in this chapter play a central role in the analysis of the jellium model in Chapter 7 and form the theoretical foundation of the chapters that follow.

What you will learn in this chapter
  • The occupation-number representation, which specifies a Slater determinant solely by “which orbitals are occupied,” and the Fock space, which accommodates states with different numbers of particles together
  • The definitions of the creation operator $a_\mu^\dagger$ and the annihilation operator $a_\mu$, and the origin of the sign factor $(-1)^{S_\mu}$
  • A complete proof of the anticommutation relations $\{a_\mu, a_\nu^\dagger\}=\delta_{\mu\nu}$, $\{a_\mu,a_\nu\}=\{a_\mu^\dagger,a_\nu^\dagger\}=0$, and their relation to the Pauli exclusion principle
  • Proofs of the second-quantized forms of one-body and two-body operators (including the derivation of the Slater–Condon rules)
  • The field operator $\hat{\psi}(x)$ and the density operator $\hat{\rho}(\rr)$
  • A calculation that rederives the HF energy in a few lines using only the anticommutation relations

In this chapter, too, we use the Hartree atomic units introduced in Chapter 1 ($\hbar = m_e = e = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 1$; the unit of energy is 1 hartree $= 27.211$ eV). That said, most of the content of this chapter is the development of a “formalism,” and the results obtained do not depend on the choice of the system of units. As in Chapter 5, $x = (\rr, \sigma)$ denotes the composite variable combining the spatial and spin coordinates, and $\int \dd x$ means $\sum_\sigma \int \dd^3 r$.

6.1 Why Second Quantization?

First, we look back quantitatively at how much of a “risky bridge” the calculation of Chapter 5 was, and make clear why second quantization is needed.

6.1.1 Direct calculation with Slater determinants is on the verge of breaking down

The normalized Slater determinant of an $N$-electron system, in terms of the one-electron orbitals $\varphi_{l_1}, \varphi_{l_2}, \ldots, \varphi_{l_N}$, can be written as

$$ \begin{equation} \Phi(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}} \begin{vmatrix} \varphi_{l_1}(x_1) & \varphi_{l_2}(x_1) & \cdots & \varphi_{l_N}(x_1) \\ \varphi_{l_1}(x_2) & \varphi_{l_2}(x_2) & \cdots & \varphi_{l_N}(x_2) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \varphi_{l_1}(x_N) & \varphi_{l_2}(x_N) & \cdots & \varphi_{l_N}(x_N) \end{vmatrix} \label{eq:6-slater-direct} \end{equation} $$

Expanding the determinant gives a sum of $N!$ products. Therefore, if we try to compute the expectation value $\langle\Phi|\hat{H}|\Phi\rangle$ “honestly,” we have to handle all combinations of the $N!$ terms on the bra side and the $N!$ terms on the ket side, that is, $(N!)^2$ multiple integrals. Let us see how rapidly this number grows.

Example: The explosion in the number of expansion terms

Listing the values of $N!$ and $(N!)^2$ gives the following.

  • $N = 2$: $N! = 2$, $(N!)^2 = 4$ (in Chapter 5 we named these four combinations (A) through (D) and handled them for each of $\hat{V}_1$ and $\hat{V}_2$)
  • $N = 10$: $N! \simeq 3.6 \times 10^{6}$, $(N!)^2 \simeq 1.3 \times 10^{13}$
  • $N = 20$: $N! \simeq 2.4 \times 10^{18}$, $(N!)^2 \simeq 5.9 \times 10^{36}$
  • $N = 100$: $N! \simeq 9.3 \times 10^{157}$. This is far larger than the total number of atoms in the observable universe (about $10^{80}$).

Even a water molecule (10 electrons) has $10^{13}$ terms, and benzene (42 electrons) has more than $10^{102}$ terms. The method of expanding the determinants and then integrating would, quite literally, not finish even after the universe has ended.

Yet if we recall the conclusion of Chapter 5, the final result was anticlimactically simple:

$$ \begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{HF}} = \sum_{l=1}^{N} H_l + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{l,l'=1}^{N}\left( J_{ll'} - K_{ll'} \right). \label{eq:6-ehf-recall} \end{equation} $$

Of the $(N!)^2$ terms, the only ones that survive are at most $O(N^2)$ integrals: the one-electron integrals $H_l$, the Coulomb integrals $J_{ll'}$, and the exchange integrals $K_{ll'}$. A huge number of terms disappeared owing to the orthonormality of the orbitals, $\langle\varphi_\mu|\varphi_\nu\rangle = \delta_{\mu\nu}$, but the way they disappear is the same pattern every time, for any determinant. Repeating the same combinatorial bookkeeping every time the system changes is wasteful and also dangerous (a single sign error reverses the sign of the exchange integral).

6.1.2 A change of viewpoint: let operators carry the antisymmetry

The idea of second quantization boils down to a single point.

Physical Meaning: The idea of second quantization

The information a Slater determinant carries is, in fact, only “which one-electron orbitals are occupied” (a change of ordering merely changes the sign and is the same physical state). So we take the job of antisymmetrization away from the wave function and represent the determinant by an abstract state vector $|n_1 n_2 n_3 \cdots\rangle$ that retains only the occupation information. The bookkeeping of signs that accompanies expanding and rearranging determinants is proved just once as the algebraic rules of the operators ($a_\mu^\dagger$, $a_\mu$) that act on the states, namely the anticommutation relations. All later calculations then become purely algebraic calculations (applications of the anticommutation relations), separated from the evaluation of integrals.

Schematically, this approach looks like Figure 6.1. On the left (first quantization), the wave function itself carries an antisymmetric sum of $N!$ terms, whereas on the right (second quantization) the state becomes discrete data, a string of occupation numbers like the “beads of an abacus,” and all the information about antisymmetry has been moved into the rules for the operators.

Math Note: On the name “second quantization”

“Second quantization” is a historical name; it does not mean that “quantization is carried out a second time.” The theory constructed below is a mere rewriting (a change of representation) that is completely equivalent to the Schrödinger equation of the $N$-electron system. The name originates from the fact that, in the early days of quantum field theory (Dirac's theory of the radiation field, 1927), this formalism was introduced from the viewpoint of regarding the wave function $\psi(x)$ as if it were a “classical field” and promoting it once more to an operator. The real advantages of this formalism are (i) that antisymmetry is automatically guaranteed, and (ii) that states with different numbers of particles can be treated on the same footing, so that it extends naturally to processes involving the creation and annihilation of particles (emission and absorption of photons, creation of electron–hole pairs, and so on). In this book we mainly make use of the benefit (i).

6.2 The Occupation-Number Representation and Fock Space

In this section we put the “state” side of second quantization in order. That is, we fix a one-electron basis and establish the convention by which a Slater determinant is expressed as a sequence of occupation numbers $|n_1 n_2 \cdots\rangle$. We emphasize that the convention for the ordering of orbitals plays an essential role in removing the ambiguity of signs.

6.2.1 Fixing a one-electron basis

As a starting point, we choose and fix a complete orthonormal set $\{\varphi_\mu(x)\}_{\mu=1,2,3,\ldots}$ of the one-electron Hilbert space. Orthonormality and completeness read, respectively,

$$ \begin{align} \langle \varphi_\mu | \varphi_\nu \rangle &= \int \dd x\, \varphi_\mu^*(x)\, \varphi_\nu(x) = \delta_{\mu\nu}, \label{eq:6-ortho}\\ \sum_{\mu=1}^{\infty} \varphi_\mu(x)\, \varphi_\mu^*(x') &= \delta(x - x') \label{eq:6-complete} \end{align} $$

Here $x = (\rr,\sigma)$, so $\delta(x-x') = \delta_{\sigma\sigma'}\,\delta^3(\rr-\rr')$. Any basis will do: orthogonalized atomic orbitals, solutions of the HF equations, plane waves (used in Chapter 7), and so on—we choose whichever is convenient for the problem at hand. The theory below holds regardless of the choice of basis. The index $\mu$ is a serial number that labels the spin orbitals (pairs of a spatial orbital and a spin).

6.2.2 Shorthand for Slater determinants and their properties

Since we will be handling determinants in large numbers from now on, let us fix the notation. For a sequence $(m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_N)$ of $N$ orbital indices (specified including their order), we define the ket of a normalized Slater determinant by

$$ \begin{equation} |\varphi_{m_1}\varphi_{m_2}\cdots\varphi_{m_N}\rangle \;\equiv\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\sum_{P} \mathrm{sgn}(P)\, \varphi_{m_{P(1)}}(x_1)\,\varphi_{m_{P(2)}}(x_2)\cdots\varphi_{m_{P(N)}}(x_N) \label{eq:6-slater-ket} \end{equation} $$

The sum runs over all permutations $P$ of $\{1,2,\ldots,N\}$ ($N!$ of them). The facts we need about permutations and the sign $\mathrm{sgn}(P)$ are collected in the Math Note below.

Math Note: Permutations and signs (even and odd permutations)

A permutation is a bijection $P$ of the set $\{1,2,\ldots,N\}$ onto itself; there are $N!$ of them in all. A permutation that interchanges just two elements and leaves the others fixed is called a transposition.

Number of inversions and sign. The number of inversions of a permutation $P$ is the number of pairs $(i,j)$ such that $i \lt j$ but $P(i) \gt P(j)$. The sign of a permutation is defined by $$\mathrm{sgn}(P) = (-1)^{(\text{number of inversions of } P)}$$ A permutation with $\mathrm{sgn}(P) = +1$ is called an even permutation, and one with $-1$ an odd permutation. The identity permutation has $0$ inversions, so it is an even permutation.

An adjacent transposition reverses the sign. If we interchange the contents of adjacent positions $i, i+1$, the order relation changes only for the pair formed by these two elements; the order relation of every other pair is unchanged. Therefore the number of inversions changes by exactly $\pm 1$, and the sign is multiplied by $-1$.

An arbitrary transposition also reverses the sign. The transposition of positions $i \lt j$ can be realized by repeated adjacent transpositions: first move the element at position $i$ to the right $j - i$ times, carrying it to position $j$; then move the element (originally at position $j$ and now at position $j-1$) to the left $j - i - 1$ times, carrying it to position $i$. The total is $2(j-i) - 1$ adjacent transpositions, that is, an odd number of them. Hence the sign is multiplied by $(-1)^{\text{odd}} = -1$; in other words, any transposition reverses the parity.

Multiplicativity. Any permutation can be decomposed into a product of adjacent transpositions (just resolve the inversions one at a time). If $P$ is a product of $p$ adjacent transpositions and $Q$ a product of $q$ of them, then $PQ$ is a product of $p+q$ of them, so $\mathrm{sgn}(PQ) = (-1)^{p+q} = \mathrm{sgn}(P)\,\mathrm{sgn}(Q)$ holds. For the same reason, $\mathrm{sgn}(P^{-1}) = \mathrm{sgn}(P)$.

Relation to determinants. The determinant of an $N \times N$ matrix $A$ is defined by $$\det A = \sum_P \mathrm{sgn}(P) \prod_{i=1}^{N} A_{i, P(i)}$$ If two rows (or columns) are interchanged, the permutation in each term of the sum is shifted by one transposition, so $\det$ is multiplied by $-1$. Therefore a determinant with two equal rows is $0$ (from $D = -D$ we get $D = 0$). Since Eq. \eqref{eq:6-slater-ket} is precisely such a determinant, it inherits the same property.

From the definition \eqref{eq:6-slater-ket} two properties follow immediately, and we will use them many times hereafter.

  • (P1) Interchanging neighbors reverses the sign: $|\cdots \varphi_a \varphi_b \cdots\rangle = -\,|\cdots \varphi_b \varphi_a \cdots\rangle$. This corresponds to interchanging columns of a determinant. In general, rearranging the sequence $(m_1,\ldots,m_N)$ by a permutation $Q$ multiplies the ket by $\mathrm{sgn}(Q)$.
  • (P2) The state vanishes if the same orbital appears twice: if $m_i = m_j$ ($i \neq j$), then $|\varphi_{m_1}\cdots\varphi_{m_N}\rangle = 0$. This corresponds to the fact that a determinant with two equal columns is $0$, and it is an expression of the Pauli exclusion principle (see Chapter 5).

Next, using the orthonormality \eqref{eq:6-ortho}, we compute the inner product of Slater determinants with each other. This is needed to justify the occupation-number representation.

Derivation: Orthonormality of Slater determinants

Let us write out, straight from the definition, the inner product of two determinants with ascending indices, $|\Phi\rangle = |\varphi_{l_1}\cdots\varphi_{l_N}\rangle$ ($l_1 \lt l_2 \lt \cdots \lt l_N$) and $|\Phi'\rangle = |\varphi_{m_1}\cdots\varphi_{m_N}\rangle$ ($m_1 \lt \cdots \lt m_N$):

$$ \begin{align} \langle \Phi' | \Phi \rangle &= \frac{1}{N!}\sum_{P}\sum_{Q} \mathrm{sgn}(P)\,\mathrm{sgn}(Q) \int \dd x_1 \cdots \dd x_N\, \prod_{i=1}^{N} \varphi_{m_{P(i)}}^*(x_i)\,\varphi_{l_{Q(i)}}(x_i) \label{eq:6-ortho-d1}\\ &= \frac{1}{N!}\sum_{P}\sum_{Q} \mathrm{sgn}(P)\,\mathrm{sgn}(Q) \prod_{i=1}^{N} \langle \varphi_{m_{P(i)}} | \varphi_{l_{Q(i)}} \rangle \label{eq:6-ortho-d2}\\ &= \frac{1}{N!}\sum_{P}\sum_{Q} \mathrm{sgn}(P)\,\mathrm{sgn}(Q) \prod_{i=1}^{N} \delta_{m_{P(i)},\, l_{Q(i)}}. \label{eq:6-ortho-d3} \end{align} $$

In going from the first line to the second, we used the fact that the $N$-fold integral separates into a product of one-electron integrals, one for each variable (this is allowed because the integrand is a product of factors, one for each $x_i$). In going from the second line to the third, we used the orthonormality \eqref{eq:6-ortho}.

For Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ortho-d3} not to vanish, $m_{P(i)} = l_{Q(i)}$ is required for every $i$. That is, as sets of orbitals we must have $\{m_1,\ldots,m_N\} = \{l_1,\ldots,l_N\}$. If the sets differ, the inner product is $0$; that is, determinants built from different sets of orbitals are orthogonal.

If the sets are the same, then since both are arranged in ascending order we have $m_i = l_i$, and the condition $l_{P(i)} = l_{Q(i)}$ means $P(i) = Q(i)$ for every $i$, that is, $P = Q$ (because the indices contain no repetitions). Hence

$$ \begin{equation} \langle \Phi | \Phi \rangle = \frac{1}{N!}\sum_{P} \mathrm{sgn}(P)^2 = \frac{N!}{N!} = 1. \label{eq:6-ortho-d4} \end{equation} $$

Here we used $\mathrm{sgn}(P)^2 = 1$ and the fact that the total number of permutations is $N!$. To summarize, the family of Slater determinants arranged in ascending order forms an orthonormal set.

6.2.3 The occupation-number representation

The result of the previous subsection shows the following: once we fix the ordering convention of ascending order, a Slater determinant is uniquely determined by the information of the set of “which orbitals were used,” and the determinants are moreover mutually orthonormal. So let us stop writing determinants as functions and instead represent them by abstract vectors that carry only the occupation information.

Definition: Occupation-number representation

To each orbital of the fixed basis $\{\varphi_\mu\}$ we assign an occupation number $n_\mu \in \{0, 1\}$ ($1$: occupied, $0$: empty). For an $N$-electron state with $\sum_\mu n_\mu = N$, we define the occupation-number state by

$$ \begin{equation} |n_1\, n_2\, n_3 \cdots\rangle \;\longleftrightarrow\; |\varphi_{\mu_1}\varphi_{\mu_2}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_N}\rangle, \qquad \mu_1 \lt \mu_2 \lt \cdots \lt \mu_N \label{eq:6-occ-def} \end{equation} $$

Here $\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_N$ are the orbitals with $n_\mu = 1$ arranged in ascending order. The inner product carries over the result of the previous subsection:

$$ \begin{equation} \langle n_1' n_2' \cdots | n_1 n_2 \cdots \rangle = \prod_{\mu} \delta_{n_\mu' n_\mu} \label{eq:6-occ-ortho} \end{equation} $$

(we define states with different numbers of electrons to be orthogonal).

Math Note: Why the ordering convention is indispensable

A determinant changes sign when its columns are interchanged: $|\varphi_2\varphi_1\rangle = -|\varphi_1\varphi_2\rangle$. Therefore, merely saying “the state in which orbitals 1 and 2 are occupied” determines the state vector only up to a sign. For the occupation-number representation $|1 1 0 0 \cdots\rangle$ to designate a single vector (sign included), the convention “associate the determinant arranged in ascending order” is indispensable. From now on, this convention is the starting point of every sign factor in this chapter. The convention itself is a human choice, and a theory can be built with descending order or anything else as long as it is consistent, but once chosen it must never be mixed.

Example: Occupation-number states for four orbitals and two electrons

Consider a “toy world” in which the basis is truncated to the four orbitals $\{\varphi_1, \varphi_2, \varphi_3, \varphi_4\}$ (since it is finite-dimensional, everything can be checked by hand; we will use this example repeatedly from now on). The two-electron states correspond to choosing two of the four orbitals, $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ ways:

$$ \begin{align} |1100\rangle &= |\varphi_1\varphi_2\rangle, & |1010\rangle &= |\varphi_1\varphi_3\rangle, & |1001\rangle &= |\varphi_1\varphi_4\rangle, \notag\\ |0110\rangle &= |\varphi_2\varphi_3\rangle, & |0101\rangle &= |\varphi_2\varphi_4\rangle, & |0011\rangle &= |\varphi_3\varphi_4\rangle. \notag \end{align} $$

For example, $|1010\rangle$, written as a function, is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\{\varphi_1(x_1)\varphi_3(x_2) - \varphi_3(x_1)\varphi_1(x_2)\}$. The determinant with the ordering $|\varphi_3\varphi_1\rangle$ represents the same physical state, but as a vector $|\varphi_3\varphi_1\rangle = -|1010\rangle$. In the occupation-number representation, the ascending side $|1010\rangle$ is always fixed as the positive representative.

6.2.4 Fock space

One advantage of the occupation-number representation is that states with different numbers of electrons can be treated within the same framework. Let us state this formally.

Definition: Fock space

Denote the state space for $N$ electrons (the space of $N$-electron antisymmetric wave functions) by $\mathcal{H}_N$. The Fock space is the direct sum of the spaces for all numbers of electrons, $$ \begin{equation} \mathcal{F} = \mathcal{H}_0 \oplus \mathcal{H}_1 \oplus \mathcal{H}_2 \oplus \cdots \label{eq:6-fock-def} \end{equation} $$ The set of all occupation-number states $|n_1 n_2 \cdots\rangle$ ($n_\mu \in \{0,1\}$, $\sum_\mu n_\mu \lt \infty$) forms an orthonormal basis of $\mathcal{F}$. In particular, the $N = 0$ space $\mathcal{H}_0$ is the one-dimensional space spanned by the single state with no electrons at all, $$|0\rangle \equiv |0\,0\,0\cdots\rangle$$ (the vacuum state).

Math Note: The vacuum $|0\rangle$ is not the zero vector

Because this is a point on which every beginner becomes confused at least once, we state it explicitly. The vacuum state $|0\rangle$ is a perfectly good physical state meaning “there are zero electrons,” and it is normalized as $\langle 0|0\rangle = 1$. The zero vector $0$, on the other hand, is a formal symbol that expresses “no such state exists (probability amplitude zero).” For example, the right-hand side of $a_\mu|0\rangle = 0$, which will appear later, is the zero vector and not the vacuum.

Example: Dimension of the four-orbital Fock space

In a world of four orbitals, each orbital is either occupied or empty—a choice of two—so the dimension of the Fock space is $$\dim \mathcal{F} = 2^4 = 16 = \underbrace{1}_{N=0} + \underbrace{4}_{N=1} + \underbrace{6}_{N=2} + \underbrace{4}_{N=3} + \underbrace{1}_{N=4}$$ Note that this is the decomposition corresponding to the sum of binomial coefficients $\sum_N \binom{4}{N} = 2^4$. In general, for $M$ orbitals the Fock space has dimension $2^M$. When we dealt only with the $N=2$ sector in first quantization, we were representing the antisymmetric functions in a six-dimensional space as “determinants of functions of two variables.”

Let us illustrate the states graphically (Figure 6.2). Placing beads of occupation on a ladder of orbitals corresponds one-to-one to a bit string $|n_1 n_2 \cdots\rangle$.

With this, the vessel for the states is complete. So far, however, $|n_1 n_2\cdots\rangle$ is merely a string of labels, and the antisymmetry of the determinant (its sign structure) is nowhere to be seen. The operators that carry this information are the creation and annihilation operators, defined in the next section.

6.3 Creation and Annihilation Operators

We now define the main actors of this chapter. The aim is to write the operation of “adding one orbital to a determinant / removing one orbital from it” as an operator. A sign appears when the position of the added orbital is moved to conform to the convention (ascending order), so we build that sign into the definition of the operator. This is the true identity of the sign factor $(-1)^{S_\mu}$, and it is the device that automates all the calculations that follow.

6.3.1 The creation operator: adding one orbital at the front of a determinant

We start from the most straightforward operation. For an $N$-electron Slater determinant $|\varphi_{\mu_1}\varphi_{\mu_2}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_N}\rangle$, consider the operation of writing the orbital $\varphi_\mu$ at the front of the list to make an $(N+1)$-electron determinant:

$$ \begin{equation} a_\mu^\dagger\,|\varphi_{\mu_1}\varphi_{\mu_2}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_N}\rangle \;\equiv\; |\varphi_{\mu}\varphi_{\mu_1}\varphi_{\mu_2}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_N}\rangle . \label{eq:6-cr-raw} \end{equation} $$

Putting it “at the front” is merely a convention, but once we have chosen it we do not change it. This definition has two virtues. First, if $\varphi_\mu$ is already contained in the list, the right-hand side is a determinant in which the same entry appears twice, so it automatically becomes $0$ by property (P2). That is, the Pauli exclusion principle is built into the definition. Second, since the right-hand side is a determinant, antisymmetry is automatically preserved.

However, the right-hand side of the definition \eqref{eq:6-cr-raw} does not satisfy the convention of the occupation-number representation (occupied orbitals are arranged in ascending order). This is because $\varphi_\mu$ is placed at the front although $\mu$ is in general larger than $\mu_1$. We therefore use property (P1) (interchanging two neighbors reverses the sign) to carry $\varphi_\mu$ to its proper position. The orbitals that $\varphi_\mu$ must pass are the occupied orbitals with indices smaller than $\mu$, that is, those among $\varphi_{\mu_1},\ldots$ whose index is less than $\mu$. We write their number as

$$ \begin{equation} S_\mu \;\equiv\; \sum_{\nu \lt \mu} n_\nu \;=\; n_1 + n_2 + \cdots + n_{\mu-1} \label{eq:6-Smu-def} \end{equation} $$

($S_\mu$ is the quantity that counts “how many electrons are packed below orbital $\mu$”). Each passing multiplies the sign by $-1$, so in total the factor $(-1)^{S_\mu}$ appears. Summarizing the above, the action in the occupation-number representation is determined.

Definition: Creation and annihilation operators

For each orbital $\mu$ of a fixed orthonormal basis $\{\varphi_\mu\}$, we define the operators $a_\mu^\dagger$ (the creation operator) and $a_\mu$ (the annihilation operator) on the Fock space $\mathcal{F}$ by their action on occupation-number states as follows.

$$ \begin{align} a_\mu^\dagger\,|n_1 \cdots n_\mu \cdots\rangle &= (-1)^{S_\mu}\,(1 - n_\mu)\,|n_1 \cdots\, n_\mu + 1\, \cdots\rangle, \label{eq:6-cr-def}\\[2pt] a_\mu\,|n_1 \cdots n_\mu \cdots\rangle &= (-1)^{S_\mu}\,n_\mu\,|n_1 \cdots\, n_\mu - 1\, \cdots\rangle, \label{eq:6-an-def}\\[2pt] S_\mu &= \sum_{\nu \lt \mu} n_\nu . \label{eq:6-Smu-repeat} \end{align} $$

Here the parts denoted by $\cdots$ (the occupation numbers other than that of $\mu$) are unchanged. The prefactors $(1-n_\mu)$ and $n_\mu$ are switches that decide whether the destination exists:

  • $a_\mu^\dagger$: if $n_\mu = 0$, then $1-n_\mu = 1$ and the number of electrons increases by one. If $n_\mu = 1$, then $1-n_\mu = 0$ and the result is the zero vector (an orbital that is already filled cannot accept another electron = the Pauli exclusion principle).
  • $a_\mu$: if $n_\mu = 1$, the number of electrons decreases by one. If $n_\mu = 0$, the factor $n_\mu = 0$ in front makes the result the zero vector (an electron that is not there cannot be removed).

In particular, for the vacuum we have $a_\mu|0\rangle = 0$ for all $\mu$ (the right-hand side is the zero vector).

The reason that $S_\mu$ in the definition \eqref{eq:6-cr-def} takes the form of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-Smu-def} is that the number of passings in the operation described above, “carrying the $\varphi_\mu$ placed at the front to its position in ascending order,” is exactly $S_\mu$. Let us confirm this with a concrete example.

Example: The sign factor in a two-electron system (counting the passings one by one)

In the four-orbital world (the example of Section 6.2), take $|1010\rangle = |\varphi_1\varphi_3\rangle$ as the starting point.

(a) Apply $a_2^\dagger$. By the definition \eqref{eq:6-cr-raw}, we write $\varphi_2$ at the front:

$$ a_2^\dagger|\varphi_1\varphi_3\rangle = |\varphi_2\varphi_1\varphi_3\rangle = -\,|\varphi_1\varphi_2\varphi_3\rangle = -\,|1110\rangle . $$

One interchange ($\varphi_2$ passes $\varphi_1$) reversed the sign. On the other hand, in the formula \eqref{eq:6-cr-def}, $S_2 = n_1 = 1$ and $1-n_2 = 1$, so $(-1)^1 = -1$. The two agree.

(b) Apply $a_4^\dagger$.

$$ a_4^\dagger|\varphi_1\varphi_3\rangle = |\varphi_4\varphi_1\varphi_3\rangle = -\,|\varphi_1\varphi_4\varphi_3\rangle = +\,|\varphi_1\varphi_3\varphi_4\rangle = |1011\rangle . $$

There are two passings ($\varphi_1$ and $\varphi_3$), so the sign returns to $+1$. In the formula, $S_4 = n_1+n_2+n_3 = 1+0+1 = 2$ and $(-1)^2 = +1$. They agree.

(c) Apply $a_3^\dagger$. Orbital 3 is already occupied, so $a_3^\dagger|\varphi_1\varphi_3\rangle = |\varphi_3\varphi_1\varphi_3\rangle$ is a determinant containing the same entry twice, and it is $0$. In the formula as well, $1 - n_3 = 1-1 = 0$, giving $0$. We see that not only the sign factor but also the Pauli exclusion principle is automatically built into the definition.

Example: The sign factor in a three-electron system

Increase the number of orbitals to five and apply $a_4^\dagger$ to $|11001\rangle = |\varphi_1\varphi_2\varphi_5\rangle$. As in the definition, write the orbital at the front and carry out the interchanges of neighbors one at a time:

$$ \begin{align} a_4^\dagger|\varphi_1\varphi_2\varphi_5\rangle &= |\varphi_4\,\varphi_1\varphi_2\varphi_5\rangle \label{eq:6-cr-ex3a}\\ &= -\,|\varphi_1\,\varphi_4\,\varphi_2\varphi_5\rangle \label{eq:6-cr-ex3b}\\ &= +\,|\varphi_1\varphi_2\,\varphi_4\,\varphi_5\rangle \;=\; |11011\rangle . \label{eq:6-cr-ex3c} \end{align} $$

The step from \eqref{eq:6-cr-ex3a} to \eqref{eq:6-cr-ex3b} is the interchange of $\varphi_4$ and $\varphi_1$, and the step from \eqref{eq:6-cr-ex3b} to \eqref{eq:6-cr-ex3c} is the interchange of $\varphi_4$ and $\varphi_2$. Since $\varphi_5$ has a larger index than $\varphi_4$, it need not be passed. The number of passings is two, which equals the number of occupied orbitals below $\mu=4$, $S_4 = n_1+n_2+n_3 = 1+1+0 = 2$. This agrees with $(-1)^{S_4} = +1$ from the formula \eqref{eq:6-cr-def}.

For comparison, applying $a_3^\dagger$ gives $S_3 = n_1+n_2 = 2$ and again $+1$; $a_1^\dagger$ gives $S_1 = 0$ (an empty sum) and $+1$; whereas applying $a_3^\dagger$ to $|01001\rangle = |\varphi_2\varphi_5\rangle$ gives $S_3 = n_1+n_2 = 0+1 = 1$ and $-1$. The sign is determined solely by “how many electrons are packed below the orbital being acted on.”

Math Note: The relation between $S_\mu$ and “which occupied orbital it is”

We prepare a rephrasing that will be used repeatedly in the following sections. Let $\mu_1 \lt \mu_2 \lt \cdots \lt \mu_N$ be the occupied orbitals of the occupation-number state $|n_1n_2\cdots\rangle$ arranged in ascending order. If orbital $\mu$ is occupied and is the $p$th one ($\mu = \mu_p$), then

$$ \begin{equation} S_{\mu_p} = \sum_{\nu \lt \mu_p} n_\nu = p - 1 \label{eq:6-Smu-position} \end{equation} $$

holds. Indeed, the occupied orbitals with indices smaller than $\mu_p$ are exactly the $p-1$ orbitals $\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_{p-1}$. In other words, the sign factor is determined solely by “which column from the left $\varphi_\mu$ occupies in the corresponding determinant”: $(-1)^{S_{\mu_p}} = (-1)^{p-1}$. This fact will be the key when we compare with the first-quantization calculation in Sections 6.5 and 6.6.

6.3.2 Constructing Slater determinants from the vacuum

By acting with creation operators on the vacuum $|0\rangle$ one after another, we can build any Slater determinant. This is the aspect of second quantization as a “construction kit.”

Theorem: Determinants from creation operators

For any sequence of (mutually distinct) orbital indices $\mu_1, \mu_2, \ldots, \mu_N$,

$$ \begin{equation} a_{\mu_1}^\dagger a_{\mu_2}^\dagger \cdots a_{\mu_N}^\dagger\,|0\rangle = |\varphi_{\mu_1}\varphi_{\mu_2}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_N}\rangle \label{eq:6-det-from-vac} \end{equation} $$

holds. In particular, when $\mu_1 \lt \mu_2 \lt \cdots \lt \mu_N$ (ascending order), the right-hand side is itself an occupation-number state.

Derivation: Proof of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-det-from-vac}

Starting from the rightmost $a_{\mu_N}^\dagger$ and working leftward, we simply apply the definition \eqref{eq:6-cr-raw} (“attach an orbital at the front of the determinant”) repeatedly. Written as an induction on $N$, it goes as follows.

The case $N=1$. The vacuum $|0\rangle$ is “the determinant made of zero orbitals,” so attaching $\varphi_{\mu_1}$ at the front gives the one-electron determinant $|\varphi_{\mu_1}\rangle = \varphi_{\mu_1}(x_1)$. That is, $a_{\mu_1}^\dagger|0\rangle = |\varphi_{\mu_1}\rangle$. Checking in the occupation-number representation: in $|0\rangle$ all $n_\nu = 0$, so $S_{\mu_1} = 0$ and $1-n_{\mu_1} = 1$, and the sign is $+1$, so the two indeed agree.

Assume the statement for $N-1$ and prove it for $N$. By the assumption, $a_{\mu_2}^\dagger\cdots a_{\mu_N}^\dagger|0\rangle = |\varphi_{\mu_2}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_N}\rangle$. Acting on this from the left with $a_{\mu_1}^\dagger$ attaches $\varphi_{\mu_1}$ at the front by the definition \eqref{eq:6-cr-raw}, giving $|\varphi_{\mu_1}\varphi_{\mu_2}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_N}\rangle$. This is the equation to be proved.

The ascending-order case. If $\mu_1 \lt \cdots \lt \mu_N$, the right-hand side is a determinant with its orbitals in ascending order, and by the definition \eqref{eq:6-occ-def} it equals the corresponding occupation-number state. For example, in a world of four orbitals, $$a_1^\dagger a_2^\dagger |0\rangle = |\varphi_1\varphi_2\rangle = |1100\rangle$$ and the formula \eqref{eq:6-cr-def} gives the same: $a_2^\dagger|0000\rangle = (-1)^{0}|0100\rangle$, followed by $a_1^\dagger|0100\rangle = (-1)^{0}|1100\rangle$ (an empty sum, so $S_1 = 0$).

Note that reversing the order changes the sign: for $a_2^\dagger a_1^\dagger|0\rangle$, we have $a_1^\dagger|0\rangle = |1000\rangle$, and then $a_2^\dagger|1000\rangle$ equals $-|1100\rangle$ because $S_2 = n_1 = 1$. This corresponds to $|\varphi_2\varphi_1\rangle = -|\varphi_1\varphi_2\rangle$, and already shows the seed of the anticommutation relation $a_1^\dagger a_2^\dagger = -a_2^\dagger a_1^\dagger$ of the next section.

Physical Meaning: What it means to “create” a state

Eq. \eqref{eq:6-det-from-vac} says that every many-electron Slater determinant can be built by the operation of “throwing electrons into the vacuum one at a time.” In first quantization we had to write an antisymmetrized sum of $N!$ terms every time we wrote down an $N$-electron wave function, but in second quantization a string of symbols of length $N$, $a_{\mu_1}^\dagger\cdots a_{\mu_N}^\dagger|0\rangle$, suffices. Moreover, the sign produced when the order of the string is rearranged is taken care of automatically by the algebra of the operators (next section). Multi-determinant wave functions $\Psi = \sum_I C_I |\Phi_I\rangle$, such as the configuration interaction (CI) introduced in Chapter 1, can also be handled mechanically thanks to this notation.

6.3.3 The annihilation operator is the Hermitian conjugate of the creation operator

In the definition \eqref{eq:6-an-def} we gave $a_\mu$ without justification, but since the symbol $a_\mu^\dagger$ means “the Hermitian conjugate of $a_\mu$,” this is something that must be proved. Let us verify it.

Derivation: Verification of $(a_\mu)^\dagger = a_\mu^\dagger$

The definition of the Hermitian conjugate is that, for any states $|\alpha\rangle, |\beta\rangle$, $\langle \beta | a_\mu^\dagger | \alpha\rangle = \overline{\langle \alpha | a_\mu | \beta\rangle}$ holds (the overline denotes the complex conjugate). If we verify this for the basis (the occupation-number states), it follows for arbitrary states by linearity.

Take the occupation-number states $|\bm{n}\rangle = |n_1n_2\cdots\rangle$ and $|\bm{n}'\rangle = |n_1'n_2'\cdots\rangle$. To simplify the notation, write $\bm{n}^{+\mu}$ for the sequence of occupation numbers obtained by replacing only the $\mu$th component of $\bm{n}$ with $1$. From the definition \eqref{eq:6-cr-def} and the inner product \eqref{eq:6-occ-ortho} we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \langle \bm{n}' | a_\mu^\dagger | \bm{n}\rangle = (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})}\,(1-n_\mu)\,\delta_{\bm{n}',\,\bm{n}^{+\mu}} \label{eq:6-adj-1} \end{equation} $$

Here $S_\mu(\bm{n})$ is \eqref{eq:6-Smu-def} evaluated in the state $\bm{n}$, and $\delta_{\bm{n}',\bm{n}^{+\mu}}$ equals $1$ when the two sequences of occupation numbers agree in every component and $0$ otherwise (it is a compact way of writing the $\prod_\nu \delta_{n'_\nu n_\nu}$ of the inner product \eqref{eq:6-occ-ortho}). This expression can differ from $0$ only in the case $n_\mu = 0$ and $\bm{n}' = \bm{n}^{+\mu}$. Its value is then $(-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})}$.

Next we compute $\langle \bm{n} | a_\mu | \bm{n}'\rangle$ in the same situation ($n_\mu = 0$, $n'_\mu = 1$, all other components common). From the definition \eqref{eq:6-an-def},

$$ \begin{equation} a_\mu|\bm{n}'\rangle = (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n}')}\,n'_\mu\,|\cdots n'_\mu - 1 \cdots\rangle = (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n}')}\,|\bm{n}\rangle . \label{eq:6-adj-2} \end{equation} $$

Here we used $n'_\mu = 1$, and the fact that the state obtained by returning the $\mu$th component of $\bm{n}'$ from $1$ to $0$ is $\bm{n}$. Therefore $\langle \bm{n}|a_\mu|\bm{n}'\rangle = (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n}')}$.

Finally we show $S_\mu(\bm{n}) = S_\mu(\bm{n}')$. $S_\mu$ is the sum of the occupation numbers with $\nu \lt \mu$, and $\bm{n}$ and $\bm{n}'$ differ only in the $\mu$th component. Since the $\mu$th component is not included in the sum $\sum_{\nu\lt\mu}$, the two $S_\mu$ are equal. Hence

$$ \begin{equation} \langle \bm{n}' | a_\mu^\dagger | \bm{n}\rangle = (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})} = (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n}')} = \langle \bm{n} | a_\mu | \bm{n}'\rangle = \overline{\langle \bm{n} | a_\mu | \bm{n}'\rangle} \label{eq:6-adj-3} \end{equation} $$

holds (the sign $\pm1$ is a real number, so taking the complex conjugate does not change it). The situation in which both differ from $0$ at the same time occurs only in the case above; otherwise both are $0$. We have thus confirmed that $(a_\mu)^\dagger = a_\mu^\dagger$.

For the annihilation operator as well, it is easier to understand if we state in words its action on a determinant. Eq. \eqref{eq:6-an-def} is equivalent to the following:

Theorem: Action of the annihilation operator on a determinant

If $\varphi_\mu$ is not contained among the columns of the determinant, then $a_\mu|\Phi\rangle = 0$. If it is contained, first use (P1) to carry $\varphi_\mu$ to the front (attaching the sign that arises), and then remove the first column:

$$ \begin{equation} a_\mu\,|\varphi_{\mu}\varphi_{\mu_1}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_{N-1}}\rangle = |\varphi_{\mu_1}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_{N-1}}\rangle . \label{eq:6-an-raw} \end{equation} $$

Derivation: Equivalence of \eqref{eq:6-an-raw} and \eqref{eq:6-an-def}

Consider a determinant $|\varphi_{\mu_1}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_N}\rangle$ with its orbitals in ascending order ($\mu_1\lt\cdots\lt\mu_N$), and suppose that $\mu = \mu_p$ is in the $p$th position. To carry $\varphi_{\mu_p}$ to the front, it must pass the orbitals to its left, $\varphi_{\mu_{p-1}}, \varphi_{\mu_{p-2}}, \ldots, \varphi_{\mu_1}$, in turn, and the number of such passings is $p-1$. Hence

$$ \begin{align} |\varphi_{\mu_1}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_p}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_N}\rangle &= (-1)^{p-1}\,|\varphi_{\mu_p}\,\varphi_{\mu_1}\cdots\widehat{\varphi_{\mu_p}}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_N}\rangle \label{eq:6-an-eq1} \end{align} $$

(the hat $\widehat{\ }$ indicates that that column is omitted). Applying the rule \eqref{eq:6-an-raw} to this removes the leading $\varphi_{\mu_p}$, and we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} a_{\mu_p}|\varphi_{\mu_1}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_N}\rangle = (-1)^{p-1}\,|\varphi_{\mu_1}\cdots\widehat{\varphi_{\mu_p}}\cdots\varphi_{\mu_N}\rangle \label{eq:6-an-eq2} \end{equation} $$

The determinant on the right-hand side is again in ascending order, so it is directly the occupation-number state (with the $\mu_p$th component set to $0$). And by \eqref{eq:6-Smu-position} of the Math Note, $(-1)^{p-1} = (-1)^{S_{\mu_p}}$, so this is nothing other than the definition \eqref{eq:6-an-def}. The argument in the opposite direction just traces the same equations backward.

Example: Action of the annihilation operator

In a world of four orbitals, let us apply annihilation operators to $|1110\rangle = |\varphi_1\varphi_2\varphi_3\rangle$.

  • $a_1|1110\rangle$: $\varphi_1$ is already at the front, so no passing is needed ($S_1 = 0$). Removing it gives $|\varphi_2\varphi_3\rangle = |0110\rangle$.
  • $a_2|1110\rangle$: swapping once, $|\varphi_1\varphi_2\varphi_3\rangle = -|\varphi_2\varphi_1\varphi_3\rangle$, and then removing, we get $-|\varphi_1\varphi_3\rangle = -|1010\rangle$. In the formula, $S_2 = n_1 = 1$, which indeed gives $-1$.
  • $a_3|1110\rangle$: swapping twice gives $+|\varphi_1\varphi_2\rangle = |1100\rangle$. In the formula, $S_3 = n_1+n_2 = 2$.
  • $a_4|1110\rangle = 0$: because orbital 4 is empty ($n_4 = 0$).

6.3.4 The number operator

The most basic combination built from the creation and annihilation operators is the operator that measures the occupation number of orbital $\mu$.

Definition: Number operator

We call $\hat{n}_\mu \equiv a_\mu^\dagger a_\mu$ the number operator of orbital $\mu$, and $\hat{N} \equiv \sum_\mu \hat{n}_\mu$ the total particle-number operator.

Derivation: $\hat{n}_\mu |n_1 n_2\cdots\rangle = n_\mu |n_1n_2\cdots\rangle$

The occupation number $n_\mu$ takes only the values $0$ or $1$, so it suffices to compute in two cases.

(i) The case $n_\mu = 0$. First apply the annihilation operator. Since the prefactor $n_\mu$ in the definition \eqref{eq:6-an-def} is $0$,

$$ \begin{equation} a_\mu|\cdots 0_\mu \cdots\rangle = (-1)^{S_\mu}\cdot 0 \cdot |\cdots\rangle = 0 . \label{eq:6-num-1} \end{equation} $$

Therefore $\hat{n}_\mu|\cdots 0_\mu\cdots\rangle = a_\mu^\dagger\,0 = 0$, which agrees with the result of multiplying by $n_\mu = 0$.

(ii) The case $n_\mu = 1$. First the annihilation operator:

$$ \begin{equation} a_\mu|\cdots 1_\mu \cdots\rangle = (-1)^{S_\mu}\cdot 1 \cdot |\cdots 0_\mu \cdots\rangle . \label{eq:6-num-2} \end{equation} $$

Next we apply the creation operator to this result. The crucial point here is that the sign factor in the state $|\cdots 0_\mu\cdots\rangle$ on the right-hand side is the same $S_\mu$. The reason is the same as in the previous subsection: $S_\mu = \sum_{\nu\lt\mu} n_\nu$ is a sum that does not include the $\mu$th component, so its value does not change when the $\mu$th component is changed from $1$ to $0$. Hence

$$ \begin{align} a_\mu^\dagger\Big[(-1)^{S_\mu}|\cdots 0_\mu\cdots\rangle\Big] &= (-1)^{S_\mu}\cdot(-1)^{S_\mu}\,(1 - 0)\,|\cdots 1_\mu\cdots\rangle \label{eq:6-num-3}\\ &= (-1)^{2S_\mu}\,|\cdots 1_\mu\cdots\rangle \label{eq:6-num-4}\\ &= |\cdots 1_\mu\cdots\rangle . \label{eq:6-num-5} \end{align} $$

In \eqref{eq:6-num-3} we used the definition \eqref{eq:6-cr-def}, whose prefactor is $1 - n_\mu = 1 - 0 = 1$. From \eqref{eq:6-num-4} to \eqref{eq:6-num-5} we used $(-1)^{2S_\mu} = \left[(-1)^2\right]^{S_\mu} = 1$ (because $S_\mu$ is an integer). The result is the original state itself, that is, the result of multiplying by $n_\mu = 1$.

Combining (i) and (ii), $\hat{n}_\mu|\bm{n}\rangle = n_\mu|\bm{n}\rangle$ holds for any occupation-number state.

This result can be summarized as follows.

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{n}_\mu\,|n_1 n_2 \cdots\rangle = n_\mu\,|n_1 n_2 \cdots\rangle, \qquad \hat{N}\,|n_1n_2\cdots\rangle = \Big(\sum_\mu n_\mu\Big)|n_1n_2\cdots\rangle = N\,|n_1n_2\cdots\rangle . \label{eq:6-number-op} \end{equation} $$

The equation for $\hat{N}$ is just the equation for $\hat{n}_\mu$ summed over $\mu$.

Physical Meaning: Occupation-number states are simultaneous eigenstates of the number operators

Eq. \eqref{eq:6-number-op} says that an occupation-number state is a simultaneous eigenstate of all the $\hat{n}_\mu$, and that the eigenvalue is precisely the occupation number $n_\mu \in \{0,1\}$. That only the eigenvalues $0$ and $1$ are allowed is itself a characteristic of fermion systems (for bosons, $n = 0,1,2,\ldots$ are allowed). Furthermore, the fact that the eigenvalue of $\hat{N}$ is the number of electrons $N$ means that Fock space decomposes into sectors with different particle numbers, and $\hat{N}$ serves as the label that identifies the sector. When we treat systems in which the number of electrons changes in later chapters (such as quantum transport in Chapter 17), this structure plays an essential role.

While we are at it, let us also compute $a_\mu a_\mu^\dagger$, which we will use later, by the same procedure. If $n_\mu = 1$, the prefactor $1-n_\mu$ of $a_\mu^\dagger$ is $0$, so the result is $0$. If $n_\mu = 0$, then $a_\mu^\dagger|\cdots 0_\mu\cdots\rangle = (-1)^{S_\mu}|\cdots 1_\mu\cdots\rangle$, and acting on this with $a_\mu$ (again $S_\mu$ is unchanged) gives $(-1)^{2S_\mu}|\cdots 0_\mu\cdots\rangle$, that is, the original state is recovered. In summary,

$$ \begin{equation} a_\mu a_\mu^\dagger\,|\bm{n}\rangle = (1 - n_\mu)\,|\bm{n}\rangle . \label{eq:6-hole-op} \end{equation} $$

Whereas $\hat{n}_\mu$ measures “whether an electron is present,” $a_\mu a_\mu^\dagger = 1 - \hat{n}_\mu$ is the operator that measures “whether the seat is vacant (whether a hole is present).” The anticommutation relation $\{a_\mu, a_\mu^\dagger\} = 1$ of the next section is nothing other than a restatement of the obvious fact that these two always add up to $1$.

6.4 Derivation of the Anticommutation Relations

In the preceding sections we assembled the states (occupation-number states) and the operators ($a_\mu^\dagger$, $a_\mu$). In this section we completely determine the algebraic rules that these operators obey. The anticommutation relations obtained here are the heart of second quantization, and every later calculation reduces to repeated application of these three equations. The proof consists only of “acting on an occupation-number state and counting signs,” but we exhaust all the cases so that not a single sign is overlooked.

Math Note: Commutators and anticommutators

For two operators $A, B$ we define

$$ [A, B] \equiv AB - BA, \qquad \{A, B\} \equiv AB + BA $$

The former is called the commutator and the latter the anticommutator. Like the commutator, the anticommutator is bilinear. That is, for arbitrary complex numbers $c_1, c_2$,

$$\{c_1A_1 + c_2A_2,\; B\} = c_1\{A_1,B\} + c_2\{A_2,B\}$$

holds (this is verified simply by substituting into the definition and expanding). It is also obviously symmetric: $\{A,B\} = \{B,A\}$. Thanks to this symmetry, in the proofs below it is sometimes enough to show the “case $\mu \lt \nu$”; the “case $\mu \gt \nu$” then follows just by renaming the indices (however, when the two operators are of different kinds, as in $\{a_\mu, a_\nu^\dagger\}$, we must check $\mu\lt\nu$ and $\mu\gt\nu$ separately. In this section we calculate both).

Note that the canonical commutation relation $[\hat{x},\hat{p}] = i\hbar$, which one first meets in quantum mechanics, is a commutator, and the creation and annihilation operators of bosons also satisfy a commutation relation, $[b_\mu, b_\nu^\dagger] = \delta_{\mu\nu}$. That only fermions obey anticommutators is a direct consequence of having required the antisymmetry of the states (the determinant).

6.4.1 Two creation operators: $\{a_\mu^\dagger, a_\nu^\dagger\} = 0$

We begin with operators of the same kind. This case is the easiest to survey.

Derivation: $\{a_\mu^\dagger, a_\nu^\dagger\} = 0$

(i) The case $\mu = \nu$. By the definition of the anticommutator, $\{a_\mu^\dagger, a_\mu^\dagger\} = 2\,(a_\mu^\dagger)^2$, so it suffices to show $(a_\mu^\dagger)^2 = 0$. We act on an arbitrary occupation-number state $|\bm{n}\rangle$ and divide into cases according to $n_\mu$.

  • When $n_\mu = 1$: the prefactor in the definition \eqref{eq:6-cr-def} is $1 - n_\mu = 0$, so already at the stage of the first $a_\mu^\dagger$ we have $a_\mu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle = 0$. Hence $(a_\mu^\dagger)^2|\bm{n}\rangle = 0$.
  • When $n_\mu = 0$: we have $a_\mu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle = (-1)^{S_\mu}|\bm{n}^{+\mu}\rangle$. In $\bm{n}^{+\mu}$, however, the $\mu$th component equals $1$, so the prefactor of the second $a_\mu^\dagger$ is $1 - 1 = 0$. Therefore $(a_\mu^\dagger)^2|\bm{n}\rangle = 0$.

In both cases the result is $0$, and since the occupation-number states form a basis of Fock space, $(a_\mu^\dagger)^2 = 0$ holds as an operator identity.

(ii) The case $\mu \neq \nu$. By the symmetry of the anticommutator, $\{A,B\} = \{B,A\}$, we may assume $\mu \lt \nu$ without loss of generality. For an arbitrary $|\bm{n}\rangle$ we compare $a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle$ and $a_\nu^\dagger a_\mu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle$.

First, if $n_\mu = 1$ or $n_\nu = 1$, both equal $0$. Indeed, if $n_\nu = 1$, then in either order of action the $\nu$th component is not changed by the other operator ($a_\mu^\dagger$, with $\mu\neq\nu$), so the prefactor $1-n_\nu$ becomes $0$ at the stage where $a_\nu^\dagger$ acts. If $n_\mu = 1$, then for the same reason the result is $0$ at the stage of $a_\mu^\dagger$. Hence both equal $0$, and their sum, the anticommutator, is also $0$.

We therefore let $n_\mu = n_\nu = 0$. We write $\bm{n}^{+\mu+\nu}$ for the sequence obtained from $\bm{n}$ by setting both the $\mu$th and the $\nu$th components to $1$.

Order 1 ($a_\nu^\dagger$ first):

$$ \begin{align} a_\nu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle &= (-1)^{S_\nu(\bm{n})}\,|\bm{n}^{+\nu}\rangle, \label{eq:6-acc-1}\\ a_\mu^\dagger\,a_\nu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle &= (-1)^{S_\nu(\bm{n})}\,(-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n}^{+\nu})}\,|\bm{n}^{+\mu+\nu}\rangle . \label{eq:6-acc-2} \end{align} $$

Here we evaluate $S_\mu(\bm{n}^{+\nu}) = \sum_{\lambda \lt \mu} n^{+\nu}_\lambda$. The sequence $\bm{n}^{+\nu}$ is $\bm{n}$ with only its $\nu$th component increased, and since $\nu \gt \mu$, the index $\nu$ is not included in the range $\lambda \lt \mu$ of the sum. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} S_\mu(\bm{n}^{+\nu}) = S_\mu(\bm{n}) . \label{eq:6-acc-3} \end{equation} $$

Hence $a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle = (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n}) + S_\nu(\bm{n})}\,|\bm{n}^{+\mu+\nu}\rangle$.

Order 2 ($a_\mu^\dagger$ first):

$$ \begin{align} a_\mu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle &= (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})}\,|\bm{n}^{+\mu}\rangle, \label{eq:6-acc-4}\\ a_\nu^\dagger\,a_\mu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle &= (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})}\,(-1)^{S_\nu(\bm{n}^{+\mu})}\,|\bm{n}^{+\mu+\nu}\rangle . \label{eq:6-acc-5} \end{align} $$

This time we have $S_\nu(\bm{n}^{+\mu}) = \sum_{\lambda \lt \nu} n^{+\mu}_\lambda$. Since $\mu \lt \nu$, the index $\mu$ is included in the range of the sum, and the $\mu$th component has increased from $0$ to $1$. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} S_\nu(\bm{n}^{+\mu}) = S_\nu(\bm{n}) + 1 . \label{eq:6-acc-6} \end{equation} $$

Substituting this, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} a_\nu^\dagger a_\mu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle = (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})+S_\nu(\bm{n})+1}\,|\bm{n}^{+\mu+\nu}\rangle = -\,a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle . \label{eq:6-acc-7} \end{equation} $$

Adding the two gives $0$. From (i) and (ii) above, $\{a_\mu^\dagger, a_\nu^\dagger\} = 0$ has been shown for all $\mu,\nu$.

Let us confirm once more where the sign difference comes from. For $\mu \lt \nu$, if we first put an electron into $\mu$, the electron put into $\nu$ afterward has one more electron “to pass over.” In the opposite order, the electron put into $\nu$ first does not affect the count of electrons to be passed for the electron put into $\mu$ (because $\nu$ lies above $\mu$). This asymmetry produces exactly the factor $-1$. This is the situation shown in Figure 6.4.

Physical Meaning: $(a_\mu^\dagger)^2 = 0$ is the Pauli exclusion principle

The relation $(a_\mu^\dagger)^2 = 0$ obtained in Derivation (i) states that “if we try to put a second electron into the same spin orbital, the state vanishes (no such state exists).” In Chapter 5, the Pauli exclusion principle appeared as a property of determinants: “a determinant with two identical columns is $0$.” In second quantization it appears as an algebraic property of the operators. Note that nowhere in the proof did we introduce an additional requirement that “electrons cannot occupy the same state.” Everything came out automatically from the starting point of “representing the state by a determinant (antisymmetrizing it).”
The same thing can also be stated by calling the $\mu = \nu$ component of the anticommutation relation, $\{a_\mu^\dagger, a_\mu^\dagger\} = 2(a_\mu^\dagger)^2 = 0$, the “operator expression of the exclusion principle.” For bosons, the corresponding relation degenerates into the trivial identity $[b_\mu^\dagger, b_\mu^\dagger] = 0$, and since $(b_\mu^\dagger)^2 \neq 0$, any number of particles can be packed into the same state (Bose–Einstein condensation).

6.4.2 Two annihilation operators: $\{a_\mu, a_\nu\} = 0$

This follows immediately by taking the Hermitian conjugate of the preceding subsection, but counting the signs by hand is worthwhile, so we present both routes.

Derivation 1: Using Hermitian conjugation

In general $(AB)^\dagger = B^\dagger A^\dagger$, so $\{A,B\}^\dagger = (AB+BA)^\dagger = B^\dagger A^\dagger + A^\dagger B^\dagger = \{A^\dagger, B^\dagger\}$ holds. Applying this to $A = a_\mu^\dagger$, $B = a_\nu^\dagger$, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} 0 = \{a_\mu^\dagger, a_\nu^\dagger\}^\dagger = \{a_\mu, a_\nu\} \label{eq:6-aa-dagger} \end{equation} $$

(the Hermitian conjugate of the zero operator is the zero operator). Here we used $(a_\mu)^\dagger = a_\mu^\dagger$, shown in the preceding subsection, and hence $(a_\mu^\dagger)^\dagger = a_\mu$.

Derivation 2: Direct calculation on occupation-number states

(i) The case $\mu = \nu$. Since $\{a_\mu,a_\mu\} = 2a_\mu^2$, we show $a_\mu^2 = 0$.

  • When $n_\mu = 0$: the prefactor $n_\mu$ in the definition \eqref{eq:6-an-def} is $0$, so $a_\mu|\bm{n}\rangle = 0$, and hence $a_\mu^2|\bm{n}\rangle = 0$.
  • When $n_\mu = 1$: $a_\mu|\bm{n}\rangle = (-1)^{S_\mu}|\bm{n}^{-\mu}\rangle$ (the sequence with the $\mu$th component set to $0$). In this state the $\mu$th component is $0$, so the prefactor of the second $a_\mu$ is $0$ and the result is $0$.

(ii) The case $\mu \lt \nu$. For both products to be different from $0$, $n_\mu = n_\nu = 1$ is necessary (if $n_\mu = 0$, the result vanishes at the stage of $a_\mu$ in either order, and likewise if $n_\nu = 0$). We therefore let $n_\mu = n_\nu = 1$ and write $\bm{n}^{-\mu-\nu}$ for the sequence with both components set to $0$.

$$ \begin{align} a_\mu\,a_\nu|\bm{n}\rangle &= a_\mu\Big[(-1)^{S_\nu(\bm{n})}|\bm{n}^{-\nu}\rangle\Big] = (-1)^{S_\nu(\bm{n})}(-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n}^{-\nu})}\,|\bm{n}^{-\mu-\nu}\rangle, \label{eq:6-aa-1}\\ a_\nu\,a_\mu|\bm{n}\rangle &= a_\nu\Big[(-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})}|\bm{n}^{-\mu}\rangle\Big] = (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})}(-1)^{S_\nu(\bm{n}^{-\mu})}\,|\bm{n}^{-\mu-\nu}\rangle . \label{eq:6-aa-2} \end{align} $$

We evaluate the sign factors. In $S_\mu(\bm{n}^{-\nu}) = \sum_{\lambda\lt\mu} n^{-\nu}_\lambda$, the changed component $\nu$ is larger than $\mu$ and so is not in the range of the sum; hence $S_\mu(\bm{n}^{-\nu}) = S_\mu(\bm{n})$. On the other hand, in $S_\nu(\bm{n}^{-\mu}) = \sum_{\lambda\lt\nu}n^{-\mu}_\lambda$, since $\mu \lt \nu$ the index lies in the range, and the sum is smaller by exactly the amount by which the $\mu$th component has decreased from $1$ to $0$: $S_\nu(\bm{n}^{-\mu}) = S_\nu(\bm{n}) - 1$. Therefore

$$ \begin{align} a_\mu a_\nu|\bm{n}\rangle &= (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})+S_\nu(\bm{n})}\,|\bm{n}^{-\mu-\nu}\rangle, \label{eq:6-aa-3}\\ a_\nu a_\mu|\bm{n}\rangle &= (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})+S_\nu(\bm{n})-1}\,|\bm{n}^{-\mu-\nu}\rangle = -\,a_\mu a_\nu|\bm{n}\rangle \label{eq:6-aa-4} \end{align} $$

and the sum is $0$. Here we used $(-1)^{-1} = -1$.

(iii) The case $\mu \gt \nu$. By the symmetry of the anticommutator, $\{a_\mu,a_\nu\} = \{a_\nu,a_\mu\}$, exchanging the names of the indices reduces this to (ii).

6.4.3 Mixed type: $\{a_\mu, a_\nu^\dagger\} = \delta_{\mu\nu}$

Finally, we turn to the mixed case of creation and annihilation. Only here is the right-hand side not $0$. The $1$ arises because this expression exhausts the two possibilities, “an electron is present” and “the orbital is empty.”

Derivation: The case $\mu = \nu$

We use the results of the previous section, Eqs. \eqref{eq:6-number-op} and \eqref{eq:6-hole-op}, as they stand. For an arbitrary occupation-number state $|\bm{n}\rangle$,

$$ \begin{align} \{a_\mu, a_\mu^\dagger\}\,|\bm{n}\rangle &= a_\mu a_\mu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle + a_\mu^\dagger a_\mu |\bm{n}\rangle \label{eq:6-ada-1}\\ &= (1 - n_\mu)\,|\bm{n}\rangle + n_\mu\,|\bm{n}\rangle \label{eq:6-ada-2}\\ &= |\bm{n}\rangle . \label{eq:6-ada-3} \end{align} $$

From Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ada-1} to Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ada-2}, we used Eq. \eqref{eq:6-hole-op} for the first term and Eq. \eqref{eq:6-number-op} for the second term. From Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ada-2} to Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ada-3}, we used the trivial identity $(1-n_\mu)+n_\mu = 1$. Since this acts like the identity operator on every element of the basis, we have $\{a_\mu,a_\mu^\dagger\} = 1$ as an operator.

To be safe, let us recall the content of Eqs. \eqref{eq:6-number-op} and \eqref{eq:6-hole-op}: when $n_\mu = 1$, the term in which $a_\mu^\dagger$ comes first (prefactor $1-n_\mu=0$) vanishes and only the $a_\mu^\dagger a_\mu$ term gives $1$; when $n_\mu = 0$, conversely, only the $a_\mu a_\mu^\dagger$ term gives $1$. Exactly one of the two always survives, so the sum is always $1$.

Derivation: The case $\mu \neq \nu$

Because creation and annihilation play asymmetric roles, we check $\mu \lt \nu$ and $\mu \gt \nu$ separately. In either case, for $a_\mu a_\nu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle$ and $a_\nu^\dagger a_\mu|\bm{n}\rangle$ to both be different from $0$, we need $n_\mu = 1$ and $n_\nu = 0$. The reason: for the annihilation operator $a_\mu$ to act, orbital $\mu$ must be occupied, and for the creation operator $a_\nu^\dagger$ to act, orbital $\nu$ must be empty. Moreover, since $\mu\neq\nu$, neither operator changes the occupation number the other one cares about. Hence the condition is the same regardless of the order. Below, we take $n_\mu = 1$ and $n_\nu = 0$, and write $\bm{n}^{-\mu+\nu}$ for the sequence in which the $\mu$th component is set to $0$ and the $\nu$th component is set to $1$.

(a) The case $\mu \lt \nu$.

$$ \begin{align} a_\mu\,a_\nu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle &= a_\mu\Big[(-1)^{S_\nu(\bm{n})}|\bm{n}^{+\nu}\rangle\Big] = (-1)^{S_\nu(\bm{n})}\,(-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n}^{+\nu})}\,|\bm{n}^{-\mu+\nu}\rangle, \label{eq:6-adn-1}\\ a_\nu^\dagger\,a_\mu|\bm{n}\rangle &= a_\nu^\dagger\Big[(-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})}|\bm{n}^{-\mu}\rangle\Big] = (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})}\,(-1)^{S_\nu(\bm{n}^{-\mu})}\,|\bm{n}^{-\mu+\nu}\rangle . \label{eq:6-adn-2} \end{align} $$

We evaluate the sign factors. In $S_\mu(\bm{n}^{+\nu})$, the changed component $\nu$ is larger than $\mu$ and thus lies outside the range of the sum, so $S_\mu(\bm{n}^{+\nu}) = S_\mu(\bm{n})$. In $S_\nu(\bm{n}^{-\mu})$, since $\mu \lt \nu$ the changed component lies within the range, and the $\mu$th component decreases as $1\to0$, so $S_\nu(\bm{n}^{-\mu}) = S_\nu(\bm{n}) - 1$. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} a_\nu^\dagger a_\mu|\bm{n}\rangle = (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})+S_\nu(\bm{n})-1}|\bm{n}^{-\mu+\nu}\rangle = -\,a_\mu a_\nu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle \label{eq:6-adn-3} \end{equation} $$

and the sum is $0$.

(b) The case $\mu \gt \nu$. We start from the same two equations, Eqs. \eqref{eq:6-adn-1} and \eqref{eq:6-adn-2}, but the evaluations of the sign factors are interchanged. In $S_\mu(\bm{n}^{+\nu})$, since now $\nu \lt \mu$, the changed component lies within the range of the sum, and the $\nu$th component increases as $0\to1$, so $S_\mu(\bm{n}^{+\nu}) = S_\mu(\bm{n}) + 1$. On the other hand, in $S_\nu(\bm{n}^{-\mu})$, since $\mu \gt \nu$ the changed component lies outside the range, and $S_\nu(\bm{n}^{-\mu}) = S_\nu(\bm{n})$. Therefore

$$ \begin{align} a_\mu a_\nu^\dagger|\bm{n}\rangle &= (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})+S_\nu(\bm{n})+1}\,|\bm{n}^{-\mu+\nu}\rangle, \label{eq:6-adn-4}\\ a_\nu^\dagger a_\mu|\bm{n}\rangle &= (-1)^{S_\mu(\bm{n})+S_\nu(\bm{n})}\,|\bm{n}^{-\mu+\nu}\rangle \label{eq:6-adn-5} \end{align} $$

and again the signs are opposite and the sum is $0$. Combining (a) and (b), we have shown that $\{a_\mu,a_\nu^\dagger\} = 0$ if $\mu\neq\nu$.

The bookkeeping of signs is confusing until one gets used to it, so we collect the accounting used in Section 6.4 into a single table. The only thing to look at in the table is “whether the changed component falls within the range of the sum $S_\lambda = \sum_{\kappa\lt\lambda}n_\kappa$.”

With this, all three kinds of anticommutation relations have been established. We collect them into one statement.

$$ \begin{align} \{a_\mu,\, a_\nu^\dagger\} &= a_\mu a_\nu^\dagger + a_\nu^\dagger a_\mu = \delta_{\mu\nu}, \label{eq:6-anticomm-1}\\ \{a_\mu,\, a_\nu\} &= \{a_\mu^\dagger,\, a_\nu^\dagger\} = 0 . \label{eq:6-anticomm-2} \end{align} $$

Physical Meaning: The anticommutation relations determine the whole theory

The definitions so far (the occupation-number representation and the sign factor $(-1)^{S_\mu}$) depended on a convention, “arrange the orbitals in ascending order,” which is a matter of human convenience. Yet the final results, Eqs. \eqref{eq:6-anticomm-1} and \eqref{eq:6-anticomm-2}, retain no trace of that convention. Indeed, in all later calculations we use neither the explicit form of the occupation-number states nor the definition of $S_\mu$; we use only the anticommutation relations. This means the following: the theory of second quantization can be reconstructed from purely abstract data, namely “a set of operators satisfying $\{a_\mu,a_\nu^\dagger\}=\delta_{\mu\nu}$ and so on, together with a vacuum satisfying $a_\mu|0\rangle = 0$.” Determinants and permutations no longer appear on the surface.
Moreover, if we replace the basis by another orthonormal set $\tilde\varphi_\mu = \sum_\nu U_{\nu\mu}\varphi_\nu$ ($U$ a unitary matrix), the corresponding operators transform as $\tilde a_\mu = \sum_\nu U_{\nu\mu}^* a_\nu$, but using the unitarity of $U$, $\sum_\lambda U_{\lambda\mu}^*U_{\lambda\nu} = \delta_{\mu\nu}$, we obtain $\{\tilde a_\mu, \tilde a_\nu^\dagger\} = \sum_{\lambda\kappa}U^*_{\lambda\mu}U_{\kappa\nu}\delta_{\lambda\kappa} = \delta_{\mu\nu}$, so the form of the anticommutation relations does not change. The anticommutation relations are a universal structure that does not depend on the choice of basis.

Example: Computing with the anticommutation relations alone

To get used to using the anticommutation relations, let us compute $a_1 a_2^\dagger a_2 a_1^\dagger|0\rangle$. We could act on the occupation-number state one operator at a time, but algebra alone carries us through.

$$ \begin{align} a_1 a_2^\dagger a_2 a_1^\dagger |0\rangle &= a_1 a_2^\dagger a_2 a_1^\dagger|0\rangle \notag\\ &= a_1 a_2^\dagger \left(\delta_{21} - a_1^\dagger a_2\right)|0\rangle \label{eq:6-alg-ex1}\\ &= -\,a_1 a_2^\dagger a_1^\dagger a_2 |0\rangle \label{eq:6-alg-ex2}\\ &= 0 . \label{eq:6-alg-ex3} \end{align} $$

In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-alg-ex1} we rewrote $a_2 a_1^\dagger = \{a_2,a_1^\dagger\} - a_1^\dagger a_2 = \delta_{21} - a_1^\dagger a_2$. In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-alg-ex2} we used $\delta_{21} = 0$ ($1\neq2$). In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-alg-ex3} we used the fact that $a_2|0\rangle = 0$ for the vacuum. That is, this state does not exist. Indeed, $a_1^\dagger|0\rangle$ is the state with one electron in orbital 1, and acting on it with $a_2$ amounts to trying to remove an electron from the empty orbital 2, which gives $0$.

Conversely, for $a_1 a_2 a_2^\dagger a_1^\dagger|0\rangle$, using $a_2a_2^\dagger = 1 - a_2^\dagger a_2$ we get $a_1(1 - a_2^\dagger a_2)a_1^\dagger|0\rangle = a_1a_1^\dagger|0\rangle - a_1a_2^\dagger a_2 a_1^\dagger|0\rangle$. The second term is $0$, as computed above. The first term is $|0\rangle$, from $a_1a_1^\dagger = 1 - a_1^\dagger a_1$ and $a_1|0\rangle = 0$. Hence the answer is $|0\rangle$. Note that the answer came out without our ever writing a determinant.

6.5 Second-Quantized Form of One-Body Operators

The tools are now in place. From here on comes the climax of this chapter. We rewrite the Hamiltonian treated in Chapter 5

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{H} = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{V}_1(x_i)}_{\textstyle \hat{V}_1} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i \neq j}^{N}\hat{V}_2(x_i,x_j)}_{\textstyle \hat{V}_2}, \qquad \hat{V}_1(x) = -\frac{1}{2}\nabla^2 + v_{\mathrm{ext}}(\rr), \quad \hat{V}_2(x,x') = \frac{1}{\abs{\rr - \rr'}} \label{eq:6-ham-first} \end{equation} $$

in terms of creation and annihilation operators. In this section we treat the one-body part. In what follows, so that the operator symbols do not clash, we distinguish by context between the left-hand side (the whole operator) and the right-hand side (the part for one electron) of the sum $\hat{V}_1 = \sum_i \hat{V}_1(x_i)$.

$$ \begin{equation} \sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{V}_1(x_i) \;\;\longleftrightarrow\;\; \sum_{\mu\nu} \langle \varphi_\mu | \hat{V}_1 | \varphi_\nu\rangle\, a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu, \qquad \langle \varphi_\mu | \hat{V}_1 | \varphi_\nu\rangle \equiv \int \dd x\, \varphi_\mu^*(x)\,\hat{V}_1(x)\,\varphi_\nu(x) \label{eq:6-onebody-key} \end{equation} $$

The meaning of “$\longleftrightarrow$” is that the matrix elements taken between any pair of Slater determinants agree exactly on both sides. Since the determinants form a complete set in each $N$-electron space $\mathcal{H}_N$, if all the matrix elements agree, the two operators are equal as operators. We prove this below.

6.5.1 Preparation 1: All terms in the sum are equal

First we show that the matrix elements of the $N$ terms $\hat{V}_1(x_1), \ldots, \hat{V}_1(x_N)$ are all equal. This collapses the $N$-fold sum into a single term.

Derivation: $\langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1(x_i)|\Phi\rangle$ does not depend on $i$

Let $|\Phi\rangle, |\Phi'\rangle$ be arbitrary antisymmetric $N$-electron wave functions (they do not even need to be determinants). The matrix element is given by the multiple integral

$$ \begin{equation} \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1(x_i)|\Phi\rangle = \int \dd x_1 \cdots \dd x_N\; \Phi'^*(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\,\hat{V}_1(x_i)\,\Phi(x_1,\ldots,x_N) \label{eq:6-sym-1} \end{equation} $$

Now we rename the integration variables: we call $x_1$ by the name $x_i$ and vice versa. Since the integral is taken over the same range for every variable, this renaming does not change the value. After the renaming, $\hat{V}_1(x_i) \to \hat{V}_1(x_1)$, and in the arguments of the wave functions the first variable and the $i$th variable are interchanged:

$$ \begin{equation} \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1(x_i)|\Phi\rangle = \int \dd x_1 \cdots \dd x_N\; \Phi'^*(x_i,\ldots,x_1,\ldots)\,\hat{V}_1(x_1)\,\Phi(x_i,\ldots,x_1,\ldots) . \label{eq:6-sym-2} \end{equation} $$

Here we use antisymmetry (the property established in Chapter 5 that the wave function changes sign under the exchange of any two electrons). Since the exchange of two variables is a single transposition, we have

$$ \Phi(x_i,\ldots,x_1,\ldots) = -\,\Phi(x_1,\ldots,x_i,\ldots), \qquad \Phi'^*(x_i,\ldots,x_1,\ldots) = -\,\Phi'^*(x_1,\ldots,x_i,\ldots) $$

A factor $-1$ appears on both, so in the product we get $(-1)\times(-1) = +1$; that is, the signs cancel. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1(x_i)|\Phi\rangle = \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1(x_1)|\Phi\rangle \qquad (i = 1,2,\ldots,N) \label{eq:6-sym-3} \end{equation} $$

Summing this over $i$ gives

$$ \begin{equation} \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1|\Phi\rangle = N\,\langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1(x_1)|\Phi\rangle \label{eq:6-sym-4} \end{equation} $$

The fact that electrons cannot be distinguished by numbering them has reduced the computational cost by a factor of $N$.

6.5.2 Preparation 2: Expansion of a determinant with respect to the first variable

Next we need a formula that decomposes an $N$-electron determinant into “the orbital carried by the first variable” and “the determinant of the remaining $N-1$ electrons.” This is a translation of the cofactor expansion (Laplace expansion) of linear algebra into the language of wave functions.

Math Note: Cofactor expansion of a determinant

The determinant of an $N\times N$ matrix $A$ can be expanded along the first row as

$$\det A = \sum_{p=1}^{N} (-1)^{1+p}\,A_{1p}\,\det A^{(1,p)}$$

Here $A^{(1,p)}$ is the $(N-1)\times(N-1)$ matrix obtained by removing the first row and the $p$th column. The sign $(-1)^{1+p}$ comes from the number of row and column interchanges needed to carry $A_{1p}$ to the upper-left corner. What we use below is this expansion applied to a Slater determinant (the first row corresponds to $x_1$, and the $p$th column to the orbital $\varphi_{m_p}$).

Derivation: First-variable expansion of a Slater determinant

Let $|\Phi\rangle = |\varphi_{m_1}\varphi_{m_2}\cdots\varphi_{m_N}\rangle$ ($m_1 \lt m_2 \lt \cdots \lt m_N$), and write $|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle$ for the $(N-1)$-electron determinant (in ascending order) obtained by removing the $p$th orbital $\varphi_{m_p}$. The claim is

$$ \begin{equation} \Phi(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{p=1}^{N} (-1)^{p-1}\,\varphi_{m_p}(x_1)\,\Phi^{(p)}(x_2,\ldots,x_N) \label{eq:6-laplace1} \end{equation} $$

Let us start from the definition \eqref{eq:6-slater-ket}. We group the sum over permutations $P$ according to the value of $P(1)$:

$$ \begin{equation} \Phi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\varphi_{m_p}(x_1) \sum_{P:\,P(1)=p}\mathrm{sgn}(P)\prod_{i=2}^{N}\varphi_{m_{P(i)}}(x_i) . \label{eq:6-laplace-d1} \end{equation} $$

A permutation $P$ satisfying $P(1)=p$ is determined by a bijection that maps the remaining positions $\{2,\ldots,N\}$ onto the set of values $\{1,\ldots,N\}\setminus\{p\}$. We arrange the elements of this set in increasing order as $\sigma_p(1) \lt \sigma_p(2) \lt \cdots \lt \sigma_p(N-1)$. Then the orbitals that make up $|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle$ are $\varphi_{m_{\sigma_p(s)}}$ ($s=1,\ldots,N-1$), and these are arranged in ascending order.

Now if we write $P(i) = \sigma_p\big(R(i-1)\big)$ ($i \ge 2$), then $R$ runs over all permutations of $\{1,\ldots,N-1\}$. As for the sign, if we write $P_0$ for the $P$ corresponding to the identity permutation $R$, then $\mathrm{sgn}(P) = \mathrm{sgn}(P_0)\,\mathrm{sgn}(R)$ holds ($P$ is obtained by composing $P_0$ with “the permutation that fixes position $1$ and rearranges $2,\ldots,N$ by $R$,” and this follows from the multiplicativity of the sign (the Math Note in Section 6.2); extending a permutation by fixing position 1 does not change its sign).

We count $\mathrm{sgn}(P_0)$. Writing $P_0$ as a sequence of values, we get $\big(p,\ 1,2,\ldots,\widehat{p},\ldots,N\big)$; that is, $p$ comes first, followed by the numbers other than $p$ in ascending order. The inversions (pairs in which the earlier one is larger) are only the pairs of the leading $p$ with $1,2,\ldots,p-1$ appearing after it, and their number is $p-1$. The later part is in ascending order and so produces no inversions. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \mathrm{sgn}(P_0) = (-1)^{p-1} . \label{eq:6-laplace-d2} \end{equation} $$

Substituting these into \eqref{eq:6-laplace-d1}, the inner sum becomes

$$ \begin{equation} \sum_{P:P(1)=p}\mathrm{sgn}(P)\prod_{i=2}^{N}\varphi_{m_{P(i)}}(x_i) = (-1)^{p-1}\sum_{R}\mathrm{sgn}(R)\prod_{s=1}^{N-1}\varphi_{m_{\sigma_p(R(s))}}(x_{s+1}) \label{eq:6-laplace-d3} \end{equation} $$

The sum on the right-hand side is nothing but the definition \eqref{eq:6-slater-ket} applied to $N-1$ electrons with variables $(x_2,\ldots,x_N)$, and equals $\sqrt{(N-1)!}\;\Phi^{(p)}(x_2,\ldots,x_N)$ (we have multiplied back the normalization factor $1/\sqrt{(N-1)!}$ contained in the definition of $\Phi^{(p)}$). Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \Phi = \frac{\sqrt{(N-1)!}}{\sqrt{N!}}\sum_{p=1}^{N}(-1)^{p-1}\varphi_{m_p}(x_1)\,\Phi^{(p)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{p=1}^{N}(-1)^{p-1}\varphi_{m_p}(x_1)\,\Phi^{(p)} \label{eq:6-laplace-d4} \end{equation} $$

This establishes Eq. \eqref{eq:6-laplace1}. In the last step we used $N!/(N-1)! = N$.

Here, please note that the sign $(-1)^{p-1}$ appearing in this expansion is exactly the same as the sign factor of the annihilation operator. Indeed, by Eq. \eqref{eq:6-an-eq2} and the Math Note \eqref{eq:6-Smu-position},

$$ \begin{equation} a_{m_p}|\Phi\rangle = (-1)^{S_{m_p}}|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle = (-1)^{p-1}|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle \label{eq:6-an-position} \end{equation} $$

This coincidence is the heart of the mechanism by which second quantization becomes equivalent to first quantization. The proof below is nothing more than the work of checking this coincidence carefully.

6.5.3 Matrix elements in first quantization (general formula)

Derivation: General formula for one-body matrix elements between determinants

Take two (ascending-order) determinants $|\Phi\rangle = |\varphi_{m_1}\cdots\varphi_{m_N}\rangle$ and $|\Phi'\rangle = |\varphi_{m'_1}\cdots\varphi_{m'_N}\rangle$. We substitute \eqref{eq:6-laplace1} into both the bra and the ket in \eqref{eq:6-sym-4}:

$$ \begin{align} \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1|\Phi\rangle &= N\,\langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1(x_1)|\Phi\rangle \label{eq:6-ob-d1}\\ &= N \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{p'=1}^{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}(-1)^{p'-1}(-1)^{p-1} \notag\\ &\qquad\times \int \dd x_1 \cdots \dd x_N\; \varphi_{m'_{p'}}^*(x_1)\,\Phi'^{(p')*}(x_2,\ldots)\,\hat{V}_1(x_1)\, \varphi_{m_p}(x_1)\,\Phi^{(p)}(x_2,\ldots) \label{eq:6-ob-d2}\\ &= \sum_{p',p}(-1)^{p+p'} \left[\int \dd x_1\,\varphi_{m'_{p'}}^*(x_1)\hat{V}_1(x_1)\varphi_{m_p}(x_1)\right] \left[\int \dd x_2\cdots \dd x_N\,\Phi'^{(p')*}\Phi^{(p)}\right] \label{eq:6-ob-d3}\\ &= \sum_{p',p}(-1)^{p+p'}\, \langle\varphi_{m'_{p'}}|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_{m_p}\rangle\, \langle\Phi'^{(p')}|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle . \label{eq:6-ob-d4} \end{align} $$

We state the justification for each step. Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ob-d1} is the result \eqref{eq:6-sym-4} of Preparation 1. In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ob-d2} we substituted the expansion \eqref{eq:6-laplace1} on both sides (two normalization factors $1/\sqrt{N}$ appear, so together with the preceding $N$ the coefficient becomes $1$). In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ob-d3} we used the fact that, because $\hat{V}_1(x_1)$ acts only on $x_1$, the integrand splits into a product of “a factor depending only on $x_1$” and “a factor depending only on $x_2,\ldots,x_N$,” and hence the multiple integral separates into a product of two integrals. In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ob-d4} we used $(-1)^{p'-1}(-1)^{p-1} = (-1)^{p+p'-2} = (-1)^{p+p'}$ (the power $-2$ is even and so does not affect the sign), and tidied up the notation.

Finally, by the orthonormality of determinants shown in Section 6.2,

$$ \begin{equation} \langle\Phi'^{(p')}|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle = \Delta\Big[\;\{m'\}\setminus\{m'_{p'}\}\;,\;\{m\}\setminus\{m_p\}\;\Big] \label{eq:6-ob-d5} \end{equation} $$

Here $\Delta[\,A, B\,]$ is a symbol that gives $1$ when the two orbital sets $A$ and $B$ coincide and $0$ when they do not (a Kronecker delta for sets). That is, only the pairs $(p,p')$ for which the remaining sets coincide after one orbital is removed from each contribute.

6.5.4 The Slater–Condon rules (one-body)

From the formula \eqref{eq:6-ob-d4} and the condition \eqref{eq:6-ob-d5}, we can read off three rules according to how much the occupied-orbital sets of $|\Phi\rangle$ and $|\Phi'\rangle$ “differ.” These are the Slater–Condon rules.

Theorem: Slater–Condon rules for a one-body operator

Let $\{m\}$ and $\{m'\}$ be the sets of occupied orbitals of the ascending-order determinants $|\Phi\rangle$ and $|\Phi'\rangle$, respectively.

  • (SC0) Identical determinants ($\{m'\}=\{m\}$): $$\langle\Phi|\hat{V}_1|\Phi\rangle = \sum_{p=1}^{N}\langle\varphi_{m_p}|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_{m_p}\rangle = \sum_{\mu} n_\mu\,\langle\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_\mu\rangle$$
  • (SC1) Differing by exactly one orbital ($c = m_q$ in $\{m\}$ is replaced by $c' = m'_{q'}$ in $\{m'\}$): $$\langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1|\Phi\rangle = (-1)^{q+q'}\,\langle\varphi_{c'}|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_{c}\rangle$$
  • (SC2) Differing by two or more orbitals: $$\langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1|\Phi\rangle = 0$$

Derivation: The three rules

(SC0). Since $\{m'\} = \{m\}$ and both are in ascending order, $m'_k = m_k$ (for all $k$). The condition \eqref{eq:6-ob-d5} requires that “the set obtained by removing $m_p$ from $\{m\}$ coincides with the one obtained by removing $m_{p'}$,” but since the orbital indices have no repetitions, this means $p = p'$. Therefore only the diagonal terms of the double sum in \eqref{eq:6-ob-d4} remain, and

$$ \begin{equation} \langle\Phi|\hat{V}_1|\Phi\rangle = \sum_{p}(-1)^{2p}\langle\varphi_{m_p}|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_{m_p}\rangle = \sum_{p=1}^{N}\langle\varphi_{m_p}|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_{m_p}\rangle \label{eq:6-sc0} \end{equation} $$

follows (since $(-1)^{2p}=1$). Using the occupation numbers, this can be written as $\sum_\mu n_\mu \langle\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_\mu\rangle$. In the notation of Chapter 5, it is $\sum_l H_l$.

(SC1). Suppose that $\{m\}$ and $\{m'\}$ have a common part $R$ (with $N-1$ elements), that $\{m\} = R \cup \{c\}$ and $\{m'\} = R\cup\{c'\}$, and that $c \neq c'$. The condition \eqref{eq:6-ob-d5} reads “$\{m\}\setminus\{m_p\} = \{m'\}\setminus\{m'_{p'}\}$,” but the left-hand side equals $R$ only when $m_p = c$ (if any other orbital is removed, $c$ remains, and since $c \notin \{m'\}$ it cannot coincide with the right-hand side), and similarly the right-hand side equals $R$ only when $m'_{p'} = c'$. If we denote the position of $c$ by $q$ ($c = m_q$) and the position of $c'$ by $q'$ ($c' = m'_{q'}$), only the single pair $(p,p') = (q,q')$ contributes. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1|\Phi\rangle = (-1)^{q+q'}\langle\varphi_{c'}|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_{c}\rangle . \label{eq:6-sc1} \end{equation} $$

(SC2). Suppose that $\{m\}$ and $\{m'\}$ differ by two or more orbitals. The condition \eqref{eq:6-ob-d5} requires that the remainders coincide after one orbital is removed from each. The difference that the operation of removing one orbital can smooth out is at most one orbital's worth, so if the sets differ by two or more, \eqref{eq:6-ob-d5} cannot be satisfied for any choice of $(p,p')$. Hence all terms of the double sum become $0$, and the matrix element is $0$.

6.5.5 Matrix elements in second quantization and the check of agreement

Now we finally compute the right-hand side. The only tool we need is Eq. \eqref{eq:6-an-position}.

Derivation: Evaluation of $\langle\Phi'|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu|\Phi\rangle$

First the ket side. By Eq. \eqref{eq:6-an-position}, when $\nu$ is an occupied orbital of $|\Phi\rangle$ and its position is $p$ ($\nu = m_p$),

$$ \begin{equation} a_\nu|\Phi\rangle = (-1)^{p-1}\,|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle, \label{eq:6-ob-sq1} \end{equation} $$

and if $\nu$ is not occupied, $a_\nu|\Phi\rangle = 0$.

Next the bra side. $\langle\Phi'|a_\mu^\dagger$ is the Hermitian conjugate (bra) of $a_\mu|\Phi'\rangle$: $\langle\Phi'|a_\mu^\dagger = \big(a_\mu|\Phi'\rangle\big)^\dagger$. Therefore, when $\mu$ is an occupied orbital of $|\Phi'\rangle$ and its position is $p'$ ($\mu = m'_{p'}$),

$$ \begin{equation} \langle\Phi'|a_\mu^\dagger = (-1)^{p'-1}\,\langle\Phi'^{(p')}| \label{eq:6-ob-sq2} \end{equation} $$

holds (taking the complex conjugate changes nothing because $(-1)^{p'-1}$ is real), and it is $0$ if $\mu$ is not occupied in $|\Phi'\rangle$. Multiplying the two, when $\nu$ is the $p$th occupied orbital of $|\Phi\rangle$ and $\mu$ is the $p'$th occupied orbital of $|\Phi'\rangle$, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \langle\Phi'|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu|\Phi\rangle = (-1)^{p+p'}\,\langle\Phi'^{(p')}|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle \label{eq:6-ob-sq3} \end{equation} $$

and otherwise ($\nu$ is empty in $|\Phi\rangle$, or $\mu$ is empty in $|\Phi'\rangle$) it is $0$. Here too we used $(-1)^{p-1}(-1)^{p'-1} = (-1)^{p+p'}$.

We substitute this into the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-onebody-key}. Of the sum over $\mu,\nu$, the only terms that survive are those with “$\nu$ occupied in $|\Phi\rangle$” and “$\mu$ occupied in $|\Phi'\rangle$”, so the sum over $\mu,\nu$ is replaced by a sum over the positions $p',p$:

$$ \begin{equation} \sum_{\mu\nu}\langle\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_\nu\rangle\,\langle\Phi'|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu|\Phi\rangle = \sum_{p'=1}^{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}(-1)^{p+p'}\, \langle\varphi_{m'_{p'}}|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_{m_p}\rangle\, \langle\Phi'^{(p')}|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle \label{eq:6-ob-sq4} \end{equation} $$

This is completely identical term by term to the first-quantization result, Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ob-d4},. Since the matrix elements agree for an arbitrary pair of determinants, the identity

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{V}_1(x_i) = \sum_{\mu\nu}\langle\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_\nu\rangle a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu $$

holds as an equality of operators on $\mathcal{H}_N$. ∎

Physical Meaning: $N$ has disappeared from the formula

The number of electrons $N$ appears explicitly in the left-hand side $\sum_{i=1}^N \hat{V}_1(x_i)$, whereas $N$ appears nowhere in the right-hand side $\sum_{\mu\nu}\langle\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_\nu\rangle a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu$. This is no accident. An operator in second-quantized form is defined on the whole Fock space $\mathcal{F}$, and it agrees with the correct first-quantized operator whichever sector of fixed particle number it acts on. That the formula does not depend on the number of particles means more than merely a simpler appearance: problems in which the number of particles changes (open systems with electrons entering and leaving, the grand canonical ensemble with a fixed chemical potential, and so on) can be treated with the same operator.
Moreover, the combination $a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu$ is the operation of “removing one electron from orbital $\nu$ and putting one into orbital $\mu$”, that is, moving an electron from $\nu$ to $\mu$. A one-body operator can thus be read as an operator that “moves electrons one at a time into other orbitals, independently of the other electrons”. The matrix element $\langle\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_\nu\rangle$ is the amplitude of that transition.

6.5.6 A complete check for a two-electron system

After an abstract proof, one should always verify the result by working through it by hand. In the four-orbital world of Section 6.2, we compute a diagonal element and an off-diagonal element in both first and second quantization and compare them. Below, we abbreviate $v_{\mu\nu} \equiv \langle\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_\nu\rangle$.

Example (Part 1): The diagonal element $\langle 1100|\hat{V}_1|1100\rangle$

First quantization. Writing $|1100\rangle = |\varphi_1\varphi_2\rangle$ as a function, we have

$$ \Phi(x_1,x_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big[\varphi_1(x_1)\varphi_2(x_2) - \varphi_2(x_1)\varphi_1(x_2)\big] . $$

The operator is $\hat{V}_1(x_1) + \hat{V}_1(x_2)$. First we compute the contribution of $\hat{V}_1(x_1)$. Writing out the four combinations of two bra-side terms times two ket-side terms, we get

$$ \begin{align} \langle\Phi|\hat{V}_1(x_1)|\Phi\rangle &= \frac{1}{2}\Big[ v_{11}\underbrace{\langle\varphi_2|\varphi_2\rangle}_{=1} -v_{12}\underbrace{\langle\varphi_2|\varphi_1\rangle}_{=0} -v_{21}\underbrace{\langle\varphi_1|\varphi_2\rangle}_{=0} +v_{22}\underbrace{\langle\varphi_1|\varphi_1\rangle}_{=1} \Big] \label{eq:6-ex1-1}\\ &= \frac{1}{2}\big(v_{11}+v_{22}\big) . \label{eq:6-ex1-2} \end{align} $$

In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ex1-1} we used the fact that the integral over $x_1$ becomes a matrix element sandwiching $\hat{V}_1$ while the integral over $x_2$ becomes a mere overlap integral, and applied orthonormality, Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ortho}. The contribution of $\hat{V}_1(x_2)$ is exactly the same calculation with the roles of $x_1$ and $x_2$ interchanged, and equals $\frac12(v_{11}+v_{22})$. Altogether,

$$ \langle\Phi|\hat{V}_1|\Phi\rangle = v_{11} + v_{22} = H_1 + H_2 . $$

This is the same result as the calculation of (A1)+(D1) in Chapter 5.

Second quantization. We compute $\sum_{\mu\nu}v_{\mu\nu}\langle 1100|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu|1100\rangle$. For $a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu$ not to change the state, we need $\mu=\nu$, and then $a_\mu^\dagger a_\mu = \hat{n}_\mu$. Hence, from Eq. \eqref{eq:6-number-op},

$$ \sum_{\mu\nu}v_{\mu\nu}\langle 1100|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu|1100\rangle = \sum_{\mu} v_{\mu\mu}\,n_\mu = v_{11}\cdot 1 + v_{22}\cdot 1 + v_{33}\cdot 0 + v_{44}\cdot 0 = v_{11}+v_{22}. $$

The results agree. First quantization required expanding four terms, whereas second quantization only requires reading off the eigenvalues of the number operators.

Example (Part 2): The off-diagonal element $\langle 0110|\hat{V}_1|1100\rangle$ (the sign is the crux)

We compute the matrix element from $|1100\rangle = |\varphi_1\varphi_2\rangle$ to $|0110\rangle = |\varphi_2\varphi_3\rangle$. Orbital 1 has been replaced by orbital 3, which is the situation of (SC1).

First quantization. The functional forms are

$$ \Phi = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big[\varphi_1(x_1)\varphi_2(x_2)-\varphi_2(x_1)\varphi_1(x_2)\big], \qquad \Phi' = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big[\varphi_2(x_1)\varphi_3(x_2)-\varphi_3(x_1)\varphi_2(x_2)\big] . $$

First the contribution of $\hat{V}_1(x_1)$. Writing the four products of the two terms of $\Phi'^*$ and the two terms of $\Phi$ separately as the $x_1$ integral (sandwiching $\hat{V}_1$) and the $x_2$ integral (overlap), we get

$$ \begin{align} \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1(x_1)|\Phi\rangle &= \frac{1}{2}\Big[ v_{21}\,\langle\varphi_3|\varphi_2\rangle -v_{22}\,\langle\varphi_3|\varphi_1\rangle -v_{31}\,\langle\varphi_2|\varphi_2\rangle +v_{32}\,\langle\varphi_2|\varphi_1\rangle \Big] \label{eq:6-ex2-1}\\ &= \frac{1}{2}\big[0 - 0 - v_{31}\cdot 1 + 0\big] = -\frac{1}{2}v_{31} . \label{eq:6-ex2-2} \end{align} $$

The four terms of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ex2-1} are, in order, ($\Phi'^*$ first term)×($\Phi$ first term), ($\Phi'^*$ first term)×($\Phi$ second term), ($\Phi'^*$ second term)×($\Phi$ first term), and ($\Phi'^*$ second term)×($\Phi$ second term). In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ex2-2}, all terms except the third vanish by orthonormality.

Next the contribution of $\hat{V}_1(x_2)$. This time the $x_2$ integral sandwiches $\hat{V}_1$ and the $x_1$ integral becomes the overlap:

$$ \begin{align} \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_1(x_2)|\Phi\rangle &= \frac{1}{2}\Big[ \langle\varphi_2|\varphi_1\rangle\,v_{32} -\langle\varphi_2|\varphi_2\rangle\,v_{31} -\langle\varphi_3|\varphi_1\rangle\,v_{22} +\langle\varphi_3|\varphi_2\rangle\,v_{21} \Big] \label{eq:6-ex2-3}\\ &= \frac{1}{2}\big[0 - v_{31} - 0 + 0\big] = -\frac{1}{2}v_{31} . \label{eq:6-ex2-4} \end{align} $$

Adding the two, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \langle 0110|\hat{V}_1|1100\rangle = -\,v_{31} = -\,\langle\varphi_3|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_1\rangle . \label{eq:6-ex2-5} \end{equation} $$

Comparison with rule (SC1). The occupied orbitals of $|\Phi\rangle$ are $(m_1,m_2)=(1,2)$; the orbital that disappears is $c=1$, at position $q=1$. The occupied orbitals of $|\Phi'\rangle$ are $(m'_1,m'_2)=(2,3)$; the orbital that appears is $c'=3$, at position $q'=2$. Hence the sign is $(-1)^{q+q'}=(-1)^{1+2}=-1$, and Eq. \eqref{eq:6-sc1} gives $-v_{31}$. This agrees with Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ex2-5}.

Second quantization. Of $\sum_{\mu\nu}v_{\mu\nu}\langle 0110|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu|1100\rangle$, the only term that is not $0$ is the one with $(\mu,\nu)=(3,1)$ (orbital 2 remains occupied in both states, so the only possible move is $1\to3$). Acting explicitly, we obtain

$$ \begin{align} a_1|1100\rangle &= (-1)^{S_1}\,n_1\,|0100\rangle = (+1)\,|0100\rangle, \qquad S_1 = 0, \label{eq:6-ex2-6}\\ a_3^\dagger|0100\rangle &= (-1)^{S_3}(1-n_3)\,|0110\rangle = (-1)^{0+1+0}\,|0110\rangle = -\,|0110\rangle . \label{eq:6-ex2-7} \end{align} $$

In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ex2-6}, $S_1$ is an empty sum, that is, $0$, because there is no $\nu$ satisfying $\nu \lt 1$. In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ex2-7} we used the fact that $S_3 = n_1+n_2 = 0+1 = 1$ in the state $|0100\rangle$. Therefore $a_3^\dagger a_1|1100\rangle = -|0110\rangle$, that is, $\langle 0110|a_3^\dagger a_1|1100\rangle = -1$, and

$$ \sum_{\mu\nu}v_{\mu\nu}\langle 0110|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu|1100\rangle = v_{31}\times(-1) = -v_{31} . $$

The three calculations (the direct expansion in first quantization, rule (SC1), and second quantization) all agree. The sign $-1$ arises from the same physical origin (passing over orbital 2) whether it appears in the expansion of the determinant or in the sign factor $(-1)^{S_3}$.

Math Note: When the signs of off-diagonal elements can be ignored and when they cannot

If we only want to compute the expectation value of the energy of a single determinant (the HF method), all we need is (SC0), so no sign problem arises. However, in configuration interaction (CI), which deals with wave functions $\Psi = \sum_I C_I|\Phi_I\rangle$ built as superpositions of several determinants, and in perturbation theory when the coupling to excited configurations is evaluated, the sign in (SC1) (and in its two-body counterpart in the next section) determines the result. Tracking signs by hand invites mistakes; in actual numerical calculation programs, the occupation numbers are represented as a bit string and $S_\mu$ is obtained by a bit operation (a popcount operation, which counts the number of set bits). The formalism of second quantization is, as it stands, also the form in which the computer implements it.

6.6 Second-Quantized Form of Two-Body Operators

What remains is the Coulomb repulsion between electrons, that is, a two-body operator. The line of argument is the same as in the one-body case, but a peculiar convention appears in the ordering of the operators. Making this convention clear at the outset is the only way to avoid sign errors.

6.6.1 Notation for two-electron integrals and the index convention

Definition: Two-electron integral (physicists' notation)

For four spin orbitals $\varphi_\mu, \varphi_\nu, \varphi_\gamma, \varphi_\delta$, we define

$$ \begin{equation} \langle \varphi_\mu \varphi_\nu | \hat{V}_2 | \varphi_\gamma \varphi_\delta \rangle \;\equiv\; \iint \dd x_1\,\dd x_2\; \varphi_\mu^*(x_1)\,\varphi_\nu^*(x_2)\;\hat{V}_2(x_1,x_2)\; \varphi_\gamma(x_1)\,\varphi_\delta(x_2) \label{eq:6-tei-def} \end{equation} $$

The reading convention is as follows.

  • The left entry of the bra ($\mu$) and the left entry of the ket ($\gamma$) carry the same variable $x_1$.
  • The right entry of the bra ($\nu$) and the right entry of the ket ($\delta$) carry the same variable $x_2$.
  • The complex conjugate is attached only on the bra side.

This notation is called physicists' notation (in contrast, there is also chemists' notation, in which the two orbitals belonging to $x_1$ are placed first and the two belonging to $x_2$ are placed after them, written $[\mu\gamma|\nu\delta]$. When reading the literature, always check which notation is being used).

Let us confirm the symmetry that follows immediately from Definition \eqref{eq:6-tei-def}. Interchanging the names of the integration variables $x_1$ and $x_2$ and using $\hat{V}_2(x_1,x_2) = \hat{V}_2(x_2,x_1)$ (the Coulomb interaction is symmetric under interchange of the two electrons), we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \langle \varphi_\mu\varphi_\nu|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle = \langle \varphi_\nu\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\delta\varphi_\gamma\rangle \label{eq:6-tei-sym} \end{equation} $$

That is, the value does not change if the “two entries of the bra” and the “two entries of the ket” are interchanged simultaneously. The correspondence with the notation of Chapter 5 is

$$ \begin{equation} J_{ll'} = \langle \varphi_l\varphi_{l'}|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_l\varphi_{l'}\rangle, \qquad K_{ll'} = \langle \varphi_l\varphi_{l'}|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_{l'}\varphi_{l}\rangle \label{eq:6-JK-in-new} \end{equation} $$

(Substitute into Definition \eqref{eq:6-tei-def} and compare with the definitions of the Coulomb integral and the exchange integral in Chapter 5. In $J$ the ordering on the ket side is the same as on the bra side, whereas in $K$ it is interchanged.)

6.6.2 The claim

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}^{N}\hat{V}_2(x_i,x_j) \;\;\longleftrightarrow\;\; \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\nu\gamma\delta} \langle \varphi_\mu\varphi_\nu|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle\; a_\mu^\dagger\, a_\nu^\dagger\, a_\delta\, a_\gamma \label{eq:6-twobody-key} \end{equation} $$

Look carefully at the ordering of the operators. The creation operators are in the order $\mu,\nu$, while the annihilation operators are in the reverse order $\delta,\gamma$. A way to remember this is “from the inside to the outside, the pairs $\gamma \to \mu$ (the $x_1$ pair) and $\delta\to\nu$ (the $x_2$ pair) are nested”: the innermost $a_\gamma$ and the outermost $a_\mu^\dagger$ form the pair for $x_1$, and the second from the inside, $a_\delta$, and the second from the outside, $a_\nu^\dagger$, form the pair for $x_2$. This nested structure will be understood naturally when we introduce the field operators in Section 6.7.

6.6.3 Preparation: Laplace expansion in two variables

We proceed in complete parallel with the one-body case (Section 6.5). First we show that all the terms of the sum are equal, and then we expand the determinant in two variables.

Derivation: $\langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_2(x_i,x_j)|\Phi\rangle$ does not depend on $(i,j)$

Fix $i \neq j$. Choose a permutation $\tau$ of $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ such that $\tau(1) = i$ and $\tau(2) = j$. Renaming the variables of the multiple integral as $x_k \to x_{\tau(k)}$ (the range of integration is the same for all variables, so the renaming does not change the value), we obtain

$$ \begin{align} \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_2(x_i,x_j)|\Phi\rangle &= \int \dd x_1\cdots \dd x_N\; \Phi'^*(x_{\tau(1)},\ldots,x_{\tau(N)})\,\hat{V}_2(x_1,x_2)\, \Phi(x_{\tau(1)},\ldots,x_{\tau(N)}) \label{eq:6-tb-sym1}\\ &= \int \dd x_1\cdots \dd x_N\; \mathrm{sgn}(\tau)\,\Phi'^*(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\,\hat{V}_2(x_1,x_2)\, \mathrm{sgn}(\tau)\,\Phi(x_1,\ldots,x_N) \label{eq:6-tb-sym2}\\ &= \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_2(x_1,x_2)|\Phi\rangle . \label{eq:6-tb-sym3} \end{align} $$

Note that in \eqref{eq:6-tb-sym1} the arguments of $\hat{V}_2$ have been reread from $(x_{\tau(1)}, x_{\tau(2)}) = (x_i,x_j)$ to $(x_1,x_2)$. In \eqref{eq:6-tb-sym2} we used the antisymmetry that rearranging the arguments of the wave function by the permutation $\tau$ multiplies it by $\mathrm{sgn}(\tau)$ (a permutation is a product of transpositions, and each transposition gives $-1$). In \eqref{eq:6-tb-sym3} we used $\mathrm{sgn}(\tau)^2 = 1$.

The number of ordered pairs $(i,j)$ satisfying $i \neq j$ is $N(N-1)$. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_2|\Phi\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\,N(N-1)\,\langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_2(x_1,x_2)|\Phi\rangle . \label{eq:6-tb-sym4} \end{equation} $$

Derivation: Two-variable expansion of the Slater determinant and the sign $\eta_{pq}$

From the ascending-order determinant $|\Phi\rangle = |\varphi_{m_1}\cdots\varphi_{m_N}\rangle$, remove the $p$th and $q$th ($p\neq q$) orbitals, and write the resulting ascending-order determinant of $(N-2)$ electrons as $|\Phi^{(pq)}\rangle$ (it is determined by the set of removed orbitals, so $\Phi^{(pq)} = \Phi^{(qp)}$). The claim is

$$ \begin{equation} \Phi(x_1,\ldots,x_N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N(N-1)}}\sum_{p \neq q}\eta_{pq}\; \varphi_{m_p}(x_1)\,\varphi_{m_q}(x_2)\;\Phi^{(pq)}(x_3,\ldots,x_N), \label{eq:6-laplace2} \end{equation} $$ $$ \begin{equation} \eta_{pq} = \begin{cases} (-1)^{p+q+1} & (p \lt q)\\ (-1)^{p+q} & (p \gt q)\end{cases} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \eta_{pq} = -\,\eta_{qp},\quad \eta_{pq} = (-1)^{p+q+1}\ \ (p \lt q) \label{eq:6-eta-def} \end{equation} $$

We follow exactly the same procedure as in Section 6.5. Grouping the permutation sum in Definition \eqref{eq:6-slater-ket} according to the values of $\big(P(1),P(2)\big) = (p,q)$, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \Phi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\sum_{p\neq q}\varphi_{m_p}(x_1)\varphi_{m_q}(x_2) \sum_{P:\,P(1)=p,\,P(2)=q}\mathrm{sgn}(P)\prod_{i=3}^{N}\varphi_{m_{P(i)}}(x_i) \label{eq:6-lp2-d1} \end{equation} $$

(since $P$ is a bijection, $p \neq q$ is imposed automatically). Among the bijections mapping the remaining positions $\{3,\ldots,N\}$ onto the set $\{1,\ldots,N\}\setminus\{p,q\}$, let $P_0$ be the reference one that arranges the values in ascending order. Then, for the same reason as in Section 6.5, $\mathrm{sgn}(P) = \mathrm{sgn}(P_0)\mathrm{sgn}(R)$ ($R$ is a permutation of the remaining positions), and the sum over $R$ gives $\sqrt{(N-2)!}\,\Phi^{(pq)}$.

It remains only to verify that $\mathrm{sgn}(P_0) = \eta_{pq}$. Writing $P_0$ as a sequence of values, it begins with $p$, has $q$ second, and is followed by the numbers other than $p,q$ in ascending order. We count the inversions (pairs in which the earlier element is larger).

  • When $p \lt q$. Inversions made by the leading $p$: with $q$ it is not an inversion, since $p\lt q$. The numbers smaller than $p$ in the ascending part that follows are the $p-1$ numbers $1,\ldots,p-1$, and all of these make inversions. Inversions made by the second element $q$: the numbers smaller than $q$ that come after it are the $q-2$ numbers obtained from $1,\ldots,q-1$ by excluding $p$. The ascending part that follows has no inversions within itself. The total is $(p-1)+(q-2) = p+q-3$. Hence $\mathrm{sgn}(P_0) = (-1)^{p+q-3} = (-1)^{p+q+1}$ (the exponents differ by $4$, an even number, so the sign does not change).
  • When $p \gt q$. Inversions made by the leading $p$: with the second element $q$ it is one inversion, since $p \gt q$. The numbers smaller than $p$ that come after are the $p-2$ numbers obtained from $1,\ldots,p-1$ by excluding $q$. Inversions made by the second element $q$: the numbers smaller than $q$ that come after it number $q-1$. The total is $1 + (p-2) + (q-1) = p+q-2$. Hence $\mathrm{sgn}(P_0) = (-1)^{p+q-2} = (-1)^{p+q}$.

This is precisely \eqref{eq:6-eta-def}. Finally, tidying up the normalization factor gives $\sqrt{(N-2)!}/\sqrt{N!} = 1/\sqrt{N(N-1)}$, and \eqref{eq:6-laplace2} is obtained.

6.6.4 A pair of annihilation operators produces the same sign

Just as $(-1)^{p-1}$ coincided with the sign factor of $a_\nu$ in the one-body case, $\eta_{pq}$ coincides exactly with the sign obtained when two annihilation operators act in succession. This is the key to the two-body case.

Theorem: Action of a pair of annihilation operators

For an ascending-order determinant $|\Phi\rangle$, when $\gamma = m_p$ and $\delta = m_q$ ($p \neq q$, both occupied), we have

$$ \begin{equation} a_\delta\, a_\gamma\,|\Phi\rangle = \eta_{pq}\,|\Phi^{(pq)}\rangle . \label{eq:6-aa-eta} \end{equation} $$

If either $\gamma$ or $\delta$ is unoccupied, or if $\gamma = \delta$, the result is $0$.

Derivation: Proof of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-aa-eta}

First we apply the inner $a_\gamma$. Since $\gamma = m_p$ is the $p$th occupied orbital of $|\Phi\rangle$, by \eqref{eq:6-an-position} we have

$$ \begin{equation} a_\gamma|\Phi\rangle = (-1)^{p-1}\,|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle . \label{eq:6-aaeta-d1} \end{equation} $$

Next we apply $a_\delta$ to $|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle$. What we need here is which occupied orbital of $|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle$ is $\delta = m_q$. Since $|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle$ is obtained from the original ascending sequence by removing the $p$th element:

  • When $q \gt p$: one element has been removed before $m_q$, so its position shifts to position $q-1$. Hence $a_\delta|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle = (-1)^{(q-1)-1}|\Phi^{(pq)}\rangle = (-1)^{q-2}|\Phi^{(pq)}\rangle$. Combining, the sign is $(-1)^{p-1}(-1)^{q-2} = (-1)^{p+q-3} = (-1)^{p+q+1} = \eta_{pq}$ (the value for the case $p\lt q$).
  • When $q \lt p$: the removed element comes after $m_q$, so the position does not change and remains the $q$th. Hence $a_\delta|\Phi^{(p)}\rangle = (-1)^{q-1}|\Phi^{(pq)}\rangle$. Combining, we get $(-1)^{p-1}(-1)^{q-1} = (-1)^{p+q-2} = (-1)^{p+q} = \eta_{pq}$ (the value for the case $p \gt q$).

In both cases the result agrees with $\eta_{pq}$ of \eqref{eq:6-eta-def}. If $\gamma$ or $\delta$ is an empty orbital, the prefactor in Definition \eqref{eq:6-an-def} is $0$, and if $\gamma=\delta$, then $a_\gamma a_\gamma = 0$ (Section 6.4); hence the result is $0$ in these cases.

We have confirmed that the sign of the Laplace expansion and the sign of the annihilation operators are identical. That the same phenomenon occurs in the two-body case as in the one-body case is no accident. In both cases we are counting “the sign of the rearrangement that arises when a specified element is extracted from a sequence arranged in ascending order.”

6.6.5 Proof

Derivation: The second-quantized form of the two-body operator \eqref{eq:6-twobody-key}

First-quantization side. Substitute the expansion \eqref{eq:6-laplace2} into both the bra and the ket of \eqref{eq:6-tb-sym4}. We write the position indices on the bra side as $p',q'$ and those on the ket side as $p,q$:

$$ \begin{align} \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_2|\Phi\rangle &= \frac{N(N-1)}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{N(N-1)}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{N(N-1)}} \sum_{p'\neq q'}\sum_{p\neq q}\eta'_{p'q'}\,\eta_{pq} \notag\\ &\qquad\times \iint \dd x_1 \dd x_2\,\varphi_{m'_{p'}}^*(x_1)\varphi_{m'_{q'}}^*(x_2) \hat{V}_2(x_1,x_2)\varphi_{m_p}(x_1)\varphi_{m_q}(x_2) \notag\\ &\qquad\times \int \dd x_3\cdots \dd x_N\,\Phi'^{(p'q')*}\,\Phi^{(pq)} \label{eq:6-tb-d1}\\[4pt] &= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{p'\neq q'}\sum_{p\neq q}\eta'_{p'q'}\,\eta_{pq}\; \langle \varphi_{m'_{p'}}\varphi_{m'_{q'}}|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_{m_p}\varphi_{m_q}\rangle\; \langle \Phi'^{(p'q')}|\Phi^{(pq)}\rangle . \label{eq:6-tb-d2} \end{align} $$

In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-tb-d1} we used the fact that $\hat{V}_2(x_1,x_2)$ acts only on $x_1,x_2$, so the multiple integral separates into an “integral over $x_1,x_2$” and an “integral over $x_3,\ldots,x_N$.” In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-tb-d2} we used that the preceding coefficient becomes $\frac{N(N-1)}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{N(N-1)} = \frac{1}{2}$, together with the definition \eqref{eq:6-tei-def} of the electron-repulsion integral. Here $\eta'$ is the sign \eqref{eq:6-eta-def} for the determinant on the bra side.

Second-quantization side. We evaluate the matrix element $\langle\Phi'|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma|\Phi\rangle$ on the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-twobody-key}. The ket side is exactly Theorem \eqref{eq:6-aa-eta} itself: when $\gamma = m_p$ and $\delta = m_q$ are occupied in $|\Phi\rangle$,

$$ \begin{equation} a_\delta a_\gamma|\Phi\rangle = \eta_{pq}|\Phi^{(pq)}\rangle . \label{eq:6-tb-d3} \end{equation} $$

On the bra side, $\langle\Phi'|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger = \big(a_\nu a_\mu|\Phi'\rangle\big)^\dagger$ (we used $(AB)^\dagger = B^\dagger A^\dagger$ twice). The factor $a_\nu a_\mu$ on the right-hand side has the form “annihilate $\mu$ first and $\nu$ afterward,” which is nothing but Eq. \eqref{eq:6-aa-eta} with $\gamma\to\mu$ and $\delta\to\nu$. When $\mu = m'_{p'}$ and $\nu = m'_{q'}$ are occupied in $|\Phi'\rangle$,

$$ \begin{equation} a_\nu a_\mu |\Phi'\rangle = \eta'_{p'q'}|\Phi'^{(p'q')}\rangle \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle\Phi'|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger = \eta'_{p'q'}\,\langle\Phi'^{(p'q')}| . \label{eq:6-tb-d4} \end{equation} $$

(Since $\eta' = \pm1$ is real, taking the complex conjugate changes nothing.) Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \langle\Phi'|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma|\Phi\rangle = \eta'_{p'q'}\,\eta_{pq}\,\langle\Phi'^{(p'q')}|\Phi^{(pq)}\rangle \label{eq:6-tb-d5} \end{equation} $$

holds, and the matrix element is $0$ if $\gamma,\delta$ are not occupied in $|\Phi\rangle$ or if $\mu,\nu$ are not occupied in $|\Phi'\rangle$. Hence, among the sums over $\mu,\nu,\gamma,\delta$ on the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-twobody-key}, the only terms that survive are those in which $\gamma,\delta$ are occupied orbitals of $|\Phi\rangle$ (at positions $p\neq q$) and $\mu,\nu$ are occupied orbitals of $|\Phi'\rangle$ (at positions $p'\neq q'$), so that

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\nu\gamma\delta} \langle\varphi_\mu\varphi_\nu|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle \langle\Phi'|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma|\Phi\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{p'\neq q'}\sum_{p\neq q}\eta'_{p'q'}\eta_{pq} \langle\varphi_{m'_{p'}}\varphi_{m'_{q'}}|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_{m_p}\varphi_{m_q}\rangle \langle\Phi'^{(p'q')}|\Phi^{(pq)}\rangle \label{eq:6-tb-d6} \end{equation} $$

This agrees completely, term by term, with the first-quantization result \eqref{eq:6-tb-d2}. Since the matrix elements agree for an arbitrary pair of determinants, Eq. \eqref{eq:6-twobody-key} holds as an operator identity. ∎

6.6.6 What the ordering of the operators guarantees

Physical Meaning: Why the ordering $a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma$ is necessary

(1) Correspondence of indices. The pair $(\mu,\gamma)$ carrying $x_1$ occupies the outermost and innermost positions, and the pair $(\nu,\delta)$ carrying $x_2$ sits inside it, forming a nested structure. If this correspondence is broken, the result is a different operator (for example, writing $a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\gamma a_\delta$ interchanges the signs of the Coulomb and exchange terms).

(2) Antisymmetry is guaranteed automatically. By the anticommutation relations, $a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger = -a_\nu^\dagger a_\mu^\dagger$ and $a_\delta a_\gamma = -a_\gamma a_\delta$. On the other hand, by Eq. \eqref{eq:6-tei-sym} the electron-repulsion integral satisfies $\langle\varphi_\mu\varphi_\nu|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle = \langle\varphi_\nu\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\delta\varphi_\gamma\rangle$. Therefore a term in the sum in which $(\mu,\gamma)\leftrightarrow(\nu,\delta)$ have been relabeled has the same integral factor and an operator factor multiplied by $(-1)\times(-1) = +1$; that is, it is exactly the same term. The factor $1/2$ in front corrects for this double counting. It plays exactly the same role as the $1/2$ in the first-quantized $\frac12\sum_{i\neq j}$.

(3) Self-interaction is excluded automatically. In first quantization we restricted the sum to $i \neq j$ and removed by hand the term in which “an electron repels itself.” In second quantization no restriction is needed: the term with $\gamma = \delta$ vanishes because $a_\gamma a_\gamma = 0$, and the term with $\mu = \nu$ vanishes because $a_\mu^\dagger a_\mu^\dagger = 0$, so they disappear automatically. The anticommutation relations, which express the Pauli exclusion principle, are thus directly responsible for excluding the self-interaction.

(4) Action on states with few particles. Since $a_\delta a_\gamma$ annihilates two electrons, acting with it on a state with one electron or fewer gives $0$. The self-evident fact that “a two-body interaction does not act unless there are at least two electrons” emerges automatically from the form of the operator.

6.6.7 The Slater–Condon rules (two-body)

As in the one-body case, rules can be read off according to how much the determinants “differ.” All the proofs follow from Eq. \eqref{eq:6-tb-d5} and the condition “$\langle\Phi'^{(p'q')}|\Phi^{(pq)}\rangle \neq 0$ ⟺ the remaining sets after removing two orbitals from each coincide.”

Theorem: The Slater–Condon rules for a two-body operator

We use the abbreviation $\langle \mu\nu|\gamma\delta\rangle \equiv \langle\varphi_\mu\varphi_\nu|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle$.

  • (T0) Identical determinants: $$\langle\Phi|\hat{V}_2|\Phi\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda,\lambda'\,\in\,\mathrm{occ}}\Big[\langle\lambda\lambda'|\lambda\lambda'\rangle - \langle\lambda\lambda'|\lambda'\lambda\rangle\Big] = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{l,l'=1}^{N}\big(J_{ll'} - K_{ll'}\big)$$
  • (T1) Differing in one orbital ($c=m_q \to c'=m'_{q'}$): $$\langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_2|\Phi\rangle = (-1)^{q+q'}\sum_{\lambda\,\in\,\mathrm{occ},\ \lambda\neq c}\Big[\langle c'\lambda|c\lambda\rangle - \langle c'\lambda|\lambda c\rangle\Big]$$
  • (T2) Differing in two orbitals ($c_1=m_{q_1},c_2=m_{q_2} \to c_1'=m'_{q_1'},c_2'=m'_{q_2'}$, $q_1\lt q_2$, $q_1'\lt q_2'$): $$\langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_2|\Phi\rangle = (-1)^{q_1+q_2+q_1'+q_2'}\Big[\langle c_1'c_2'|c_1c_2\rangle - \langle c_1'c_2'|c_2c_1\rangle\Big]$$
  • (T3) Differing in three or more orbitals: $\langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_2|\Phi\rangle = 0$

Derivation: Rules (T3), (T2), (T1), (T0)

(T3). $\langle\Phi'^{(p'q')}|\Phi^{(pq)}\rangle \neq 0$ requires that “the remainder after removing two orbitals from $\{m\}$ and two from $\{m'\}$ coincide.” Since the removal operation can eliminate a difference of at most two orbitals, when the determinants differ in three or more orbitals no choice makes the remainders coincide, and every term of the double sum is $0$.

(T2). If the difference is exactly two orbitals, the orbitals to be removed are uniquely determined: $\{m_p, m_q\} = \{c_1,c_2\}$ and $\{m'_{p'},m'_{q'}\} = \{c_1',c_2'\}$. There are two ways to choose $(p,q)$ on the ket side, namely $(q_1,q_2)$ and $(q_2,q_1)$, and likewise two on the bra side, so a total of four terms contribute. Arranging them with the antisymmetry $\eta_{qp} = -\eta_{pq}$ of $\eta$ and the symmetry \eqref{eq:6-tei-sym} of the electron-repulsion integral gives

$$ \begin{align} \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_2|\Phi\rangle &= \frac{1}{2}\eta'_{q_1'q_2'}\eta_{q_1q_2}\Big[ \langle c_1'c_2'|c_1c_2\rangle - \langle c_1'c_2'|c_2c_1\rangle - \langle c_2'c_1'|c_1c_2\rangle + \langle c_2'c_1'|c_2c_1\rangle \Big] \label{eq:6-T2-d1}\\ &= \frac{1}{2}\eta'_{q_1'q_2'}\eta_{q_1q_2}\cdot 2\Big[ \langle c_1'c_2'|c_1c_2\rangle - \langle c_1'c_2'|c_2c_1\rangle \Big] \label{eq:6-T2-d2}\\ &= (-1)^{q_1+q_2+1}(-1)^{q_1'+q_2'+1}\Big[ \langle c_1'c_2'|c_1c_2\rangle - \langle c_1'c_2'|c_2c_1\rangle\Big] \label{eq:6-T2-d3}\\ &= (-1)^{q_1+q_2+q_1'+q_2'}\Big[ \langle c_1'c_2'|c_1c_2\rangle - \langle c_1'c_2'|c_2c_1\rangle\Big] . \label{eq:6-T2-d4} \end{align} $$

The four terms of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-T2-d1} correspond to the choices (ket, bra) of $\big((q_1,q_2),(q_1',q_2')\big)$, $\big((q_2,q_1),(q_1',q_2')\big)$, $\big((q_1,q_2),(q_2',q_1')\big)$, $\big((q_2,q_1),(q_2',q_1')\big)$, and by the antisymmetry of $\eta$ they carry the signs $+,-,-,+$ in this order. In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-T2-d2} we used Eq. \eqref{eq:6-tei-sym} (the value does not change when bra and ket are interchanged simultaneously) to see that the fourth term $=$ the first term and the third term $=$ the second term. In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-T2-d3} we substituted Eq. \eqref{eq:6-eta-def} for $q_1\lt q_2$ and $q_1'\lt q_2'$. In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-T2-d4} we used that $(+1)+(+1)=2$ is even.

(T1). If the difference is one orbital ($c \to c'$), then one of the two orbitals to be removed must be $c$ (on the ket side) and $c'$ (on the bra side), and the other must be a common occupied orbital $\lambda$ ($\lambda \neq c$), which moreover must be the same $\lambda$ on the bra side and the ket side. There are $N-1$ ways to choose $\lambda$, and for each of them the same arrangement of four terms as in (T2) can be used. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \langle\Phi'|\hat{V}_2|\Phi\rangle = \sum_{\lambda}\eta'_{q'r'}\,\eta_{qr}\Big[\langle c'\lambda|c\lambda\rangle - \langle c'\lambda|\lambda c\rangle\Big] \label{eq:6-T1-d1} \end{equation} $$

Here $r$ is the position of $\lambda$ in $|\Phi\rangle$, and $r'$ is the position of $\lambda$ in $|\Phi'\rangle$. At first sight the sign seems to depend on $\lambda$, but in fact it does not. Suppose $\lambda$ is the $k$th smallest within the common part $R$ (the $N-1$ orbitals). Then:

  • When $\lambda \gt c$: in $|\Phi\rangle$, $\lambda$ comes after $c$, so $r = k+1$. Since the order of positions is the same as the order of the orbital indices, $q \lt r$, and by Eq. \eqref{eq:6-eta-def}, $\eta_{qr} = (-1)^{q+r+1} = (-1)^{q+k+2} = (-1)^{q+k}$.
  • When $\lambda \lt c$: $r = k$ and $q \gt r$, so $\eta_{qr} = (-1)^{q+r} = (-1)^{q+k}$.

In both cases $\eta_{qr} = (-1)^{q+k}$. Applying exactly the same argument to the bra side gives $\eta'_{q'r'} = (-1)^{q'+k}$ (the rank $k$ of $\lambda$ within $R$ is common). Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \eta'_{q'r'}\eta_{qr} = (-1)^{q+q'+2k} = (-1)^{q+q'} \label{eq:6-T1-d2} \end{equation} $$

which does not depend on $\lambda$. Substituting this into Eq. \eqref{eq:6-T1-d1} gives (T1).

(T0). If $|\Phi'\rangle = |\Phi\rangle$, the two orbitals removed must form the same set on the bra and ket sides: $\{p',q'\} = \{p,q\}$. For $(p',q') = (p,q)$ we have $\eta_{pq}^2 = 1$ and the integral $\langle m_pm_q|m_pm_q\rangle = J$; for $(p',q')=(q,p)$ we have $\eta_{qp}\eta_{pq} = -1$ and the integral $\langle m_qm_p|m_pm_q\rangle$, which by Eq. \eqref{eq:6-tei-sym} equals $\langle m_pm_q|m_qm_p\rangle = K$. Hence

$$ \begin{align} \langle\Phi|\hat{V}_2|\Phi\rangle &= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\neq q}\Big[\langle m_pm_q|m_pm_q\rangle - \langle m_pm_q|m_qm_p\rangle\Big] \label{eq:6-T0-d1}\\ &= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{l \neq l'}\big(J_{ll'} - K_{ll'}\big) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{l,l'}\big(J_{ll'} - K_{ll'}\big) \label{eq:6-T0-d2} \end{align} $$

In the last equality of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-T0-d2} we used that the $l = l'$ term satisfies $J_{ll} - K_{ll} = 0$ (if all four orbitals in the definition \eqref{eq:6-tei-def} are taken to be $\varphi_l$, the two integrals become the same expression), so the sum is the same whether or not it includes that term. This is the fact shown in Chapter 5 as “the self-interaction cancels exactly,” and the resulting expression is nothing but the two-body part of the HF energy \eqref{eq:6-ehf-recall} recalled at the beginning of this chapter.

6.6.8 Validation for a two-electron system: the Coulomb and exchange integrals

Finally, we confirm that $J - K$ is correctly reproduced for a two-electron system by carrying out the calculation to the end in both first and second quantization.

Example: Computing $\langle 1100|\hat{V}_2|1100\rangle$ in two ways

In what follows, since there is no risk of confusion, we omit the operator symbol $\hat{V}_2$ from the two-electron integrals and abbreviate them as $\langle\varphi_\mu\varphi_\nu|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle$.

First quantization. Since $N=2$, the sum $\frac12\sum_{i\neq j}$ contains only the two terms $(i,j)=(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$, and because $\hat{V}_2(x_1,x_2) = \hat{V}_2(x_2,x_1)$, we have $\hat{V}_2 = \frac12\big[\hat{V}_2(x_1,x_2)+\hat{V}_2(x_2,x_1)\big] = \hat{V}_2(x_1,x_2)$. The wave function is

$$ \Phi(x_1,x_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big[\varphi_1(x_1)\varphi_2(x_2) - \varphi_2(x_1)\varphi_1(x_2)\big] $$

Forming $|\Phi|^2$ produces four terms:

$$ \begin{align} \langle\Phi|\hat{V}_2(x_1,x_2)|\Phi\rangle &= \frac{1}{2}\Big[ \langle \varphi_1\varphi_2|\varphi_1\varphi_2\rangle - \langle \varphi_1\varphi_2|\varphi_2\varphi_1\rangle - \langle \varphi_2\varphi_1|\varphi_1\varphi_2\rangle + \langle \varphi_2\varphi_1|\varphi_2\varphi_1\rangle \Big] \label{eq:6-ex3-1}\\ &= \frac{1}{2}\Big[ J_{12} - K_{12} - K_{12} + J_{12} \Big] \label{eq:6-ex3-2}\\ &= J_{12} - K_{12} . \label{eq:6-ex3-3} \end{align} $$

The four terms in Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ex3-1} are the combinations of the two terms on the bra side and the two terms on the ket side (the ones named (A2), (B2), (C2), (D2) in Chapter 5). In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-ex3-2} we used the correspondence \eqref{eq:6-JK-in-new}. By Eq. \eqref{eq:6-tei-sym}, the third term $\langle\varphi_2\varphi_1|\varphi_1\varphi_2\rangle$ equals $\langle\varphi_1\varphi_2|\varphi_2\varphi_1\rangle = K_{12}$, and for the same reason the fourth term $\langle\varphi_2\varphi_1|\varphi_2\varphi_1\rangle$ equals $J_{21} = J_{12}$. This agrees with the two-body part of the HF energy of the two-electron system obtained in Chapter 5.

Second quantization. What we now have to compute is

$$ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\nu\gamma\delta}\langle\varphi_\mu\varphi_\nu|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle\; \langle 1100|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma|1100\rangle $$

For $a_\delta a_\gamma|1100\rangle$ to be different from $0$, we need $\{\gamma,\delta\} = \{1,2\}$. We actually compute the two cases:

$$ \begin{align} (\gamma,\delta) = (1,2):\quad a_1|1100\rangle &= (-1)^{S_1}|0100\rangle = |0100\rangle \quad (S_1 = 0), \notag\\ a_2|0100\rangle &= (-1)^{S_2}|0000\rangle = |0\rangle \quad (S_2 = n_1 = 0), \label{eq:6-ex3-4}\\ \Rightarrow\quad a_2 a_1|1100\rangle &= |0\rangle , \notag \end{align} $$ $$ \begin{align} (\gamma,\delta) = (2,1):\quad a_2|1100\rangle &= (-1)^{S_2}|1000\rangle = -|1000\rangle \quad (S_2 = n_1 = 1), \notag\\ a_1(-|1000\rangle) &= -(-1)^{S_1}|0000\rangle = -|0\rangle \quad (S_1 = 0), \label{eq:6-ex3-5}\\ \Rightarrow\quad a_1 a_2|1100\rangle &= -|0\rangle . \notag \end{align} $$

The opposite signs are a concrete manifestation of the anticommutation relation $a_1a_2 = -a_2a_1$. Similarly, on the bra side,

$$ \begin{equation} \langle 1100|a_1^\dagger a_2^\dagger = \big(a_2a_1|1100\rangle\big)^\dagger = \langle 0|, \qquad \langle 1100|a_2^\dagger a_1^\dagger = \big(a_1a_2|1100\rangle\big)^\dagger = -\langle 0| . \label{eq:6-ex3-6} \end{equation} $$

Therefore, for the four cases in which $(\mu,\nu)$ and $(\gamma,\delta)$ are each either $(1,2)$ or $(2,1)$, $\langle 1100|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma|1100\rangle$ is, using $\langle0|0\rangle = 1$,

$$ \begin{equation} \begin{array}{c|cc} (\mu,\nu)\backslash(\gamma,\delta) & (1,2) & (2,1)\\ \hline (1,2) & +1 & -1\\ (2,1) & -1 & +1 \end{array} \label{eq:6-ex3-7} \end{equation} $$

Substituting this gives

$$ \begin{align} \frac{1}{2}\Big[& (+1)\langle\varphi_1\varphi_2|\varphi_1\varphi_2\rangle +(-1)\langle\varphi_1\varphi_2|\varphi_2\varphi_1\rangle \notag\\ &+(-1)\langle\varphi_2\varphi_1|\varphi_1\varphi_2\rangle +(+1)\langle\varphi_2\varphi_1|\varphi_2\varphi_1\rangle\Big] = \frac{1}{2}\big[J_{12}-K_{12}-K_{12}+J_{12}\big] = J_{12}-K_{12} . \label{eq:6-ex3-8} \end{align} $$

This agrees exactly with the first-quantization result \eqref{eq:6-ex3-3}. Note that the structure of the four terms corresponds exactly: in first quantization they come from expanding the wave function, and in second quantization they come from two choices of the pair of annihilation operators times two choices of the pair of creation operators. The Coulomb integral is the term in which “the order is the same,” and the exchange integral is the term in which “the order is swapped”; the $-1$ attached to the latter is supplied by the anticommutation relations.

Physical Meaning: Where does the exchange term come from?

In Chapter 5, the exchange integral $K$ appeared as “the term in which the assignment of orbitals is swapped between the bra and the ket,” and its sign $-1$ came from the $-1$ attached to the second term of the determinant. In second quantization, the same $-1$ comes from the anticommutation relation $a_1a_2 = -a_2a_1$. In both views the origin is the same, the “antisymmetry of electrons,” but the difference is that in second quantization it is gathered in one place as an algebraic rule for operators. The rule does not change when the system changes or when the number of electrons increases. This advantage is exploited to the fullest in the next section.

6.7 Field Operators

One dissatisfaction remains with the formulation so far. Everything starts from fixing a basis $\{\varphi_\mu\}$, and the symbol $a_\mu$ itself depends on the basis. Physics, however, should not depend on the choice of basis. In this section we introduce a formulation that does not bring the choice of basis to the surface: field operators. This is not merely for the elegance of the notation. We obtain a tool for expressing the density $\rho(\rr)$ as an operator, and this becomes the starting point of density functional theory from Chapter 9 onward.

6.7.1 Definition of the field operators

Definition: Field operators

Using a complete orthonormal set $\{\varphi_\mu\}$ and the corresponding annihilation operators $\{a_\mu\}$, we define

$$ \begin{align} \hat{\psi}(x) &\equiv \sum_{\mu} \varphi_\mu(x)\, a_\mu, \label{eq:6-field-def}\\ \hat{\psi}^\dagger(x) &= \sum_{\mu} \varphi_\mu^*(x)\, a_\mu^\dagger \label{eq:6-fieldd-def} \end{align} $$

We call $\hat{\psi}(x)$ the field annihilation operator and $\hat{\psi}^\dagger(x)$ the field creation operator. Here $x = (\rr,\sigma)$ consists of a continuous variable (and a discrete spin), and $\hat\psi(x)$ is an “operator field” that gives one operator for each $x$.

That Eq. \eqref{eq:6-fieldd-def} is the Hermitian conjugate of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-field-def} follows from the fact that $\varphi_\mu(x)$ is just a complex number (not an operator), so that $\big(\varphi_\mu(x)a_\mu\big)^\dagger = \varphi_\mu^*(x)\,a_\mu^\dagger$.

Theorem: The field operators do not depend on the choice of basis

The field operator built from another complete orthonormal set $\tilde\varphi_\mu = \sum_\nu U_{\nu\mu}\varphi_\nu$ ($U$ is a unitary matrix) and the corresponding operators $\tilde a_\mu$ is identical to the original one: $\sum_\mu \tilde\varphi_\mu(x)\tilde a_\mu = \hat\psi(x)$.

Derivation: Basis independence

A creation operator is the operation of “adding one electron in that orbital,” so it transforms under a linear combination of orbitals by the same linear combination: $\tilde a_\mu^\dagger = \sum_\nu U_{\nu\mu}\,a_\nu^\dagger$. Taking its Hermitian conjugate gives $\tilde a_\mu = \sum_\nu U_{\nu\mu}^*\,a_\nu$. Substituting these, we obtain

$$ \begin{align} \sum_\mu \tilde\varphi_\mu(x)\,\tilde a_\mu &= \sum_\mu \left(\sum_\nu U_{\nu\mu}\varphi_\nu(x)\right)\left(\sum_\lambda U^*_{\lambda\mu} a_\lambda\right) \label{eq:6-basis-1}\\ &= \sum_{\nu\lambda}\varphi_\nu(x)\,a_\lambda \sum_\mu U_{\nu\mu}U^*_{\lambda\mu} \label{eq:6-basis-2}\\ &= \sum_{\nu\lambda}\varphi_\nu(x)\,a_\lambda\,\delta_{\nu\lambda} \label{eq:6-basis-3}\\ &= \sum_\nu \varphi_\nu(x)\,a_\nu = \hat\psi(x) . \label{eq:6-basis-4} \end{align} $$

From Eq. \eqref{eq:6-basis-1} to Eq. \eqref{eq:6-basis-2} we merely interchanged the order of the sums. From Eq. \eqref{eq:6-basis-2} to Eq. \eqref{eq:6-basis-3} we used the unitarity of $U$, $UU^\dagger = I$, that is, $\sum_\mu U_{\nu\mu}U^*_{\lambda\mu} = \delta_{\nu\lambda}$. Therefore $\hat\psi(x)$ does not depend on the choice of basis; it is a quantity belonging to the system itself.

6.7.2 Anticommutation relations of the field operators

Derivation: $\{\hat\psi(x), \hat\psi^\dagger(x')\} = \delta(x - x')$

We substitute the definitions. The expansion coefficients $\varphi_\mu(x)$ and $\varphi_\nu^*(x')$ are mere numbers, not operators, so by the bilinearity of the anticommutator (the Math Note in Section 6.4) they can be taken outside the anticommutator:

$$ \begin{align} \{\hat\psi(x),\, \hat\psi^\dagger(x')\} &= \left\{\sum_\mu \varphi_\mu(x)a_\mu,\;\sum_\nu \varphi_\nu^*(x')a_\nu^\dagger\right\} \label{eq:6-fac-1}\\ &= \sum_{\mu\nu}\varphi_\mu(x)\,\varphi_\nu^*(x')\,\{a_\mu, a_\nu^\dagger\} \label{eq:6-fac-2}\\ &= \sum_{\mu\nu}\varphi_\mu(x)\,\varphi_\nu^*(x')\,\delta_{\mu\nu} \label{eq:6-fac-3}\\ &= \sum_{\mu}\varphi_\mu(x)\,\varphi_\mu^*(x') \label{eq:6-fac-4}\\ &= \delta(x - x') . \label{eq:6-fac-5} \end{align} $$

Eq. \eqref{eq:6-fac-2} is the bilinearity, Eq. \eqref{eq:6-fac-3} is the anticommutation relation \eqref{eq:6-anticomm-1}, Eq. \eqref{eq:6-fac-4} is the evaluation of the sum over $\nu$ by the Kronecker delta, and Eq. \eqref{eq:6-fac-5} is the completeness of the basis \eqref{eq:6-complete}.

Similarly, from $\{a_\mu,a_\nu\} = \{a_\mu^\dagger,a_\nu^\dagger\} = 0$ we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \{\hat\psi(x), \hat\psi(x')\} = \sum_{\mu\nu}\varphi_\mu(x)\varphi_\nu(x')\{a_\mu,a_\nu\} = 0, \qquad \{\hat\psi^\dagger(x), \hat\psi^\dagger(x')\} = 0 \label{eq:6-fac-6} \end{equation} $$

.

$$ \begin{equation} \{\hat\psi(x),\, \hat\psi^\dagger(x')\} = \delta(x-x'), \qquad \{\hat\psi(x),\, \hat\psi(x')\} = \{\hat\psi^\dagger(x),\, \hat\psi^\dagger(x')\} = 0 \label{eq:6-field-anticomm} \end{equation} $$

Math Note: From the Kronecker delta to the Dirac delta

The right-hand side of the anticommutation relations of $a_\mu$ was the Kronecker delta $\delta_{\mu\nu}$ (a discrete index, with value $0$ or $1$), but for $\hat\psi$ it has changed to the Dirac delta function $\delta(x-x')$ (a continuous variable, a distribution). This is a natural replacement corresponding to the change of the index from discrete to continuous, and the completeness relation \eqref{eq:6-complete} provides the bridge.

This replacement comes at a price. Since $\delta(x-x')$ is a distribution that diverges at $x = x'$, $\hat\psi(x)$ is, strictly speaking, not “an operator defined at each point $x$” but an operator-valued distribution, which acquires meaning only when weighted by a smooth function $f$ in the form $\int \dd x\, f(x)\hat\psi(x)$. In physical calculations we may treat $\hat\psi(x)$ like an ordinary operator, but forming products at the same point, such as $\hat\psi(x)^2$, can produce divergences (in fact $\hat\psi(x)\hat\psi(x) = 0$ follows from the anticommutation relations and is harmless, but care is needed with, for example, the square at $x$ of $\hat\psi^\dagger(x)\hat\psi(x)$). This book does not go into situations where difficulties of this kind arise.

6.7.3 Physical meaning of the field operators

What does the operator $\hat\psi^\dagger(x)$ do? Let us act with it on the vacuum:

$$ \begin{equation} \hat\psi^\dagger(x)|0\rangle = \sum_\mu \varphi_\mu^*(x)\,a_\mu^\dagger|0\rangle = \sum_\mu \varphi_\mu^*(x)\,|\varphi_\mu\rangle . \label{eq:6-field-onepart} \end{equation} $$

This is a superposition of one-electron states. Let us confirm that this state represents “an electron localized at the point $x$.”

Derivation: $\hat\psi^\dagger(x)|0\rangle$ is an eigenstate of position

We compute the inner product of the two states $\hat\psi^\dagger(x)|0\rangle$ and $\hat\psi^\dagger(x')|0\rangle$.

$$ \begin{align} \langle 0|\hat\psi(x)\,\hat\psi^\dagger(x')|0\rangle &= \langle 0|\Big[\{\hat\psi(x),\hat\psi^\dagger(x')\} - \hat\psi^\dagger(x')\hat\psi(x)\Big]|0\rangle \label{eq:6-loc-1}\\ &= \delta(x-x')\,\langle 0|0\rangle - \langle 0|\hat\psi^\dagger(x')\,\hat\psi(x)|0\rangle \label{eq:6-loc-2}\\ &= \delta(x-x') . \label{eq:6-loc-3} \end{align} $$

In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-loc-1} we used the identity $AB = \{A,B\} - BA$ (the definition of the anticommutator with a term moved to the other side). In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-loc-2} we used the anticommutation relations \eqref{eq:6-field-anticomm}. In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-loc-3} we used $\hat\psi(x)|0\rangle = \sum_\mu\varphi_\mu(x)a_\mu|0\rangle = 0$ (the vacuum has no electron in any orbital, so every $a_\mu$ annihilates the vacuum) and $\langle 0|0\rangle = 1$.

The result $\langle 0|\hat\psi(x)\hat\psi^\dagger(x')|0\rangle = \delta(x-x')$ shows that these states are position eigenstates with delta-function normalization (the many-body version of $\langle \rr|\rr'\rangle = \delta^3(\rr-\rr')$ learned in Chapter 1). That is, $\hat\psi^\dagger(x)$ is the operator that “places one electron at the point $x$,” and $\hat\psi(x)$ is the operator that “removes one electron from the point $x$.”

6.7.4 The Hamiltonian in terms of field operators

Let us rewrite the results obtained in Sections 6.5 and 6.6 using field operators. This is the final form of the second-quantized Hamiltonian.

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{H} = \int \dd x\; \hat\psi^\dagger(x)\left[-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^2 + v_{\mathrm{ext}}(\rr)\right]\hat\psi(x) \;+\; \frac{1}{2}\iint \dd x\,\dd x'\; \hat\psi^\dagger(x)\,\hat\psi^\dagger(x')\;\frac{1}{\abs{\rr-\rr'}}\;\hat\psi(x')\,\hat\psi(x) \label{eq:6-H-field} \end{equation} $$

Derivation: Eq. \eqref{eq:6-H-field} agrees with Eqs. \eqref{eq:6-onebody-key} and \eqref{eq:6-twobody-key}

One-body term. Substitute the definitions \eqref{eq:6-field-def} and \eqref{eq:6-fieldd-def}. Since $a_\mu, a_\nu$ are operators that do not depend on $x$, the differential operator $\nabla^2$ acts only on the expansion coefficient $\varphi_\nu(x)$:

$$ \begin{align} \int \dd x\, \hat\psi^\dagger(x)\hat{V}_1(x)\hat\psi(x) &= \int \dd x \left(\sum_\mu \varphi_\mu^*(x)a_\mu^\dagger\right)\hat{V}_1(x)\left(\sum_\nu \varphi_\nu(x)a_\nu\right) \label{eq:6-Hf-1}\\ &= \sum_{\mu\nu}\left[\int \dd x\, \varphi_\mu^*(x)\,\hat{V}_1(x)\,\varphi_\nu(x)\right] a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu \label{eq:6-Hf-2}\\ &= \sum_{\mu\nu}\langle\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_\nu\rangle\,a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu . \label{eq:6-Hf-3} \end{align} $$

In going from Eq. \eqref{eq:6-Hf-1} to Eq. \eqref{eq:6-Hf-2}, we interchanged the order of the sum and the integral and moved the operator factor $a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu$ (which does not depend on $x$) outside the integral. Equation \eqref{eq:6-Hf-3} is nothing but the definition of the one-electron matrix element. It agrees with the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-onebody-key}.

Two-body term. Similarly, substitute the four expansions. This time we must pay attention to the order of the operator factors:

$$ \begin{align} &\frac{1}{2}\iint \dd x\,\dd x'\,\hat\psi^\dagger(x)\hat\psi^\dagger(x')\hat{V}_2(x,x')\hat\psi(x')\hat\psi(x) \notag\\ &\qquad= \frac{1}{2}\iint \dd x \dd x' \left(\sum_\mu \varphi_\mu^*(x)a_\mu^\dagger\right) \left(\sum_\nu \varphi_\nu^*(x')a_\nu^\dagger\right) \hat{V}_2(x,x') \left(\sum_\delta \varphi_\delta(x')a_\delta\right) \left(\sum_\gamma \varphi_\gamma(x)a_\gamma\right) \label{eq:6-Hf-4}\\ &\qquad= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\nu\gamma\delta} \left[\iint \dd x \dd x'\,\varphi_\mu^*(x)\varphi_\nu^*(x')\,\hat{V}_2(x,x')\,\varphi_\gamma(x)\varphi_\delta(x')\right] a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma \label{eq:6-Hf-5}\\ &\qquad= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\nu\gamma\delta} \langle\varphi_\mu\varphi_\nu|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle\; a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma . \label{eq:6-Hf-6} \end{align} $$

In going from Eq. \eqref{eq:6-Hf-4} to Eq. \eqref{eq:6-Hf-5}, we gathered the coefficients (numbers) inside the integral and moved the operators outside. The relative order of the operators must never be changed (they anticommute, so the sign would change). Since the original order is $\hat\psi^\dagger(x)\,\hat\psi^\dagger(x')\,\hat\psi(x')\,\hat\psi(x)$, the operator factors become $a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma$ as they are. Equation \eqref{eq:6-Hf-6} is the definition \eqref{eq:6-tei-def}. It agrees with the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-twobody-key}.

This clarifies the origin of the convention in Section 6.6 that seemed to be handed down from above, namely that “the annihilation operators appear in reverse order.” The natural form in the field-operator representation, in which $x$ and $x'$ are nested (in the order $x, x', x', x$ from the inside to the outside), becomes the ordering $a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma$ in the orbital representation.

6.7.5 The density operator

We now define an operator that is decisively important for DFT.

Definition: Density operator

We define the particle density operator at a point $x$ including spin, and the electron density operator obtained by summing over spin, as

$$ \begin{align} \hat{n}(x) &\equiv \hat\psi^\dagger(x)\,\hat\psi(x), \label{eq:6-nx-def}\\ \hat{\rho}(\rr) &\equiv \sum_\sigma \hat{n}(\rr,\sigma) = \sum_\sigma \hat\psi^\dagger(\rr,\sigma)\,\hat\psi(\rr,\sigma) \label{eq:6-rho-def} \end{align} $$

.

Derivation: $\int \dd^3 r\,\hat\rho(\rr) = \hat{N}$

Carry out the spatial integral and the spin sum according to the definition. Recalling the convention $\int \dd x = \sum_\sigma\int\dd^3 r$, we obtain

$$ \begin{align} \int \dd^3 r\; \hat\rho(\rr) &= \int \dd x\; \hat\psi^\dagger(x)\hat\psi(x) \label{eq:6-rhoN-1}\\ &= \int \dd x \sum_{\mu\nu}\varphi_\mu^*(x)\varphi_\nu(x)\,a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu \label{eq:6-rhoN-2}\\ &= \sum_{\mu\nu}\left[\int \dd x\,\varphi_\mu^*(x)\varphi_\nu(x)\right] a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu \label{eq:6-rhoN-3}\\ &= \sum_{\mu\nu}\delta_{\mu\nu}\,a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu = \sum_\mu a_\mu^\dagger a_\mu = \sum_\mu \hat{n}_\mu = \hat{N} . \label{eq:6-rhoN-4} \end{align} $$

Equation \eqref{eq:6-rhoN-2} is the substitution of the definition of the field operators, Eq. \eqref{eq:6-rhoN-3} is the interchange of the integral and the sum (the operators do not depend on $x$, so they can be taken outside), and Eq. \eqref{eq:6-rhoN-4} follows from the orthonormality \eqref{eq:6-ortho} and the definition of the number operator (Section 6.3). The property that is naturally required of the density, namely that integrating the density over all space gives the number of electrons, thus holds at the operator level.

Derivation: Expectation value of the density for a single determinant

We compute the expectation value of $\hat\rho$ for the determinant $|\Phi\rangle$ whose occupied orbitals are $\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_N$. As we saw in Section 6.5, $\langle\Phi|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu|\Phi\rangle$ survives only when $\mu = \nu$, where it equals $n_\mu$ (between different orbitals, the state is carried to a different determinant, which is orthogonal to the original $\langle\Phi|$). Therefore

$$ \begin{align} \langle\Phi|\hat{n}(x)|\Phi\rangle &= \sum_{\mu\nu}\varphi_\mu^*(x)\varphi_\nu(x)\,\langle\Phi|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu|\Phi\rangle \label{eq:6-dens-1}\\ &= \sum_{\mu}\varphi_\mu^*(x)\varphi_\mu(x)\, n_\mu \label{eq:6-dens-2}\\ &= \sum_{l=1}^{N} \abs{\varphi_l(x)}^2 . \label{eq:6-dens-3} \end{align} $$

Summing over spin gives $\langle\Phi|\hat\rho(\rr)|\Phi\rangle = \sum_{l=1}^{N}\sum_\sigma |\varphi_l(\rr,\sigma)|^2$. This is none other than the familiar formula, seen since Chapter 5, that “the sum of the squared absolute values of the occupied orbitals is the electron density.” It is precisely this quantity that plays the central role in the Kohn–Sham equations of Chapter 10.

Physical Meaning: The external potential term is directly tied to the density

The external-potential part $v_{\mathrm{ext}}(\rr)$ of the one-body term is a mere multiplication operator containing no derivatives. Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{V}_{\mathrm{ext}} = \int \dd x\,\hat\psi^\dagger(x)\,v_{\mathrm{ext}}(\rr)\,\hat\psi(x) = \int \dd^3 r\; v_{\mathrm{ext}}(\rr)\,\hat\rho(\rr) \label{eq:6-vext-rho} \end{equation} $$

can be written (since $v_{\mathrm{ext}}$ does not depend on spin, the spin sum builds $\hat\rho$). Taking the expectation value gives $\langle \hat{V}_{\mathrm{ext}}\rangle = \int \dd^3r\, v_{\mathrm{ext}}(\rr)\rho(\rr)$, so the energy of the external potential is determined exactly by the density alone. This may look trivial, but the Hohenberg–Kohn theorems of Chapter 9 extend this observation into a far more powerful statement: even the kinetic energy and the energy of the electron–electron interaction can, in principle, be written as functionals of the density alone.

Derivation: Writing the two-body term in terms of the density (making the removal of self-interaction visible)

If we try to express the two-body term as a product of density operators, a correction term arises from the reordering. First, we rearrange the order of the product using the anticommutation relations of the field operators:

$$ \begin{align} \hat{n}(x)\,\hat{n}(x') &= \hat\psi^\dagger(x)\,\hat\psi(x)\,\hat\psi^\dagger(x')\,\hat\psi(x') \label{eq:6-nn-1}\\ &= \hat\psi^\dagger(x)\Big[\delta(x-x') - \hat\psi^\dagger(x')\hat\psi(x)\Big]\hat\psi(x') \label{eq:6-nn-2}\\ &= \delta(x-x')\,\hat\psi^\dagger(x)\hat\psi(x') \;-\;\hat\psi^\dagger(x)\hat\psi^\dagger(x')\,\hat\psi(x)\,\hat\psi(x') \label{eq:6-nn-3}\\ &= \delta(x-x')\,\hat{n}(x) \;+\;\hat\psi^\dagger(x)\hat\psi^\dagger(x')\,\hat\psi(x')\,\hat\psi(x) . \label{eq:6-nn-4} \end{align} $$

In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-nn-2} we substituted $\hat\psi(x)\hat\psi^\dagger(x') = \{\hat\psi(x),\hat\psi^\dagger(x')\} - \hat\psi^\dagger(x')\hat\psi(x) = \delta(x-x') - \hat\psi^\dagger(x')\hat\psi(x)$. In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-nn-3} we expanded the brackets. In Eq. \eqref{eq:6-nn-4} we used two facts: in the first term, thanks to the delta function we may set $x' = x$ ($\delta(x-x')f(x') = \delta(x-x')f(x)$); in the second term, the sign is reversed by $\hat\psi(x)\hat\psi(x') = -\hat\psi(x')\hat\psi(x)$ (the second relation of Eq. \eqref{eq:6-field-anticomm}).

Solving this for the two-body term and substituting, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \hat{V}_2 = \frac{1}{2}\iint \dd^3r\,\dd^3r'\; \frac{\hat\rho(\rr)\hat\rho(\rr') - \delta^3(\rr-\rr')\,\hat\rho(\rr)}{\abs{\rr-\rr'}} \label{eq:6-V2-rho} \end{equation} $$

(the spin sum has been taken; $\hat{V}_2$ does not depend on spin). The first term is the “classical Coulomb repulsion between densities,” and is the origin of the Hartree term in Chapter 9 and later. The second term, $-\delta^3(\rr-\rr')\hat\rho(\rr)$, is a correction that subtracts the part in which the first term includes “the electron repelling itself.” This equation shows clearly that the classical repulsion between densities alone cannot correctly represent the electron–electron interaction. The exchange and correlation energies treated from Chapter 7 onward describe this difference together (along with the exchange effect arising from antisymmetry, and furthermore the correlated motion of the electrons).

6.8 Example: Re-deriving the HF Energy

To appreciate the value of the tools developed so far, we re-derive the HF energy, which took a whole section of Chapter 5 to calculate, for an $N$-electron system using only the anticommutation relations. We never write a determinant. No permutations appear either. All we use are the three relations \eqref{eq:6-anticomm-1} and \eqref{eq:6-anticomm-2} together with $a_\mu|0\rangle = 0$.

6.8.1 Setup

Using the result of Section 6.3, \eqref{eq:6-det-from-vac}, we write the single determinant in which $N$ spin orbitals $\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_N$ are occupied as

$$ \begin{equation} |\Phi\rangle = a_1^\dagger a_2^\dagger \cdots a_N^\dagger\,|0\rangle \label{eq:6-hf-state} \end{equation} $$

The occupation numbers are $n_\mu = 1$ ($\mu \le N$) and $n_\mu = 0$ ($\mu \gt N$). From now on, the indices $l, l'$ run over the occupied orbitals ($\le N$), and $\mu,\nu,\gamma,\delta$ run over all orbitals. What we want to find is

$$ \begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{HF}} = \langle\Phi|\hat{H}|\Phi\rangle = \sum_{\mu\nu}\langle\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_\nu\rangle\,\langle\Phi|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu|\Phi\rangle + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\nu\gamma\delta}\langle\varphi_\mu\varphi_\nu|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle\, \langle\Phi|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma|\Phi\rangle \label{eq:6-hf-start} \end{equation} $$

Note that the part consisting of integrals (numbers) and the part consisting of expectation values of operators are cleanly separated. All that remains is to evaluate the two kinds of expectation values.

6.8.2 The one-body expectation value

Derivation: $\langle\Phi|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu|\Phi\rangle = \delta_{\mu\nu}\,n_\mu$

(i) The case $\mu \neq \nu$. If it is nonzero, $a_\nu|\Phi\rangle$ is an occupation-number state in which the occupation number of orbital $\nu$ has decreased by $1$, and the following $a_\mu^\dagger$ increases the occupation number of orbital $\mu$ by $1$. Since $\mu\neq\nu$, the result is (a constant multiple of) an occupation-number state that differs from $|\Phi\rangle$ in the occupation numbers of at least two orbitals. Occupation-number states are orthonormal \eqref{eq:6-occ-ortho}, so the inner product with $\langle\Phi|$ is $0$.

(ii) The case $\mu = \nu$. $a_\mu^\dagger a_\mu = \hat{n}_\mu$ is the number operator, and by \eqref{eq:6-number-op} $\hat{n}_\mu|\Phi\rangle = n_\mu|\Phi\rangle$. Hence $\langle\Phi|\hat{n}_\mu|\Phi\rangle = n_\mu\langle\Phi|\Phi\rangle = n_\mu$.

Together, $\langle\Phi|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu|\Phi\rangle = \delta_{\mu\nu}n_\mu$. Therefore the one-body part of \eqref{eq:6-hf-start} becomes

$$ \begin{equation} \sum_{\mu\nu}\langle\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_\nu\rangle\,\delta_{\mu\nu}n_\mu = \sum_\mu n_\mu \langle\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_\mu\rangle = \sum_{l=1}^{N} H_l \label{eq:6-hf-one} \end{equation} $$

Here $H_l = \langle\varphi_l|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_l\rangle$ is the one-electron integral of Chapter 5. This completes the one-body part. In Chapter 5 we had to calculate the four terms (A1), (B1), (C1), (D1) individually.

6.8.3 The two-body expectation value: there are only two contractions

Now for the main part. We evaluate $\langle\Phi|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma|\Phi\rangle$.

Derivation: $\langle\Phi|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma|\Phi\rangle = n_\gamma n_\delta\big(\delta_{\mu\gamma}\delta_{\nu\delta} - \delta_{\mu\delta}\delta_{\nu\gamma}\big)$

Step 1: Narrow down the surviving index combinations. The rightmost $a_\delta a_\gamma$ removes one electron each from orbitals $\gamma$ and $\delta$ of $|\Phi\rangle$. If $\gamma = \delta$, then $a_\gamma a_\gamma = 0$ (Section 6.4), so $\gamma \neq \delta$ is necessary. For the same reason, $\mu\neq\nu$ is also necessary. Moreover, if $\gamma,\delta$ are not occupied ($n_\gamma n_\delta = 0$), the result is $0$.

Next, the $a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger$ on the left puts one electron each into orbitals $\mu, \nu$. For the whole to return to $|\Phi\rangle$ (otherwise the inner product with $\langle\Phi|$ is $0$), the set of orbitals removed and the set of orbitals filled must coincide: $\{\mu,\nu\} = \{\gamma,\delta\}$. This narrows the possibilities down to two: $(\mu,\nu) = (\gamma,\delta)$ or $(\mu,\nu)=(\delta,\gamma)$.

Step 2: The case $(\mu,\nu)=(\gamma,\delta)$ (direct term). The operator is $a_\gamma^\dagger a_\delta^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma$. Since the middle two form a number operator,

$$ \begin{equation} a_\gamma^\dagger a_\delta^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma = a_\gamma^\dagger\,\hat{n}_\delta\,a_\gamma . \label{eq:6-hf-d1} \end{equation} $$

Here we show that $\hat{n}_\delta$ and $a_\gamma$ commute when $\gamma \neq \delta$:

$$ \begin{align} \hat{n}_\delta\, a_\gamma = a_\delta^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma &= -\,a_\delta^\dagger a_\gamma a_\delta \label{eq:6-hf-d2}\\ &= +\,a_\gamma a_\delta^\dagger a_\delta = a_\gamma\,\hat{n}_\delta . \label{eq:6-hf-d3} \end{align} $$

In \eqref{eq:6-hf-d2} we used $\{a_\delta, a_\gamma\} = 0$, that is, $a_\delta a_\gamma = -a_\gamma a_\delta$, and in \eqref{eq:6-hf-d3} we used $\{a_\delta^\dagger, a_\gamma\} = \delta_{\delta\gamma} = 0$ (because $\gamma\neq\delta$), that is, $a_\delta^\dagger a_\gamma = -a_\gamma a_\delta^\dagger$. Hence

$$ \begin{equation} a_\gamma^\dagger\hat{n}_\delta a_\gamma = a_\gamma^\dagger a_\gamma\,\hat{n}_\delta = \hat{n}_\gamma\,\hat{n}_\delta , \label{eq:6-hf-d4} \end{equation} $$

and its expectation value is $n_\gamma n_\delta$ by \eqref{eq:6-number-op}.

Step 3: The case $(\mu,\nu)=(\delta,\gamma)$ (exchange term). The operator is $a_\delta^\dagger a_\gamma^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma$. Interchanging the two creation operators gives

$$ \begin{equation} a_\delta^\dagger a_\gamma^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma = -\,a_\gamma^\dagger a_\delta^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma = -\,\hat{n}_\gamma\hat{n}_\delta , \label{eq:6-hf-d5} \end{equation} $$

and the expectation value is $-n_\gamma n_\delta$. Here we used $\{a_\delta^\dagger, a_\gamma^\dagger\} = 0$ and the result of Step 2, \eqref{eq:6-hf-d4}. The $-1$ of the exchange term comes solely from having interchanged the creation operators once.

Summary. Combining the two cases into a single expression, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \langle\Phi|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma|\Phi\rangle = n_\gamma n_\delta\big(\delta_{\mu\gamma}\delta_{\nu\delta} - \delta_{\mu\delta}\delta_{\nu\gamma}\big) \label{eq:6-hf-two-exp} \end{equation} $$

This expression is also correct when $\gamma = \delta$: then the parenthesis on the right-hand side becomes $\delta_{\mu\gamma}\delta_{\nu\gamma} - \delta_{\mu\gamma}\delta_{\nu\gamma} = 0$, which agrees with the left-hand side ($0$ because $a_\gamma a_\gamma = 0$). The same holds when $\mu=\nu$.

6.8.4 The HF energy

Derivation: $E_{\mathrm{HF}}$

We substitute \eqref{eq:6-hf-two-exp} into the two-body part of \eqref{eq:6-hf-start}.

$$ \begin{align} \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\nu\gamma\delta}\langle\varphi_\mu\varphi_\nu|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle\, n_\gamma n_\delta\big(\delta_{\mu\gamma}\delta_{\nu\delta} - \delta_{\mu\delta}\delta_{\nu\gamma}\big) &= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma\delta}n_\gamma n_\delta \Big[\langle\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle -\langle\varphi_\delta\varphi_\gamma|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle\Big] \label{eq:6-hf-t1}\\ &= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma\delta}n_\gamma n_\delta \Big[\langle\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle -\langle\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\delta\varphi_\gamma\rangle\Big] \label{eq:6-hf-t2}\\ &= \frac{1}{2}\sum_{l,l'=1}^{N}\big(J_{ll'} - K_{ll'}\big) . \label{eq:6-hf-t3} \end{align} $$

In \eqref{eq:6-hf-t1}, we carried out the sums over $\mu,\nu$ using the Kronecker delta (for the first term, $\mu\to\gamma$, $\nu\to\delta$; for the second term, $\mu\to\delta$, $\nu\to\gamma$). In \eqref{eq:6-hf-t2}, we applied the symmetry of the two-electron integral \eqref{eq:6-tei-sym} (the value does not change if the two bras and the two kets are interchanged simultaneously) to the second term, giving $\langle\varphi_\delta\varphi_\gamma|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle = \langle\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\delta\varphi_\gamma\rangle$. In \eqref{eq:6-hf-t3}, since $n_\gamma n_\delta$ equals $1$ only for pairs of occupied orbitals, we restricted the sums to $l,l' \le N$, and rewrote the result in the notation of the Coulomb integral $J_{ll'}$ and the exchange integral $K_{ll'}$ using the correspondence \eqref{eq:6-JK-in-new}.

Combining this with the one-body part \eqref{eq:6-hf-one}, we obtain the final result.

$$ \begin{equation} E_{\mathrm{HF}} = \langle\Phi|\hat{H}|\Phi\rangle = \sum_{l=1}^{N} H_l + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{l,l'=1}^{N}\big(J_{ll'} - K_{ll'}\big) \label{eq:6-hf-final} \end{equation} $$

This is exactly the same as the result of Chapter 5, \eqref{eq:6-ehf-recall}. Moreover, this time we obtained it in general for an $N$-electron system, and in a few lines.

Physical Meaning: What happened?

In Chapter 5, even for a two-electron system, we had to handle individually four terms each for $\hat{V}_1$ and $\hat{V}_2$, eight multiple integrals in total, and then argue the generalization to $N$-electron systems by combinatorics. In this section, for an $N$-electron system, we finished with just

  • the one-body term: the single statement “the expectation value of a number operator is the occupation number” (\eqref{eq:6-hf-one})
  • the two-body term: the single statement “there are two contractions that can return, and the one that crosses gets $-1$” (\eqref{eq:6-hf-two-exp})

The difference came from settling the bookkeeping of signs due to antisymmetry once and for all in advance and building it into the anticommutation relations. The calculation of Chapter 5 and that of this section do exactly the same thing in substance. The difference is whether we redo the same combinatorics every time or do it once and turn it into a formula.

The cancellation of the self-interaction (the vanishing of the $l=l'$ terms because $J_{ll} = K_{ll}$) likewise requires no special care here: we may write the sum in \eqref{eq:6-hf-t3} including $l = l'$ because that term is zero. In second quantization, the terms with $\gamma=\delta$ never appear in the first place because $a_\gamma a_\gamma = 0$, so there is no need to worry about the cancellation at all.

Math Note: The gateway to Wick's theorem

The counting performed in Section 6.8.3, “pair up the creation and annihilation operators and connect them (contract them),” is in fact a special case of a general theorem. Wick's theorem states that the expectation value of a product of operators can be written as a sum over all possible contractions (ways of grouping the operators into pairs), and that the sign of each term is determined by the parity of the number of crossings of the connecting lines:

$$ \langle\Phi| A_1 A_2 \cdots A_{2n}|\Phi\rangle = \sum_{\mathcal{P}} (\pm 1)\prod_{(i,j)\,\in\,\mathcal{P}} \langle\Phi|A_iA_j|\Phi\rangle $$

Here the sum runs over all ways $\mathcal{P}$ of dividing the $2n$ operators into pairs. For the present $\langle\Phi|a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma|\Phi\rangle$, there are two contractions connecting creation and annihilation operators (Figure 6.7), and the one that crosses gets $-1$. Because the same rule lets us compute mechanically however many operators there are, Wick's theorem is the main workhorse in perturbation theory and in Green's function calculations (Chapter 17). We do not go further into it in this book, but it is worth remembering that the calculation of this section is its prototype.

Example: Checking \eqref{eq:6-hf-final} for a two-electron system

For $N=2$ with occupied orbitals $\varphi_1,\varphi_2$, we have

$$ \begin{align} E_{\mathrm{HF}} &= H_1 + H_2 + \frac{1}{2}\Big[(J_{11}-K_{11}) + (J_{12}-K_{12}) + (J_{21}-K_{21}) + (J_{22}-K_{22})\Big] \label{eq:6-hf-ex1}\\ &= H_1 + H_2 + \frac{1}{2}\Big[0 + (J_{12}-K_{12}) + (J_{12}-K_{12}) + 0\Big] \label{eq:6-hf-ex2}\\ &= H_1 + H_2 + J_{12} - K_{12} . \label{eq:6-hf-ex3} \end{align} $$

In \eqref{eq:6-hf-ex2} we used $J_{11} = K_{11}$ and $J_{22}=K_{22}$ (if all four orbitals in the definition \eqref{eq:6-tei-def} are taken to be the same, the two integrals become the same expression), and also $J_{21}=J_{12}$ and $K_{21}=K_{12}$ (index symmetry, Chapter 5). The result \eqref{eq:6-hf-ex3} agrees with the result derived at length for the two-electron system in Chapter 5, and is also consistent with the direct calculation in Section 6.6.8.

6.9 Summary and Exercises

6.9.1 Summary

  • Motivation. Expanding a Slater determinant and computing expectation values produces $(N!)^2$ terms, which is in principle infeasible once $N$ exceeds 10. However, the terms vanish in the same pattern every time, so second quantization organizes this combinatorics once and for all and translates it into an operator algebra (Section 6.1).
  • States. Fix a one-electron basis $\{\varphi_\mu\}$ and adopt the convention that the occupied orbitals are arranged in ascending order; a Slater determinant is then represented by an occupation-number state $|n_1n_2\cdots\rangle$. The space collecting all states with different particle numbers is the Fock space $\mathcal{F}$, and the vacuum $|0\rangle$ is also one of its elements (Section 6.2).
  • Operators. The creation operator $a_\mu^\dagger$ is the operation of “writing the orbital $\varphi_\mu$ at the front of the determinant”; when this is reordered to fit the ascending-order convention, the sign factor $(-1)^{S_\mu}$ (with $S_\mu = \sum_{\nu\lt\mu}n_\nu$) appears. The eigenvalue of the number operator $\hat{n}_\mu = a_\mu^\dagger a_\mu$ is the occupation number $n_\mu \in \{0,1\}$ (Section 6.3).
  • Anticommutation relations. $\{a_\mu, a_\nu^\dagger\} = \delta_{\mu\nu}$, $\{a_\mu,a_\nu\} = \{a_\mu^\dagger,a_\nu^\dagger\} = 0$. These are proved by counting the signs one by one in the action on occupation-number states. In particular, $(a_\mu^\dagger)^2 = 0$ is the operator expression of the Pauli exclusion principle. No trace of the ascending-order convention remains in the final result, and all subsequent calculations proceed with these three equations alone (Section 6.4).
  • One-body operators. $\sum_i \hat{V}_1(x_i) = \sum_{\mu\nu}\langle\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_\nu\rangle a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu$. The key to the proof is that the sign $(-1)^{p-1}$ appearing in the Laplace expansion of a determinant in one variable is identical to the sign factor of the annihilation operator. As a by-product we obtain the Slater–Condon rules (the trace if the determinants are identical, a single integral if they differ by one orbital, and zero if they differ by two or more orbitals) (Section 6.5).
  • Two-body operators. $\frac12\sum_{i\neq j}\hat{V}_2(x_i,x_j) = \frac12\sum_{\mu\nu\gamma\delta}\langle\varphi_\mu\varphi_\nu|\hat{V}_2|\varphi_\gamma\varphi_\delta\rangle a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu^\dagger a_\delta a_\gamma$. The essence of the convention is that the annihilation operators appear in reverse order and that the indices correspond in a nested fashion. Terms with $\gamma=\delta$ or $\mu=\nu$ vanish automatically by the anticommutation relations, which guarantees the removal of self-interaction (Section 6.6).
  • Field operators. $\hat\psi(x) = \sum_\mu \varphi_\mu(x)a_\mu$ satisfies $\{\hat\psi(x),\hat\psi^\dagger(x')\} = \delta(x-x')$ independently of the choice of basis. The Hamiltonian can be written in the nested form $\hat\psi^\dagger\hat\psi^\dagger \cdots \hat\psi\hat\psi$, and the density operator is $\hat\rho(\rr) = \sum_\sigma\hat\psi^\dagger\hat\psi$, whose integral over all space is the particle-number operator $\hat{N}$ (Section 6.7).
  • A demonstration of the power of the method. The expectation value in a single determinant can be evaluated by merely “reading off the eigenvalue of the number operator” for the one-body part, and by merely “counting the two contractions that return, with $-1$ for the crossing one” for the two-body part; the HF energy of an $N$-electron system, $E_{\mathrm{HF}} = \sum_l H_l + \frac12\sum_{ll'}(J_{ll'}-K_{ll'})$, is reproduced in a few lines (Section 6.8).

6.9.2 A bridge to the next chapter

The tools built in this chapter will be used immediately and extensively in the next chapter. Chapter 7 treats the jellium model—an interacting electron gas moving in a uniform positive background charge. There we choose plane waves $\varphi_{\kk\lambda}(x) = V^{-1/2}e^{i\kk\cdot\rr}\eta_\lambda(\sigma)$ as the one-electron basis ($\eta_\lambda$ is the spin function introduced in Chapter 5, and $\lambda=\uparrow,\downarrow$ denotes the direction of the spin; it is a symbol unrelated to the sign factor $\eta_{pq}$ of Section 6.6.3). Since plane waves are eigenfunctions of the kinetic energy operator, the one-body term immediately becomes the diagonal form $\sum_{\kk\lambda}\frac{|\kk|^2}{2}a_{\kk\lambda}^\dagger a_{\kk\lambda}$ by \eqref{eq:6-onebody-key}, and the two-body term is cast into a form using the momentum transfer $\bm{q}$ by \eqref{eq:6-twobody-key}. Using these, we will show that the kinetic energy and the exchange energy of the electron gas can be written as functions of the electron density $\rho$ (as $\rho^{2/3}$ and $\rho^{1/3}$). The prototype of density functional theory, in which the energy becomes a functional of the density, first appears there. Second quantization is the essential tool that makes that calculation possible.

6.9.3 Exercises

Exercise 6.1: Practice with sign factors

Consider a system with six orbitals, and take the occupation-number state $|110101\rangle$ ($\varphi_1,\varphi_2,\varphi_4,\varphi_6$ occupied) as the starting point.

  1. Compute $a_3^\dagger a_2\,|110101\rangle$.
  2. Compute $a_2\, a_3^\dagger\,|110101\rangle$.
  3. Verify that the sum of the two results is consistent with $\{a_2, a_3^\dagger\} = \delta_{23} = 0$.
  4. Confirm the result of 1. also as a reordering of the columns of the corresponding Slater determinant (that is, carry out the operation of removing $\varphi_2$ from $|\varphi_1\varphi_2\varphi_4\varphi_6\rangle$ and inserting $\varphi_3$ by counting the number of swaps of adjacent orbitals).

Hint: At each stage, recount $S_\mu = \sum_{\nu\lt\mu}n_\nu$ for the state at that moment. In 1., evaluate $S_3$ in the state after $a_2$ has acted. See also Table 6.1.

Exercise 6.2: Conservation of the particle number

Using only the anticommutation relations, show the following.

  1. Verify, by expanding both sides, the identity $[AB, C] = A\{B,C\} - \{A,C\}B$, which holds for arbitrary operators.
  2. Use it to show that $[\hat{N}, a_\mu^\dagger] = a_\mu^\dagger$ and $[\hat{N}, a_\mu] = -a_\mu$ (with $\hat{N} = \sum_\nu a_\nu^\dagger a_\nu$).
  3. Show that the one-body operator $\sum_{\mu\nu}\langle\varphi_\mu|\hat{V}_1|\varphi_\nu\rangle a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu$ and the two-body operator \eqref{eq:6-twobody-key} commute with $\hat{N}$. Therefore $[\hat{N},\hat{H}] = 0$, and the number of electrons is a conserved quantity.

Hint: In 2., write $[\hat N, a_\mu^\dagger] = \sum_\nu [a_\nu^\dagger a_\nu, a_\mu^\dagger]$ and apply the identity of 1. with $A = a_\nu^\dagger$, $B = a_\nu$, $C = a_\mu^\dagger$. In 3., since $a_\mu^\dagger a_\nu$ contains one $a^\dagger$ and one $a$, use the result of 2. that the contributions $+1$ and $-1$ cancel.

Exercise 6.3: Extracting the wave function from the field operators

The first-quantized wave function corresponding to an $N$-electron state $|\Psi\rangle$ is given by the following expression.

$$ \Psi(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\,\langle 0|\,\hat\psi(x_N)\cdots\hat\psi(x_2)\,\hat\psi(x_1)\,|\Psi\rangle $$

Evaluate this expression explicitly for $N = 2$ and $|\Psi\rangle = a_1^\dagger a_2^\dagger|0\rangle$, and confirm that the result is $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big[\varphi_1(x_1)\varphi_2(x_2) - \varphi_2(x_1)\varphi_1(x_2)\big]$.

Hint: Substitute $\hat\psi(x_1) = \sum_\mu \varphi_\mu(x_1)a_\mu$, and simplify $a_\mu a_1^\dagger a_2^\dagger|0\rangle$ using the anticommutation relation $a_\mu a_1^\dagger = \delta_{\mu1} - a_1^\dagger a_\mu$. Finally, using $a_\mu|0\rangle = 0$, only the terms with $\mu = 1$ and $\mu = 2$ survive.

Exercise 6.4: The kinetic energy in the plane-wave basis (preview of Chapter 7)

For a system in a cube of volume $V = L^3$ with periodic boundary conditions, take the plane waves $\varphi_{\kk\lambda}(x) = V^{-1/2}e^{i\kk\cdot\rr}\,\eta_\lambda(\sigma)$ (the components of $\kk$ are $2\pi n_i/L$, and $\eta_\lambda$ is the spin function) as the complete orthonormal set.

  1. Verify that $-\frac12\nabla^2 \varphi_{\kk\lambda} = \frac{\abs{\kk}^2}{2}\varphi_{\kk\lambda}$.
  2. Compute the one-body matrix element $\langle\varphi_{\kk\lambda}|-\tfrac12\nabla^2|\varphi_{\kk'\lambda'}\rangle$, and show that it equals $\frac{\abs{\kk}^2}{2}\delta_{\kk\kk'}\delta_{\lambda\lambda'}$.
  3. Using \eqref{eq:6-onebody-key}, show that the kinetic energy operator takes the diagonal form $\hat{T} = \sum_{\kk\lambda}\frac{\abs{\kk}^2}{2}\,a_{\kk\lambda}^\dagger a_{\kk\lambda}$.
  4. Using this result, write down, in terms of the eigenvalues of $\hat{n}_{\kk\lambda}$, the expectation value of the kinetic energy for the determinant $|\Phi_0\rangle$ corresponding to the Fermi sphere (all states with $\abs{\kk}\le k_F$ occupied).

Hint: In 2., use the orthonormality $\frac{1}{V}\int_V \dd^3 r\, e^{i(\kk'-\kk)\cdot\rr} = \delta_{\kk\kk'}$ (which holds under periodic boundary conditions). In 4., by \eqref{eq:6-number-op} we have $\langle\Phi_0|\hat{n}_{\kk\lambda}|\Phi_0\rangle = n_{\kk\lambda}$, where $n_{\kk\lambda} = 1$ ($\abs{\kk}\le k_F$) and $0$ (otherwise).
