Introductory University Physics— From Mechanics to Special Relativity: University Physics for Precocious High-School Students —

This book is a teaching resource that gathers into a single volume the foundations of the physics studied in the first few years at a university of science and engineering. It is based on the eight volumes of Yasuhide Mochizuki's handwritten notebooks, “Physics Notes” (mechanics, waves, mechanics of materials, thermodynamics, electromagnetism, analytical mechanics, statistical mechanics, and special relativity). The laws, definitions, derivations, worked calculations, and figures of the original notes are included without omitting a single one, and, so that even a precocious high-school student who wants to understand university physics can read the book, we have supplied explanations of how new symbols are read, the steps between lines of equations, and concrete examples and figures. Content that is not written in the original notes but is indispensable to a textbook (fracture mechanics, full-fledged statistical mechanics, Minkowski spacetime and four-vectors, and so on) has been added in chapters of its own.

Each section contains worked examples in which no intermediate steps are omitted, and each chapter ends with exercises. The mathematics you need can be filled in through links to the relevant sections of the companion volume “University Mathematics” (AD 02). We have placed importance on showing how the results you memorized as formulas in high school physics (the equations for motion with constant acceleration, the period of simple harmonic motion, the Doppler effect, the equation of state of gases, Coulomb's law, and so on) are derived once calculus is used, and how they are connected with one another. The worked examples draw many of their subjects from materials science, such as metals, ceramics, and semiconductors. Quantum mechanics is not treated (it is covered in the companion volume “Quantum Mechanics (AD 04, in Japanese)”). The parts on statistical mechanics and special relativity also serve as preparation for moving on to quantum mechanics, condensed matter physics, and particle physics.

How to read this book
  • Part I (Mechanics) is the foundation for everything. First read Chapters 1 through 10 in order.
  • Part II (Waves), Part III (Mechanics of Materials and Fracture Mechanics), Part IV (Thermodynamics), and Part V (Electromagnetism) may be read, after Part I, starting with whichever part interests you. The last chapter of Part V (electromagnetic waves) is connected with the treatment of light in Part II.
  • Part VI (Analytical Mechanics) presupposes Part I, Part VII (Statistical Mechanics) presupposes Parts IV and VI, and Part VIII (Special Relativity) presupposes Parts I and V.
  • Physical constants, units, and symbols can be looked up in Appendix B. Solutions to the exercises are in Appendix A.
