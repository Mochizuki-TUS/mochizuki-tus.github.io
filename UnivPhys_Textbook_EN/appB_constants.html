Appendix BPhysical Constants, Units, and Symbols

Physics writing is built from symbols such as $c$ (the speed of light), $k_{\mathrm B}$ (the Boltzmann constant), and $\rho$ (rho); from units such as $\mathrm{N}$ (newton) and $\mathrm{Pa}$ (pascal); and from hundreds of technical terms. This appendix is a “quick reference” that gathers all of them in one place. When you come across an unfamiliar letter, unit, or word while reading the main text, open this appendix first. Most entries come with their reading and a one-line meaning, and every entry has a link to the chapter and section where the main text first explains it properly. If you follow the link back, you can reread the definition and the worked examples.

Many readers probably memorized values in high school physics, such as “$g=9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$” and “$k=9.0\times10^9\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$.” In university physics, the connections among the constants (for example $R=N_{\mathrm A}k_{\mathrm B}$ and $c=1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\mu_0}$) come into view, and you also begin to wonder how precisely each value is known. The table in B.1 distinguishes the constants that became defined values with zero uncertainty in the 2019 revision of the SI (International System of Units) from the constants that are measured in experiments.

B.1 Table of Physical Constants

The values are the 2018 CODATA (Committee on Data for Science and Technology) recommended values. A constant marked exact (defined) value is one that the May 2019 revision of the SI decided to “set to that value,” and it carries no uncertainty (it is not a quantity determined by measurement; rather, the meter and the kilogram are determined from these values). A “value determined by the defined values” is a quantity that can be computed exactly from a combination of defined values (for example $R=N_{\mathrm A}k_{\mathrm B}$); in this book we show its leading digits and indicate the continuation with $\ldots$. All the other values were measured in experiments, and the number in parentheses at the end denotes the standard uncertainty: $8.854\,187\,8128(13)\times10^{-12}$ means that the last two digits, “28,” have an uncertainty of about $\pm13$.

Why are there “defined values”?

In the past, the kilogram was a single lump of metal called the “International Prototype of the Kilogram,” and the meter was fixed by “the length of the Earth's meridian” or by a “prototype bar.” Artifacts get scratched and dirty, so there was a danger that their values would change every time they were measured. So in 2019 the system was revised: the numerical values were fixed for quantities that nature itself determines (the speed of light, the Planck constant, the elementary charge, the Boltzmann constant, the Avogadro constant, and so on), and the units are determined from them. Fixing $c=299\,792\,458\ \mathrm{m/s}$ determines “1 m,” and fixing $h=6.626\,070\,15\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$ determines “1 kg.” The same measuring stick can be reproduced in any laboratory in any country. On the other hand, $\varepsilon_0,\mu_0,G,m_{\mathrm e}$, and so on are still quantities measured in experiments, so they have uncertainties. Note that the relation $\mu_0=1/(\varepsilon_0c^2)$ still holds, but $\mu_0$ is no longer exactly $4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{N/A^2}$ (the relative difference is about $5\times10^{-10}$).

Illustration B.1: Checking the connections among the constants

Compute the values of (a) $R=N_{\mathrm A}k_{\mathrm B}$, (b) $F=N_{\mathrm A}e$, and (c) $\mu_0\varepsilon_0c^2$ from the numerical values in the table.

Solution. (a) $R=6.022\,140\,76\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}\times1.380\,649\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}=8.314\,462\,618\ldots\ \mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}$ (the exponents cancel: $10^{23}\times10^{-23}=1$). (b) $F=6.022\,140\,76\times10^{23}\times1.602\,176\,634\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C/mol}=9.648\,533\,212\ldots\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{C/mol}=96\,485.332\,12\ldots\ \mathrm{C/mol}$. (c) $\mu_0\varepsilon_0c^2=1.256\,637\,062\,12\times10^{-6}\times8.854\,187\,8128\times10^{-12}\times\left(2.997\,924\,58\times10^{8}\right)^2$. Using $c^2=8.987\,551\,787\times10^{16}$ gives $\mu_0\varepsilon_0=1.112\,650\,056\times10^{-17}$, and the product is $0.999\,999\,999\,999\,96\ldots$, which equals 1 to a precision better than $10^{-11}$. Therefore $c=1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\mu_0}$ is confirmed numerically as well (the speed of an electromagnetic wave; see Section 42.3). As for units, in (a) we get $\mathrm{mol^{-1}\,J\,K^{-1}}$, which agrees with $\mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}$, and in (b) we get $\mathrm{mol^{-1}\,C}$, which agrees with $\mathrm{C/mol}$. (The calculations were checked with Python.)

Illustration B.2: Converting units to base units to check the dimensions of an equation

Express the unit $\mathrm F$ of the capacitance $C=Q/V$ in terms of the base units. Also verify that the unit of the energy $U=\tfrac12CV^2$ stored in a capacitor is $\mathrm J$.

Solution. The unit of $Q$ is $\mathrm C=\mathrm{A\,s}$, and the unit of $V$ is $\mathrm V=\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-3}\,A^{-1}}$, so $$ \mathrm F=\frac{\mathrm C}{\mathrm V}=\frac{\mathrm{A\,s}}{\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-3}\,A^{-1}}}=\mathrm{kg^{-1}\,m^{-2}\,s^{4}\,A^{2}} $$ (when $\mathrm{s^{-3}A^{-1}}$ in the denominator is moved up to the numerator, it becomes $\mathrm{s^{1+3}A^{1+1}}$). For the energy we have $$ [CV^2]=\mathrm{kg^{-1}m^{-2}s^{4}A^{2}}\cdot\left(\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-3}\,A^{-1}}\right)^2=\mathrm{kg^{-1+2}\,m^{-2+4}\,s^{4-6}\,A^{2-2}}=\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}}=\mathrm J $$ which agrees with $\mathrm{J}=\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}}$ (Table B.3). The fact that the exponent of the current $\mathrm A$ vanishes, $2-2=0$, is how the “transformation” of an electrical quantity into mechanical energy shows up in the units.

Caution: conventions for writing units

  • Write unit symbols in upright (roman) type: $\mathrm{m}$, $\mathrm{kg}$. This distinguishes them from the italic $m$ (mass) and $K$ (the bulk modulus, etc.). Put a small space between the number and the unit: $g=9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
  • For units named after people, the symbol is written with a capital letter ($\mathrm{N},\mathrm{Pa},\mathrm{K}$), but the name is written in lowercase (newton, pascal, kelvin). The symbol for the liter is $\mathrm{L}$ (or $\mathrm l$).
  • Do not stack prefixes (not $\mathrm{\mu\mu F}$, but $\mathrm{pF}$). The kilogram is the base unit of mass, but prefixes are attached to “gram,” as in $\mathrm{mg}$ and $\mathrm{Mg}$ (one does not write $\mathrm{\mu kg}$).
  • The $\mathrm{m}$ (meter) and the prefix milli, $\mathrm m$, are the same letter, so $\mathrm{mm}$ is read as millimeter, $\mathrm{m^2}$ as square meter, and $\mathrm{ms}$ as millisecond. The last of these is distinguished from $\mathrm{m\,s}$ (meter second) by the space.
  • Do not attach “degree” to $\mathrm{K}$ (kelvin) ($300\ \mathrm K$). Celsius temperature is written ${}^\circ\mathrm C$. A temperature difference has the same numerical value whether it is written in $\mathrm{K}$ or in ${}^\circ\mathrm C$.

B.2.3 SI Prefixes

These are the prefixes placed before a unit to indicate a multiple by some power of 10. The quantities used in science and technology span a very wide range (the size of an atomic nucleus is $10^{-15}\ \mathrm m$, and a galaxy is $10^{21}\ \mathrm m$), so different prefixes are used as appropriate.

Symbols with Different Meanings (Caution)

Physical symbols number only about 100, even counting the Latin alphabet and the Greek letters together, whereas there are several hundred physical quantities. As a result, the same letter may denote different quantities depending on the part, and sometimes even within the same part. For example, $m$ is mass, but it is also Poisson's number in mechanics of materials and the Weibull modulus in fracture mechanics, and $\rho$ can be any of density, charge density, resistivity, and radius of curvature. Which meaning is intended is determined by the part and chapter in which the letter is used, and by the unit. The table below lists the letters that this book uses with different meanings, together with the section in which each meaning first appears. (The symbols in this table are collected from the tables of the parts above; the rightmost item of each entry is the section of first appearance.)

Caution: how to tell apart different quantities written with the same letter

  1. Look at the unit: $\rho\ [\mathrm{kg/m^3}]$ is the density, $\rho\ [\mathrm{C/m^3}]$ is the charge density, and $\rho\ [\Omega\,\mathrm{m}]$ is the resistivity. If the unit is written along with the symbol, there is no confusion.
  2. Look at the subscripts: By convention, some letters are distinguished by subscripts or typeface, such as $K$ (bulk modulus) and $K_{\mathrm I}$ (stress intensity factor), or $G$ (shear modulus), $G$ (Gibbs energy), and $\mathcal G$ (energy release rate).
  3. Look at the note at the beginning of the chapter: In some places, such as Chapters 21 and 22, where the meaning of $m$ changes, the text states explicitly “In this chapter we write …”.
