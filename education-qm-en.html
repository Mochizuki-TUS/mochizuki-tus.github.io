Single-Slit Experiment — Where Do Particles Go After Passing Through a Slit?

QM 01

Change the slit width and the particle mass and speed, then launch particles individually or 10,000 at a time to accumulate their landing positions. Watch the histogram approach the theoretical Fraunhofer diffraction curve |ψ(x)|2 . Keeping the display range fixed while increasing the mass from an electron to a baseball makes the distribution collapse to a single point, approaching the classical picture.

Double-Slit Experiment — Interference Fringes, One Particle at a Time

QM 02

Adjust a (half the width of the central barrier) and b (the width of each slit), then click “▶ Fire 1 particle” to send one particle at a time or “Fire 10,000” to accumulate landing points. For the same particle and screen distance, compare the on-screen readouts to see that the center-to-center distance d=2a+b determines the fringe spacing, while b determines the width of the overall pattern. In the default view, each particle beyond the slits is depicted as a wave spreading from both openings and is recorded as a single point only on the screen.

Quantum Tunneling — Transmission Through a Barrier and the Splitting of a Wave Packet

QM 03

Adjust the barrier height and thickness. Panel ① redraws the transmission coefficient T and reflection coefficient R as functions of incident energy, marking the values at your chosen kinetic energy E. See that T remains nonzero even when E<V0, that in this regime a slight increase in thickness changes T by orders of magnitude (the principle behind scanning tunneling microscopy), and that resonant transmission appears above the barrier. In ②, propagate a wave packet and compare the probabilities remaining on each side with theoretical values averaged over the wave packet.

Planck's Law — From Temperature to the Color of Light

QM 04

Move the temperature T slider from 3 K to 30,000 K, or select red-hot iron or a star, to see the blackbody spectrum and its perceived color. As temperature rises, the peak shifts toward shorter wavelengths (Wien's displacement law). Explore how a star's color reveals its surface temperature.

Ehrenfest's Theorem — A Trajectory Becomes Blurred as Mass Decreases

QM 05

Reduce the mass from a baseball to an electron and then to an even lighter hypothetical particle. Set the launch speed and angle. In a uniform gravitational field, the mean position follows the same classical parabola regardless of mass; only the surrounding spread σ(t) changes. As the mass decreases, the trajectory disappears into a cloud and the landing positions become more scattered.

Harmonic Oscillator — Springs, Wave Functions, and Second Quantization

QM 06

Choose a system ranging from a vibrating molecule to a tabletop spring. In ①, increase n to 20 and watch the distribution approach the classical probability density. The energies are discrete, En=(n+12)ℏω, and in ②, the lowest energy never reaches zero even as the temperature decreases. In ③, points sampled from the probability distribution oscillate while remaining blurred. In ④, add or remove one quantum at a time and follow the changing number of wave-function nodes. In ⑤, compare the same operations for bosons, which can share a state, and fermions, with at most one particle per state.

Many-Electron States — How Spin Arrangements and Ligand Fields Change the Total Energy

QM 08

In ①, click to fill 2p, 3d, or 4f levels with electrons and build the total energy using an orbital-independent Coulomb integral U0 and exchange integral J. With no coordination, observe Hund's first rule (maximum S). For octahedral coordination with 4–7 electrons in 3d orbitals, adjust the ligand-field strength Δ to explore the crossover between high-spin and low-spin states. In ②, model ligands as point charges and select octahedral, tetrahedral, or tetragonally distorted arrangements—an introduction to the Jahn–Teller effect—while comparing a rotatable diagram of the arrangement with the energy-level diagram.

Green's Functions and Feynman Diagrams — Adding and Removing an Electron in an Electron Sea, and Perturbing an Oscillator with Anharmonic Terms

QM 09

Select a diagram and click its straight lines, wavy lines, and loops to compare the mathematical factors with animations of an electron sea (①–②). In ③, change the approximation order and temperature to follow how the imaginary part of the self-energy produces spectral broadening and decay of the survival amplitude. In ④, switch to phonons: vary the potential depth and temperature, toggle the quartic loop and the two cubic diagrams, and compare frequency shifts with the exact Morse-type solution. Relativistic Quantum Mechanics — From Schrödinger to Klein–Gordon and Dirac: What Changes in a Free-Electron Gas?

QM 10

Choose a particle and vary its momentum from 10−3mc to 5mc. In ①, see where the Schrödinger parabola departs from the Klein–Gordon and Dirac hyperbola E=±p2c2+m2c4, whose lower branch represents antiparticles. Vary the box width in ②, electron density in ③, and atomic number from 1 to 120 in ④. Explore how replacing the dispersion relation and boundary conditions changes the energy levels in a box, the pressure of an electron gas, and the electron distribution near the nucleus.

Introduction to Elementary Particles — Zoom Into an Atom and Compare the Four Forces and Particle Sizes

QM 11

Click the glowing particles to descend through an atom to the quark scale (①). In ②, vary distance from 10−19 to 10−9 m and see how the relative strengths of the four forces trade places. In ③, examine how electrons and quarks, whose intrinsic sizes have only upper bounds, can fluctuate over vastly larger distances, while the comparison reverses for atoms in materials. In ④, replace the exchanged photon with a massive particle to see the interaction range shrink to ℏ/mc.

Lie Groups and Lie Algebras — How to Read Group Names, from SU(2) and SO(3) to SO(10)

QM 12

Choose SO(3), SU(2), or Sp(2,ℝ), vary its parameters, and monitor the invariants. Deliberately step outside the group and watch those values change: each name declares which quantities its transformations preserve. Rotate through 720° to see that an orientation returns after one turn but a spinor requires two. Continue through raising and lowering operators, planar SU(3) diagrams, the classification of Lie algebras, and grand unification grouping the 16 states of one generation.

Superconductivity — Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking, Cooper Pairs, and “Phonons with Imaginary Frequencies”

QM 13

In the upper simulator, choose among 12 elements and vary amplitude (Higgs) and phase (Nambu–Goldstone) around the rim of the “Mexican hat” Ginzburg–Landau free energy. Raise the temperature to see the rim shrink into a bowl and explore what sets the transition temperature (①–③). In ④, a lattice order parameter produces the same free-energy shape, with imaginary-frequency phonons corresponding to the negative curvature at the center. In the lower simulator, heavier ions leave a longer wake that attracts distant electrons; stronger coupling changes pairs from bubbles larger than the electron spacing into small molecules.
