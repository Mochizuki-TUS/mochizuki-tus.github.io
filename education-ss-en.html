From Molecular Orbitals to Bands — Lining Up Atoms Turns Energy Levels into a Band

SS 01

In ①, increase the number of atoms N from 2 to 60 and click energy levels to see the shapes of their orbitals. The levels crowd into a fixed energy range and form a band. In ②–③, close the chain into a ring: all allowed k values lie on one dispersion curve. As more atoms are added, the points become denser and the distribution concentrated near the band edges approaches the theoretical curve. In ④, alternate atoms or bonds to open a gap, splitting a half-filled band into a full band and an empty band to produce an insulator. In ⑤, choose an orbital from s to f and the phase pattern, reciprocal-lattice points, and band diagram respond together; differences in the way the orbitals overlap determine the bandwidths and splittings.

Heat Equation — How Temperatures in a Slab and the Surrounding Air Evolve

SS 02

Choose a material, thickness, and initial temperature. In ①, switch between a fixed surface temperature and convective cooling to follow the temperature profile and mode amplitudes together. Fine temperature variations decay fastest, leaving a single broad peak. With radiation disabled, the series and numerical solutions coincide; convection changes the eigenvalue problem itself. In ②, surround the slab with air, water, or refractory brick and solve the surrounding region too. You will find that the interface temperature immediately after contact is determined not by thermal diffusivity but by thermal effusivity (the reason metal feels cold to the touch).

The Origin of Heat Storage in Materials — Phonons, Specific Heat, and Energy Uncertainty

SS 03

Choose a material and temperature. View the nonzero vibration amplitude of a two-dimensional crystal even at 0 K in ①, compare classical (Dulong–Petit), Debye, and Einstein lattice specific heats in ②, and inspect the Boltzmann distribution on an energy-level ladder in ③. Specific heat is nothing other than how much the energy can fluctuate. Modes with ħω≫kBT remain in their lowest level and cannot absorb appreciable heat. Whether room temperature counts as high temperature depends on its ratio to ΘD.

Equation of State of an Ideal Solid — Mie–Grüneisen and What Determines Thermal Expansion

SS 04

Choose a material. In ①, rotate the supercell while increasing temperature to see atomic fluctuations and lattice expansion. Ideal springs leave the mean position unchanged, so expansion requires anharmonicity. Summarizing it with the Grüneisen parameter in the quasiharmonic approximation gives αV= γCV/ (BTV). In ②, compare 38 cubic crystals and change the horizontal axis to see that the magnitude of positive thermal expansion is approximately inversely proportional to the bulk modulus. In ③, overlay the temperature dependence of expansion, stiffness, and specific heat for two materials.
