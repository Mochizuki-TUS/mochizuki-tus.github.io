Appendix BEssentials of Second Quantization

From Chapter 9 through Chapter 12, this book suddenly begins to use symbols such as $\hat c^\dagger_\kk$ and $\hat\psi(\xx)$. In a physics curriculum these would already have been learned in a course called “Quantum Mechanics of Many-Body Systems,” but readers in engineering have almost certainly not met them. This appendix is written to fill that gap.

Second quantization has a grand name, so one tends to brace oneself. But its content is not new physics at all. It is merely the Schrödinger equation for $N$ particles, rewritten in different symbols with the same content. Not a single new assumption enters. The only thing that enters is the postulate, already learned in quantum mechanics, that “the wave function of bosons is symmetric under exchange, and that of fermions is antisymmetric.”

Then why rewrite it? The reason is blunt: the $N$-body wave function $\Psi(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_N)$ becomes unmanageable, for humans and computers alike, once $N$ exceeds about $10$. Moreover, electrons carry no name tags. The information that the electron sitting at $x_1$ is “electron number 1” is physically meaningless from the start. The equations swell up because we dutifully carry this meaningless information around. Second quantization is nothing more than a single change of viewpoint: stop naming the particles, and say only “how many particles occupy which one-particle state.”

In this appendix we state the minimum needed to read Chapters 9–12, but without skipping any intermediate steps. The destination is clear: to be able to read this single line—when the density operator $\hat\rho_\qq=\sum_\kk\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_{\kk+\qq}$ acts on the Fermi sphere, it creates one particle–hole pair. The Lindhard function (Chapter 10), the Landau damping of plasmons (Chapter 11), and the dynamic structure factor (Chapter 12) are all built on this one line.

B.1 Why Rewrite? — The Despair of the $N$-Body Wave Function

B.1.1 First, seeing the size of the despair in numbers

The quantum state of a system of $N$ identical particles is, in principle, completely described by a complex function of $N$ variables:

$$ \begin{equation} \Psi(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_N), \qquad x_\alpha \equiv (\xx_\alpha,\sigma_\alpha) \label{eq:B-psi-N} \end{equation} $$

Here $x_\alpha$ is a symbol that combines the position $\xx_\alpha$ and the spin $\sigma_\alpha$ of the $\alpha$th particle. All we have to do is solve the Schrödinger equation:

$$ \begin{equation} \left[\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\left(-\frac{\hbar^2\nabla_\alpha^2}{2m}+U(\xx_\alpha)\right) +\frac12\sum_{\alpha\ne\beta}v(\xx_\alpha-\xx_\beta)\right]\Psi = E\,\Psi \label{eq:B-schr-N} \end{equation} $$

As a mathematical expression, there is nothing difficult here. What is difficult is the size.

Why is it “impossible”? — A naive count

Suppose we store a one-particle wave function in discretized form. Even a coarse mesh with $10$ divisions per side requires $10^3=1000$ complex numbers in three dimensions. That is nothing. But the $N$-particle wave function, Eq. \eqref{eq:B-psi-N}, is a function on a $3N$-dimensional space, so the number required is $\left(10^3\right)^N=10^{3N}$.

For $N=10$ this is $10^{30}$ numbers. If one double-precision complex number takes $16$ bytes, this is $1.6\times10^{31}$ bytes, that is, $1.6\times10^{13}$ EB (exabytes). All the storage devices on Earth put together hold only about $10^{22}$–$10^{23}$ bytes, so this is hopeless. And that is for $N=10$. The conduction electrons in $1\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ of a metal number $N\sim10^{23}$.

Conclusion: the strategy of handling the $N$-body wave function as it stands is correct in principle but has completely broken down in practice. We must change strategy.

B.1.2 The requirement of identical particles

The key to the change of strategy is the fact that identical particles carry no name tags. Exchanging electron 1 and electron 2 causes no change whatever in the world. Therefore observable quantities must be invariant under the exchange, and the wave function can change by at most a phase factor. In three-dimensional space that phase factor is limited to $+1$ or $-1$ (in two dimensions fractional statistics are allowed, but we do not treat them in this book):

$$ \begin{equation} \Psi(\dots,x_\alpha,\dots,x_\beta,\dots) =\pm\,\Psi(\dots,x_\beta,\dots,x_\alpha,\dots) \qquad \begin{cases} + & \text{bosons (integer spin)}\\ - & \text{fermions (half-integer spin)} \end{cases} \label{eq:B-symmetry} \end{equation} $$

Electrons, protons, neutrons, and $^3$He atoms are fermions; photons, phonons, magnons, and $^4$He atoms are bosons. The particles we mainly treat in this book are electrons (fermions), but collective excitations (plasmons and phonons) behave in a bosonic way, so we prepare the notation for both.

Notation Note: Using $\pm$ and $\mp$

Hereafter, the upper sign (the upper one in $\pm$) always represents bosons, and the lower sign always represents fermions. For example, if we write $[\hat\psi(\xx),\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx')]_\mp$, it means the commutator $[\hat A,\hat B]_-=\hat A\hat B-\hat B\hat A$ for bosons and the anticommutator $[\hat A,\hat B]_+=\hat A\hat B+\hat B\hat A$ for fermions. Because this is easily confused, in important equations we will write out the boson and fermion forms separately.

B.1.3 Slater determinants — a tool for removing name tags

When there are no interactions, the solution of Eq. \eqref{eq:B-schr-N} can be written in terms of products of one-particle eigenfunctions $\phi_i(x)$. However, the simple product $\phi_1(x_1)\phi_2(x_2)\cdots$ does not satisfy the symmetry requirement, Eq. \eqref{eq:B-symmetry}. Antisymmetrizing the product so that the requirement is met gives

$$ \begin{equation} \Psi_{\text{Fermi}}(x_1,\dots,x_N) =\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}} \begin{vmatrix} \phi_{i_1}(x_1) & \phi_{i_1}(x_2) & \cdots & \phi_{i_1}(x_N)\\ \phi_{i_2}(x_1) & \phi_{i_2}(x_2) & \cdots & \phi_{i_2}(x_N)\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ \phi_{i_N}(x_1) & \phi_{i_N}(x_2) & \cdots & \phi_{i_N}(x_N) \end{vmatrix} \label{eq:B-slater} \end{equation} $$

This is called a Slater determinant. Since a determinant changes sign when two columns are interchanged, the lower sign (fermions) of Eq. \eqref{eq:B-symmetry} is automatically satisfied. Moreover, if two rows are the same ($i_\alpha=i_\beta$, that is, the same one-particle state is used twice), the determinant vanishes. This is the Pauli exclusion principle.

Example: Writing out the case $N=2$ by hand

Placing one particle each in the one-particle states $a$ and $b$ gives $\Psi(x_1,x_2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left[\phi_a(x_1)\phi_b(x_2)-\phi_a(x_2)\phi_b(x_1)\right]$. There are two things to note.

(i) The sign changes under the interchange $x_1\leftrightarrow x_2$. This is Fermi statistics.

(ii) But the sign also changes under the interchange $a\leftrightarrow b$. That is, $\Psi_{ab}=-\Psi_{ba}$. Even though we are specifying the physically identical situation, “state $a$ and state $b$ are each filled by one particle,” the sign differs depending on which one we write first. This will appear later as $\hat c^\dagger_a\hat c^\dagger_b=-\hat c^\dagger_b\hat c^\dagger_a$. The origin of the “troublesome signs of fermions” treated in Section B.2.4 lies here.

For bosons, all the signs are made $+$ (use the permanent instead of the determinant). Since the result is invariant under $a\leftrightarrow b$, no sign problem arises for bosons.

B.1.4 A change of viewpoint: we only need to state the occupation numbers

Let us stop here and look again at what Eq. \eqref{eq:B-slater} contains. It has $N!$ terms, and each of them records an assignment of “which particle is in which state.” But electrons have no name tags, so that assignment is meaningless from the start. The $N!$ terms merely line up meaningless information mechanically.

Then what is the meaningful information? It is only how many particles fill each one-particle state. If we number the one-particle states $i=1,2,3,\dots$ and write the occupation number of state $i$ as $n_i$, the state of the system is

$$ \begin{equation} \ket{n_1,n_2,n_3,\dots}, \qquad \sum_i n_i = N \label{eq:B-occ} \end{equation} $$

and can be specified by nothing more than this sequence of numbers. For bosons $n_i=0,1,2,\dots$; for fermions $n_i=0$ or $1$ only (the Pauli principle). This is called the occupation-number representation (also called the number representation or the Fock representation).

Why is this called “second quantization”?

It is a matter of history. The transition from classical mechanics to quantum mechanics (turning $x,p$ into operators) is called “first quantization,” whereas the transition from a classical field (regarding the Schrödinger wave function $\psi(\xx)$ as a classical field) to quantum field theory was called “second quantization.” Indeed, the field operator $\hat\psi(\xx)$ that we are about to construct has the form of “the wave function promoted to an operator.”

Note, however, that physically we do not quantize twice. Eq. \eqref{eq:B-schr-N} and the formalism we are about to build are completely equivalent, and no new physics enters. Do not be misled by the name. Calling it the “occupation-number representation” is closer to the substance.

What this rewriting buys us (three points)

  1. We can treat problems in which the particle number changes. Problems in which the particle number is not conserved or not fixed, such as the creation and annihilation of photons, the creation of electron–hole pairs, and superconductivity, can be written naturally. For this book this is decisive. The “particle–hole excitation” treated in Chapter 10 is the operation of annihilating one electron inside the Fermi sphere and creating one outside, so it is precisely the product $\hat c^\dagger_{\kk+\qq}\hat c_\kk$ itself.
  2. Statistics become automatic. The antisymmetry of Fermi statistics is compressed into the single line $\{\hat c_i,\hat c_j\}=0$, and from then on we need only turn the crank of operator algebra mechanically. We never need to expand a Slater determinant again.
  3. Interactions can be seen as “scattering.” A two-body operator takes the form $\hat c^\dagger\hat c^\dagger\hat c\hat c$, which can be read as “annihilate two and create two” = a scattering process (Section B.6, Figure B.2). A Feynman diagram is a picture of this reading.

B.2 Creation and Annihilation Operators

B.2.1 The starting point is the harmonic oscillator

Starting directly with a many-body system would feel too abrupt, so we begin with the one-dimensional harmonic oscillator, which you should already know. It is the very oscillator that we treated at such length in Chapters 2 and 3 of this book.

The Hamiltonian is $\Ham=\dfrac{\hat p^2}{2m}+\dfrac12 m\omega_0^2\hat x^2$. Here we define the operators

$$ \begin{equation} \hat a \equiv \sqrt{\frac{m\omega_0}{2\hbar}}\left(\hat x+\frac{\ii\hat p}{m\omega_0}\right), \qquad \hat a^\dagger \equiv \sqrt{\frac{m\omega_0}{2\hbar}}\left(\hat x-\frac{\ii\hat p}{m\omega_0}\right) \label{eq:B-ho-a} \end{equation} $$

The canonical commutation relation $[\hat x,\hat p]=\ii\hbar$ implies the following.

Derivation 1: $[\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=1$

Abbreviate $\lambda\equiv\sqrt{m\omega_0/2\hbar}$ and $\mu\equiv1/(m\omega_0)$; then $\hat a=\lambda(\hat x+\ii\mu\hat p)$ and $\hat a^\dagger=\lambda(\hat x-\ii\mu\hat p)$. Since the commutator is bilinear, we split it into four terms:

$$ [\hat a,\hat a^\dagger] =\lambda^2\Big( \underbrace{[\hat x,\hat x]}_{=0} -\ii\mu[\hat x,\hat p] +\ii\mu[\hat p,\hat x] +\underbrace{\mu^2[\hat p,\hat p]}_{=0} \Big) =\lambda^2\left(\mu\hbar+\mu\hbar\right) =\frac{m\omega_0}{2\hbar}\cdot\frac{2\hbar}{m\omega_0}=1 $$

∎ The second term is $-\ii\mu\cdot\ii\hbar=\mu\hbar$, and the third term is $\ii\mu\cdot(-\ii\hbar)=\mu\hbar$. With this, the Hamiltonian can be written as $\Ham=\hbar\omega_0\left(\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\frac12\right)$.

And, as you learned in quantum mechanics, the eigenvalues of $\hat n\equiv\hat a^\dagger\hat a$ are restricted to $0,1,2,\dots$; $\hat a^\dagger$ raises the energy by one step, and $\hat a$ lowers it by one step. Here, changing our viewpoint is the entrance to second quantization.

A change of viewpoint: from “the $n$th level” to “$n$ quanta”

We reinterpret the $n$th excited state $\ket{n}$ of the harmonic oscillator as “the state in which there are $n$ quanta (particles) of energy $\hbar\omega_0$.” Then $\hat a^\dagger$ becomes an operator that creates one quantum, and $\hat a$ becomes an operator that annihilates one. $\hat n$ is the operator that counts the number.

This reinterpretation is not mere wordplay. In fact, when the lattice vibrations are decomposed into normal modes, each normal mode becomes an independent harmonic oscillator, and its quantum is the phonon. When the electromagnetic field is decomposed into normal modes, its quantum is the photon. The “excitation number of a harmonic oscillator” and the “particle number” are the same thing, and this is the true nature of bosons.

In this sense, the classical damped oscillator treated in Chapters 2 and 3 was a “model of one mode of boson quanta.” (This does not mean that “there is one kind of particle”; it means that “there is one independent vibrational mode.” If there are many modes, as for phonons or photons, we simply line up as many oscillators. We do this in the next section.)

B.2.2 Bose particles: commutation relations and their action

When there are many one-particle states $i=1,2,3,\dots$, it suffices to prepare one independent harmonic oscillator for each state.

Definition: Creation and annihilation operators for bosons

$$ \begin{equation} [\hat a_i,\hat a_j^\dagger]=\delta_{ij}, \qquad [\hat a_i,\hat a_j]=0, \qquad [\hat a_i^\dagger,\hat a_j^\dagger]=0 \label{eq:B-bose-comm} \end{equation} $$

Here $[\hat A,\hat B]\equiv\hat A\hat B-\hat B\hat A$. We write the vacuum (the state with no particles at all) as $\ket{0}$, and require $\hat a_i\ket{0}=0$ for every $i$. The occupation-number states are built by acting on the vacuum with creation operators:

$$ \begin{equation} \ket{n_1,n_2,\dots} =\prod_i\frac{(\hat a_i^\dagger)^{n_i}}{\sqrt{n_i!}}\ket{0} \label{eq:B-bose-state} \end{equation} $$

Since the $\hat a_i^\dagger$ commute with one another, we obtain the same state no matter in which order we multiply them. For bosons we need not worry about the order.

Derivation 2: $\hat a^\dagger\ket{n}=\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1}$, $\hat a\ket{n}=\sqrt{n}\ket{n-1}$

We consider only one state, so we drop the index. From Eq. \eqref{eq:B-bose-state} we have $\ket{n}=(\hat a^\dagger)^n\ket{0}/\sqrt{n!}$, so

$$ \hat a^\dagger\ket{n}=\frac{(\hat a^\dagger)^{n+1}}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{0} =\sqrt{\frac{(n+1)!}{n!}}\cdot\frac{(\hat a^\dagger)^{n+1}}{\sqrt{(n+1)!}}\ket{0} =\sqrt{n+1}\,\ket{n+1} $$

For $\hat a\ket{n}$ we use the formula $[\hat a,(\hat a^\dagger)^n]=n(\hat a^\dagger)^{n-1}$ (which can be shown by induction using $[\hat A,\hat B\hat C]=[\hat A,\hat B]\hat C+\hat B[\hat A,\hat C]$). Noting that $\hat a\ket0=0$, we obtain

$$ \hat a\ket{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\left([\hat a,(\hat a^\dagger)^n]+(\hat a^\dagger)^n\hat a\right)\ket{0} =\frac{n(\hat a^\dagger)^{n-1}}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{0} =\frac{n}{\sqrt n}\ket{n-1}=\sqrt{n}\,\ket{n-1} $$

∎ In particular, $\hat a\ket{0}=0$ (the coefficient vanishes at $n=0$) comes out automatically. This means that a state with a “negative number” of particles cannot be made.

Note also that the square of the coefficient of $\hat a^\dagger$, namely $n+1$, expresses the property of Bose statistics that “the more companions there are, the easier it is to enter,” and it is the origin of stimulated emission (the $n$ part) and spontaneous emission (the $+1$ part) in lasers.

B.2.3 Fermi particles: anticommutation relations

For fermions we must realize the lower sign in Eq. \eqref{eq:B-symmetry}. As we saw in the example in Section B.1.3, a two-particle state satisfies $\Psi_{ab}=-\Psi_{ba}$. In the language of creation operators, this becomes

$$ \hat c_a^\dagger\hat c_b^\dagger\ket{0}=-\,\hat c_b^\dagger\hat c_a^\dagger\ket{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat c_a^\dagger\hat c_b^\dagger+\hat c_b^\dagger\hat c_a^\dagger=0 $$

In other words, it is not the commutator but the anticommutator that vanishes. We take this as our starting point.

Definition: Creation and annihilation operators for fermions

$$ \begin{equation} \{\hat c_i,\hat c_j^\dagger\}=\delta_{ij}, \qquad \{\hat c_i,\hat c_j\}=0, \qquad \{\hat c_i^\dagger,\hat c_j^\dagger\}=0 \label{eq:B-fermi-anticomm} \end{equation} $$

Here $\{\hat A,\hat B\}\equiv\hat A\hat B+\hat B\hat A$ (the anticommutator). The vacuum is defined by $\hat c_i\ket{0}=0$. The occupation-number states are defined by

$$ \begin{equation} \ket{n_1,n_2,\dots} =(\hat c_1^\dagger)^{n_1}(\hat c_2^\dagger)^{n_2}\cdots\ket{0}, \qquad n_i\in\{0,1\} \label{eq:B-fermi-state} \end{equation} $$

The essential point is to fix the order of multiplication in advance as “from the left, in order of increasing index.” Unlike for bosons, changing the order changes the sign, so a convention is needed.

Derivation 3: The Pauli principle follows automatically as $(\hat c_i^\dagger)^2=0$

Setting $i=j$ in the third relation of Eq. \eqref{eq:B-fermi-anticomm}, we get

$$ \{\hat c_i^\dagger,\hat c_i^\dagger\}=\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i^\dagger+\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i^\dagger=2(\hat c_i^\dagger)^2=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (\hat c_i^\dagger)^2=0 $$

That is, if we try to create a second particle in the same one-particle state, the state itself becomes zero. This is the Pauli exclusion principle. Similarly $(\hat c_i)^2=0$, which means that “what is not there cannot be annihilated.”

∎ What deserves attention is that we have not assumed the Pauli principle separately. It came out by itself when we translated the antisymmetry, Eq. \eqref{eq:B-symmetry}, into the language of operators. The same content as saying “if two rows are the same, the determinant vanishes” for the Slater determinant has become a far shorter single line. This is the power of second quantization.

B.2.4 Signs for Fermi particles — the biggest hurdle

For bosons, it sufficed to act with $\hat a_i^\dagger$ on an occupation-number state and simply set $n_i\to n_i+1$. For fermions this does not work. If we insert $\hat c_i^\dagger$ from the left end of Eq. \eqref{eq:B-fermi-state}, then on the way to the $i$th position it must pass through the other $\hat c_j^\dagger$, and each time the sign is multiplied by $-1$.

Definition: The action of fermion operators and the phase factor

$$ \begin{equation} \hat c_i^\dagger\ket{n_1,\dots,n_i,\dots} =(-1)^{S_i}\,(1-n_i)\,\ket{n_1,\dots,n_i+1,\dots} \label{eq:B-fermi-cdag} \end{equation} $$ $$ \begin{equation} \hat c_i\ket{n_1,\dots,n_i,\dots} =(-1)^{S_i}\,n_i\,\ket{n_1,\dots,n_i-1,\dots}, \qquad S_i \equiv \sum_{j<i} n_j \label{eq:B-fermi-c} \end{equation} $$

$S_i$ is the total number of particles located before state $i$ (on the side of smaller indices). The factor $(1-n_i)$ expresses “zero if already filled” (Pauli), and $n_i$ expresses “cannot be annihilated if empty.”

Where does $(-1)^{S_i}$ come from? — The number of operators passed through

A concrete example is the quickest. Act with $\hat c_3^\dagger$ on the state $\ket{1,1,0,1}=\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_2^\dagger\hat c_4^\dagger\ket{0}$. To bring it into the form that fits the convention (from the left, in order of increasing index), we need to move $\hat c_3^\dagger$ to the right of $\hat c_2^\dagger$ and to the left of $\hat c_4^\dagger$:

$$ \hat c_3^\dagger\,\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_2^\dagger\hat c_4^\dagger\ket{0} \;=\;(-1)\,\hat c_1^\dagger\,\hat c_3^\dagger\,\hat c_2^\dagger\hat c_4^\dagger\ket{0} \;=\;(-1)^2\,\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_2^\dagger\,\hat c_3^\dagger\,\hat c_4^\dagger\ket{0} $$

We have passed through $\hat c_1^\dagger$ once and $\hat c_2^\dagger$ once, twice in total, so $(-1)^2=+1$. In general, the number of passes is “the number of particles located before $i$,” $S_i=\sum_{j<i}n_j$. In this case $S_3=n_1+n_2=1+1=2$, so $(-1)^2=+1$. This agrees with Eq. \eqref{eq:B-fermi-cdag}.

How to remember it: “the sign flips each time we overtake one companion on the left.” That is all.

Derivation 4: Checking that the sign convention in Eqs. \eqref{eq:B-fermi-cdag} and \eqref{eq:B-fermi-c} reproduces the anticommutation relations

Having given the definition, we must check that it really satisfies Eq. \eqref{eq:B-fermi-anticomm}. Many textbooks skip this, so let us do it carefully.

Step 1: $\{\hat c_i,\hat c_j^\dagger\}=0$ for $i\ne j$. Without loss of generality let $i<j$, and compare the results of acting on the occupation-number state $\ket{n}$ in both orders. First consider the order in which $\hat c_j^\dagger$ acts first (from the right) and then $\hat c_i$. Since $i<j$, $S_i=\sum_{l<i}n_l$ does not change even when the $j$th occupation number changes. Hence

$$ \hat c_i\hat c_j^\dagger\ket{n} =(-1)^{S_i+S_j}\,n_i(1-n_j)\,\ket{\dots,n_i-1,\dots,n_j+1,\dots}, \qquad S_j=\sum_{l<j}n_l $$

In the reverse order, $\hat c_i$ acts first, so that $n_i'=n_i-1$. Since $i<j$, $n_i$ is included in the sum $S_j$, so the exponent of the phase factor of $\hat c_j^\dagger$, which acts afterward, changes to $S_j'=S_j-1$. This is the key point. Therefore

$$ \hat c_j^\dagger\hat c_i\ket{n} =(-1)^{S_i}(-1)^{S_j-1}n_i(1-n_j)\ket{\dots,n_i-1,\dots,n_j+1,\dots} =-\,\hat c_i\hat c_j^\dagger\ket{n} $$

Adding the two, we get $\left(\hat c_i\hat c_j^\dagger+\hat c_j^\dagger\hat c_i\right)\ket{n}=0$ ($i\ne j$) $\;\checkmark$

Step 2: $\{\hat c_i,\hat c_i^\dagger\}=1$ for $i=j$. A change in the $i$th occupation number does not affect $S_i$, so the phase factor is always multiplied twice, giving $(-1)^{2S_i}=1$. All that remains are the factors $(1-n_i)$ and $n_i$.

$$ \hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i\ket{n}=n_i\ket{n}, \qquad \hat c_i\hat c_i^\dagger\ket{n}=(1-n_i)(n_i+1)\ket{n}=(1-n_i)\ket{n} $$

In the second expression we used $n_i\in\{0,1\}$, that is, $n_i^2=n_i$, to write $(1-n_i)(n_i+1)=1-n_i^2=1-n_i$. Adding, we get $n_i+(1-n_i)=1$, that is, $\{\hat c_i,\hat c_i^\dagger\}\ket{n}=\ket{n}\;\checkmark$

∎ The definition in Eqs. \eqref{eq:B-fermi-cdag} and \eqref{eq:B-fermi-c}, which contains the phase factor $(-1)^{S_i}$, is perfectly consistent with the anticommutation relations \eqref{eq:B-fermi-anticomm}. Incidentally, the important result $\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i\ket{n}=n_i\ket{n}$ (the number operator) emerges as a by-product.

Three rules of thumb for not agonizing over signs in actual calculations

  1. Treat the pair $\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_j$ as a unit. Since this pair is made of an “even number” of fermion operators, it can be handled with a commutator (not an anticommutator) with another pair $\hat c_k^\dagger\hat c_l$. The density operator $\hat\rho_\qq=\sum_\kk\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_{\kk+\qq}$ that appears in Chapters 9–11 has this form, so the sign nightmare does not occur. We will confirm this explicitly in Section B.7.
  2. The final observables are made of an even number of fermion operators. The factors $(-1)^{S_i}$ that appear along the way always appear in pairs and cancel. (This holds for observables that do not change the particle number, such as the density, current, and spin treated in this book. Quantities containing an odd number of fermion operators, like the one-particle Green's function of Chapter 13, are also important in theory, and they obey fermionic statistics.)
  3. Inside $\braket{\cdots}$, the result is automatically zero unless the numbers of $\hat c$ and $\hat c^\dagger$ match. In a system where the particle number is conserved, an expectation value vanishes unless creation and annihilation operators are equal in number. Before calculating, we can use this to sift out most of the terms.

Correspondence with Chapter 3 — watch the sign convention

As we saw in Section B.2.1, the position operator of the harmonic oscillator can be written as

$$ \hat x=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_0}}\left(\hat a+\hat a^\dagger\right) $$

In Chapter 3, we treated classically the response of this oscillator to an applied external force $F(t)$ and obtained the mechanical susceptibility

$$ \chi_{\text{mech}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{m\left[\omega_0^2-\omega^2\right]} \qquad(\text{damping }\gamma\to0) $$

Note that in Chapter 3 the external force entered with a minus sign, as $\Ham'=-\hat x F$.

On the other hand, from Chapter 7 onward in this book (and in Chapters 8–13), following the source text Fetter–Walecka, we write the external perturbation with a plus sign: $\Ham_{\text{ext}}(t)=f(t)\hat B\,\ee^{\eta t}$. For $\hat B=\hat x$, since $-\hat xF=+\hat x\,(-F)$, we have $f=-F$. Therefore the retarded response function is

$$ \chi^R_{xx}(\omega)=-\frac{1}{m\left[\omega_0^2-(\omega+\ii\eta)^2\right]} $$

and it has the opposite sign to $\chi_{\text{mech}}$ of Chapter 3. The physics, however, is the same:

$$ \delta\braket{\hat x}=\chi^R_{xx}\,f=\chi^R_{xx}\cdot(-F) =+\frac{F}{m\left(\omega_0^2-\omega^2\right)} $$

so we obtain exactly the same answer as in Chapter 3. This is not a contradiction but a “difference in how the coupling is written.” Very many students compare only the symbol $\chi$ with other books and worry that the signs do not match, so be sure to trace the comparison back to “how the perturbation Hamiltonian was written.”

For the same reason, for the density response we write $\Ham_{\text{ext}}=\int\dd^3x\,\phi_{\text{ext}}(\xx,t)\hat\rho(\xx)$ (that is, $f=\phi_{\text{ext}}$), and in this convention $\chi^0(\qq\to0,0)=-N(\epsilon_F)<0$ and $\epsilon(\qq,\omega)=1-v(q)\chi^0(\qq,\omega)$ (Chapters 9 and 10).

B.3 The Number Operator and the Total Particle Number

B.3.1 The number operator

In Step 2 of Derivation 4, we found $\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i\ket{n}=n_i\ket{n}$ for fermions. The same holds for bosons.

Definition: The number operator

$$ \begin{equation} \hat n_i \equiv \hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_i \qquad\text{(for fermions, }\hat n_i=\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i\text{)} \label{eq:B-numop} \end{equation} $$

This is the operator that counts the occupation number of state $i$, and every occupation-number state is necessarily an eigenstate of it:

$$ \begin{equation} \hat n_i\ket{n_1,n_2,\dots}=n_i\ket{n_1,n_2,\dots} \label{eq:B-numop-eig} \end{equation} $$

Derivation 5: The eigenvalues are restricted to nonnegative integers (to $0,1$ for fermions)

(i) The eigenvalue is $n$. Using Derivation 2 in succession, we have $\hat n\ket n=\hat a^\dagger\left(\sqrt n\ket{n-1}\right)=\sqrt n\cdot\sqrt n\ket n=n\ket n$.

(ii) It is nonnegative. Let $\ket\nu$ be an eigenstate (with eigenvalue $\nu$, normalized). Then

$$ \nu=\bra{\nu}\hat a^\dagger\hat a\ket{\nu}=\left\|\hat a\ket{\nu}\right\|^2\ge0 $$

Since it is the square of a norm, it cannot be negative.

(iii) It is an integer. From $[\hat n,\hat a]=\hat a^\dagger[\hat a,\hat a]+[\hat a^\dagger,\hat a]\hat a=-\hat a$ we obtain $\hat n\left(\hat a\ket\nu\right)=(\nu-1)\hat a\ket\nu$; that is, $\hat a$ lowers the eigenvalue by one. If $\nu$ were not an integer, repeated multiplication by $\hat a$ would produce arbitrarily negative eigenvalues, contradicting (ii). The only way to avoid the contradiction is for the ladder to stop after a finite number of steps with $\hat a\ket{\nu}=0$, and for this to happen $\nu$ must be a nonnegative integer.

∎ For fermions, by Step 2 of Derivation 4, $\hat n_i^2=\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i=\hat c_i^\dagger(1-\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i)\hat c_i=\hat n_i$ (we used $\hat c_i^2=0$). An operator that satisfies $\hat n^2=\hat n$ has only $0$ or $1$ as its eigenvalues. This, too, is the Pauli principle.

B.3.2 The total particle-number operator

$$ \begin{equation} \hat N \equiv \sum_i \hat n_i = \sum_i \hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_i, \qquad \hat N\ket{n_1,n_2,\dots}=\left(\sum_i n_i\right)\ket{n_1,n_2,\dots} \label{eq:B-Nop} \end{equation} $$

Since we add up the occupation numbers over all one-particle states, this is the operator that counts the total particle number.

Fock space — states with indefinite particle number are allowed too

In first quantization we fix $N$ first and then build the $N$-particle Hilbert space $\mathcal H_N$. In second quantization we work on the space $\mathcal F=\mathcal H_0\oplus\mathcal H_1\oplus\mathcal H_2\oplus\cdots$ (the Fock space), the direct sum of all the spaces with different particle numbers. $\mathcal H_0$ is the one-dimensional space consisting of the single vacuum $\ket0$ alone.

This breadth matters when we want to write a superposition of components with different particle numbers, as for the BCS state in superconductivity, and when we treat the grand canonical ensemble ($\Ham-\mu\hat N$) in the Matsubara formalism of Chapter 13. For a system in which the particle number is conserved, $[\hat N,\Ham]=0$, so the space splits according to the eigenvalues of $\hat N$, and we are in effect saying the same thing as in first quantization.

B.3.3 The most frequently used commutator formulas

Here we collect and prove the formulas that will be used many times in the following sections. If you remember these, the calculations in Chapters 9–11 proceed almost mechanically.

First, one notational convention. At the beginning we distinguished $\hat a$ for bosons and $\hat c$ for fermions, but from now on, in general statements that do not specify the statistics, we use $\hat a$ as a symbol common to both statistics. We use $\hat c$ only when we want to make explicit which statistics is meant.

Lemma B.1 (Commutators of products of the type $\hat a^\dagger\hat a$)

For both bosons and fermions,

$$ \begin{equation} \left[\hat a_a^\dagger\hat a_b,\;\hat a_c^\dagger\hat a_d\right] =\delta_{bc}\,\hat a_a^\dagger\hat a_d-\delta_{ad}\,\hat a_c^\dagger\hat a_b \label{eq:B-lemma1} \end{equation} $$

In particular, setting $c=d$ gives $\left[\hat a_a^\dagger\hat a_b,\hat n_c\right]=(\delta_{bc}-\delta_{ac})\hat a_a^\dagger\hat a_b$, and setting $b=a$, $d=c$ gives $[\hat n_a,\hat n_c]=0$.

Derivation 6: Proof of Lemma B.1 (the fermion case; the boson case is similar)

First we prepare the operator identity

$$ \begin{equation} [\hat A\hat B,\hat C]=\hat A\{\hat B,\hat C\}-\{\hat A,\hat C\}\hat B \label{eq:B-identity} \end{equation} $$

Expanding the right-hand side gives $\hat A\hat B\hat C+\hat A\hat C\hat B-\hat A\hat C\hat B-\hat C\hat A\hat B=[\hat A\hat B,\hat C]$, so the identity indeed holds. The key point is that “two anticommutators can express one commutator,” and this makes calculations with fermion operators much easier at a stroke.

Steps 1 and 2: Commutators with $\hat c_c^\dagger$ and $\hat c_d$. In Eq. \eqref{eq:B-identity}, set $\hat A=\hat c_a^\dagger$ and $\hat B=\hat c_b$, and take $\hat C$ to be $\hat c_c^\dagger$ and $\hat c_d$, respectively. Then

$$ [\hat c_a^\dagger\hat c_b,\hat c_c^\dagger] =\hat c_a^\dagger\underbrace{\{\hat c_b,\hat c_c^\dagger\}}_{\delta_{bc}} -\underbrace{\{\hat c_a^\dagger,\hat c_c^\dagger\}}_{0}\hat c_b =\delta_{bc}\hat c_a^\dagger, \qquad [\hat c_a^\dagger\hat c_b,\hat c_d] =-\underbrace{\{\hat c_a^\dagger,\hat c_d\}}_{\delta_{ad}}\hat c_b =-\delta_{ad}\hat c_b $$

Step 3: Combining them with the Leibniz rule. Since $\hat X=\hat c_a^\dagger\hat c_b$ is made of an even number of fermion operators, the ordinary Leibniz rule $[\hat X,\hat Y\hat Z]=[\hat X,\hat Y]\hat Z+\hat Y[\hat X,\hat Z]$ can be used as it is. With $\hat Y=\hat c_c^\dagger$ and $\hat Z=\hat c_d$,

$$ [\hat c_a^\dagger\hat c_b,\hat c_c^\dagger\hat c_d] =\underbrace{[\hat c_a^\dagger\hat c_b,\hat c_c^\dagger]}_{\delta_{bc}\hat c_a^\dagger}\hat c_d +\hat c_c^\dagger\underbrace{[\hat c_a^\dagger\hat c_b,\hat c_d]}_{-\delta_{ad}\hat c_b} =\delta_{bc}\hat c_a^\dagger\hat c_d-\delta_{ad}\hat c_c^\dagger\hat c_b $$

∎ For bosons, read every $\{\;,\;\}$ as $[\;,\;]$ and use the identity $[\hat A\hat B,\hat C]=\hat A[\hat B,\hat C]+[\hat A,\hat C]\hat B$; the same result follows. The convenient feature of this lemma is that the difference in statistics does not appear in the final result.

B.4 The Field Operator $\hat\psi(\xx)$

B.4.1 Changing the basis

So far we have written abstractly “the one-particle state $i$,” but $i$ may be anything: an energy eigenstate, a plane wave, or a position eigenstate. We now fix how the creation and annihilation operators change when the basis is changed.

Derivation 7: The basis-change rule

Two complete orthonormal bases $\{\ket{i}\}$ and $\{\ket{\alpha}\}$ are related by $\ket{\alpha}=\sum_i\ket{i}\braket{i|\alpha}$ (we merely inserted $\sum_i\ket i\bra i=1$). Since $\hat a_\alpha^\dagger$ is the operator that “creates $\ket\alpha$ when it acts on the vacuum,” it suffices to read the right-hand side of this relation as an operator and set

$$ \begin{equation} \hat a_\alpha^\dagger=\sum_i \braket{i|\alpha}\,\hat a_i^\dagger, \qquad \hat a_\alpha=\sum_i \braket{i|\alpha}^*\,\hat a_i=\sum_i\braket{\alpha|i}\,\hat a_i \label{eq:B-basis-change} \end{equation} $$

(the second is the Hermitian conjugate of the first). We also check that this transformation preserves the (anti)commutation relations. For fermions,

$$ \{\hat a_\alpha,\hat a_\beta^\dagger\} =\sum_{ij}\braket{\alpha|i}\braket{j|\beta}\underbrace{\{\hat a_i,\hat a_j^\dagger\}}_{\delta_{ij}} =\sum_i\braket{\alpha|i}\braket{i|\beta} =\braket{\alpha|\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta} $$

∎ Along the way we used the completeness relation $\sum_i\ket i\bra i=1$. However we change the basis, the form of the (anti)commutation relations does not change. This is one of the reasons why the formalism of second quantization is convenient.

Easily Misunderstood Point: It is the “coefficients,” not the “basis,” that became operators

In first quantization, we expanded the state of one particle in an orthonormal basis $\{\phi_j\}$ as

$$ \ket{\Psi}=\sum_j c_j\ket{\phi_j},\qquad c_j\in\mathbb{C} $$

Here $c_j$ is “the probability amplitude that the particle is in orbital $j$,” which is just a complex number. Yet when we move to second quantization, as we will see in the next subsection, we write

$$ \hat\psi(\xx)=\sum_j\phi_j(\xx)\,\hat a_j, \qquad \hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)=\sum_j\phi_j^*(\xx)\,\hat a_j^\dagger $$

Many people read this as saying that “the basis has become an operator.” That is not so.

  • $\phi_j(\xx)$ is a c-number basis function, just as before. Nothing has changed.
  • What became an operator is the place where $c_j$ used to sit—that is, the part that is “the coefficient that changes the occupation number of that orbital.” $\hat a_j$ decreases the number of particles in orbital $j$ by one, and $\hat a_j^\dagger$ increases it by one.

Because the symbols $c_j$ (a complex coefficient) and $\hat c_j$ (an operator on Fock space) look alike, this is all the more confusing. The presence or absence of the hat distinguishes a complex number from an operator.

So where did the complex coefficients go? They did not disappear; they merely moved in front of the Fock states:

$$ \ket{\Psi}=\sum_{n_1,n_2,\dots}C_{n_1n_2\dots}\ket{n_1,n_2,\dots},\qquad C_{n_1n_2\dots}\in\mathbb{C}. $$

In fact, if we return to the one-particle subspace, we have

$$ \Big(\sum_j c_j\,\hat a_j^\dagger\Big)\ket{0}=\sum_j c_j\ket{\phi_j} $$

which completely reproduces the first-quantized expansion. The claim of Section B.1 that second quantization is not new physics holds here as well.

Analogy: First quantization writes a superposition over “which room a single student is in.” Second quantization writes “how many people are in each room,” and $\hat a_j^\dagger,\hat a_j$ are the operations of putting a person into, or taking a person out of, room $j$. The rooms themselves ($\phi_j$) have not turned into operations.

B.4.2 Choosing the position basis gives the field operator

Now we choose as the basis $\alpha$ the position eigenstates $\ket{\xx}$. Since $\braket{\xx|i}=\phi_i(\xx)$, that is, $\braket{i|\xx}=\phi_i^*(\xx)$, Eq. \eqref{eq:B-basis-change} becomes

Definition: The field operator

$$ \begin{equation} \hat\psi(\xx)\equiv\sum_i \phi_i(\xx)\,\hat a_i, \qquad \hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\equiv\sum_i \phi_i^*(\xx)\,\hat a_i^\dagger \label{eq:B-field-op} \end{equation} $$

$\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)$ is the operator that creates one particle at position $\xx$, and $\hat\psi(\xx)$ is the operator that annihilates one particle at position $\xx$.

A note on the confusing symbol $\hat\psi$ (important)

The symbol $\hat\psi(\xx)$ looks just like the wave function $\psi(\xx)$ of quantum mechanics, but it is something entirely different.

  • Wave function $\psi(\xx)$: a complex number. A probability amplitude. $\abs{\psi}^2$ is the probability density.
  • Field operator $\hat\psi(\xx)$: an operator. It acts on a state of Fock space and reduces the number of particles at $\xx$ by one. Since it is not a number, it is not a “probability amplitude.”

To avoid confusion, this book always puts a hat on the field operator. Looking at Eq. \eqref{eq:B-field-op}, we see that the coefficients are the one-particle wave functions $\phi_i(\xx)$, and what they multiply are the operators $\hat a_i$. It suffices to read it as “an expansion of operators with the wave functions as coefficients.” Because this form looks as if “the wave function had been promoted to an operator,” the name “second quantization” came to be attached to it (see the sidebar in Section B.1.4).

Derivation 8: (Anti)commutation relations of the field operator

We substitute the definition \eqref{eq:B-field-op} and compute straightforwardly. For fermions,

$$ \{\hat\psi(\xx),\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx')\} =\sum_{ij}\phi_i(\xx)\phi_j^*(\xx')\,\underbrace{\{\hat c_i,\hat c_j^\dagger\}}_{=\delta_{ij}} =\sum_i\phi_i(\xx)\phi_i^*(\xx') $$

The last sum is itself the completeness relation of the one-particle basis:

$$ \sum_i\phi_i(\xx)\phi_i^*(\xx') =\sum_i\braket{\xx|i}\braket{i|\xx'} =\braket{\xx|\xx'}=\delta(\xx-\xx') $$

Therefore

$$ \begin{equation} \left[\hat\psi(\xx),\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx')\right]_\mp=\delta(\xx-\xx'), \qquad \left[\hat\psi(\xx),\hat\psi(\xx')\right]_\mp=0 \label{eq:B-field-comm} \end{equation} $$

(the upper sign is the commutator for bosons, the lower the anticommutator for fermions). ∎

How to read it: “Creating a particle at $\xx$ and then annihilating one at $\xx'$” and “annihilating the particle at $\xx'$ and then creating one at $\xx$” are the same thing unless $\xx=\xx'$. Only when $\xx=\xx'$ does the order matter, giving rise to the delta function. $\delta_{ij}$ has simply become $\delta(\xx-\xx')$, so in essence this is the same as the discrete-basis case.

B.4.3 The plane-wave basis — the form actually used in Chapters 9–12

When we treat the electron gas, we choose plane waves as the one-particle states. If we impose periodic boundary conditions on a cube of side $L$ (volume $V=L^3$), the allowed wave vectors are discretized as

$$ \begin{equation} \kk=\frac{2\pi}{L}\left(n_x,n_y,n_z\right), \qquad n_x,n_y,n_z\in\mathbb{Z} \label{eq:B-kgrid} \end{equation} $$

and the normalized one-particle wave functions are

$$ \begin{equation} \phi_{\kk}(\xx)=\frac{1}{\sqrt V}\,\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\xx}, \qquad \int_V\dd^3x\;\phi_\kk^*(\xx)\phi_{\kk'}(\xx) =\frac1V\int_V\dd^3x\,\ee^{\ii(\kk'-\kk)\cdot\xx}=\delta_{\kk\kk'} \label{eq:B-planewave} \end{equation} $$

Substituting these into Eq. \eqref{eq:B-field-op}, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \hat\psi_\sigma(\xx)=\frac{1}{\sqrt V}\sum_{\kk}\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\xx}\,\hat c_{\kk\sigma}, \qquad \hat\psi_\sigma^\dagger(\xx)=\frac{1}{\sqrt V}\sum_{\kk}\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\xx}\,\hat c_{\kk\sigma}^\dagger \label{eq:B-field-plane} \end{equation} $$

Here $\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow$ denotes the spin. Solving these in reverse, we find

$$ \begin{equation} \hat c_{\kk\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt V}\int_V\dd^3x\,\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\xx}\,\hat\psi_\sigma(\xx) \label{eq:B-c-from-psi} \end{equation} $$

(to see this, multiply both sides by $\ee^{-\ii\kk'\cdot\xx}$, integrate over $\xx$, and use Eq. \eqref{eq:B-planewave}). The field operators and $\hat c_\kk$ are related simply by a Fourier transform.

Converting sums to integrals (continuum limit)

Since the spacing of $\kk$ is $2\pi/L$, the number of states per unit volume of $\kk$-space is $(L/2\pi)^3=V/(2\pi)^3$. Therefore, in the limit $V\to\infty$, we make the replacements

$$ \begin{equation} \sum_{\kk}\;\longrightarrow\;V\int\frac{\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3}, \qquad \delta_{\kk\kk'}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{(2\pi)^3}{V}\delta(\kk-\kk') \label{eq:B-sum-to-int} \end{equation} $$

Including the spin sum, $\sum_{\kk\sigma}\to 2V\int\dd^3k/(2\pi)^3$ (the factor $2$ is the spin). The calculation of the Lindhard function in Chapter 10 begins by converting sums into integrals with this replacement. Checking every time that $V$ cancels in the end is the best way to avoid calculational mistakes.

B.5 One-Body Operators

B.5.1 What do we want to rewrite?

In first quantization, a “one-body operator” is an operator in which the same operator acts once on each particle, that is, an operator of the form

$$ \begin{equation} \hat T=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\hat t\left(\xx_\alpha,\pp_\alpha\right) \label{eq:B-onebody-1q} \end{equation} $$

Examples include the kinetic energy $\hat t=-\hbar^2\nabla^2/2m$, the external potential $\hat t=U(\xx)$, the momentum $\hat t=\pp$, and the density $\hat t=\delta(\xx-\hat{\bm r})$. “One-body” means that the operator does not tie particles to one another.

Theorem B.1 (Second-quantized representation of one-body operators)

On Fock space, Eq. \eqref{eq:B-onebody-1q} can be written as

$$ \begin{equation} \hat T=\sum_{ij}\bra{i}\hat t\ket{j}\;\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j, \qquad \bra{i}\hat t\ket{j}\equiv\int\dd^3x\;\phi_i^*(\xx)\,\hat t\,\phi_j(\xx) \label{eq:B-onebody-2q} \end{equation} $$

The form is the same for both Bose and Fermi particles.

How to Read This Equation

$\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j$ is the operation “remove one particle that was in state $j$ and create one in state $i$”; that is, a transition from $j$ to $i$. Its weight is the matrix element $\bra{i}\hat t\ket{j}$.

In other words, Eq. \eqref{eq:B-onebody-2q} says that “a one-body operator is the sum of all possible one-particle transitions $j\to i$, each weighted by its matrix element.” The first-quantized sum “over particles,” $\sum_\alpha$, has been replaced by a sum “over transitions,” $\sum_{ij}$. Since particles carry no name tags, the latter is the more natural way of counting.

Derivation 9: Proof of Theorem B.1

The backbone has two stages. (a) We check that the operator agrees with first quantization on one-particle states. (b) We use commutators to show the structure “even when there are more particles, the operator acts once on each particle.”

(a) Action on a one-particle state. Apply Eq. \eqref{eq:B-onebody-2q} to $\ket{j}=\hat a_j^\dagger\ket0$, and use $\hat a_l\hat a_j^\dagger=\delta_{lj}\pm\hat a_j^\dagger\hat a_l$ (upper sign for bosons, lower sign for fermions) together with $\hat a_l\ket0=0$. The second term vanishes, and we obtain

$$ \hat T\hat a_j^\dagger\ket0 =\sum_{kl}\bra{k}\hat t\ket{l}\hat a_k^\dagger\hat a_l\hat a_j^\dagger\ket0 =\sum_{k}\ket{k}\bra{k}\hat t\ket{j}=\hat t\ket{j} $$

(The last step uses completeness, $\sum_k\ket k\bra k=1$.) For a single particle, the operator acts exactly as in first quantization.

(b) Action on a many-particle state. The key is the following commutator. By the identity \eqref{eq:B-identity}, $[\hat a_k^\dagger\hat a_l,\hat a_j^\dagger]=\hat a_k^\dagger\{\hat a_l,\hat a_j^\dagger\}-\{\hat a_k^\dagger,\hat a_j^\dagger\}\hat a_l=\delta_{lj}\hat a_k^\dagger$, so multiplying by $\bra{k}\hat t\ket{l}$ and summing over $k,l$ gives

$$ \begin{equation} \left[\hat T,\hat a_j^\dagger\right]=\sum_i\bra{i}\hat t\ket{j}\,\hat a_i^\dagger \label{eq:B-T-comm} \end{equation} $$

(The same holds for bosons.) Act with $\hat T$ on an $N$-particle state $\ket{\Phi}=\hat a_{j_1}^\dagger\cdots\hat a_{j_N}^\dagger\ket0$ and pass $\hat T$ through to the right. Each time we pass through an operator, the “parting gift” of Eq. \eqref{eq:B-T-comm} is left behind. Since $\hat T\ket0=0$, the last term vanishes, and

$$ \hat T\ket{\Phi} =\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\hat a_{j_1}^\dagger\cdots\left[\hat T,\hat a_{j_\alpha}^\dagger\right]\cdots\hat a_{j_N}^\dagger\ket0 =\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\sum_i\bra{i}\hat t\ket{j_\alpha}\; \hat a_{j_1}^\dagger\cdots \hat a_i^\dagger\cdots\hat a_{j_N}^\dagger\ket0 $$

∎ This is the sum over $\alpha$ of the terms in which “only the $\alpha$th particle is kicked by $\hat t$ and the others are left as they are”, which is exactly the action of the first-quantized $\sum_\alpha\hat t(\xx_\alpha)$. Note also that the statistics are absorbed in the computation of $[\hat T,\hat a_j^\dagger]$ and do not appear in the result.

B.5.2 Writing it with field operators

Substitute the definition of the matrix element into Eq. \eqref{eq:B-onebody-2q} and regroup the sums into field operators.

Derivation 10: $\hat T=\int\dd^3x\,\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\,\hat t\,\hat\psi(\xx)$

Substituting the definition of the matrix element, Eq. \eqref{eq:B-onebody-2q}, we obtain

$$ \hat T=\sum_{ij}\left[\int\dd^3x\,\phi_i^*(\xx)\,\hat t\,\phi_j(\xx)\right]\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j $$

Interchange the order of the integral and the sums, and collect the sum over $i$ and the sum over $j$ separately into field operators. By Eq. \eqref{eq:B-field-op}, $\sum_i\phi_i^*(\xx)\hat a_i^\dagger=\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)$. In the sum $\sum_j\phi_j(\xx)\hat a_j$, the operator $\hat a_j$ multiplies the result after $\hat t$ has acted on $\phi_j$, so $\hat t$ can be taken outside: $\hat t\sum_j\phi_j(\xx)\hat a_j=\hat t\,\hat\psi(\xx)$. Hence

$$ \begin{equation} \hat T=\int\dd^3x\;\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\,\hat t(\xx,-\ii\hbar\nabla)\,\hat\psi(\xx) \label{eq:B-onebody-field} \end{equation} $$

∎ In particular, for the kinetic energy we have

$$ \begin{equation} \hat T=\int\dd^3x\;\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\left(-\frac{\hbar^2\nabla^2}{2m}\right)\hat\psi(\xx) =\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int\dd^3x\;\nabla\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\cdot\nabla\hat\psi(\xx) \label{eq:B-kinetic-field} \end{equation} $$

The second equality is by integration by parts. With periodic boundary conditions (or $\hat\psi\to0$ at infinity), the boundary term drops out. The latter form is preferred because its Hermiticity is manifest.

On the appearance of Eq. \eqref{eq:B-onebody-field}

Eq. \eqref{eq:B-onebody-field} looks just like the expression $\braket{\hat t}=\int\dd^3x\,\psi^*\hat t\psi$ used to compute an expectation value in quantum mechanics. The only difference is that $\psi^*,\psi$ have become the operators $\hat\psi^\dagger,\hat\psi$. This agreement is no accident but the very design of second quantization: if you remember the formulas of one-particle quantum mechanics, you can write the operators of a many-body system just by replacing $\psi\to\hat\psi$—the most welcome property in practice.

B.5.3 The kinetic energy in plane waves

Derivation 11: $\hat T=\sum_{\kk\sigma}\epsilon_\kk\,\hat n_{\kk\sigma}$

Substitute Eq. \eqref{eq:B-field-plane} into Eq. \eqref{eq:B-kinetic-field}. We keep the spin $\sigma$ explicit for now:

$$ \hat T=\sum_\sigma\int_V\dd^3x\; \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt V}\sum_{\kk}\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\xx}\hat c_{\kk\sigma}^\dagger\right) \left(-\frac{\hbar^2\nabla^2}{2m}\right) \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt V}\sum_{\kk'}\ee^{\ii\kk'\cdot\xx}\hat c_{\kk'\sigma}\right) $$

Step 1: Carrying out the differentiation. Since $\nabla^2\ee^{\ii\kk'\cdot\xx}=(\ii\kk')\cdot(\ii\kk')\ee^{\ii\kk'\cdot\xx}=-k'^2\ee^{\ii\kk'\cdot\xx}$, we have

$$ \left(-\frac{\hbar^2\nabla^2}{2m}\right)\ee^{\ii\kk'\cdot\xx} =\frac{\hbar^2k'^2}{2m}\,\ee^{\ii\kk'\cdot\xx} \equiv\epsilon_{\kk'}\,\ee^{\ii\kk'\cdot\xx} $$

Step 2: Carrying out the spatial integral. What remains is

$$ \hat T=\sum_\sigma\sum_{\kk\kk'}\epsilon_{\kk'}\,\hat c_{\kk\sigma}^\dagger\hat c_{\kk'\sigma} \cdot\frac1V\int_V\dd^3x\;\ee^{\ii(\kk'-\kk)\cdot\xx} $$

By Eq. \eqref{eq:B-planewave}, the last integral equals $\delta_{\kk\kk'}$.

Step 3: Collapsing the sum over $\kk'$.

$$ \begin{equation} \hat T=\sum_{\kk\sigma}\epsilon_\kk\,\hat c_{\kk\sigma}^\dagger\hat c_{\kk\sigma} =\sum_{\kk\sigma}\epsilon_\kk\,\hat n_{\kk\sigma}, \qquad \epsilon_\kk=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} \label{eq:B-kinetic-plane} \end{equation} $$

∎ We have arrived at the obvious formula “multiply the number of electrons in each $\kk$ by the energy of that $\kk$, and sum.” Obvious as it is, this is the $\Ham_0$ of the free-electron gas, and the starting point of Chapters 9–11.

Since $\Ham_0$ of this form obviously commutes with $\hat n_{\kk\sigma}$, $\ket{n_{\kk\sigma}}$ is an eigenstate of $\Ham_0$. When we say that the free-electron gas is “solved,” we mean this one line.

The same calculation also gives the total momentum. For $\hat t=\pp=-\ii\hbar\nabla$, since $-\ii\hbar\nabla\ee^{\ii\kk'\cdot\xx}=\hbar\kk'\ee^{\ii\kk'\cdot\xx}$, the only change in Step 1 of Derivation 11 is that $\epsilon_{\kk'}$ becomes $\hbar\kk'$, and we obtain $\hat{\bm P}=\sum_{\kk\sigma}\hbar\kk\,\hat n_{\kk\sigma}$. If we choose a basis in which the one-body operator is diagonal (here, plane waves), the off-diagonal terms of $\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j$ vanish and only $\hat n_i$ remains—this is what is meant by “choosing the basis well.”

Example B.1: External potential term

Write the term $\sum_\alpha U(\xx_\alpha)$ for the external potential $U(\xx)$ in the plane-wave basis.

Solution. By Eq. \eqref{eq:B-onebody-field}, $\hat U=\int\dd^3x\,\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)U(\xx)\hat\psi(\xx)$. Substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:B-field-plane} gives

$$ \hat U=\frac1V\sum_{\kk\kk'\sigma}\hat c_{\kk\sigma}^\dagger\hat c_{\kk'\sigma} \int_V\dd^3x\;\ee^{-\ii(\kk-\kk')\cdot\xx}U(\xx) =\frac1V\sum_{\kk\kk'\sigma}U(\kk-\kk')\;\hat c_{\kk\sigma}^\dagger\hat c_{\kk'\sigma} $$

Here $U(\qq)\equiv\int\dd^3x\,\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx}U(\xx)$. Rewriting $\kk'=\kk-\qq$, we obtain

$$ \hat U=\frac1V\sum_{\qq}U(\qq)\sum_{\kk\sigma}\hat c_{\kk\sigma}^\dagger\hat c_{\kk-\qq,\sigma} =\frac1V\sum_{\qq}U(\qq)\,\hat\rho_{-\qq} $$

(the last equality uses Eq. \eqref{eq:B-rho-q} of Section B.7). “The Fourier component of the external potential couples to the Fourier component of the density” is exactly the starting point of Chapter 9. We can read off that if $U$ is uniform (only $\qq=0$), it does not scatter the electrons, and only when there is spatial variation ($\qq\ne0$) does it change the electron momentum, by $\hbar\qq$.

B.6 Two-Body Operators and the Coulomb Interaction

B.6.1 The second-quantized form of two-body operators

In first quantization, a two-body operator acts on pairs of particles and has the form

$$ \begin{equation} \hat V=\frac12\sum_{\alpha\ne\beta}v(\xx_\alpha,\xx_\beta) \label{eq:B-twobody-1q} \end{equation} $$

The factor $\frac12$ corrects for counting the same pair twice, as $(\alpha,\beta)$ and $(\beta,\alpha)$. The condition $\alpha\ne\beta$ expresses “no interaction with itself.” These two conventions fully determine the form of the second-quantized representation.

Theorem B.2 (Second-quantized representation of two-body operators)

$$ \begin{equation} \hat V=\frac12\sum_{ijkl}\bra{ij}v\ket{kl}\; \hat a_i^\dagger\,\hat a_j^\dagger\,\hat a_l\,\hat a_k \label{eq:B-twobody-2q} \end{equation} $$

Here the matrix element is

$$ \begin{equation} \bra{ij}v\ket{kl}\equiv \int\dd^3x\int\dd^3x'\; \phi_i^*(\xx)\,\phi_j^*(\xx')\;v(\xx,\xx')\;\phi_k(\xx)\,\phi_l(\xx') \label{eq:B-twobody-me} \end{equation} $$

Note that the indices of the annihilation operators appear in “reverse order,” $\hat a_l\hat a_k$. The form is the same for both bosons and fermions.

★ Why are the indices in the reverse order $l,k$? — three reasons

Beginners always stumble on this point, so we give the reasons from three angles.

Reason 1: To preserve the correspondence of coordinates (the most essential). In the matrix element \eqref{eq:B-twobody-me}, the coordinate $\xx$ carries $\phi_i^*$ and $\phi_k$, and the coordinate $\xx'$ carries $\phi_j^*$ and $\phi_l$. That is, $i\leftrightarrow k$ (coordinate $\xx$) and $j\leftrightarrow l$ (coordinate $\xx'$) are the pairs belonging to “the same particle.” We want the operator side to preserve this correspondence as well, so for the field operators the natural ordering is

$$ \hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\;\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx')\;\hat\psi(\xx')\;\hat\psi(\xx) $$

an onion-like arrangement (the outer ones paired with each other, the inner ones paired with each other). The outer pair is $i,k$ and the inner pair is $j,l$; written with indices, this is $\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j^\dagger\hat a_l\hat a_k$. It suffices to read them in the order of the onion's layers.

Reason 2: To remove the self-interaction automatically. This arrangement is in normal order (creation operators on the left, annihilation operators on the right). A normal-ordered operator always gives zero when it acts on the vacuum, and it also gives zero when it acts on a one-particle state (there are two $\hat a$'s, so it tries to remove two particles and fails). This is precisely what $\alpha\ne\beta$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:B-twobody-1q} demands. “If there is only one particle, the interaction energy is zero” is guaranteed by the ordering alone. We confirm this in equation form in Derivation 12.

Reason 3 (for fermions): Getting the order wrong gives the wrong sign. Since $\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_j^\dagger\hat c_k\hat c_l=-\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_j^\dagger\hat c_l\hat c_k$, writing the operators in the order $k,l$ reverses the sign of the interaction energy. (For bosons the annihilation operators commute with one another, so the order does not affect the sign; but Reasons 1 and 2 still apply, so it is best to keep the onion-like arrangement.) The definition of the indices of $\bra{ij}v\ket{kl}$ can differ from textbook to textbook, so when comparing with other books, always check which index belongs to which coordinate.

Derivation 12: The field-operator expression, and the vanishing of the self-interaction

Substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:B-twobody-me} into Eq. \eqref{eq:B-twobody-2q} and collecting the sums into field operators in the same way as in Section B.5.2, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \hat V=\frac12\int\dd^3x\int\dd^3x'\; \hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\,\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx')\;v(\xx,\xx')\; \hat\psi(\xx')\,\hat\psi(\xx) \label{eq:B-twobody-field} \end{equation} $$

(Because $v(\xx,\xx')$ is just a number, it can be placed anywhere.) Naively, the interaction energy is “density $\times$ density,” so one is tempted to write $\frac12\int\int v\,\hat\rho(\xx)\hat\rho(\xx')$. We show that this is not correct.

First we compute $[\hat\rho(\xx'),\hat\psi(\xx)]$. By the identity \eqref{eq:B-identity},

$$ [\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx')\hat\psi(\xx'),\hat\psi(\xx)] =\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx')\underbrace{\{\hat\psi(\xx'),\hat\psi(\xx)\}}_{=0} -\underbrace{\{\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx'),\hat\psi(\xx)\}}_{=\delta(\xx-\xx')}\hat\psi(\xx') =-\delta(\xx-\xx')\,\hat\psi(\xx) $$

(In the last step, thanks to the delta function, we set $\hat\psi(\xx')=\hat\psi(\xx)$. The result is the same for bosons.) That is, $\hat\rho(\xx')\hat\psi(\xx)=\hat\psi(\xx)\hat\rho(\xx')-\delta(\xx-\xx')\hat\psi(\xx)$. Using this to rewrite the integrand operator of Eq. \eqref{eq:B-twobody-field}, the middle two operators combine into $\hat\rho(\xx')$:

$$ \hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx')\hat\psi(\xx')\hat\psi(\xx) =\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\,\hat\rho(\xx')\,\hat\psi(\xx) =\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\left[\hat\psi(\xx)\hat\rho(\xx')-\delta(\xx-\xx')\hat\psi(\xx)\right] $$
$$ \begin{equation} \hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx')\hat\psi(\xx')\hat\psi(\xx) =\hat\rho(\xx)\hat\rho(\xx')-\delta(\xx-\xx')\,\hat\rho(\xx) \label{eq:B-normal-order} \end{equation} $$

∎ The second term is the subtraction of the self-interaction. If we write only $\hat\rho\hat\rho$, a contribution of “the same particle interacting with itself” sneaks in, so we must subtract it with the delta function. The onion-like arrangement does this subtraction automatically. In fact, when we apply Eq. \eqref{eq:B-twobody-field} to the one-particle state $\hat\psi^\dagger(\bm y)\ket0$, the rightmost $\hat\psi(\xx)$ removes the single particle, and the $\hat\psi(\xx')$ to its left then tries to remove a particle from the vacuum and gives zero. The interaction energy of a one-particle system is exactly zero. This is what was stated in Reason 2.

B.6.2 The Coulomb interaction in the plane-wave basis

For the electron gas, $v(\xx,\xx')=v(\abs{\xx-\xx'})=\dfrac{e^2}{\abs{\xx-\xx'}}$ (Gaussian units). We write this in the plane-wave basis.

Derivation 13: Computing the matrix element — momentum conservation emerges

We insert the plane waves $\phi_\kk(\xx)=V^{-1/2}\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\xx}$ into Eq. \eqref{eq:B-twobody-me}. Writing the four states as $\kk_1,\kk_2,\kk_3,\kk_4$, we have

$$ \bra{\kk_1\kk_2}v\ket{\kk_3\kk_4} =\frac{1}{V^2}\int\dd^3x\int\dd^3x'\; \ee^{-\ii\kk_1\cdot\xx}\ee^{-\ii\kk_2\cdot\xx'}\; v(\abs{\xx-\xx'})\; \ee^{\ii\kk_3\cdot\xx}\ee^{\ii\kk_4\cdot\xx'} $$

Step 1: Separating into relative and center-of-mass coordinates. We change variables to $\bm r\equiv\xx-\xx'$ and $\bm R\equiv\xx'$ (the Jacobian is $1$). Since $\xx=\bm r+\bm R$, the exponent becomes

$$ -\ii\kk_1\cdot(\bm r+\bm R)-\ii\kk_2\cdot\bm R+\ii\kk_3\cdot(\bm r+\bm R)+\ii\kk_4\cdot\bm R $$ $$ =\ii(\kk_3-\kk_1)\cdot\bm r+\ii(\kk_3+\kk_4-\kk_1-\kk_2)\cdot\bm R $$

Step 2: The $\bm R$ integral. Since $v$ does not depend on $\bm R$,

$$ \frac{1}{V}\int_V\dd^3R\;\ee^{\ii(\kk_3+\kk_4-\kk_1-\kk_2)\cdot\bm R} =\delta_{\kk_1+\kk_2,\;\kk_3+\kk_4} $$

This is the law of momentum conservation. “The interaction does not change the total momentum” came out of the integral automatically.

Step 3: The $\bm r$ integral. What remains is

$$ \frac1V\int\dd^3r\;\ee^{-\ii(\kk_1-\kk_3)\cdot\bm r}\,v(r) =\frac{1}{V}\,v(\kk_1-\kk_3), \qquad v(\qq)\equiv\int\dd^3r\;\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\bm r}v(r) $$

Summarizing,

$$ \begin{equation} \bra{\kk_1\kk_2}v\ket{\kk_3\kk_4} =\frac1V\,v(\kk_1-\kk_3)\;\delta_{\kk_1+\kk_2,\,\kk_3+\kk_4} \label{eq:B-coulomb-me} \end{equation} $$

∎

Derivation 14: The Fourier transform of the Coulomb potential, $v(q)=4\pi e^2/q^2$

If we Fourier-transform $v(r)=e^2/r$ as it stands, the integral does not converge as $r\to\infty$. We therefore carry out the calculation with a Yukawa-type screening factor $\ee^{-\mu r}$ inserted, and set $\mu\to0$ at the end (this procedure is also physically meaningful: in a real metal screening occurs, so $q_{TF}$ of Chapter 10 plays the role of $\mu$).

Taking spherical coordinates with $\qq$ as the polar axis, we have $\qq\cdot\bm r=qr\cos\theta$. The $\varphi$ integral gives $2\pi$, and the integral over $t=\cos\theta$ is

$$ \int_{-1}^{1}\dd t\;\ee^{-\ii qrt} =\left[\frac{\ee^{-\ii qrt}}{-\ii qr}\right]_{-1}^{1} =\frac{\ee^{\ii qr}-\ee^{-\ii qr}}{\ii qr} =\frac{2\sin qr}{qr} $$

Hence, carrying out the last integral by writing $\sin qr=\frac{1}{2\ii}(\ee^{\ii qr}-\ee^{-\ii qr})$, we obtain

$$ v_\mu(q)=2\pi e^2\int_0^\infty\dd r\;r^2\cdot\frac{\ee^{-\mu r}}{r}\cdot\frac{2\sin qr}{qr} =\frac{4\pi e^2}{q}\int_0^\infty\dd r\;\ee^{-\mu r}\sin qr =\frac{4\pi e^2}{q}\cdot\frac{q}{\mu^2+q^2} $$

Therefore

$$ v_\mu(q)=\frac{4\pi e^2}{q^2+\mu^2} $$

Now, removing the screening by taking the limit $\mu\to0$, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} v(q)=\frac{4\pi e^2}{q^2} \label{eq:B-vq} \end{equation} $$

which is the Fourier transform of the bare Coulomb interaction.

∎ In SI units, $v(q)=e^2/(\varepsilon_0q^2)$. To match Chapters 9–11, this book uses Gaussian units.

We substitute Eqs. \eqref{eq:B-coulomb-me} and \eqref{eq:B-vq} into Theorem B.2. To satisfy momentum conservation, we parametrize the momenta as

$$ \kk_1=\kk+\qq,\qquad \kk_3=\kk,\qquad \kk_4=\kk',\qquad \kk_2=\kk'-\qq $$

(with $\kk_1+\kk_2=\kk+\kk'=\kk_3+\kk_4$, the conservation law is satisfied). Then $v(\kk_1-\kk_3)=v(\qq)$, and the sums run over the three independent variables $\kk,\kk',\qq$. Restoring the spin as well, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \hat V=\frac{1}{2V}\sum_{\kk\kk'\qq}\sum_{\sigma\sigma'} v(q)\; \hat c^\dagger_{\kk+\qq,\sigma}\, \hat c^\dagger_{\kk'-\qq,\sigma'}\, \hat c_{\kk'\sigma'}\, \hat c_{\kk\sigma} \label{eq:B-coulomb-2q} \end{equation} $$

This is the interaction term used in Chapters 9–11.

Treatment of the $\qq=0$ term (the jellium model)

Setting $\qq=0$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:B-coulomb-2q} gives $v(0)=\infty$. Physically, however, this divergence is exactly canceled by the interaction of the electrons with the uniform positive background charge (the ions). This is because the electron–electron term is $+\frac{1}{2V}v(0)N^2$, the electron–background term is $-\frac{1}{V}v(0)N^2$, and the background–background term is $+\frac{1}{2V}v(0)N^2$, and the sum of the coefficients is $\frac12-1+\frac12=0$. (To be precise, the electron–electron term is $\frac{1}{2V}v(0)\hat N(\hat N-1)$, and after the $N^2$ terms cancel one another, $-\frac{1}{2V}v(0)\hat N$ remains. This is $-2\pi e^2/\mu^2V$ per particle, and it vanishes if one first takes $V\to\infty$ while keeping the Yukawa-type screening. The precise statement is that what cancels exactly is the $O(N^2)$ part.) Therefore, in practice we write

$$ \hat V=\frac{1}{2V}\sum_{\qq\ne0}\sum_{\kk\kk'\sigma\sigma'}v(q)\, \hat c^\dagger_{\kk+\qq,\sigma}\hat c^\dagger_{\kk'-\qq,\sigma'}\hat c_{\kk'\sigma'}\hat c_{\kk\sigma} $$

with $\qq=0$ excluded. This model is called the jellium model (an electron gas with a uniform positive background charge), and Chapters 9–11 all discuss this model.

B.7 The Density Operator and Its Commutators

B.7.1 The density operator

In first quantization, the particle number density is the one-body operator $\hat\rho(\xx)=\sum_\alpha\delta(\xx-\xx_\alpha)$ (we use $\hat\rho$ for the density so that it is not confused with the number operator $\hat n_i$). Its matrix elements are

$$ \bra{i}\delta(\xx-\hat{\bm r})\ket{j}=\int\dd^3y\;\phi_i^*(\bm y)\delta(\xx-\bm y)\phi_j(\bm y)=\phi_i^*(\xx)\phi_j(\xx) $$

so, by Theorem B.1,

$$ \begin{equation} \hat\rho(\xx)=\sum_{ij}\phi_i^*(\xx)\phi_j(\xx)\,\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j =\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\,\hat\psi(\xx) \label{eq:B-rho-x} \end{equation} $$

holds. “Annihilate the particle at $\xx$ and immediately recreate it at the same place” = “count how many particles are at $\xx$.” Since setting $\hat t=1$ (the identity operator) in Eq. \eqref{eq:B-onebody-field} gives $\hat N$, we can also verify that

$$ \int\dd^3x\;\hat\rho(\xx)=\int\dd^3x\;\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\hat\psi(\xx)=\hat N $$

holds. This is the obvious relation that integrating the density over all space gives the particle number.

Number density and charge density

In this book we take $\hat\rho(\xx)$ to be the number density (in units of $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$). When the charge density is needed, we write $\hat\rho_{\text{charge}}=-e\hat\rho$ (the electron charge is $-e$, with $e>0$). When we treat the dielectric function in Chapters 9–11, $e^2$ is already contained in $v(q)=4\pi e^2/q^2$, so $\hat\rho$ can remain the number density. When comparing with other books, check each time whether $\rho$ is the number density or the charge density. This is the cause of most mismatches in the factors of $e$.

B.7.2 The Fourier components $\hat\rho_\qq$

Derivation 15: $\hat\rho_\qq=\sum_{\kk\sigma}\hat c^\dagger_{\kk\sigma}\hat c_{\kk+\qq,\sigma}$

We define the Fourier component of the density, in accordance with the conventions of this book (Appendix A), as

$$ \begin{equation} \hat\rho_\qq\equiv\int_V\dd^3x\;\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx}\,\hat\rho(\xx) \label{eq:B-rho-q-def} \end{equation} $$

Substituting Eqs. \eqref{eq:B-rho-x} and \eqref{eq:B-field-plane}, we obtain

$$ \hat\rho_\qq=\int_V\dd^3x\;\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx} \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt V}\sum_{\kk\sigma}\ee^{-\ii\kk\cdot\xx}\hat c^\dagger_{\kk\sigma}\right) \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt V}\sum_{\kk'}\ee^{\ii\kk'\cdot\xx}\hat c_{\kk'\sigma}\right) $$

Combining the exponentials, the exponent is $\ii(\kk'-\kk-\qq)\cdot\xx$, and the spatial integral is

$$ \frac1V\int_V\dd^3x\;\ee^{\ii(\kk'-\kk-\qq)\cdot\xx}=\delta_{\kk',\,\kk+\qq} $$

This collapses the sum over $\kk'$, and we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \hat\rho_\qq=\sum_{\kk\sigma}\hat c^\dagger_{\kk\sigma}\,\hat c_{\kk+\qq,\sigma} \label{eq:B-rho-q} \end{equation} $$

∎ Relabeling the dummy index as $\kk\to\kk-\qq$, we can also write

$$ \begin{equation} \hat\rho_\qq=\sum_{\kk\sigma}\hat c^\dagger_{\kk-\qq,\sigma}\hat c_{\kk\sigma} \label{eq:B-rho-q-alt} \end{equation} $$

We also use this form in Chapter 10.

Hermitian conjugate. Simply taking the Hermitian conjugate of Eq. \eqref{eq:B-rho-q} gives

$$ \begin{equation} \hat\rho_\qq^\dagger=\sum_{\kk\sigma}\hat c^\dagger_{\kk+\qq,\sigma}\hat c_{\kk\sigma}=\hat\rho_{-\qq} \label{eq:B-rho-dagger} \end{equation} $$

(The last equality follows immediately by setting $\qq\to-\qq$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:B-rho-q-def} and using the fact that $\hat\rho(\xx)$ is Hermitian.)

The physical reading of $\hat\rho_\qq$

Looking at Eq. \eqref{eq:B-rho-q}, we see that $\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_{\kk+\qq}$ is the operation “annihilate an electron of wave vector $\kk+\qq$ and recreate it with wave vector $\kk$.” On balance, the electron's momentum decreases by $\hbar\qq$.

Conversely, $\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger=\hat\rho_{-\qq}$ consists, by Eq. \eqref{eq:B-rho-dagger}, of the terms $\hat c^\dagger_{\kk+\qq}\hat c_\kk$, so it increases the momentum by $\hbar\qq$.

Therefore, “acting with $\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger$ on the ground state” means pouring momentum $\hbar\qq$ into the system. Neutron scattering and inelastic electron scattering (Chapter 12) carry out exactly this operation in the laboratory. What kind of superposition of states $\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger\ket{\mathrm{FS}}$ is ($\ket{\mathrm{FS}}$ is the ground state defined in Section B.8, that is, the Fermi sphere) directly determines the measured excitation spectrum—this is the content of the Lehmann representation in Chapter 8 and of the dynamic structure factor in Chapter 12.

B.7.3 $[\hat\rho_\qq,\hat\rho_{\qq'}^\dagger]$ is exactly zero

Derivation 16: Density operators commute with each other

We use Lemma B.1 \eqref{eq:B-lemma1}. Since the spin is the same throughout, we omit it for the moment.

$$ \hat\rho_\qq=\sum_\kk\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_{\kk+\qq}, \qquad \hat\rho_{\qq'}^\dagger=\sum_{\kk'}\hat c^\dagger_{\kk'+\qq'}\hat c_{\kk'} $$

Setting $a=\kk$, $b=\kk+\qq$, $c=\kk'+\qq'$, $d=\kk'$ in Lemma B.1, we obtain

$$ \left[\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_{\kk+\qq},\;\hat c^\dagger_{\kk'+\qq'}\hat c_{\kk'}\right] =\delta_{\kk+\qq,\;\kk'+\qq'}\;\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_{\kk'} \;-\;\delta_{\kk,\kk'}\;\hat c^\dagger_{\kk'+\qq'}\hat c_{\kk+\qq} $$

First term: The Kronecker delta requires $\kk'=\kk+\qq-\qq'$. The sum over $\kk'$ collapses, and we obtain

$$ \sum_\kk\hat c^\dagger_\kk\,\hat c_{\kk+\qq-\qq'}=\hat\rho_{\qq-\qq'} $$

Second term: The delta requires $\kk'=\kk$, so we have

$$ -\sum_\kk\hat c^\dagger_{\kk+\qq'}\hat c_{\kk+\qq} $$

Now, relabeling the dummy index as $\kk\to\kk-\qq'$, we obtain

$$ =-\sum_\kk\hat c^\dagger_{\kk}\hat c_{\kk+\qq-\qq'}=-\hat\rho_{\qq-\qq'} $$

Adding the two terms gives

$$ \begin{equation} \left[\hat\rho_\qq,\;\hat\rho_{\qq'}^\dagger\right] =\hat\rho_{\qq-\qq'}-\hat\rho_{\qq-\qq'}=0 \label{eq:B-rho-rho-comm} \end{equation} $$

∎ Density operators commute with each other exactly, for any $\qq,\qq'$.

This result is obvious (and extremely important)

In real space it is obvious. $\hat\rho(\xx)$ is built only from functions of position, and position operators commute with each other, so $[\hat\rho(\xx),\hat\rho(\xx')]=0$. Their Fourier transforms $\hat\rho_\qq$ must of course commute with each other as well.

But this “obvious” fact has a great payoff. The Coulomb interaction is built only from the density (Eq. \eqref{eq:B-twobody-field} is $\hat\rho\hat\rho$ minus the self-interaction), so $[\hat\rho_\qq,\hat V]=0$, and the interaction contributes nothing at all to the double commutator computed in the next subsection. This is why the $f$-sum rule holds exactly regardless of the strength of the interaction (Chapter 8, Section 8.7). Conversely, since the entire time evolution of $\hat\rho_\qq$ comes from the kinetic-energy term, it suffices to compute $[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham_0]$.

Note also that if the band is linearized in one dimension (the Tomonaga–Luttinger model), an anomalous term appears in this commutator, and that is the starting point of bosonization; but this goes beyond the scope of this book, so we do not touch on it.

B.7.4 $[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham_0]$ and the $f$-sum rule

Derivation 17: $[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham_0]$

We use the free-electron $\Ham_0=\sum_{\kk''\sigma}\epsilon_{\kk''}\hat c^\dagger_{\kk''\sigma}\hat c_{\kk''\sigma}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:B-kinetic-plane}). Setting $a=\kk$, $b=\kk+\qq$, $c=d=\kk''$ in Lemma B.1, we obtain

$$ \left[\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_{\kk+\qq},\;\hat c^\dagger_{\kk''}\hat c_{\kk''}\right] =\delta_{\kk+\qq,\kk''}\,\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_{\kk''} -\delta_{\kk,\kk''}\,\hat c^\dagger_{\kk''}\hat c_{\kk+\qq} $$

Multiply by $\epsilon_{\kk''}$ and sum over $\kk''$. The first term selects $\kk''=\kk+\qq$ and the second selects $\kk''=\kk$, so we have

$$ =\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}\,\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_{\kk+\qq}-\epsilon_\kk\,\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_{\kk+\qq} =\left(\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}-\epsilon_\kk\right)\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_{\kk+\qq} $$

Therefore, summing over $\kk$ and the spin, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \left[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham_0\right] =\sum_{\kk\sigma}\left(\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}-\epsilon_\kk\right) \hat c^\dagger_{\kk\sigma}\hat c_{\kk+\qq,\sigma} \label{eq:B-rho-H-comm} \end{equation} $$

∎ How to read it: each term of $\hat\rho_\qq$ is multiplied, as a weight, by the energy change $\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}-\epsilon_\kk$ that the term causes. Recalling the Heisenberg equation of motion $\ii\hbar\,\dd\hat\rho_\qq/\dd t=[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham]$, we see that this is the expression that gives the “frequency of the density fluctuation.”

Derivation 18: The right-hand side of the $f$-sum rule, $\braket{[[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham_0],\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger]}=N\dfrac{\hbar^2q^2}{m}$

Here we actually compute the quantity used in Chapter 8, Section 8.7.

Step 1: Taking the commutator once more. Denote Eq. \eqref{eq:B-rho-H-comm} by $\hat X$ and put $\Delta_\kk\equiv\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}-\epsilon_\kk$. We again compute, using Lemma B.1, the commutator with $\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger=\sum_{\kk'\sigma'}\hat c^\dagger_{\kk'+\qq,\sigma'}\hat c_{\kk'\sigma'}$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:B-rho-dagger}). With $a=\kk$, $b=\kk+\qq$, $c=\kk'+\qq$, $d=\kk'$, we have

$$ \left[\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_{\kk+\qq},\;\hat c^\dagger_{\kk'+\qq}\hat c_{\kk'}\right] =\delta_{\kk+\qq,\kk'+\qq}\,\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_{\kk'} -\delta_{\kk,\kk'}\,\hat c^\dagger_{\kk'+\qq}\hat c_{\kk+\qq} $$

Both deltas require $\kk'=\kk$, so we can combine them:

$$ =\delta_{\kk\kk'}\left(\hat c^\dagger_\kk\hat c_\kk-\hat c^\dagger_{\kk+\qq}\hat c_{\kk+\qq}\right) =\delta_{\kk\kk'}\left(\hat n_\kk-\hat n_{\kk+\qq}\right) $$

Therefore

$$ \left[\left[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham_0\right],\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger\right] =\sum_{\kk\sigma}\left(\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}-\epsilon_\kk\right) \left(\hat n_{\kk\sigma}-\hat n_{\kk+\qq,\sigma}\right) $$

Step 2: Shifting the dummy index in the second term. Take the expectation value and write $\braket{\hat n_{\kk\sigma}}=n_\kk$. Relabeling $\kk\to\kk-\qq$ in the second term turns $\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}-\epsilon_\kk\to\epsilon_\kk-\epsilon_{\kk-\qq}$ and $n_{\kk+\qq}\to n_\kk$, so

$$ \braket{\cdots} =\sum_{\kk\sigma}\left(\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}-\epsilon_\kk\right)n_\kk -\sum_{\kk\sigma}\left(\epsilon_\kk-\epsilon_{\kk-\qq}\right)n_\kk $$ $$ =\sum_{\kk\sigma}\left(\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}+\epsilon_{\kk-\qq}-2\epsilon_\kk\right)n_\kk $$

Step 3: Evaluating the bracket. This is the satisfying part. Substituting $\epsilon_\kk=\hbar^2k^2/2m$, we obtain

$$ \epsilon_{\kk+\qq}+\epsilon_{\kk-\qq}-2\epsilon_\kk =\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left[(\kk+\qq)^2+(\kk-\qq)^2-2k^2\right] $$ $$ =\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left[k^2+2\kk\cdot\qq+q^2+k^2-2\kk\cdot\qq+q^2-2k^2\right] =\frac{\hbar^2q^2}{m} $$

It has become a constant independent of $\kk$. This is because the cross terms $2\kk\cdot\qq$ cancel cleanly. Therefore

$$ \braket{\cdots}=\frac{\hbar^2q^2}{m}\sum_{\kk\sigma}n_\kk $$

The last sum is the total particle number $N$. Hence

$$ \begin{equation} \braket{\left[\left[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham_0\right],\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger\right]} =\frac{N\hbar^2q^2}{m} \label{eq:B-fsum} \end{equation} $$

∎

Why is this “exact”?

In Derivation 18 we used only $\Ham_0$ (the kinetic energy). Even if we add the interaction, $[\hat\rho_\qq,\hat V]=0$ by Section B.7.3, so $[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham]=[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham_0]$, and the double commutator does not depend on the interaction at all. Moreover, because the $\kk$ dependence disappeared in Step 3, the result also does not depend on the details of $n_\kk$ (even a distribution distorted by the interaction is allowed). All that is required is that $\sum_\kk n_\kk=N$ hold.

Conclusion: the $f$-sum rule \eqref{eq:B-fsum} holds exactly, regardless of the strength of the interaction, the temperature, or the form of the distribution function. Converted to a per-unit-volume form it is $n\hbar^2q^2/m$, and it is this single line that fixes both the $f$-sum rule of Chapter 8 and the plasma frequency $\omega_p=\sqrt{4\pi ne^2/m}$ of Chapter 11.

The conditions actually used are only two: “the kinetic energy is $\hat{\bm p}^2/2m_e$,” and “all the remaining terms are functions of position only (a local potential).” The band does not even have to be parabolic. In the next Derivation 18′, we show this without using plane waves even once.

Derivation 18′: For a local potential, the mass that appears in the sum rule is the bare mass $m_e$

It is quickest to write this in first quantization. Substituting $\hat\rho(\xx)=\sum_\alpha\delta(\xx-\hat{\bm r}_\alpha)$ into the definition in Eq. \eqref{eq:B-rho-q-def}, we obtain

$$ \hat\rho_\qq=\sum_\alpha\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\hat{\bm r}_\alpha}, \qquad \hat\rho_\qq^\dagger=\sum_\alpha\ee^{+\ii\qq\cdot\hat{\bm r}_\alpha} $$

We take the Hamiltonian in the form actually used for solids,

$$ \Ham=\sum_\alpha\frac{\hat{\bm p}_\alpha^2}{2m_e}+\hat U, \qquad \hat U\equiv\sum_\alpha V(\hat{\bm r}_\alpha)+\frac12\sum_{\alpha\ne\beta}v(\hat{\bm r}_\alpha-\hat{\bm r}_\beta) $$

where $V$ is the periodic potential created by the atomic nuclei (it may be as strong and as complicated as you like), and $v$ is the Coulomb interaction between electrons.

Step 1: $\hat U$ has no effect from the start. $\hat U$ is built from position operators alone, and so is $\hat\rho_\qq$. Since position operators commute with one another,

$$ \left[\hat\rho_\qq,\hat U\right]=0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \left[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham\right]=\left[\hat\rho_\qq,\;\sum_\alpha\frac{\hat{\bm p}_\alpha^2}{2m_e}\right] $$

With this single line, both the periodic potential $V$ and the electron correlation $v$ disappear. This is the key point. No information about the solid enters the subsequent calculation at all, so there is no room for the band structure to show up on the right-hand side.

Step 2: The commutator with the kinetic energy. Write $f_\alpha\equiv\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\hat{\bm r}_\alpha}$. The canonical commutation relation $[\hat{\bm p},F(\hat{\bm r})]=-\ii\hbar\nabla F$ gives $[\hat{\bm p}_\alpha,f_\alpha]=-\hbar\qq f_\alpha$, that is, $[f_\alpha,\hat{\bm p}_\alpha]=\hbar\qq f_\alpha$. Applying the formula for $[\hat A,\hat B\hat C]$ (Appendix A) to $\hat{\bm p}^2=\hat{\bm p}\cdot\hat{\bm p}$, we get

$$ \left[f_\alpha,\hat{\bm p}_\alpha^2\right] =\hat{\bm p}_\alpha\cdot\left[f_\alpha,\hat{\bm p}_\alpha\right]+\left[f_\alpha,\hat{\bm p}_\alpha\right]\cdot\hat{\bm p}_\alpha =\hbar\left[(\hat{\bm p}_\alpha\cdot\qq)f_\alpha+f_\alpha(\qq\cdot\hat{\bm p}_\alpha)\right] $$

Hence

$$ \left[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham\right] =\frac{\hbar}{2m_e}\sum_\alpha\left[(\hat{\bm p}_\alpha\cdot\qq)f_\alpha+f_\alpha(\qq\cdot\hat{\bm p}_\alpha)\right] $$

Step 3: Taking the commutator once more. With $g_\beta\equiv\ee^{+\ii\qq\cdot\hat{\bm r}_\beta}$, the same formula gives $[\hat{\bm p}_\alpha,g_\beta]=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\hbar\qq\,g_\alpha$. Since $f$ and $g$ are both functions of position, they commute, and $f_\alpha g_\alpha=1$. Therefore only the factor of $\hat{\bm p}$ contributes, and

$$ \left[(\hat{\bm p}_\alpha\cdot\qq)f_\alpha,\;g_\alpha\right]=\hbar q^2\,g_\alpha f_\alpha=\hbar q^2, \qquad \left[f_\alpha(\qq\cdot\hat{\bm p}_\alpha),\;g_\alpha\right]=\hbar q^2\,f_\alpha g_\alpha=\hbar q^2 $$

Summing over $\alpha$ gives $2\hbar q^2$ for each particle. Hence

$$ \begin{equation} \left[\left[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham\right],\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger\right] =\frac{\hbar}{2m_e}\sum_\alpha 2\hbar q^2 =\frac{\hbar^2q^2}{m_e}\,\hat N \label{eq:B-fsum-exact} \end{equation} $$

∎ This is an operator identity, before any expectation value is taken, and its right-hand side is nothing more than a c-number multiple of the particle-number operator. Evaluating it in any state of an $N$-electron system (the ground state, an excited state, or thermal equilibrium alike) gives $N\hbar^2q^2/m_e$. Eq. \eqref{eq:B-fsum} of Derivation 18 is merely a check of this for the special case of the free-electron gas in the plane-wave basis.

A substitution you must not make: $m_e\to m^*$

One is tempted to think, “In a real solid the bands deviate from a parabola, so the $m_e$ in the sum rule should also be replaced by the band effective mass $m^*$.” This is wrong. The form of $\epsilon_\kk$ never appears in the derivation of Eq. \eqref{eq:B-fsum-exact}. This is because the periodic potential drops out in Step 1 as $[\hat\rho_\qq,\hat U]=0$. The mass that appears in the exact total $f$-sum rule is always the bare electron mass $m_e$.

Then where did the band curvature go? Look back at Derivation 18 up to Step 2. Up to that point the form of the dispersion $\epsilon_\kk$ is not used at all, so for a single-band model we have in general

$$ \braket{\left[\left[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham_0\right],\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger\right]}_{\text{single band}} =\sum_{\kk\sigma}\left(\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}+\epsilon_{\kk-\qq}-2\epsilon_\kk\right)n_\kk \;\xrightarrow[\;q\to0\;]{}\; q^2\sum_{\kk\sigma}n_\kk\frac{\partial^2\epsilon_\kk}{\partial k_\parallel^2} $$

Here $k_\parallel$ is the component along $\qq$. For a parabolic band $\epsilon_\kk=\hbar^2k^2/2m$ we have $\partial^2\epsilon_\kk/\partial k_\parallel^2=\hbar^2/m$, and we return to Step 3 of Derivation 18. For a general band, this quantity is $\hbar^2/m^*$, that is, the effective mass.

But this is a partial sum rule that retains only that band, not the total $f$-sum rule. The interband transitions discarded in the projection onto a single band make up exactly the difference $N\hbar^2q^2/m_e-q^2\sum n_\kk\,\partial^2\epsilon_\kk/\partial k_\parallel^2$ on the high-frequency side. The effective mass appears only when we look at just “a part” of the sum rule.

The power of the sum rule — why it can be used as a check

The right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:B-fsum-exact} contains only $N$, $q$, $\hbar$ and $m_e$. Neither the strength of the interaction, nor the crystal structure, nor the temperature, nor the kind of approximation used enters at all. Whether one computes with the RPA, with $GW$, or with time-dependent density functional theory, a correctly computed spectrum must return the same value. There are not many relations that constrain a theory's answer from the outside, so this one is valuable.

Using it is simple. Integrate the numerically obtained spectrum with weight $\omega$ and compare the result with the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:B-fsum-exact}. The size of the discrepancy itself gives a direct measure of the soundness of the calculation.

However, watch the factor 2. When Eq. \eqref{eq:B-fsum-exact} is Lehmann-expanded, matrix elements of both $\hat\rho_\qq$ and $\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger$ appear, and in an inversion-symmetric system the two contribute equally. Therefore the “one-sided $f$-sum rule,” obtained by integrating only over positive frequencies with weight $\hbar\omega$, is

$$ \int_0^\infty\dd(\hbar\omega)\;\hbar\omega\,S(\qq,\omega)=\frac{N\hbar^2q^2}{2m_e} $$

which is exactly half of the double commutator in Eq. \eqref{eq:B-fsum-exact}. When comparing with a numerical spectrum, first check whether the dynamic structure factor you are using carries a factor $1/N$ and whether the integration range is one-sided or two-sided, and make clear which value you are comparing against. If you compare per unit volume, read $N\to n$.

  • Off by orders of magnitude: either the equation itself or the unit conversion is wrong. First check the definition (the convention of this book or that of another book).
  • Short by a few to a dozen or so percent: truncation of the integral is the prime suspect. The contributions of interband transitions on the high-energy side and of core electrons have not been included.
  • The value shifts when the basis or the pseudopotential is changed: suspect an inconsistency in the velocity operator caused by a nonlocal pseudopotential, or incompleteness of the basis (the third row of Table B.1, and Exercise 8.4 in Chapter 8).

In any case, one must never explain the failure of a sum rule by claiming, “Because this is a solid, we should use $m^*$ instead of $m_e$.” The mass in the denominator of the exact sum rule is $m_e$ in every solid.

B.8 The Fermi Sphere and Particle–Hole Excitations

B.8.1 The ground state — the Fermi sphere

The Hamiltonian of the free-electron gas is $\Ham_0=\sum_{\kk\sigma}\epsilon_\kk\hat n_{\kk\sigma}$, given in Eq. \eqref{eq:B-kinetic-plane}. To minimize it with $N$ electrons, it suffices to fill the states in order, starting from the lowest energy. Because the Pauli principle allows only one electron in each $(\kk,\sigma)$, everything inside a sphere of radius $k_F$ in $\kk$-space is filled. We call this sphere the Fermi sphere and its surface the Fermi surface.

$$ \begin{equation} \ket{\mathrm{FS}}=\prod_{\abs{\kk}<k_F}\prod_{\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow}\hat c^\dagger_{\kk\sigma}\ket{0}, \qquad \braket{\mathrm{FS}|\hat n_{\kk\sigma}|\mathrm{FS}}=n_\kk=\theta(k_F-k) \label{eq:B-FS} \end{equation} $$

Here $\theta$ is the step function, equal to $1$ for $k<k_F$ and to $0$ for $k>k_F$. The order of the product (which produces a sign, since the operators are fermionic) affects only the overall phase, so it suffices to fix it once and for all.

$\ket{\mathrm{FS}}$ is the “new vacuum”

For states inside the Fermi sphere, it is $\hat c_{\kk\sigma}$ that becomes the “creating” operator. In fact, for $k<k_F$ we have $\hat c^\dagger_{\kk\sigma}\ket{\mathrm{FS}}=0$ (the state is already filled) and $\hat c_{\kk\sigma}\ket{\mathrm{FS}}\ne0$ (a hole is opened). For $k>k_F$ it is the other way around.

So for $k<k_F$, define $\hat b^\dagger_{\kk\sigma}\equiv\hat c_{\kk\sigma}$ and regard it as the creation operator of a hole. Then $\ket{\mathrm{FS}}$ becomes the “state with neither particles nor holes,” that is, the new vacuum. Moving an electron outside the Fermi surface is “creating a particle,” and leaving a vacancy inside is “creating a hole.”

A hole behaves like an “antiparticle” with charge $+e$, momentum $-\hbar\kk$, and energy $-\epsilon_\kk$. This is the same idea as that of holes in a semiconductor.

B.8.2 $\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger\ket{\mathrm{FS}}$ is a superposition of particle–hole pairs

Derivation 19: What happens when we kick with the density operator

Apply Eq. \eqref{eq:B-rho-dagger} to the Fermi sphere:

$$ \hat\rho_\qq^\dagger\ket{\mathrm{FS}} =\sum_{\kk\sigma}\hat c^\dagger_{\kk+\qq,\sigma}\,\hat c_{\kk\sigma}\ket{\mathrm{FS}} $$

There are two conditions for each term to survive.

  • For $\hat c_{\kk\sigma}$ to act, $\kk$ must be occupied: $k<k_F$ (that is, $n_\kk=1$).
  • For $\hat c^\dagger_{\kk+\qq,\sigma}$ to act, $\kk+\qq$ must be empty: $\abs{\kk+\qq}>k_F$ (that is, $n_{\kk+\qq}=0$).

Only the $\kk$ satisfying these two conditions remain in the sum, so

$$ \begin{equation} \hat\rho_\qq^\dagger\ket{\mathrm{FS}} =\sum_{\kk\sigma}n_\kk\left(1-n_{\kk+\qq}\right)\;\ket{\kk\qq\sigma}, \qquad \ket{\kk\qq\sigma}\equiv\hat c^\dagger_{\kk+\qq,\sigma}\hat c_{\kk\sigma}\ket{\mathrm{FS}} \label{eq:B-ph-state} \end{equation} $$

∎ That is, $\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger\ket{\mathrm{FS}}$ is a superposition obtained by adding up, over all $\kk$ satisfying the conditions, the particle–hole-pair state in which “one electron is taken from inside the Fermi sphere and placed outside.”

This state $\ket{\kk\qq\sigma}$ is an eigenstate of $\Ham_0$, and its excitation energy is

$$ \begin{equation} \hbar\omega_{\kk\qq}=\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}-\epsilon_\kk =\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(2\kk\cdot\qq+q^2\right) \label{eq:B-ph-energy} \end{equation} $$

(as it must be, since we have moved an electron from $\epsilon_\kk$ to $\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}$). That $\ket{\kk\qq\sigma}$ is normalized can be checked from $\braket{\kk\qq\sigma|\kk\qq\sigma}=n_\kk(1-n_{\kk+\qq})=1$ when the conditions are satisfied (Chapter 10, Section 10.1.4).

B.8.3 The $(q,\omega)$ diagram of the particle–hole continuum

Derivation 20: The upper and lower limits of the continuum

Eq. \eqref{eq:B-ph-energy}, written with $\kk\cdot\qq=kq\cos\theta$, reads $\hbar\omega=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(q^2+2kq\cos\theta)$. The constraints are $k<k_F$ (the starting point is inside the sphere) and $\abs{\kk+\qq}>k_F$ (the endpoint is outside the sphere).

Upper limit. The maximum occurs for $k=k_F$, $\cos\theta=+1$ ($\kk$ and $\qq$ are parallel, and moreover the starting point lies on the Fermi surface). The endpoint then satisfies $\abs{\kk+\qq}=k_F+q>k_F$, so it is indeed allowed, and $\hbar\omega_+=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(q^2+2k_Fq)$.

Lower limit. We would like to take $\cos\theta=-1$, but the endpoint might fall back inside the sphere, so we distinguish cases.

  • $q<2k_F$: An electron can be moved from just inside the Fermi surface to just outside it with almost no change in energy. That is, $\hbar\omega\to0$ is allowed, and the lower limit is $0$.
  • $q>2k_F$: $\qq$ is too large, so whatever starting point we choose, the electron necessarily ends up outside the sphere. The minimum occurs for $k=k_F$, $\cos\theta=-1$, where $\hbar\omega_-=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(q^2-2k_Fq)>0$.

To summarize,

$$ \begin{equation} \hbar\omega_\pm(q)=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(q^2\pm2k_Fq\right) =\frac{\hbar^2 q^2}{2m}\pm\hbar q v_F, \qquad v_F\equiv\frac{\hbar k_F}{m} \label{eq:B-ph-continuum} \end{equation} $$

so the continuum occupies the region $0\le\hbar\omega\le\hbar\omega_+$ for $q<2k_F$ and the region $\hbar\omega_-\le\hbar\omega\le\hbar\omega_+$ for $q>2k_F$. ∎

The behavior as $q\to0$ is important. If we drop the $q^2$ term, the upper limit approaches $\hbar\omega_+\simeq\hbar qv_F$. Since the lower limit remains $0$ as long as $q<2k_F$, the continuum becomes a “wedge” between the straight line rising from the origin with slope $v_F$ and the axis $\omega=0$. As long as the plasmon dispersion $\omega\simeq\omega_p$ of Chapter 11 lies outside this wedge, the plasmon cannot decay into particle–hole pairs and has a long lifetime. The decay begins the moment it enters the wedge—this is Landau damping.

B.9 Basic Quantities of the Electron Gas and Actual Values

B.9.1 $k_F$, $\epsilon_F$, $N(\epsilon_F)$

Derivation 21: $k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$

We count the states inside the Fermi sphere. By Eq. \eqref{eq:B-sum-to-int}, including the $2$ spin species,

$$ N=\sum_{\kk\sigma}n_\kk =2\cdot V\int_{k<k_F}\frac{\dd^3k}{(2\pi)^3} =\frac{2V}{8\pi^3}\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3}k_F^3 =\frac{Vk_F^3}{3\pi^2} $$

Therefore, for the number density $n=N/V$,

$$ \begin{equation} n=\frac{k_F^3}{3\pi^2} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad k_F=\left(3\pi^2 n\right)^{1/3} \label{eq:B-kF} \end{equation} $$

∎ Furthermore, we have

$$ \begin{equation} \epsilon_F=\frac{\hbar^2k_F^2}{2m}, \qquad v_F=\frac{\hbar k_F}{m}, \qquad T_F=\frac{\epsilon_F}{k_B} \label{eq:B-eF} \end{equation} $$

As derived in Chapter 10, Section 10.2.2, the density of states is

$$ \begin{equation} N(\epsilon_F)=\frac{mk_F}{\pi^2\hbar^2}=\frac{3n}{2\epsilon_F} \label{eq:B-dos} \end{equation} $$

(per unit volume, including both spins). This $N(\epsilon_F)$ appears directly in the static response $\chi^0(\qq\to0,0)=-N(\epsilon_F)$ in the sign convention of this book.

B.9.2 The $r_s$ parameter

As a dimensionless quantity expressing how “dense” the electron gas is, we use the radius of the sphere occupied by one electron, measured in units of the Bohr radius $a_0=\hbar^2/me^2=0.529\ \text{Å}$:

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{4\pi}{3}\left(r_s a_0\right)^3=\frac1n \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad r_s=\frac{1}{a_0}\left(\frac{3}{4\pi n}\right)^{1/3} \label{eq:B-rs} \end{equation} $$

Combining this with Eq. \eqref{eq:B-kF} gives the convenient relation $k_F r_s a_0=(9\pi/4)^{1/3}=1.9192$. From this,

$$ \begin{equation} \epsilon_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2ma_0^2}\cdot\frac{(1.9192)^2}{r_s^2} =\frac{50.1\ \mathrm{eV}}{r_s^2} \label{eq:B-eF-rs} \end{equation} $$

(we used $\hbar^2/2ma_0^2=13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$).

Why does $r_s$ measure the “strength of the interaction”?

The kinetic energy per electron is $\sim\epsilon_F\propto k_F^2\propto r_s^{-2}$, and the Coulomb energy is $\sim e^2/(r_sa_0)\propto r_s^{-1}$. Therefore

$$ \frac{\text{Coulomb energy}}{\text{kinetic energy}}\propto\frac{r_s^{-1}}{r_s^{-2}}=r_s $$

The higher the density (the smaller $r_s$), the weaker the interaction is in relative terms. This runs counter to intuition, but the reason is that, owing to the Pauli principle, the kinetic energy rises rapidly with density. For $r_s\ll1$, the RPA (Chapter 9) is justified as an exact high-density limit. Real metals have $r_s\approx2$–$6$, which lies at the borderline of how far the RPA can be used quantitatively.

B.10 Wick's Theorem (Result Only)

B.10.1 What is the problem?

To compute a response function, we must evaluate correlation functions such as $\braket{\hat\rho_\qq(t)\hat\rho_{-\qq}(0)}$. Since $\hat\rho_\qq$ has the form $\hat c^\dagger\hat c$, this is an expectation value of four creation and annihilation operators. When the interaction is treated perturbatively, $\hat V$ brings in $\hat c^\dagger\hat c^\dagger\hat c\hat c$, so the number of operators keeps growing without bound: six, eight, and so on.

Fortunately, as long as $\Ham_0$ is a quadratic form (of the form $\hat c^\dagger\hat c$), all of these can be decomposed into products of expectation values of two operators. This is Wick's theorem.

Theorem B.3 (Wick's theorem, result only)

Suppose that $\Ham_0$ is a quadratic form in the creation and annihilation operators, and that the expectation value $\braket{\cdots}$ is taken in the ground state of $\Ham_0$ or is the thermal average with $\ee^{-\beta\Ham_0}$. Then the expectation value of $2n$ operators is equal to the sum over all “ways of pairing (contractions)”:

$$ \begin{equation} \braket{\hat A_1\hat A_2\cdots\hat A_{2n}} =\sum_{\text{ways of pairing}}(\pm1)^{P}\prod_{\text{pairs}}\braket{\hat A_i\hat A_j} \label{eq:B-wick} \end{equation} $$

For fermions the sign is $(-1)^P$ ($P$ is the number of crossings made in forming the pairs), and for bosons it is always $+1$. The expectation value of an odd number of operators is zero.

Written out explicitly for four operators (note that only the contractions between $\hat c^\dagger$ and $\hat c$ survive), this reads

$$ \begin{equation} \braket{\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_2^\dagger\hat c_3\hat c_4} =\braket{\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_4}\braket{\hat c_2^\dagger\hat c_3} -\braket{\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_3}\braket{\hat c_2^\dagger\hat c_4} \label{eq:B-wick4} \end{equation} $$

The first term, “inner with inner, outer with outer” (no crossing), carries $+$; the second term involves a crossing, so it carries $-$.

Why must it be a quadratic form?

The heart of the proof is that, if $\Ham_0$ is a quadratic form, then the operator comes back multiplied by a number, as in $\ee^{+\beta\Ham_0}\hat c_i\ee^{-\beta\Ham_0}=\ee^{-\beta\epsilon_i}\hat c_i$ (this is obvious when $\Ham_0$ is diagonal). Using this property, we can build a recursion relation that “takes out one operator at a time from the end” of the expectation value, and as a result the expectation value decomposes into a product of two-point functions. If $\Ham_0$ contains a quartic term (an interaction), this property breaks down. That is why we treat the interaction as a perturbation, reduce the problem to expectation values in the free system, and only then use Wick's theorem. This is the basic strategy when we build the perturbation expansion in the Matsubara formalism in Chapter 13.

B.10.2 Putting it to use

Example B.2: Occupation-number fluctuations $\braket{\hat n_1\hat n_2}$

Find $\braket{\hat n_1\hat n_2}$ for the free Fermi gas ($1,2$ are labels of one-particle states, $\braket{\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_j}=\delta_{ij}f_i$, and $f_i$ is the Fermi distribution).

Solution. First we bring the operators into normal order using the anticommutation relation. From $\hat c_1\hat c_2^\dagger=\delta_{12}-\hat c_2^\dagger\hat c_1$ we get

$$ \braket{\hat n_1\hat n_2}=\braket{\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_1\hat c_2^\dagger\hat c_2} =\delta_{12}\braket{\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_2}-\braket{\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_2^\dagger\hat c_1\hat c_2} $$

Apply Eq. \eqref{eq:B-wick4} to the second term ($\hat c_3\to\hat c_1$, $\hat c_4\to\hat c_2$):

$$ \braket{\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_2^\dagger\hat c_1\hat c_2} =\braket{\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_2}\braket{\hat c_2^\dagger\hat c_1} -\braket{\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_1}\braket{\hat c_2^\dagger\hat c_2} =\delta_{12}f_1^2-f_1f_2 $$

Note that in the first term $\braket{\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_2}\braket{\hat c_2^\dagger\hat c_1}=(\delta_{12}f_1)(\delta_{12}f_2)=\delta_{12}f_1^2$ (because of the factor $\delta_{12}$, $f_2$ equals $f_1$). It is not $\delta_{12}f_1$. At finite temperature $f_1^2\ne f_1$, so mixing these up changes the answer. Therefore

$$ \braket{\hat n_1\hat n_2}=\delta_{12}f_1-\delta_{12}f_1^2+f_1f_2 =f_1f_2+\delta_{12}\,f_1(1-f_1) $$

holds. Even without splitting into cases, the answer came out directly. As a check at both ends: if $1\ne2$, then $\braket{\hat n_1\hat n_2}=f_1f_2$ (independent); if $1=2$, then since $\hat n_1^2=\hat n_1$ we must have $\braket{\hat n_1^2}=f_1$, and putting $\delta_{12}=1$ and $f_2=f_1$ into the expression above gives $f_1^2+f_1(1-f_1)=f_1$, which indeed agrees. The second term represents the fluctuation of the occupation number $\braket{\hat n_1^2}-\braket{\hat n_1}^2=f_1(1-f_1)$. At $T=0$, $f=0$ or $1$, so the fluctuation vanishes, and it reaches its maximum value $1/4$ at $\epsilon=\mu$ ($f=1/2$). Only the neighborhood of the Fermi surface fluctuates—this is the key to the physics of metals.

Gateway to Feynman diagrams

In the time-dependent case, Wick's theorem is formulated for time-ordered products. If we draw the propagator (Green's function) of a free particle

$$ \ii G^0(\kk,t-t')\equiv\braket{T\,\hat c_\kk(t)\hat c_\kk^\dagger(t')} $$

as a single line, then each contraction in Eq. \eqref{eq:B-wick} corresponds to one line. Therefore the following correspondence holds:

  • The combinations of contractions = the ways of drawing the diagrams
  • $(-1)^P$ = a factor $(-1)$ for each Fermi loop
  • Each order of the interaction $\hat V$ = the number of vertices

That is the correspondence. Feynman diagrams are nothing other than a notation that classifies the terms generated by Wick's theorem pictorially. The RPA (Chapter 9) is the approximation that sums, up to infinite order, only the “diagrams in which bubbles are chained together.”

In this book we do not carry out calculations using diagrams, but to understand “what does the RPA actually sum?” in Chapter 9, it helps to know this correspondence.

B.11 Summary and Exercises

B.11.1 Summary

  • Second quantization is not new physics. It is a notation that discards the meaningless information of “particle name tags” from the $N$-body wave function and states only the occupation number of each one-particle state \eqref{eq:B-occ}. The $N!$ terms of a Slater determinant become a single term (Figure B.1).
  • The symmetry postulate \eqref{eq:B-symmetry} is completely absorbed into the (anti)commutation relations of the operators \eqref{eq:B-bose-comm}, \eqref{eq:B-fermi-anticomm}. The Pauli principle comes out automatically as $(\hat c^\dagger)^2=0$.
  • The phase factor $(-1)^{S_i}$ of the fermion operators ($S_i=\sum_{j<i}n_j$) is “the number of companions on the left that we have passed.” However, since observables are built from an even number of $\hat c^\dagger\hat c$, one rarely has to worry about signs in actual calculations.
  • Lemma B.1 $[\hat a_a^\dagger\hat a_b,\hat a_c^\dagger\hat a_d]=\delta_{bc}\hat a_a^\dagger\hat a_d-\delta_{ad}\hat a_c^\dagger\hat a_b$ is the tool used most in this appendix. It takes the same form for bosons and fermions.
  • Field operators $\hat\psi(\xx)=\sum_i\phi_i(\xx)\hat a_i$, $[\hat\psi(\xx),\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx')]_\mp=\delta(\xx-\xx')$. In the plane-wave basis, $\hat\psi_\sigma(\xx)=V^{-1/2}\sum_\kk\ee^{\ii\kk\cdot\xx}\hat c_{\kk\sigma}$.
  • One-body operators $\hat T=\sum_{ij}\bra i\hat t\ket j\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j=\int\dd^3x\,\hat\psi^\dagger\hat t\hat\psi$. In the plane-wave basis the kinetic energy becomes $\sum_{\kk\sigma}\epsilon_\kk\hat n_{\kk\sigma}$, so one sees at a glance that the free-electron gas is “solved.”
  • Two-body operators $\hat V=\frac12\sum\bra{ij}v\ket{kl}\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j^\dagger\hat a_l\hat a_k$. The indices are in the reverse order $l,k$ because the nested, onion-like ordering $\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx)\hat\psi^\dagger(\xx')\hat\psi(\xx')\hat\psi(\xx)$ preserves the correspondence of the coordinates and, at the same time, automatically subtracts the self-interaction \eqref{eq:B-normal-order}. The Coulomb case is Eq. \eqref{eq:B-coulomb-2q} (Figure B.2).
  • Density operator $\hat\rho(\xx)=\hat\psi^\dagger\hat\psi$, $\hat\rho_\qq=\sum_{\kk\sigma}\hat c^\dagger_{\kk\sigma}\hat c_{\kk+\qq,\sigma}$. Densities commute with one another exactly \eqref{eq:B-rho-rho-comm}, and thanks to this, $[\hat\rho_\qq,\hat V]=0$ holds and, in turn, the $f$-sum rule \eqref{eq:B-fsum} becomes exact independently of the interaction.
  • $\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger\ket{\mathrm{FS}}$ is a superposition of particle–hole pairs \eqref{eq:B-ph-state}. The excitation energy is $\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}-\epsilon_\kk$, and since there are innumerable ways to choose $\kk$, it forms a continuum (Figures B.3 and B.4). The boundaries are $\hbar\omega_\pm=\hbar^2q^2/2m\pm\hbar qv_F$. However, the actual lower edge is zero for $q<2k_F$ ($\hbar\omega_-$ becomes negative there, so it has no meaning), and it becomes $\hbar\omega_-$ itself only for $q>2k_F$.
  • Basic quantities of the electron gas: $k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$, $\epsilon_F=\hbar^2k_F^2/2m=50.1\ \mathrm{eV}/r_s^2$, $N(\epsilon_F)=3n/2\epsilon_F$. For the metals listed in Table B.2, $r_s\approx2$–$4$, $\epsilon_F\approx3$–$12\ \mathrm{eV}$, and the screening length is $\sim0.6\ \text{Å}$ (for real metals as a whole, $r_s\approx2$–$6$).
  • Wick's theorem: if $\Ham_0$ is a quadratic form, a multipoint correlation function unravels into a sum of products of two-point functions \eqref{eq:B-wick}. For fermions, a sign appears according to the number of crossings. A Feynman diagram is a picture of this decomposition.
  • Sign convention: this book adopts $\Ham_{\text{ext}}=+f(t)\hat B$ and $\chi^R_{AB}=-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\theta(t-t')\braket{[\hat A_H(t),\hat B_H(t')]}$. The sign is opposite to that of the mechanical susceptibility of Chapter 3 because $f=-F$, but the physics is the same (the warning box in Section B.2.4).

Exercise B.1: Basic check of the commutation relations

(3) Show that $\hat N=\sum_i\hat n_i$ commutes with the one-body operator $\hat T=\sum_{ij}t_{ij}\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j$, that is, $[\hat N,\hat T]=0$, and confirm that “a one-body operator does not change the particle number.”

(4) Show also that $[\hat N,\hat V]=0$ holds for the two-body operator \eqref{eq:B-twobody-2q}.

Hint: (1) In the identity \eqref{eq:B-identity}, take $\hat A=\hat c_i^\dagger$, $\hat B=\hat c_i$, $\hat C=\hat c_j^\dagger$. Exercise B.2: Tracking the Fermi signs by hand

Consider a system with four one-particle states ($i=1,2,3,4$). Define $\ket{n_1n_2n_3n_4}$ by the convention of Eq. \eqref{eq:B-fermi-state} (in numerical order from the left).

(1) Find $\hat c_2^\dagger\ket{1,0,1,0}$, including the sign.

(2) Find $\hat c_3\ket{1,1,1,0}$, including the sign.

(3) Find $\hat c_4^\dagger\hat c_1\ket{1,1,0,0}$. This is the operation of “moving the electron in state $1$ to state $4$.”

(4) Find $\hat c_1\hat c_4^\dagger\ket{1,1,0,0}$ (the reversed order) and compare it with (3). Do the signs agree? State the reason.

Hint: (1) Since $S_2=n_1=1$, the result is $-\ket{1,1,1,0}$. (2) Since $S_3=n_1+n_2=2$, the result is $+\ket{1,1,0,0}$. (3) $\hat c_1$ acts with $S_1=0$ and gives $+\ket{0,1,0,0}$, and the following $\hat c_4^\dagger$ acts with $S_4=0+1+0=1$ and gives $-\ket{0,1,0,1}$. (4) Since $i\ne j$, $\hat c_1$ and $\hat c_4^\dagger$ anticommute. Therefore the signs should be opposite. In the order $\hat c_1\hat c_4^\dagger$, the first operator $\hat c_4^\dagger$ has $S_4=2$ and gives $+$, the next $\hat c_1$ gives $+$, and the total is $+\ket{0,1,0,1}$. The signs are indeed opposite. If we treat things in “pairs” $\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_j$, this sign problem does not arise.

Exercise B.3: Field operators and the current density

(1) Show that the particle number written with field operators, $\hat N=\int\dd^3x\,\hat\psi^\dagger\hat\psi$, becomes $\sum_{\kk\sigma}\hat n_{\kk\sigma}$ in the plane-wave basis.

(2) Second-quantize the quantum-mechanical expression for the probability current density, $\bm j=\frac{\hbar}{2m\ii}\left(\psi^*\nabla\psi-(\nabla\psi^*)\psi\right)$, and write it as

Show that this is the case.

(3) Verify that for $\qq\to0$ this becomes $\hat{\bm j}_0=\hat{\bm P}/m$ (the total momentum divided by the mass).

(4) Show that the continuity equation $\dot{\hat\rho}_\qq+\ii\qq\cdot\hat{\bm j}_\qq=0$ follows from the Heisenberg equation of motion $\ii\hbar\dot{\hat\rho}_\qq=[\hat\rho_\qq,\Ham_0]$ and Eq. \eqref{eq:B-rho-H-comm}.

Hint: (2) Follow the same procedure as in Derivation 15. The differentiation produces $\ii\kk'$ and $-\ii\kk$, and one obtains $\frac{1}{2\ii}(\ii(\kk+\qq)+\ii\kk)=\kk+\qq/2$. Exercise B.5: The particle–hole continuum and numerical values

(1) Rederive Eq. \eqref{eq:B-ph-continuum}, and explain in words, using Figure B.3, why the lower edge touches $\omega=0$ for $q<2k_F$.

Hint: (2) For $\hbar^2q^2/2m$: at $q=0.5\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $\hbar^2q^2/2m=0.95\ \mathrm{eV}$. $\hbar qv_F=1.0546\times10^{-34}\times0.5\times10^{10}\times2.03\times10^6=1.07\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm{J}=6.7\ \mathrm{eV}$.
