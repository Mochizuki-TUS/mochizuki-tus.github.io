Chapter 8General Properties of Response Functions

In Chapter 7 we derived the Kubo formula:

$$ \chi^R_{AB}(t-t') = -\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\,\theta(t-t')\,\braket{\,[\hat A_H(t),\hat B_H(t')]\,} $$

It asserts that “when we apply a force from outside to $\hat B$, the response of $\hat A$ is determined solely by the expectation value of a commutator in the equilibrium state with no force applied.” It is beautiful, but as it stands we cannot compute anything with it. How do we turn the expectation value of a commutator into concrete numbers?

The answer is so simple that it is almost anticlimactic: insert the complete set of states $\sum_n\ket{n}\bra{n}=1$ in the middle of the commutator, and that is all. The time dependence of $\hat A_H(t)$ then unravels into simple phase factors $\ee^{\pm\ii\omega_{n0}t}$, the time integral can be carried out by elementary means, and we obtain

$$ \chi^R_{AB}(\omega) = \frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_n\left[\frac{\bra{0}\hat A\ket{n}\bra{n}\hat B\ket{0}}{\omega-\omega_{n0}+\ii\eta} - \frac{\bra{0}\hat B\ket{n}\bra{n}\hat A\ket{0}}{\omega+\omega_{n0}+\ii\eta}\right] $$

This is called the Lehmann representation (or spectral representation). Look at the denominators. They diverge at $\omega=\omega_{n0}$, that is, at $\hbar\omega=E_n-E_0$. The poles of the response function are the excitation energies of the system themselves. This is the true meaning of what we wrote in Chapter 1: “if we kick the system and listen to the reply, we can tell what the system wants to do even when we do not kick it.”

In this chapter we derive this single equation and read off from it everything that can be read off. Analyticity in the upper half-plane (causality), the separation into real and imaginary parts, the fluctuation–dissipation theorem, sum rules—what is called the “general theory” of linear response theory is almost all written into this one equation.

What you will learn in this chapter
  • The procedure for rewriting the Kubo formula in the Lehmann (spectral) representation by inserting a complete set of states (all the way through the time integral)
  • That the poles of $\chi^R(\omega)$ coincide with the excitation energies $\omega_{n0}=(E_n-E_0)/\hbar$, and that all of these poles lie slightly below the real axis
  • That, as a result, analyticity in the upper half-plane (= causality) holds automatically, and the Kramers–Kronig relations of Chapter 4 can be used for quantum systems as they are
  • That separating the real and imaginary parts with the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula turns the imaginary part into a “comb of delta functions”; the line spectrum of a finite system and the continuous spectrum of an infinite system
  • The definition of the spectral function $S_{AB}(\omega)$ and its Lehmann representation; detailed balance $S_{BA}(-\omega)=\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}S_{AB}(\omega)$
  • The fluctuation–dissipation theorem: that fluctuations $S$ and dissipation $\chi''$ are linked by the same function; that in the classical limit it reduces to Nyquist's formula for thermal noise
  • The $f$-sum rule (Thomas–Reiche–Kuhn): that the integral of $\omega\chi''$ becomes the expectation value of a double commutator, and that for the density operator it becomes $nq^2/m$
  • The difference between the time-ordered $\chi^T$ and the retarded $\chi^R$: Feynman diagrams build the former, whereas experiments measure the latter
Prerequisites: Chapter 7 (the Kubo formula), Chapter 6 (the Heisenberg picture and adiabatic switching), Chapter 5 (especially the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula $\frac{1}{x\pm\ii\eta}=\PV\frac1x\mp\ii\pi\delta(x)$), and Chapter 4 (the Kramers–Kronig relations). For quantum mechanics, you need only the insertion of a complete set of states and the basics of perturbation theory. For statistical mechanics, it suffices to understand the meaning of the partition function $Z=\Tr\,\ee^{-\beta\Ham_0}$.

8.1 Derivation of the Lehmann (Spectral) Representation

8.1.1 Setting the stage and notation

Let $\Ham_0$ be the Hamiltonian of the system. It is the “real” Hamiltonian, including the interactions, before any external perturbation is applied. We write its eigenstates as $\ket{n}$ and its eigenenergies as $E_n$:

$$ \begin{equation} \Ham_0\ket{n} = E_n\ket{n}, \qquad \sum_n \ket{n}\bra{n} = 1, \qquad \braket{n|m} = \delta_{nm} \label{eq:8-eigen} \end{equation} $$

We take $n=0$ to be the ground state and number the states so that $E_0 \le E_1 \le E_2 \le \cdots$. In Sections 8.1–8.5, we first treat absolute zero, $T=0$, that is, the case in which the system is in the ground state $\ket{0}$. The extension to finite temperature is given in Sections 8.1.4 and 8.5.3, and its full justification is given in Chapter 13 (the Matsubara formalism).

Notation

We write the matrix elements compactly as $A_{mn} \equiv \bra{m}\hat A\ket{n}$ and $B_{mn} \equiv \bra{m}\hat B\ket{n}$. We also define the excitation frequency measured from the ground state as follows:

$$ \begin{equation} \hbar\,\omega_{n0} \equiv E_n - E_0 \;\;(\ge 0), \qquad \hbar\,\omega_{nm} \equiv E_n - E_m \label{eq:8-omega-n0} \end{equation} $$

Note that $\omega_{n0}$ is a frequency (in units of $\mathrm{s}^{-1}$) and not an energy. Multiplying by $\hbar$ gives an energy. If this distinction is neglected, a factor of $\hbar$ will fail to match here and there in the equations that follow.

Let us rewrite the retarded response function derived in Chapter 7 by taking $t'=0$. By time-translation invariance (Postulate 2 of Chapter 1), $\chi^R_{AB}$ depends only on $t-t'$, so nothing is lost in generality:

$$ \begin{equation} \chi^R_{AB}(t) = -\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\,\theta(t)\,\bra{0}\,[\hat A_H(t),\hat B(0)]\,\ket{0} \label{eq:8-kubo} \end{equation} $$

Here $\hat A_H(t)$ is the operator in the Heisenberg picture with respect to $\Ham_0$ (Chapter 6):

$$ \begin{equation} \hat A_H(t) = \ee^{\ii\Ham_0 t/\hbar}\,\hat A\,\ee^{-\ii\Ham_0 t/\hbar} \label{eq:8-heisenberg} \end{equation} $$

Sign Convention — this book adopts $\Ham_{\text{ext}}=+f(t)\hat B$

As stated in Chapter 7, this book follows Fetter–Walecka and couples the external perturbation with a plus sign, $\Ham_{\text{ext}}(t) = f(t)\,\hat B$ (the FW $\Ham_{\text{ext}}=\int\dd^3x\,\phi(\xx,t)\hat\rho(\xx)$ has this form). Then the response is $\delta\braket{A(t)}=\int\chi^R_{AB}(t-t')f(t')\dd t'$, and $\chi^R$ takes the form of Eq. \eqref{eq:8-kubo}, with the minus sign.

On the other hand, Kubo's original paper and many statistical-mechanics textbooks adopt $\Ham_{\text{ext}}=-f(t)\hat B$ (the standard form in mechanics, in which a force $f$ couples to a coordinate $\hat B$ as $-f\hat B$). In this convention the overall sign of the response function is reversed:

$$ \chi^{\text{Kubo}}_{AB}(\omega) = -\,\chi^R_{AB}(\omega) $$

The mechanical susceptibility of Chapters 2 and 3 (the spring's $\chi(\omega)=[m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)-\ii\gamma\omega]^{-1}$) follows this Kubo-style convention because the potential was $-F\hat x$. Hence the apparent contradiction arises that “in Chapter 3, $\mathrm{Im}\,\chi>0$ meant absorption, but in this chapter $\chi''<0$ means absorption.” This is not a difference in physics but merely a difference in how the sign of $f$ is chosen. In Section 8.4.3 we confirm this correspondence, including signs and coefficients. If you become confused, please return to this box.

8.1.2 Inserting a complete set of states

Why does inserting a complete set of states work?

The difficulty with Eq. \eqref{eq:8-kubo} is that $\hat A_H(t)$ contains the exponential of an operator, $\ee^{-\ii\Ham_0t/\hbar}$. The exponential of an operator is, in general, hard even to expand.

When it acts on an eigenstate of $\Ham_0$, however, it becomes just a number: $\ee^{-\ii\Ham_0t/\hbar}\ket{n}=\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}\ket{n}$. So the only breakthrough is this: “insert a complete set of eigenstates, and have $\ee^{-\ii\Ham_0t/\hbar}$ mesh with the eigenstates.” The moment we insert it, every exponential of an operator turns into a phase factor that is a $c$-number. This is not a mere technique; it is almost the only method. From here on, every operation in quantum many-body theory that goes by the name “spectral representation” or “Lehmann representation” is this one.

Derivation 1: Unpacking the expectation value of the commutator

Step 1: Expanding the commutator into two terms.

$$ \bra{0}[\hat A_H(t),\hat B(0)]\ket{0} = \underbrace{\bra{0}\hat A_H(t)\hat B\ket{0}}_{(\mathrm{I})} - \underbrace{\bra{0}\hat B\hat A_H(t)\ket{0}}_{(\mathrm{II})} $$

Step 2: Inserting a complete set into the first term $(\mathrm{I})$. We put $\sum_n\ket{n}\bra{n}=1$ between $\hat A_H(t)$ and $\hat B$:

$$ (\mathrm{I}) = \bra{0}\hat A_H(t)\left(\sum_n\ket{n}\bra{n}\right)\hat B\ket{0} = \sum_n \bra{0}\hat A_H(t)\ket{n}\,B_{n0} $$

Step 3: Computing the matrix element of the Heisenberg operator. This is the heart of this section. We substitute Eq. \eqref{eq:8-heisenberg}, letting the exponential on the left act on the bra $\bra{0}$ on the left and the exponential on the right act on the ket $\ket{n}$ on the right. Since $\Ham_0$ is Hermitian, $\bra{0}\ee^{\ii\Ham_0t/\hbar} = \ee^{\ii E_0t/\hbar}\bra{0}$, and also $\ee^{-\ii\Ham_0t/\hbar}\ket{n}=\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}\ket{n}$. Hence

$$ \bra{0}\hat A_H(t)\ket{n} = \ee^{\ii E_0 t/\hbar}\,\ee^{-\ii E_n t/\hbar}\,\bra{0}\hat A\ket{n} = \ee^{-\ii(E_n-E_0)t/\hbar}\,A_{0n} = A_{0n}\,\ee^{-\ii\omega_{n0}t} $$

That is, the matrix element of the Heisenberg operator is just the static matrix element with a simple phase $\ee^{-\ii\omega_{n0}t}$ attached. The exponential of an operator has disappeared. Therefore $(\mathrm{I}) = \sum_n A_{0n}B_{n0}\,\ee^{-\ii\omega_{n0}t}$.

Step 4: The second term $(\mathrm{II})$ in the same way. This time we insert the complete set between $\hat B$ and $\hat A_H(t)$:

$$ (\mathrm{II}) = \sum_n \bra{0}\hat B\ket{n}\bra{n}\hat A_H(t)\ket{0} = \sum_n B_{0n}\,\bra{n}\hat A_H(t)\ket{0} $$

Noting that the order of the matrix elements is reversed, the same calculation as in Step 3 gives

$$ \bra{n}\hat A_H(t)\ket{0} = \ee^{\ii E_n t/\hbar}\,\ee^{-\ii E_0 t/\hbar}\,A_{n0} = A_{n0}\,\ee^{+\ii\omega_{n0}t} $$

The sign of the phase has become opposite. This is what later produces the “poles at positive frequencies” and the “poles at negative frequencies.” Therefore $(\mathrm{II}) = \sum_n B_{0n}A_{n0}\,\ee^{+\ii\omega_{n0}t}$.

Step 5: Putting the terms together. Returning to Eq. \eqref{eq:8-kubo}, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \chi^R_{AB}(t) = -\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\,\theta(t)\sum_n\left[ A_{0n}B_{n0}\,\ee^{-\ii\omega_{n0}t} - B_{0n}A_{n0}\,\ee^{+\ii\omega_{n0}t} \right] \label{eq:8-lehmann-t} \end{equation} $$

∎ The expectation value of the commutator has become a superposition of oscillations with the excitation frequencies $\omega_{n0}$. The weight $A_{0n}B_{n0}$ is the amplitude of the process in which we kick $\ket{n}$ out of the ground state with $\hat B$ and come back with $\hat A$.

Physical Meaning: How to read Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann-t}

When we “kick” the system with the operator $\hat B$ at time $0$, the ground state changes into the superposition of excited states $\hat B\ket{0}=\sum_n B_{n0}\ket{n}$. After that, each component $\ket{n}$ evolves in time, turning its phase $\ee^{-\ii\omega_{n0}t}$ at its own frequency $\omega_{n0}$. When we “listen to the reply” with $\hat A$ at time $t$, the amplitude $A_{0n}$ for returning to $\ket{0}$ enters as a multiplicative factor.

In other words, the response function is the sum over the processes in which the excitations created by the kick, each ringing at its own frequency, come back as the reply. This is exactly the same structure as when we said, for the damped oscillator of Chapter 2, that “$G(t)$ rings as $\sin\omega_1 t$”; the only difference is that there are countless ringing frequencies rather than one.

8.1.3 Carrying out the time integral

Derivation 2: The Fourier transform — without skipping a single integral

Following the convention of Chapter 1, we compute $\chi^R_{AB}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dd t\,\chi^R_{AB}(t)\,\ee^{\ii\omega t}$. Substituting Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann-t}, the lower limit of integration becomes $0$ thanks to $\theta(t)$:

$$ \chi^R_{AB}(\omega) = -\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\sum_n\left[ A_{0n}B_{n0}\int_0^{\infty}\!\!\dd t\,\ee^{\ii(\omega-\omega_{n0})t} - B_{0n}A_{n0}\int_0^{\infty}\!\!\dd t\,\ee^{\ii(\omega+\omega_{n0})t} \right] $$

This is where one gets stuck. If $X$ remains real, $\int_0^\infty \ee^{\ii X t}\dd t$ keeps oscillating as $t\to\infty$ and does not converge.

Inserting the convergence factor $\ee^{-\eta t}$. Recall the adiabatic switching introduced in Chapter 6. The external perturbation was switched on slowly from $0$ at $t=-\infty$ as $\ee^{\eta t}$ ($\eta>0$ is infinitesimal). From the side of the response function, this corresponds to damping the response with $\ee^{-\eta t}$ so that it does not drag along a memory of the infinite past. That is, we make the replacement

$$ \chi^R_{AB}(t) \;\longrightarrow\; \chi^R_{AB}(t)\,\ee^{-\eta t} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \omega \;\longrightarrow\; \omega + \ii\eta $$

and finally let $\eta\to0^+$. Physically, this means that we measure not over an infinitely long time but over a very long yet finite time $1/\eta$.

The first integral. Letting $X_1 \equiv \omega-\omega_{n0}+\ii\eta$, we have $\mathrm{Im}\,X_1=\eta>0$, so that $\abs{\ee^{\ii X_1 t}}=\ee^{-\eta t}\to0$ (as $t\to\infty$), and the integral indeed converges:

$$ \int_0^{\infty}\dd t\,\ee^{\ii X_1 t} = \left[\frac{\ee^{\ii X_1 t}}{\ii X_1}\right]_0^{\infty} = \frac{0 - 1}{\ii X_1} = -\frac{1}{\ii X_1} = \frac{\ii}{X_1} $$

In the last equality we used $1/\ii=-\ii$ ($-\frac{1}{\ii X}=-\frac{-\ii}{X}=\frac{\ii}{X}$). The second integral is exactly the same, with only the sign of $\omega_{n0}$ different: $\displaystyle\int_0^{\infty}\dd t\,\ee^{\ii(\omega+\omega_{n0}+\ii\eta)t} = \frac{\ii}{\omega+\omega_{n0}+\ii\eta}$.

Substituting. The factor multiplying everything is $-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\times\ii = -\frac{\ii^2}{\hbar} = +\frac{1}{\hbar}$.

$$ \begin{equation} \boxed{\; \chi^R_{AB}(\omega) = \frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_n\left[ \frac{A_{0n}B_{n0}}{\omega-\omega_{n0}+\ii\eta} -\frac{B_{0n}A_{n0}}{\omega+\omega_{n0}+\ii\eta} \right]\;} \label{eq:8-lehmann} \end{equation} $$

This is the Lehmann representation (the spectral representation). In Fetter–Walecka, Sections 7 and 31 contain equations of the same structure for Green's functions. From here on, everything in this chapter follows from looking at this single equation.

Notation Note: How seriously should we carry $\eta$ around?

The convention is to take the limit $\eta\to0^+$, but if we drop $\eta$, the equation becomes meaningless at $\omega=\omega_{n0}$. So in practice we proceed as follows.

  • When computing the real part: drop $\eta$ and interpret the expression as a principal value (Cauchy principal value $\PV$).
  • When computing the imaginary part: $\eta$ generates the delta function (Section 8.4). Do not drop $\eta$ here.
  • When computing numerically: take $\hbar\eta$ to be a small finite value (for example, $0.01$ eV) and replace the delta function by a Lorentzian of width $\eta$. This is what the parameters called “smearing” and “broadening” in optical spectra from first-principles calculations really are.

8.1.4 Generalization to finite temperature (a preview)

For $T>0$ the system is not in the ground state. It suffices to replace the expectation value in $\ket{0}$ by the thermal average with Boltzmann weights:

$$ \braket{\hat O} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{Z}\sum_m \ee^{-\beta E_m}\bra{m}\hat O\ket{m}, \qquad Z=\sum_m\ee^{-\beta E_m},\quad \beta=\frac{1}{k_BT} $$

In each step of the derivation we simply read $\ket{0}\to\ket{m}$ and $\omega_{n0}\to\omega_{nm}$ and, at the end, take a weighted sum over $m$, so the result is

$$ \begin{equation} \chi^R_{AB}(\omega) = \frac{1}{\hbar Z}\sum_{m,n}\ee^{-\beta E_m}\left[ \frac{A_{mn}B_{nm}}{\omega-\omega_{nm}+\ii\eta} -\frac{B_{mn}A_{nm}}{\omega+\omega_{nm}+\ii\eta} \right] \label{eq:8-lehmann-T} \end{equation} $$

Here $\hbar\omega_{nm}=E_n-E_m$. As $T\to0$, $\ee^{-\beta E_m}/Z\to\delta_{m0}$, and we return to Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann}. This corresponds to the “generalized Lehmann representation” of Fetter–Walecka, Section 31. The fluctuation–dissipation theorem of Section 8.6 follows from this form.

What to watch for at finite temperature

For $T>0$, $\omega_{nm}$ can also be negative ($E_n<E_m$, that is, a process of dropping down from a thermally excited state). This is decisively different from $T=0$, where only $\omega_{n0}\ge0$ appeared. This asymmetry gives rise to detailed balance (Section 8.5.3) and to the factor $1-\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}$ of the fluctuation–dissipation theorem.

8.2 The Poles Are the Excitation Energies

8.2.1 Reading off the pole positions

Let us view Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann} as a function on the complex $\omega$ plane. The points where the denominator vanishes, that is, the poles, are

$$ \begin{equation} \omega = +\omega_{n0} - \ii\eta \qquad\text{and}\qquad \omega = -\omega_{n0} - \ii\eta \label{eq:8-poles} \end{equation} $$

(with $n=1,2,3,\dots$). As $\eta\to0^+$ they approach $\pm\omega_{n0}$ on the real axis arbitrarily closely, but they always remain slightly below the real axis. Moreover,

$$ \hbar\,\omega_{n0} = E_n - E_0 = \text{excitation energy} $$

In other words, the real part of a pole of the response function is the excitation energy of the system itself. This is one of the most important conclusions of linear response theory, and it is the mathematical proof of the declaration made in Chapter 1: “if we kick the system and listen to the reply, we can find out what kinds of excitations it has.”

Physical Meaning: The two kinds of poles

  • $\omega=+\omega_{n0}$: when the frequency of the external force coincides with an excitation energy, the system absorbs the energy $\hbar\omega$ and is excited as $\ket{0}\to\ket{n}$. This is resonant absorption.
  • $\omega=-\omega_{n0}$: in the $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$ convention, a component with $\omega<0$ is one that rotates in the opposite sense, and it corresponds to a process in which the system emits energy. At $T=0$ there is nothing below the ground state, so this does not actually occur, but it appears symmetrically in the expression for the response function. As we will see in Section 8.5, this asymmetry (it occurs / it does not occur) is precisely the origin of the factor $1-\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}$ in the fluctuation–dissipation theorem.

This is exactly the same structure as that of $\chi(\omega)$ of the damped oscillator (Chapter 3), which had resonances at the two places $\omega\simeq\pm\omega_0$. The only difference is that in a quantum many-body system there are innumerably many resonances, $\omega_{10},\omega_{20},\dots$.

Advice from Prof. Takao Kotani: The response function is nothing but a superposition of $1/(\omega-\omega'+\ii\eta)$

Let us pause here and look at Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann} for its structure alone. If we gather the matrix elements together and call them weights $w_\alpha$, and write the pole positions as $\Omega_\alpha$, the Lehmann representation is

$$ \chi^R_{AB}(\omega) \;=\; \sum_\alpha \frac{w_\alpha}{\omega-\Omega_\alpha+\ii\eta} $$

and that single line says it all (the pole positions are $\Omega_\alpha=\pm\omega_{n0}$, and the weights are of two kinds, $w_\alpha=A_{0n}B_{n0}/\hbar$ and $-B_{0n}A_{n0}/\hbar$). Every denominator is a linear function of $\omega$, and the way $\ii\eta$ enters is the same in all the terms. In other words, the response function is nothing more than a superposition of first-order poles (simple poles) with specified positions and weights. The analyticity in the upper half-plane that we derive in the rest of this chapter, the Kramers–Kronig relations, the comb of delta functions, and the sum rules are all nothing but ways of reading this one line.

Why do only first-order poles appear? Because the Schrödinger equation $\ii\hbar\,\partial_t\ket{\psi}=\Ham\ket{\psi}$ is first order in time. Because it is first order, the time evolution of an eigenstate is a single factor of a single frequency, $\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}$, and the matrix elements of Heisenberg operators likewise unravel into a single phase factor, $\ee^{\mp\ii\omega_{n0}t}$ (Section 8.1.2). If we multiply this by the $\theta(t)$ of causality and Fourier transform it, then, exactly as we did in Derivation 2,

$$ \int_0^{\infty}\dd t\,\ee^{\ii(\omega-\omega_{n0}+\ii\eta)t} \;=\; \frac{\ii}{\omega-\omega_{n0}+\ii\eta} $$

so that one phase factor gives rise to one first-order pole. For contrast, recall the damped oscillator of Chapters 2 and 3. There the equation of motion was second order in time, so the denominator of $\chi(\omega)=\left[m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)-\ii\gamma\omega\right]^{-1}$ is a quadratic in $\omega$, and two poles appeared, $\omega_\pm=\pm\omega_1-\ii\Gamma$. On the classical side, the fact that the equation of motion is second order in time shows up directly as the degree 2 of the denominator. To be sure, on the quantum side, too, the commutator produces a pair of phase factors $\ee^{\mp\ii\omega_{n0}t}$, so if we combine the two terms as in Example 8.1, the denominator again becomes a quadratic. The difference lies not in the overall degree but in the fact that each term before the combination is necessarily first order. And a quadratic, too, returns to two first-order poles when expanded in partial fractions; indeed

$$ \frac{1}{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)-\ii\gamma\omega} = -\frac{1}{2m\omega_1}\left[\frac{1}{\omega-\omega_1+\ii\Gamma}-\frac{1}{\omega+\omega_1+\ii\Gamma}\right] \qquad\left(\Gamma=\frac{\gamma}{2m},\ \omega_1=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\Gamma^2}\right) $$

holds identically (this is the same decomposition that was shown in the box in Chapter 4, Section 4.4.4, by writing $\gamma=2m\Gamma$). It agrees exactly with the quantum result obtained in Example 8.1 of the next subsection, under the replacements $\omega_1\to\omega_0$ and $\Gamma\to\eta$. In the end, the only job left for a quantum many-body system is “how to find the pole positions $\Omega_\alpha$ and the weights $w_\alpha$.”

8.2.2 Example: the harmonic oscillator — quantum agrees with classical

Example 8.1: The Lehmann representation of the one-dimensional harmonic oscillator

For $\Ham_0=\dfrac{\hat p^2}{2m}+\dfrac12 m\omega_0^2\hat x^2$, find the response function $\chi^R_{xx}(\omega)$ for $\hat A=\hat B=\hat x$ from the Lehmann representation, and compare it with the classical result of Chapter 3.

Solution. In terms of the creation and annihilation operators we can write $\hat x=\sqrt{\dfrac{\hbar}{2m\omega_0}}(\hat a+\hat a^\dagger)$. Since $\hat a\ket{0}=0$ and $\hat a^\dagger\ket{0}=\ket{1}$,

$$ \hat x\ket{0} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_0}}\,\ket{1} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x_{n0} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_0}}\,\delta_{n1} $$

that is, the only state that can be reached from the ground state is $\ket{1}$. The excitation frequency is $\omega_{10}=\omega_0$. The sum in the Lehmann representation, Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann}, reduces to the single term $n=1$, and we obtain

$$ \chi^R_{xx}(\omega) = \frac{1}{\hbar}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_0}\left[ \frac{1}{\omega-\omega_0+\ii\eta} - \frac{1}{\omega+\omega_0+\ii\eta}\right] $$

The $\hbar$ has cancelled out. Bringing the terms in the square brackets to a common denominator (setting $\eta\to0$),

$$ \frac{1}{\omega-\omega_0}-\frac{1}{\omega+\omega_0} = \frac{(\omega+\omega_0)-(\omega-\omega_0)}{\omega^2-\omega_0^2} = \frac{2\omega_0}{\omega^2-\omega_0^2} $$

Therefore

$$ \chi^R_{xx}(\omega) = \frac{1}{2m\omega_0}\cdot\frac{2\omega_0}{\omega^2-\omega_0^2} = \frac{1}{m(\omega^2-\omega_0^2)} = -\frac{1}{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)} $$

By the sign convention of this book (see the warning in Section 8.1.1), the mechanical susceptibility with respect to a force $F$ is $\chi^{\text{mech}}=-\chi^R_{xx}$, so

$$ \chi^{\text{mech}}(\omega) = \frac{1}{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)} $$

This is precisely the expression obtained from the response function $\chi(\omega)=[m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)-\ii\gamma\omega]^{-1}$ of the damped oscillator of Chapter 3 by setting $\gamma\to0$. ∎

What is surprising here. The $\hbar$ has disappeared completely. The linear response of a harmonic oscillator is exactly the same whether we compute it quantum mechanically or classically. The $\hbar$ cancelled between the numerator of $x_{01}^2=\hbar/2m\omega_0$ and the denominator of the prefactor $1/\hbar$ of the Lehmann representation.

This is no accident. It is because, for the harmonic oscillator, Ehrenfest's theorem holds exactly and expectation values obey the classical equation of motion exactly. Conversely, as long as we look only at linear response, the harmonic oscillator does not tell us whether it is quantum or classical. The quantum nature appears when an anharmonic term is included, or when we look at the fluctuation $\braket{\hat x^2}$ itself (Section 8.6).

A feel for the numbers. Take the electron mass $m=9.11\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$ and an excitation energy $\hbar\omega_0=2\ \mathrm{eV}$ (visible light); then $\omega_0=3.04\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and

$$ x_{01} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.055\times10^{-34}}{2\times9.11\times10^{-31}\times3.04\times10^{15}}} = 1.4\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m} = 1.4\ \text{Å} $$

This is exactly the size of an atom. Since the matrix element is a quantity that expresses “how far the electron cloud can oscillate,” this is an entirely reasonable value.

Why is it that “looking at the poles tells us about the collective excitations”?

In Example 8.1 there was only one pole. In a system of $10^{23}$ particles, however, the number of states $\ket{n}$ is astronomical, and the $\omega_{n0}$ are packed densely and continuously. Even so, the spectra seen in experiments often show only a few sharp peaks. Why?

The answer lies in the magnitude of the matrix element $A_{0n}B_{n0}$. Among the innumerable excited states, only a very small fraction can be kicked out of the ground state by $\hat B$ (those with $B_{n0}\ne0$). For example, if we kick with the density operator $\hat\rho_\qq$, we can create only excitations that bring in the momentum $\hbar\qq$. Among those, the special states in which the amplitude is concentrated—these are the collective excitations (plasmons, phonons, magnons, zero sound).

In Chapter 11 we find the plasmon as a solution of $\epsilon(\qq,\omega)=0$, but its true identity is “the one among the poles of $\chi_{\rho\rho}$ whose matrix element is abnormally large.” This is why following the poles of the response function amounts directly to searching for the collective excitations.

8.3 Analyticity in the Upper Half-Plane — Causality Comes for Free

8.3.1 The meaning of all the poles lying in the lower half-plane

Theorem 8.1 (Analyticity in the upper half-plane)

Whether $T=0$ or $T>0$, the retarded response function $\chi^R_{AB}(\omega)$ is analytic in the upper half-plane $\mathrm{Im}\,\omega>0$ of the complex $\omega$ plane.

Proof (two ways)

Proof 1: From the Lehmann representation. The denominator of each term in Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann-T} is $\omega\mp\omega_{nm}+\ii\eta$. For $\mathrm{Im}\,\omega>0$ we have

$$ \mathrm{Im}(\omega\mp\omega_{nm}+\ii\eta) = \mathrm{Im}\,\omega + \eta > 0 $$

so the denominator never vanishes (since $\omega_{nm}$ is a real number, it does not contribute to the imaginary part). Hence there is no pole in the upper half-plane. $\blacksquare$

Proof 2: Directly from causality. Since Eq. \eqref{eq:8-kubo} carries a factor $\theta(t)$, we have $\chi^R_{AB}(\omega) = \int_0^{\infty}\dd t\,\chi^R_{AB}(t)\,\ee^{\ii\omega t}$. Writing $\omega=\omega'+\ii\omega''$ ($\omega''>0$), the absolute value of the integrand is

$$ \abs{\chi^R_{AB}(t)\,\ee^{\ii\omega t}} = \abs{\chi^R_{AB}(t)}\,\ee^{-\omega'' t} $$

so it is exponentially suppressed as $t\to+\infty$. The integral converges ever better in the upper half-plane, and it can be differentiated with respect to $\omega$. That is, it is analytic. $\blacksquare$

∎ It is only natural that the two proofs reach the same conclusion: since $\theta(t)$ is what produced the $+\ii\eta$ in the denominator of Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann}, the two are restatements of the same thing. The correspondence “causality $\Longleftrightarrow$ analyticity in the upper half-plane” that we wrote down in Chapter 1, Section 1.4, holds for quantum systems just as it stands.

Physical Meaning: Why “upper”? A way to remember it

$\theta(t)$ is the instruction “use only the future.” In the Fourier convention of this book, we multiply by $\ee^{+\ii\omega t}$ and integrate over $t>0$, so if we make the imaginary part of $\omega$ positive, $\ee^{-(\mathrm{Im}\,\omega)t}$ decays. That is why a retarded function is analytic in the upper half-plane. Conversely, poles can lie only in the lower half-plane.

“The reply comes late” = “only the future is used” = “analytic in the upper half-plane” = “the poles lie in the lower half-plane.” These four statements are all the same thing. Conversely, for the advanced function $\chi^A$, which uses only $t<0$, the poles lie in the upper half-plane.

8.3.2 The Kramers–Kronig relations apply to quantum systems as they are

In Chapter 4, for classical response functions, we derived from analyticity in the upper half-plane and the decay as $\abs{\omega}\to\infty$ alone the Kramers–Kronig (KK) relations

$$ \begin{equation} \chi'_{AB}(\omega) = \frac{1}{\pi}\,\PV\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\dd\omega'\, \frac{\chi''_{AB}(\omega')}{\omega'-\omega}, \qquad \chi''_{AB}(\omega) = -\frac{1}{\pi}\,\PV\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\dd\omega'\, \frac{\chi'_{AB}(\omega')}{\omega'-\omega} \label{eq:8-kk} \end{equation} $$

(with $\chi^R=\chi'+\ii\chi''$). By Theorem 8.1, this derivation carries over to quantum many-body systems exactly as it stands. No matter how strong the interactions are and no matter how many particles there are, the KK relations hold exactly.

Let us also check the decay as $\abs{\omega}\to\infty$

The derivation of the KK relations required that “the contribution from the large semicircle vanishes” (Jordan's lemma, Chapter 5). Letting $\abs{\omega}\to\infty$ in the Lehmann representation, Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann}, we find that each term decays as $1/\omega$, and summing gives

$$ \chi^R_{AB}(\omega) \;\xrightarrow{\ \abs{\omega}\to\infty\ }\; \frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\sum_n\left(A_{0n}B_{n0}-B_{0n}A_{n0}\right) = \frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\braket{[\hat A,\hat B]} $$

Here we have used the complete set of states to fold the sums back, $\sum_n A_{0n}B_{n0}=\bra{0}\hat A\hat B\ket{0}$ and $\sum_n B_{0n}A_{n0}=\bra{0}\hat B\hat A\ket{0}$. Since $\chi^R\sim 1/\omega$, it indeed tends to zero, and so it satisfies the condition of Jordan's lemma.

This high-frequency asymptotic form is also physically meaningful. If $\hat A$ and $\hat B$ commute, $\chi^R$ decays faster than $1/\omega$, and then the $1/\omega^2$ term becomes the leading one. That is the $f$-sum rule of Section 8.7. Each order of the high-frequency expansion corresponds to one sum rule.

Physical Meaning: Why are the KK relations so welcome (from the experimentalist's standpoint)?

In an experiment, what we can measure is usually only one of $\chi'$ and $\chi''$.

  • Optical absorption measurement → absorption coefficient → only $\chi''$ (the imaginary part)
  • Reflectance measurement → only $\abs{r}$ (the phase cannot be measured)
  • Inelastic neutron and X-ray scattering → the dynamic structure factor $S(\qq,\omega)$, that is, only a quantity proportional to $\chi''$ (Chapter 12)

The KK relations, Eq. \eqref{eq:8-kk}, guarantee that if one of the two is measured over all frequencies, the other follows from an integral. The standard method for separating the refractive index and the extinction coefficient from a reflectance spectrum (Kramers–Kronig analysis) is exactly this.

And the claim of this section is that this procedure is rigorously correct whether the system is a strongly correlated electron system or a superconductor. KK analysis is called a “model-independent method of analysis” because it uses nothing but the law of causality.

8.4 Separating the Real and Imaginary Parts — A Comb of Delta Functions

8.4.1 Using the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula

We restate the formula derived in Chapter 5, Section 5.8. For real $x$ and $\eta\to0^+$, in the sense of distributions,

$$ \begin{equation} \frac{1}{x\pm\ii\eta} = \PV\frac{1}{x} \mp \ii\pi\,\delta(x) \label{eq:8-sp} \end{equation} $$

holds ($\PV$ denotes the Cauchy principal value). We apply this formula to both denominators of the Lehmann representation \eqref{eq:8-lehmann}. Both denominators are of the $+\ii\eta$ type, so we use the upper sign ($-\ii\pi\delta$).

Derivation 3: Decomposition into $\chi^R=\chi'+\ii\chi''$

We substitute Eq. \eqref{eq:8-sp} into Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann}:

$$ \chi^R_{AB}(\omega) = \frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_n\Big\{ A_{0n}B_{n0}\Big[\PV\frac{1}{\omega-\omega_{n0}} - \ii\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_{n0})\Big] - B_{0n}A_{n0}\Big[\PV\frac{1}{\omega+\omega_{n0}} - \ii\pi\delta(\omega+\omega_{n0})\Big] \Big\} $$

Sorting the result into a principal-value part and a delta-function part, we name them $\chi'_{AB}$ and $\chi''_{AB}$, respectively:

$$ \begin{equation} \chi'_{AB}(\omega) = \frac{1}{\hbar}\,\PV\sum_n\left[ \frac{A_{0n}B_{n0}}{\omega-\omega_{n0}} - \frac{B_{0n}A_{n0}}{\omega+\omega_{n0}}\right] \label{eq:8-chi-re} \end{equation} $$
$$ \begin{equation} \chi''_{AB}(\omega) = -\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\sum_n\left[ A_{0n}B_{n0}\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{n0}) - B_{0n}A_{n0}\,\delta(\omega+\omega_{n0})\right] \label{eq:8-chi-im} \end{equation} $$

∎ Thus $\chi^R_{AB}=\chi'_{AB}+\ii\chi''_{AB}$.

Is $\chi''$ the “imaginary part”? — Care is needed in general

Since $A_{0n}B_{n0}$ is in general a complex number, the quantities $\chi'$ and $\chi''$ defined by Eqs. \eqref{eq:8-chi-re} and \eqref{eq:8-chi-im} are not necessarily the real and imaginary parts of $\chi^R$ themselves. Strictly speaking, $\chi'$ and $\chi''$ should be called the “principal-value part” and the “delta-function part.”

In most of the situations treated in this book, however, namely when

  • $\hat A=\hat B$ is a Hermitian operator (density, current, magnetization, displacement, etc.), or
  • $\hat B=\hat A^\dagger$ (such as the pair $\hat\rho_\qq$ and $\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger$),

$A_{0n}B_{n0}=\abs{A_{0n}}^2\ge0$ is real, and $\chi'=\mathrm{Re}\,\chi^R$ and $\chi''=\mathrm{Im}\,\chi^R$ hold exactly. From here on we keep this case in mind and state it explicitly when necessary.

8.4.2 $\chi''$ as a comb of delta functions

Writing out the case $\hat A=\hat B$ (Hermitian), Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chi-im} becomes

$$ \begin{equation} \chi''_{AA}(\omega) = -\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\sum_n \abs{A_{0n}}^2 \left[\delta(\omega-\omega_{n0}) - \delta(\omega+\omega_{n0})\right] \label{eq:8-chi-im-AA} \end{equation} $$

Put into words, this reads as follows.

Physical Meaning: $\chi''$ is a bar graph of the excitation spectrum

Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chi-im-AA} says that $\chi''_{AA}(\omega)$ is a “comb” of delta functions lined up at the following positions and with the following heights:

  • Position: $\omega=\pm\omega_{n0}$, that is, the excitation energies $\pm(E_n-E_0)/\hbar$
  • Height (weight): $\abs{A_{0n}}^2$, that is, how strongly the operator $\hat A$ can kick the state $\ket{n}$ out of the ground state

Measuring $\chi''$ is, quite literally, equivalent to measuring the positions and intensities of the excitation levels of the system.

Also, from Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chi-im-AA} we can read off at once that $\chi''_{AA}$ is an odd function of $\omega$ (under $\omega\to-\omega$ the two delta functions are interchanged). The property that we showed classically in Exercise 1.2 of Chapter 1 is seen here at the level of matrix elements.

8.4.3 Sign and energy dissipation — agreement with Fermi's golden rule

Looking at Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chi-im-AA} for $\omega>0$, the delta function in the second term does not contribute, and we obtain

$$ \chi''_{AA}(\omega) = -\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\sum_n \abs{A_{0n}}^2\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{n0}) \;\le\; 0 \qquad (\omega>0) $$

Hence this quantity is negative, as announced in the warning of Section 8.1.1. Let us confirm by an explicit calculation that this is consistent with energy absorption.

Derivation 4: Calculating the absorbed power

Let the external perturbation be $\Ham_{\text{ext}}(t)=f(t)\hat A$ with $f(t)=f_0\cos\omega t$ (the quantity we measure is also the same $\hat A$). The rate of change of the energy of the system, which comes from the explicit time dependence of the Hamiltonian, is

$$ \frac{\dd E}{\dd t} = \braket{\frac{\partial \Ham}{\partial t}} = \dot f(t)\,\braket{\hat A(t)} $$

By linear response, $\delta\braket{\hat A(t)}=\int\chi^R_{AA}(t-t')f(t')\dd t'$. Substituting $f(t)=\frac{f_0}{2}(\ee^{-\ii\omega t}+\ee^{\ii\omega t})$ and using $\chi^R(-\omega)=\chi^R(\omega)^*$ (a property of real response functions), we obtain

$$ \delta\braket{\hat A(t)} = \frac{f_0}{2}\left[\chi^R(\omega)\ee^{-\ii\omega t}+\chi^R(\omega)^*\ee^{\ii\omega t}\right] = f_0\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left[\chi^R(\omega)\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\right] $$

Multiplying out $\chi^R=\chi'+\ii\chi''$ and $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}=\cos\omega t-\ii\sin\omega t$ and taking the real part, we get

$$ \delta\braket{\hat A(t)} = f_0\left[\chi'(\omega)\cos\omega t + \chi''(\omega)\sin\omega t\right] $$

Meanwhile, $\dot f(t) = -f_0\omega\sin\omega t$. Hence

$$ \frac{\dd E}{\dd t} = -f_0\omega\sin\omega t \cdot f_0\left[\chi'\cos\omega t+\chi''\sin\omega t\right] $$

We average over one period. Since $\overline{\sin\omega t\cos\omega t}=0$ and $\overline{\sin^2\omega t}=\tfrac12$,

$$ \begin{equation} P \equiv \overline{\frac{\dd E}{\dd t}} = -\frac{1}{2}\,f_0^2\,\omega\,\chi''_{AA}(\omega) \label{eq:8-power} \end{equation} $$

∎ By the second law of thermodynamics, the energy flowing from the external force into the system must be positive ($P\ge0$). Therefore, in the convention of this book,

$$ \omega\,\chi''_{AA}(\omega) \le 0 \qquad\text{(for all }\omega\text{)} $$

is required, and Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chi-im-AA} satisfies precisely this ($\chi''\le0$ for $\omega>0$ and $\chi''\ge0$ for $\omega<0$). Switching to the Kubo-style convention $\chi^{\text{Kubo}}=-\chi^R$ gives $\omega\chi''^{\text{Kubo}}\ge0$, which agrees with the statement of Chapter 3 that “$\mathrm{Im}\,\chi>0$ means absorption.” Only the sign convention differs; the physics is exactly the same.

Check: This is Fermi's golden rule itself

We substitute Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chi-im-AA} into Eq. \eqref{eq:8-power} ($\omega>0$):

$$ P = \frac{\pi f_0^2\,\omega}{2\hbar}\sum_n\abs{A_{0n}}^2\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{n0}) $$

Rewriting $\delta(\omega-\omega_{n0}) = \hbar\,\delta(\hbar\omega-\hbar\omega_{n0})$ and rearranging, we obtain

$$ P = \hbar\omega\times\underbrace{\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\sum_n\abs{\frac{f_0}{2}A_{n0}}^2\,\delta\big(\hbar\omega-(E_n-E_0)\big)}_{\text{sum of the transition probabilities}\ W_{0\to n}\ \text{per unit time}} $$

The part enclosed by the underbrace is exactly the expression of Fermi's golden rule for the perturbation $\frac{f_0}{2}\hat A$ (the $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$ component of $f_0\cos\omega t$, which is the one that causes absorption). And the factor $\hbar\omega$ in front is the energy absorbed in a single transition.

That is, $P = (\text{energy absorbed per transition})\times(\text{number of transitions per unit time})$. The absorbed power of linear response theory agrees perfectly with an elementary result of quantum mechanics. We may understand that $\chi''$ is the golden rule written for all frequencies at once.

8.4.4 From a line spectrum to a continuous spectrum

Read as it stands, Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chi-im-AA} is a “comb of delta functions,” that is, a line spectrum. But the optical spectra of metals and semiconductors seen in experiments are smooth, continuous curves. Is this not a contradiction?

The difference between finite and infinite systems

In a finite system (an atom, a molecule, a quantum dot) the levels are discrete, and the spacing of the $\omega_{n0}$ is finite. Therefore $\chi''$ literally becomes a line spectrum. The absorption lines of atoms and the vibrational spectra of molecules are of this kind.

In an infinite system (the thermodynamic limit) the situation is different. As the volume $V\to\infty$, the level spacing becomes $\propto 1/V\to0$, and the delta functions line up infinitely densely. Replacing the sum with an integral gives

$$ \sum_n \abs{A_{0n}}^2\delta(\omega-\omega_{n0}) \;\longrightarrow\; \int \dd\omega'\,\rho_A(\omega')\,\delta(\omega-\omega') = \rho_A(\omega) $$

which is a smooth function (a weighted density of states). This is the continuous spectrum.

A feel for the numbers. Consider a copper cube of side $1\ \mu\mathrm{m}$. The volume is $10^{-18}\ \mathrm{m^3}$ and the electron density is $n=8.5\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, so the number of electrons is $N\approx8.5\times10^{10}$. The level spacing near the Fermi surface is roughly

$$ \Delta E \approx \frac{2\varepsilon_F}{3N} = \frac{2\times7\ \mathrm{eV}}{3\times8.5\times10^{10}} \approx 5\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{eV} $$

Compared with the room-temperature thermal energy $k_BT=25.85\ \mathrm{meV}$ or the resolution of a spectrometer (at best $1\ \mathrm{meV}$), this is more than seven orders of magnitude smaller. So experimentally it can be regarded as perfectly continuous. Even a micrometer-size sample is already an “infinite system.”

8.4.5 Checking the Kramers–Kronig relations with the Lehmann representation

Example 8.2: Direct verification of the KK relations

Substitute $\chi''$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chi-im} into the right-hand side of the KK relations \eqref{eq:8-kk}, and verify that $\chi'$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chi-re} results.

Solution. We substitute into the right-hand side of the KK relations. Because of the delta functions, the $\omega'$ integral is finished in an instant:

$$ \frac{1}{\pi}\PV\!\!\int\!\dd\omega'\,\frac{\chi''_{AB}(\omega')}{\omega'-\omega} = \frac{1}{\pi}\PV\!\!\int\!\dd\omega'\,\frac{1}{\omega'-\omega} \left(-\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\right)\sum_n\left[A_{0n}B_{n0}\delta(\omega'-\omega_{n0})-B_{0n}A_{n0}\delta(\omega'+\omega_{n0})\right] $$ $$ = -\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_n\left[\frac{A_{0n}B_{n0}}{\omega_{n0}-\omega}-\frac{B_{0n}A_{n0}}{-\omega_{n0}-\omega}\right] = \frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_n\left[\frac{A_{0n}B_{n0}}{\omega-\omega_{n0}}-\frac{B_{0n}A_{n0}}{\omega+\omega_{n0}}\right] $$

The last step merely flips the signs of the numerators and denominators. This is nothing other than $\chi'_{AB}(\omega)$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chi-re}. ∎

That this check works confirms that the argument of Chapter 4 (derivation by complex integration) and that of Chapter 8 (construction by inserting a complete set of states) describe exactly the same physics with different tools.

8.5 The Spectral Function $S_{AB}(\omega)$

8.5.1 A correlation function that is not a commutator

The $\chi^R$ treated so far contained the commutator $[\hat A_H(t),\hat B]$. In experiments, however, and especially in scattering experiments (Chapter 12), what is measured directly is not a commutator but the expectation value of the plain product $\hat A_H(t)\hat B(0)$. We therefore make the following definition.

Definition: Spectral function (dynamic correlation function)

$$ \begin{equation} S_{AB}(\omega) \equiv \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dd t\;\ee^{\ii\omega t}\, \braket{\,\hat A_H(t)\,\hat B(0)\,} \label{eq:8-S-def} \end{equation} $$

$\braket{\cdots}$ is the ground-state expectation value $\bra{0}\cdots\ket{0}$ for $T=0$ and the thermal average for $T>0$. The prefactor $1/2\pi$ depends on the convention, so always check it when comparing with other books (with the convention of this book, Eq. \eqref{eq:8-S-lehmann} below comes out clean).

Taking $\hat A=\hat\rho_\qq$ and $\hat B=\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger=\hat\rho_{-\qq}$, the quantity $S_{\rho\rho^\dagger}(\qq,\omega)$ is directly proportional to the cross sections of neutron scattering and inelastic X-ray scattering (Chapter 12). Since $\hat\rho_\qq$ is not Hermitian for finite $\qq$, we must not take $\hat A=\hat B=\hat\rho_\qq$: in a uniform system, momentum conservation would then make the result vanish identically for $\qq\ne0$. Always pair $\hat\rho_\qq$ with $\hat\rho_\qq^\dagger$. In Chapter 12, moreover, the quantity $S(\qq,\omega)=S_{\rho\rho^\dagger}(\qq,\omega)/N$, obtained by further dividing by the particle number $N$, is called the dynamic structure factor (this is the Fetter–Walecka convention; dividing by $N$ puts the sum rule in a clean form that does not contain $N$).

Why do we need two correlation functions?

The difference between $\chi^R$ (the commutator) and $S$ (the plain product) is physically clear.

  • $\chi^R$ is “the reply when we kick the system.” It has meaning only once we apply a force from outside. It represents dissipation.
  • $S$ is “the size of the shaking that goes on by itself.” No external force is applied at all. It is the spectrum of the fluctuation, that is, of how the value of $\hat A$ fluctuates in time in the equilibrium state. It represents fluctuation.

These two correspond to entirely different experiments. Nevertheless, as we will see in Section 8.6, the two are linked by a simple relation. That relation is the fluctuation–dissipation theorem, the pinnacle of linear response theory.

8.5.2 Lehmann representation of $S_{AB}$

Derivation 5: Spectral representation of $S_{AB}(\omega)$

Suppose $T=0$. By exactly the same calculation as in Steps 2 and 3 of Section 8.1.2, we have

$$ \bra{0}\hat A_H(t)\hat B\ket{0} = \sum_n A_{0n}B_{n0}\,\ee^{-\ii\omega_{n0}t} $$

Substituting this into Eq. \eqref{eq:8-S-def}:

$$ S_{AB}(\omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_n A_{0n}B_{n0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dd t\;\ee^{\ii(\omega-\omega_{n0})t} $$

This time there is no $\theta(t)$, so the range of integration is all of time, and no convergence factor is needed. Using the integral representation of the delta function

$$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dd t\;\ee^{\ii X t} = 2\pi\,\delta(X) $$

the factor $2\pi$ cancels exactly against the prefactor $1/2\pi$, and we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} S_{AB}(\omega) = \sum_n A_{0n}B_{n0}\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{n0}) \qquad (T=0) \label{eq:8-S-lehmann} \end{equation} $$

∎ We have arrived at an extremely simple form. Unlike $\chi^R$, it has no principal-value part; it is a pure comb of delta functions.

Physical Meaning: At $T=0$, $S(\omega<0)=0$

Since $\omega_{n0}\ge0$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:8-S-lehmann}, $S_{AB}(\omega)$ is identically zero for $\omega<0$.

This says something obvious. At $T=0$ the system is in its ground state, and there are no states of lower energy. So the system can only receive energy; it cannot give any up. That is why no fluctuation spectrum appears at $\omega<0$ (the side on which the system emits energy).

This asymmetry corresponds to the $T\to0$ limit ($\to1$) of the factor $1-\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}$ in Section 8.6.

Sum Rule: Integrating $S$ over all frequencies gives the size of the fluctuation

Integrating Eq. \eqref{eq:8-S-def} over all $\omega$, we obtain

$$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\dd\omega\,S_{AB}(\omega) = \int\!\dd\omega\,\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\!\dd t\,\ee^{\ii\omega t}\braket{\hat A_H(t)\hat B} = \int\!\dd t\,\delta(t)\braket{\hat A_H(t)\hat B} = \braket{\hat A\hat B} $$

(we used $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\dd\omega\,\ee^{\ii\omega t}=\delta(t)$). In particular, when $\hat A=\hat B$ and $\braket{\hat A}=0$,

$$ \begin{equation} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dd\omega\;S_{AA}(\omega) = \braket{\hat A^2} = \braket{(\Delta A)^2} \label{eq:8-S-sum} \end{equation} $$

That is, the total area of $S$ is the mean-square fluctuation itself. This shows that the statement “$S$ represents fluctuation” is literally correct.

8.5.3 Detailed balance (finite temperature)

At finite temperature, by the same procedure as was used to derive Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann-T}, we obtain:

$$ \begin{equation} S_{AB}(\omega) = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{m,n}\ee^{-\beta E_m}\,A_{mn}B_{nm}\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{nm}) \label{eq:8-S-lehmann-T} \end{equation} $$

From this follows the relation that is the heart of the fluctuation–dissipation theorem.

Theorem 8.2 (Detailed balance)

$$ \begin{equation} S_{BA}(-\omega) = \ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}\,S_{AB}(\omega) \label{eq:8-detailed-balance} \end{equation} $$

Derivation 6: Proof of detailed balance

Step 1: Writing down the left-hand side. In Eq. \eqref{eq:8-S-lehmann-T}, interchange $A\leftrightarrow B$ and set $\omega\to-\omega$:

$$ S_{BA}(-\omega) = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{m,n}\ee^{-\beta E_m}\,B_{mn}A_{nm}\,\delta(-\omega-\omega_{nm}) $$

Step 2: Interchanging the summation indices $m$ and $n$. This is merely a relabeling of names, and the value of the sum does not change:

$$ S_{BA}(-\omega) = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{m,n}\ee^{-\beta E_n}\,B_{nm}A_{mn}\,\delta(-\omega-\omega_{mn}) $$

Here $\omega_{mn}=-\omega_{nm}$, so $\delta(-\omega-\omega_{mn})=\delta(-\omega+\omega_{nm})=\delta(\omega-\omega_{nm})$ (the delta function is an even function). Hence

$$ S_{BA}(-\omega) = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{m,n}\ee^{-\beta E_n}\,A_{mn}B_{nm}\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{nm}) $$

Comparing with Eq. \eqref{eq:8-S-lehmann-T}, the only difference is that the weight has changed from $\ee^{-\beta E_m}$ to $\ee^{-\beta E_n}$.

Step 3: Rewriting the weight on the support of the delta function. The delta function $\delta(\omega-\omega_{nm})$ is nonzero only when $\omega=\omega_{nm}=(E_n-E_m)/\hbar$, that is, only when

$$ E_n = E_m + \hbar\omega $$

Therefore, on the support of the delta function we can write

$$ \ee^{-\beta E_n} = \ee^{-\beta(E_m+\hbar\omega)} = \ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}\,\ee^{-\beta E_m} $$

Since $\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}$ is a common factor that does not depend on $m,n$, it can be taken outside the sum:

$$ S_{BA}(-\omega) = \ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}\cdot\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{m,n}\ee^{-\beta E_m}A_{mn}B_{nm}\delta(\omega-\omega_{nm}) = \ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}\,S_{AB}(\omega) $$

∎

Physical Meaning: Intensity ratio of the Stokes and anti-Stokes lines

Eq. \eqref{eq:8-detailed-balance} asserts that the ratio between the likelihoods of “the process in which energy $\hbar\omega$ is given to the system” and “the process in which it is taken away from the system” is the Boltzmann factor $\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}$. This is because, for the system to give up energy, it must first possess that much energy through thermal excitation, and the probability of that is $\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}$.

This can be seen directly in experiments. In Raman scattering, the process that creates one phonon (the Stokes line, $\omega>0$) and the process that annihilates one phonon (the anti-Stokes line, $\omega<0$) appear as a pair. Their intensity ratio comes out as

$$ \frac{I_{\text{anti-Stokes}}}{I_{\text{Stokes}}} = \ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega_{\text{ph}}} $$

Note, however, that what is compared here is strictly the ratio of the $S$'s. The measured Raman intensity is multiplied by a cross-section factor that depends on the frequency of the scattered light, $(\omega_L+\omega_{\text{ph}})^4/(\omega_L-\omega_{\text{ph}})^4$ (about $1.25$ when the $520\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ line of silicon is excited at $532\ \mathrm{nm}$), and by the instrument response, so in actual temperature measurements one uses the ratio extracted after correcting for these. The numbers below are the ratios after this correction.

Numerical example. The optical phonon of silicon is at $520\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, that is, $\hbar\omega_{\text{ph}}=64.5\ \mathrm{meV}$. At room temperature, $300\ \mathrm{K}$ ($k_BT=25.85\ \mathrm{meV}$),

$$ \frac{I_{\text{anti-Stokes}}}{I_{\text{Stokes}}} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{64.5}{25.85}\right) = \ee^{-2.495} = 0.082 $$

The anti-Stokes line is only about $8\%$ of the Stokes line. Raising the temperature to $600\ \mathrm{K}$ gives $k_BT=51.7\ \mathrm{meV}$, and the ratio increases to $0.29$. The technique of measuring this ratio to determine the local temperature at the point irradiated by the laser (Raman thermometry) is used routinely to evaluate heating in semiconductor devices. Detailed balance is not an equation confined to paper; it has become a measuring instrument.

8.6 The Fluctuation–Dissipation Theorem

8.6.1 Writing $\chi''$ in terms of $S$

Derivation 7: The fluctuation–dissipation theorem (the form valid both at $T=0$ and at finite temperature)

Compare $\chi''_{AB}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chi-im} with $S_{AB}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:8-S-lehmann-T}. Applying the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula \eqref{eq:8-sp} to Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann-T}, the finite-temperature $\chi''$ becomes

$$ \chi''_{AB}(\omega) = -\frac{\pi}{\hbar Z}\sum_{m,n}\ee^{-\beta E_m}\left[ A_{mn}B_{nm}\,\delta(\omega-\omega_{nm}) - B_{mn}A_{nm}\,\delta(\omega+\omega_{nm})\right] $$

The first term is Eq. \eqref{eq:8-S-lehmann-T} itself: $\;-\dfrac{\pi}{\hbar}S_{AB}(\omega)$.

The second term, as in Step 1 of Derivation 6, is $\dfrac{1}{Z}\sum_{m,n}\ee^{-\beta E_m}B_{mn}A_{nm}\delta(\omega+\omega_{nm}) = S_{BA}(-\omega)$ (since the delta function is an even function, $\delta(\omega+\omega_{nm})=\delta(-\omega-\omega_{nm})$). Hence

$$ \begin{equation} \chi''_{AB}(\omega) = -\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\left[\,S_{AB}(\omega) - S_{BA}(-\omega)\,\right] \label{eq:8-fdt-general} \end{equation} $$

Inserting detailed balance, Eq. \eqref{eq:8-detailed-balance}, $S_{BA}(-\omega)=\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}S_{AB}(\omega)$, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \boxed{\;\chi''_{AB}(\omega) = -\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\left(1-\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}\right) S_{AB}(\omega)\;} \label{eq:8-fdt} \end{equation} $$

∎ This is the fluctuation–dissipation theorem (FDT).

As $T\to0$, $\beta\to\infty$; then $\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}\to0$ for $\omega>0$, and $S_{AB}(\omega)=0$ for $\omega<0$. Therefore Eq. \eqref{eq:8-fdt-general} remains $\chi''_{AB}(\omega)=-\frac{\pi}{\hbar}[S_{AB}(\omega)-S_{BA}(-\omega)]$ as it stands, and it can be used as the expression at $T=0$.

Physical Meaning: How the system loses energy when kicked and how it shakes on its own are the same thing

The left-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:8-fdt}, $\chi''$, is the dissipation. As we saw in Section 8.4.3, it determines the energy that the system absorbs when an external force is applied (Eq. \eqref{eq:8-power}). The $S$ on the right-hand side is the fluctuation. It determines how strongly $\hat A$ shakes on its own in the equilibrium state when no external force at all is applied (Eq. \eqref{eq:8-S-sum}).

These two are linked only through the temperature-dependent factor $1-\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}$. This is a remarkable claim. How much energy is lost when we kick the system can be found out by looking at how the system was shaking before we kicked it.

The intuitive reason is this. Dissipation means “the energy we put in is scattered among the countless degrees of freedom of the system and never comes back.” If there are many degrees of freedom to scatter it into, the dissipation is large. But those same degrees of freedom are shaking thermally in the equilibrium state. A system with many places to scatter the energy into also shakes a great deal on its own. That is why both are determined by the same quantity.

8.6.2 The classical limit — toward Nyquist's formula for thermal noise

Derivation 8: The classical limit and Johnson–Nyquist noise

Step 1: Expanding the factor. For $\hbar\omega\ll k_BT$ ($\beta\hbar\omega\ll1$),

$$ 1-\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega} \simeq \beta\hbar\omega = \frac{\hbar\omega}{k_BT} $$

Substituting this into Eq. \eqref{eq:8-fdt}, $\hbar$ drops out and we obtain

$$ \chi''_{AB}(\omega) = -\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\cdot\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_BT}S_{AB}(\omega) = -\frac{\pi\omega}{k_BT}S_{AB}(\omega) $$
$$ \begin{equation} S_{AB}(\omega) = -\frac{k_BT}{\pi\,\omega}\,\chi''_{AB}(\omega) \qquad(\hbar\omega\ll k_BT) \label{eq:8-fdt-classical} \end{equation} $$

No $\hbar$ appears. This is the classical fluctuation–dissipation theorem. In the convention without the prefactor $1/2\pi$, $\tilde S_{AB}(\omega)\equiv2\pi S_{AB}(\omega)=\int\dd t\,\ee^{\ii\omega t}\braket{\hat A_H(t)\hat B}$, it reads

$$ \tilde S_{AB}(\omega) = -\frac{2k_BT}{\omega}\,\chi''_{AB}(\omega) $$

This is the form often seen in statistical mechanics textbooks (with the Kubo-style $\chi^{\text{Kubo}}=-\chi^R$, the minus sign disappears and $\tilde S=\frac{2k_BT}{\omega}\chi''^{\text{Kubo}}$).

Step 2: Applying it to a resistor. Consider a resistor of resistance $R$. Let the external force be the voltage $V$ and the responding quantity be the charge $Q$ that has passed through the resistor (the coupling energy is $-QV$, so this is the Kubo-style sign). Then

$$ Q(\omega) = \chi_{QQ}(\omega)\,V(\omega) $$

Since the current is $I=\dot Q$, in the Fourier convention of this book ($\partial_t\to-\ii\omega$) we have $I(\omega)=-\ii\omega Q(\omega)$. Comparing with Ohm's law $I=YV$ ($Y=1/R$ is the admittance), we get

$$ -\ii\omega\,\chi_{QQ}(\omega) = Y = \frac{1}{R} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \chi_{QQ}(\omega) = \frac{\ii}{\omega R} $$

Since it is a purely imaginary number, $\chi'_{QQ}=0$ and $\chi''_{QQ}(\omega)=\dfrac{1}{\omega R}$.

Step 3: Obtaining the fluctuation spectrum of the charge. Putting this into the classical FDT (with the Kubo-style sign) gives

$$ \tilde S_{QQ}(\omega) = \frac{2k_BT}{\omega}\cdot\frac{1}{\omega R} = \frac{2k_BT}{\omega^2R} $$

Step 4: Converting to current fluctuations. Since $I=\dot Q$ gives $I(\omega)=-\ii\omega Q(\omega)$, the fluctuation spectrum $\tilde S$ is multiplied by $\abs{-\ii\omega}^2=\omega^2$:

$$ \tilde S_{II}(\omega) = \omega^2\,\tilde S_{QQ}(\omega) = \frac{2k_BT}{R} $$

$\omega$ has disappeared. This is white noise (the same strength at all frequencies).

Step 5: Converting to the “one-sided” form used in experiments. Experimentalists write everything in terms of the positive frequency $\nu$ only (in Hz), and define the one-sided spectral density $S^{(1)}$ so that $\braket{I^2}=\int_0^\infty S^{(1)}_I(\nu)\dd\nu$. Since $\braket{I^2}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd\omega}{2\pi}\tilde S_{II}$, since $\tilde S_{II}$ is an even function, and since $\omega=2\pi\nu$, we have

$$ \braket{I^2} = \int_0^{\infty}\frac{2\,\dd\omega}{2\pi}\tilde S_{II} = \int_0^{\infty}2\,\tilde S_{II}\,\dd\nu \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad S^{(1)}_I(\nu) = 2\tilde S_{II} = \frac{4k_BT}{R} $$

Written in terms of the voltage, $S^{(1)}_V = R^2 S^{(1)}_I$ gives

$$ \begin{equation} \braket{V^2} = 4k_BT R\,\Delta\nu \label{eq:8-nyquist} \end{equation} $$

∎ This is Nyquist's formula for thermal noise (Johnson–Nyquist noise). The relation that Johnson found experimentally in 1928 and that Nyquist derived from thermodynamics alone has now fallen out of the general theory of quantum many-body systems as a special case.

Example 8.3: Checking with numbers

A resistor with $R=1\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ is used at room temperature $T=300\ \mathrm{K}$ and measured with an amplifier of bandwidth $\Delta\nu=1\ \mathrm{MHz}$. What is the root-mean-square value of the noise voltage?

Solution. Substituting into Eq. \eqref{eq:8-nyquist}:

$$ \braket{V^2} = 4\times(1.381\times10^{-23})\times300\times(1.0\times10^{3})\times(1.0\times10^{6}) = 1.66\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{V^2} $$ $$ V_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{1.66\times10^{-11}} = 4.1\ \mu\mathrm{V} $$

This is of the order of microvolts. This $\sqrt{R\,T\,\Delta\nu}$ dependence is the reason why, in measurement circuits that handle tiny signals, we are told to “lower the resistance,” “narrow the bandwidth,” and “cool it down.” ∎

Validity of the classical approximation. This formula can be used when $\hbar\omega\ll k_BT$. At $300\ \mathrm{K}$, $k_BT=25.85\ \mathrm{meV}$, and the corresponding frequency is $\nu=k_BT/h=6.2\ \mathrm{THz}$. Measurements at $\mathrm{MHz}$ and $\mathrm{GHz}$ frequencies are entirely in the classical regime. Conversely, if we measure at $\mathrm{GHz}$ frequencies in a dilution refrigerator at $10\ \mathrm{mK}$ ($k_BT/h=0.21\ \mathrm{GHz}$), quantum corrections matter, and $1-\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}$ must be used properly. Experiments on superconducting qubits are in this regime.

8.6.3 Relation to Brownian motion and the Einstein relation

The three faces of the same theorem

Historically, the fluctuation–dissipation theorem was discovered three times, in separate contexts.

Table 8.1: Typical manifestations of the fluctuation–dissipation theorem
PhenomenonFluctuation ($S$ side)Dissipation ($\chi''$ side)Relation
Brownian motion (Einstein 1905)diffusion coefficient $D$mobility $\mu$ (= inverse of friction)$D=\mu k_BT$
Thermal noise (Nyquist 1928)noise voltage $\braket{V^2}$resistance $R$$\braket{V^2}=4k_BTR\Delta\nu$
General linear response (Kubo 1957)$S_{AB}(\omega)$$\chi''_{AB}(\omega)$Eq. \eqref{eq:8-fdt}

How to read the Einstein relation $D=\mu k_BT$. A fine particle in water diffuses because it is randomly kicked by water molecules (fluctuation, $D$). If we pinch the same particle with a finger and pull it, its speed levels off because of the viscous resistance of the water (dissipation, $\mu=v/F$). What links these two is $D=\mu k_BT$. How the particle is kicked (fluctuation) and how it is dragged (dissipation) are both caused by collisions with the same water molecules, so they cannot possibly be unrelated. This is the physical core of the FDT.

To derive this from Eq. \eqref{eq:8-fdt-classical}, it suffices to take the $\omega\to0$ limit for $\hat A=\hat x$ (the position). This is treated in Exercise 8.5.

Common Misconception: The FDT does not hold “in every situation”

What we used in an essential way in the proof of the fluctuation–dissipation theorem (Derivations 6 and 7) is that the system is in thermal equilibrium, with its states distributed as $\ee^{-\beta E_m}/Z$. For systems in which this assumption breaks down, the FDT does not hold.

  • Nonequilibrium steady states in which a current keeps flowing (shot noise is proportional to $2eI$, which is a different thing from $4k_BT/R$)
  • Systems such as glasses, whose relaxation time is longer than the measurement time (an effective temperature $T_{\text{eff}}$ becomes necessary)
  • Active systems such as biological molecular motors

Conversely, measuring whether the FDT is violated serves as a test of whether the system is in equilibrium. In recent years, nonequilibrium statistical mechanics has developed precisely in the direction of quantifying this “violation of the FDT.”

8.7 The $f$-Sum Rule (Thomas–Reiche–Kuhn)

8.7.1 The integral of $\omega\chi''$ becomes a double commutator

Derivation 9: The $f$-sum rule

Let $\hat A$ be a Hermitian operator and consider the case $T=0$. Multiply Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chi-im-AA} by $\omega$ and integrate over $\omega$.

Step 1: Carrying out the integral with the delta functions.

$$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd\omega}{\pi}\,\omega\,\chi''_{AA}(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd\omega}{\pi}\,\omega\left(-\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\right)\sum_n\abs{A_{0n}}^2 \left[\delta(\omega-\omega_{n0})-\delta(\omega+\omega_{n0})\right] $$

The factors of $\pi$ cancel, and the delta functions fix $\omega$ at $\pm\omega_{n0}$:

$$ = -\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_n\abs{A_{0n}}^2\left[\omega_{n0}-(-\omega_{n0})\right] = -\frac{2}{\hbar}\sum_n \abs{A_{0n}}^2\,\omega_{n0} = -\frac{2}{\hbar^2}\sum_n \abs{A_{0n}}^2\,(E_n-E_0) $$

Note that the two delta functions add up (rather than subtract). This is because $\chi''$ is an odd function and $\omega$ is also an odd function, so the product $\omega\chi''$ is an even function, and both sides, $\pm$, contribute equally.

Step 2: Returning $\sum_n\abs{A_{0n}}^2(E_n-E_0)$ to the language of operators. This is the part that requires some technique. To produce the energy difference $E_n-E_0$, we use the commutator with $\Ham_0$:

$$ \bra{0}[\hat A,\Ham_0]\ket{n} = \bra{0}\hat A\Ham_0\ket{n} - \bra{0}\Ham_0\hat A\ket{n} = A_{0n}E_n - E_0 A_{0n} = A_{0n}(E_n-E_0) $$

The factor $(E_n-E_0)$ that we wanted has appeared. We use it to reorganize the sum. First,

$$ \bra{0}[\hat A,\Ham_0]\,\hat A\ket{0} = \sum_n \bra{0}[\hat A,\Ham_0]\ket{n}\bra{n}\hat A\ket{0} = \sum_n A_{0n}(E_n-E_0)A_{n0} = \sum_n \abs{A_{0n}}^2 (E_n-E_0) $$

(Since $\hat A$ is Hermitian, $A_{n0}=A_{0n}^*$, and hence $A_{0n}A_{n0}=\abs{A_{0n}}^2$.)

Next we compute the quantity with the order reversed. Since $\bra{n}[\hat A,\Ham_0]\ket{0}=A_{n0}E_0 - E_nA_{n0}=-A_{n0}(E_n-E_0)$, we have

$$ \bra{0}\hat A\,[\hat A,\Ham_0]\ket{0} = \sum_n A_{0n}\bra{n}[\hat A,\Ham_0]\ket{0} = -\sum_n \abs{A_{0n}}^2(E_n-E_0) $$

Only the sign is reversed. Subtracting these two, the sum appears doubled:

$$ \braket{\,[\,[\hat A,\Ham_0],\hat A\,]\,} = \bra{0}[\hat A,\Ham_0]\hat A\ket{0} - \bra{0}\hat A[\hat A,\Ham_0]\ket{0} = 2\sum_n\abs{A_{0n}}^2(E_n-E_0) $$

This is why the double commutator appears. With only one of the orderings, the sum over matrix elements would remain “signed”; by taking the commutator, however, the $\pm$ signs line up and give a factor of 2, and everything collapses into a quantity expressed in terms of operators alone.

Step 3: Putting it together. Substituting into the result of Step 1, we obtain the following sum rule.

$$ \begin{equation} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd\omega}{\pi}\;\omega\;\bigl(-\chi''_{AA}(\omega)\bigr) = \frac{1}{\hbar^2}\braket{\,[\,[\hat A,\Ham_0],\hat A\,]\,} \label{eq:8-fsum} \end{equation} $$

This is the $f$-sum rule. As we saw in Section 8.4.3, the $-\chi''$ on the left-hand side is the dissipative part in the sign convention of this book (positive for $\omega>0$). In textbooks that use the Kubo-style convention $\chi^{\text{Kubo}}=-\chi^R$, this minus sign is simply absorbed, and the rule becomes

$$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd\omega}{\pi}\,\omega\,\chi''^{\text{Kubo}}_{AA}(\omega) = \frac{1}{\hbar^2}\braket{[[\hat A,\Ham_0],\hat A]} $$

which is the form one often sees. Since the signs of both sides are fixed together, the physical content is identical.

Why are sum rules so valuable?

The left-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:8-fsum} is a quantity integrated over the entire spectrum. That is, it contains the information of all the excited states. The right-hand side, however, is merely the ground-state expectation value of a double commutator. Moreover, $\hat A$ often commutes with the potential term, so only the kinetic-energy term contributes to $[\hat A,\Ham_0]$, and the right-hand side takes a surprisingly simple value (often a constant independent of the interaction).

In other words, a sum rule is the statement that “even without knowing the excitation spectrum line by line, its integral alone is known exactly.” It has two uses.

  • Check: If the $\chi''(\omega)$ obtained from a numerical calculation does not satisfy the sum rule when integrated, something in the calculation is wrong. In first-principles calculations (TDDFT, GW, BSE), checking the $f$-sum rule of the optical spectrum is a standard sanity test.
  • Normalization of experiments: A measured intensity spectrum often has no known absolute scale (the detection efficiency is unknown). With a sum rule, it can be converted to absolute units. This is a method used routinely in neutron scattering and electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS).

8.7.2 The one-electron case: the Thomas–Reiche–Kuhn sum rule

Example 8.4: The sum rule for $\hat A=\hat x$

For a one-particle system of mass $m$ with $\Ham_0=\dfrac{\hat p^2}{2m}+V(\hat x)$, let $\hat A=\hat x$. Calculate the right-hand side.

Solution. First, $[\hat x,\Ham_0]$. The potential $V(\hat x)$ commutes with $\hat x$, so only the kinetic energy contributes:

$$ [\hat x,\Ham_0] = \left[\hat x,\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}\right] = \frac{1}{2m}\left([\hat x,\hat p]\hat p + \hat p[\hat x,\hat p]\right) = \frac{1}{2m}\left(\ii\hbar\,\hat p + \hat p\,\ii\hbar\right) = \frac{\ii\hbar\,\hat p}{m} $$

(We used $[\hat x,\hat p]=\ii\hbar$.) Next we take the commutator with $\hat x$ once more:

$$ [[\hat x,\Ham_0],\hat x] = \left[\frac{\ii\hbar\hat p}{m},\hat x\right] = \frac{\ii\hbar}{m}[\hat p,\hat x] = \frac{\ii\hbar}{m}(-\ii\hbar) = \frac{\hbar^2}{m} $$

It has become a mere number. There is no need even to take the expectation value. Therefore the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:8-fsum} is

$$ \frac{1}{\hbar^2}\cdot\frac{\hbar^2}{m} = \frac{1}{m} $$

That is, combining this with the result of Step 1,

$$ \frac{2}{\hbar^2}\sum_n \abs{x_{0n}}^2(E_n-E_0) = \frac{1}{m} $$

Now we define the oscillator strength by

$$ f_{n0} \equiv \frac{2m\,\omega_{n0}}{\hbar}\abs{x_{0n}}^2 = \frac{2m(E_n-E_0)}{\hbar^2}\abs{x_{0n}}^2 $$

With this definition, the above equation becomes simply

$$ \begin{equation} \sum_n f_{n0} = 1 \label{eq:8-trk} \end{equation} $$

This is the Thomas–Reiche–Kuhn (TRK) sum rule. If there are $Z$ electrons, the right-hand side becomes $Z$. ∎

Looking at the TRK sum rule numerically

  • Hydrogen atom. The oscillator strength of $1s\to2p$ is $f=0.4162$, and the total over the Lyman series ($1s\to np$, $n=2,3,\dots$) is $0.5650$. The remaining $0.4350$ is carried not by bound states but by the ionization continuum ($1s\to$ free electron). These two add up to exactly $1$. The reason that “the sum rule does not work if one counts only the spectral lines” is that one has forgotten the continuum.
  • Sodium atom. For the single valence electron, the sum of the oscillator strengths of the D lines ($3s\to3p$, $589.0$ nm and $589.6$ nm) is $\approx0.96$. A single transition (strictly speaking two, owing to the fine structure) nearly exhausts the sum rule of the valence electron. This is why sodium vapor absorbs light so strongly at this wavelength.
  • Harmonic oscillator. In Example 8.1 we had $\abs{x_{01}}^2=\hbar/2m\omega_0$ and $\omega_{10}=\omega_0$, so $f_{10}=\frac{2m\omega_0}{\hbar}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_0}=1$. A single transition completely exhausts the sum rule. This is the reason why Lorentz's classical “electron = oscillator attached to a spring” model reproduces the optical response of metals and dielectrics so remarkably well.

8.7.3 The density-operator case: $nq^2/m$

What we use from Chapter 9 onward is the case where $\hat A$ is the density operator. We write the Fourier component of the density of an $N$-particle system as

$$ \hat n_\qq = \sum_{j=1}^{N}\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j}, \qquad \hat n_\qq^\dagger = \hat n_{-\qq} = \sum_{j=1}^{N}\ee^{+\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j} $$

Let $\Ham_0=\sum_j\dfrac{\pp_j^2}{2m}+V(\{\xx_j\})$ (the interaction $V$ is a function of the positions only).

Derivation 10: The double commutator of the density operator

Step 1: Computing $[\hat n_\qq,\Ham_0]$. Since $V$ is a function of the positions, it commutes with $\hat n_\qq$. Only the kinetic energy remains, and we use the formula $[\hat f(\xx),\hat p^2]=\hat p\cdot[\hat f,\hat p]+[\hat f,\hat p]\cdot\hat p$ and $[\hat f(\xx),\pp]=\ii\hbar\nabla \hat f$. Since $\nabla_j\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j}=-\ii\qq\,\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j}$,

$$ [\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j},\pp_j] = \ii\hbar(-\ii\qq)\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j} = \hbar\qq\,\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j} $$

Hence

$$ [\hat n_\qq,\Ham_0] = \sum_j\frac{1}{2m}\left(\pp_j\cdot\hbar\qq\,\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j} + \hbar\qq\,\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j}\cdot\pp_j\right) = \frac{\hbar}{2m}\sum_j \qq\cdot\left(\pp_j\,\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j}+\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j}\,\pp_j\right) $$

(Since $\pp_j$ and $\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j}$ do not commute, we need to write both orderings.)

Step 2: Taking the commutator with $\hat n_\qq^\dagger$ once more. We have $[\pp_j,\ee^{\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j}]=-\ii\hbar(\ii\qq)\ee^{\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j}=\hbar\qq\,\ee^{\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j}$, and the exponentials commute with each other. For the term of particle $j$,

$$ \left[\qq\cdot\pp_j\,\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j},\;\ee^{\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j}\right] = \qq\cdot[\pp_j,\ee^{\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j}]\,\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j} = \hbar q^2\,\ee^{\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j}\ee^{-\ii\qq\cdot\xx_j} = \hbar q^2 $$

The other ordering likewise gives $\hbar q^2$. The commutator between different particles ($j\ne k$) is zero. Therefore each particle $j$ yields $\dfrac{\hbar}{2m}(\hbar q^2+\hbar q^2)=\dfrac{\hbar^2q^2}{m}$, and

$$ \begin{equation} \left[\,[\hat n_\qq,\Ham_0],\,\hat n_\qq^\dagger\,\right] = \frac{N\hbar^2 q^2}{m} \label{eq:8-double-comm-density} \end{equation} $$

∎ It is not even an operator, just a number. No matter how complicated the interaction $V$ is, it contributes nothing at all.

A brief remark is in order here. In Derivation 9 we assumed that $\hat A$ is Hermitian, but $\hat n_\qq$ is not Hermitian for finite $\qq$. However, the situation is unchanged for the case $\hat B=\hat A^\dagger$. The weights of the two delta functions of $\chi''_{AA^\dagger}$ split into $\abs{A_{0n}}^2$ and $\abs{A_{n0}}^2$, so that only their sum $\abs{A_{0n}}^2+\abs{A_{n0}}^2$ appears on the right-hand side of the sum rule, and exactly the same procedure as in Derivation 9 shows that a sum rule of the same form

$$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd\omega}{\pi}\;\omega\;\bigl(-\chi''_{AA^\dagger}(\omega)\bigr) = \frac{1}{\hbar^2}\braket{\,[\,[\hat A,\Ham_0],\hat A^\dagger\,]\,} $$

holds. Hereafter, when we write $\chi''_{nn}(\qq,\omega)$, we mean the dissipative part of the response function for the pair $(\hat n_\qq,\hat n_\qq^\dagger)$.

Therefore, for the density response function, the $f$-sum rule, Eq. \eqref{eq:8-fsum}, becomes

$$ \begin{equation} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd\omega}{\pi}\,\omega\,\bigl(-\chi''_{nn}(\qq,\omega)\bigr) = \frac{N q^2}{m} \qquad\Bigl(\text{per unit volume}\;\frac{n q^2}{m}\Bigr) \label{eq:8-fsum-density} \end{equation} $$

($n=N/V$ is the number density.) In terms of the charge density $e\,\hat n_\qq$, the right-hand side is $ne^2q^2/m$ (what we write as $\hat\rho_\qq$ from Chapter 9 onward is the number-density operator, which does not include the charge, so do not confuse the two). When we treat the dielectric function and plasmons in Chapters 9–11, this sum rule will appear repeatedly.

Physical Meaning: The sum rule determines the plasma frequency

The right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:8-fsum-density} contains neither $\hbar$, nor the interaction, nor the temperature. It contains only the particle number, the charge, and the mass. This means that the total capacity of an electron system to absorb energy from light or electron beams is fixed in advance.

As we shall see in Chapter 11, in a metal most of the absorption is concentrated in a single mode called the plasmon. The sum rule is then nearly exhausted by this one mode, and from the form $\int\omega(-\chi'')\dd\omega/\pi \approx \omega_p^2\times(\text{weight})$ the plasma frequency

$$ \omega_p = \sqrt{\frac{ne^2}{\epsilon_0 m}} $$

is determined. This has exactly the same structure as the harmonic oscillator of Example 8.1 exhausting the sum rule with a single transition.

Numerical example (aluminum). Inserting $n=1.81\times10^{29}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$ (three valence electrons per atom), we obtain

$$ \omega_p = \sqrt{\frac{1.81\times10^{29}\times(1.602\times10^{-19})^2}{8.854\times10^{-12}\times9.11\times10^{-31}}} = 2.4\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}, \qquad \hbar\omega_p = 15.8\ \mathrm{eV} $$

The bulk plasmon of aluminum measured by electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) is at $15.0\ \mathrm{eV}$, which agrees to within about 5%. The principal excitation energy of a strongly interacting electron ensemble with a density of $10^{29}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$ is determined by a one-line equality, the sum rule. This is the power of sum rules.

8.8 The Time-Ordered $\chi^T$ and the Retarded $\chi^R$

8.8.1 Why are there two kinds?

The retarded response function $\chi^R$ that we have treated so far contains $\theta(t-t')$, and so it embodied causality, the principle that “the cause comes first and the effect comes after.” It is this one that can be measured physically.

When we actually expand perturbation theory, however, a different function appears. Recall the Dyson series of Chapter 6: the time-evolution operator carried the time-ordered product $T$. Wick's theorem (Appendix B) can likewise be used only for $T$-products. Therefore, what we can compute with Feynman diagrams is the following.

Definition: Time-ordered response function

$$ \begin{equation} \chi^T_{AB}(t-t') \equiv -\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\bra{0}T\!\left[\hat A_H(t)\hat B_H(t')\right]\ket{0}, \qquad T\!\left[\hat A(t)\hat B(t')\right] = \begin{cases} \hat A(t)\hat B(t') & (t>t')\\[2pt] \hat B(t')\hat A(t) & (t<t') \end{cases} \label{eq:8-chiT-def} \end{equation} $$

In terms of the $\theta$ function, $T[\hat A(t)\hat B(t')]=\theta(t-t')\hat A(t)\hat B(t')+\theta(t'-t)\hat B(t')\hat A(t)$.

In some references $\chi^T$ is called the “causal Green's function” (the convention of Fetter–Walecka and AGD). Note, however, that this “causal” refers to the time ordering of perturbation theory, and, as we shall see in Section 8.8.3, $\chi^T$ does not satisfy causality in the sense of a response (the response vanishes for $t<t'$).

Whereas $\chi^R$ was a commutator (a subtraction), $\chi^T$ is an addition. This difference is decisive.

8.8.2 Lehmann representation of $\chi^T$

Derivation 11: Spectral representation of $\chi^T$

Step 1: Time representation. Set $t'=0$ and use the result of Section 8.1.2 as it stands. For $t>0$ we have $\bra{0}\hat A_H(t)\hat B\ket{0}=\sum_n A_{0n}B_{n0}\ee^{-\ii\omega_{n0}t}$, and for $t<0$ we have $\bra{0}\hat B\hat A_H(t)\ket{0}=\sum_n B_{0n}A_{n0}\ee^{+\ii\omega_{n0}t}$. Hence

$$ \chi^T_{AB}(t) = -\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\left[ \theta(t)\sum_n A_{0n}B_{n0}\ee^{-\ii\omega_{n0}t} + \theta(-t)\sum_n B_{0n}A_{n0}\ee^{+\ii\omega_{n0}t}\right] $$

Compared with Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann-t}, in the second term $\theta(t)$ has become $\theta(-t)$, and the sign has changed from $-$ to $+$.

Step 2: The Fourier transform of the first term is exactly the same as in Section 8.1.3, namely $\dfrac{\ii}{\omega-\omega_{n0}+\ii\eta}$.

Step 3: The Fourier transform of the second term. This time we have $\theta(-t)$, so the range of integration becomes $-\infty<t<0$. To make the integral converge we insert a factor that decays as $t\to-\infty$, that is, $\ee^{+\eta t}$ (decaying for $t<0$). This corresponds to $\omega\to\omega-\ii\eta$:

$$ \int_{-\infty}^{0}\dd t\;\ee^{\ii(\omega+\omega_{n0}-\ii\eta)t} = \left[\frac{\ee^{\ii(\omega+\omega_{n0}-\ii\eta)t}}{\ii(\omega+\omega_{n0}-\ii\eta)}\right]_{-\infty}^{0} = \frac{1-0}{\ii(\omega+\omega_{n0}-\ii\eta)} = \frac{-\ii}{\omega+\omega_{n0}-\ii\eta} $$

The sign of $\ii\eta$ has flipped. This produces the only difference from $\chi^R$.

Step 4: Putting it together. Using $-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\times\ii=+\frac1\hbar$ and $-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\times(-\ii)=-\frac1\hbar$, we obtain

$$ \begin{equation} \chi^T_{AB}(\omega) = \frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_n\left[ \frac{A_{0n}B_{n0}}{\omega-\omega_{n0}+\ii\eta} -\frac{B_{0n}A_{n0}}{\omega+\omega_{n0}-\ii\eta}\right] \label{eq:8-chiT} \end{equation} $$

∎ Compare this with Eq. \eqref{eq:8-lehmann}. The only difference is the sign of $\ii\eta$ in the denominator of the second term. That one small difference, as we shall see next, destroys analyticity completely.

8.8.3 The arrangement of the poles, and the lost analyticity

The poles of Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chiT} are located at

$$ \omega = +\omega_{n0}-\ii\eta \quad(\text{lower half-plane}), \qquad \omega = -\omega_{n0}+\ii\eta \quad(\text{upper half-plane}) $$

$\chi^T$ has poles in the upper half-plane. Therefore Theorem 8.1 does not hold, and the Kramers–Kronig relations cannot be applied to $\chi^T$ as they stand. Measuring $\chi^T$ would not give a causal response—which is only natural, since $\chi^T(t)$ is nonzero even for $t<0$ to begin with.

8.8.4 The bridge at $T=0$

Derivation 12: The relation between $\chi^T$ and $\chi^R$ ($T=0$)

Take $\hat A=\hat B$ (Hermitian) and apply the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula \eqref{eq:8-sp} to both functions. Both denominators of $\chi^R$ contain $+\ii\eta$, so both give $-\ii\pi\delta$; only the second term of $\chi^T$ contains $-\ii\eta$, so it gives $+\ii\pi\delta$.

Principal-value part (real part): The $\PV$ part is exactly the same for the two. That is,

$$ \mathrm{Re}\,\chi^T_{AA}(\omega) = \mathrm{Re}\,\chi^R_{AA}(\omega) $$

Delta-function part (imaginary part):

$$ \mathrm{Im}\,\chi^R_{AA}(\omega) = -\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\sum_n\abs{A_{0n}}^2\left[\delta(\omega-\omega_{n0})-\delta(\omega+\omega_{n0})\right] $$ $$ \mathrm{Im}\,\chi^T_{AA}(\omega) = -\frac{\pi}{\hbar}\sum_n\abs{A_{0n}}^2\left[\delta(\omega-\omega_{n0})+\delta(\omega+\omega_{n0})\right] $$

For $\omega>0$ only the first delta function contributes, so the two coincide. For $\omega<0$ only the second delta function contributes, and the sign is reversed. To summarize,

$$ \begin{equation} \chi^R_{AA}(\omega) = \mathrm{Re}\,\chi^T_{AA}(\omega) + \ii\,\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)\,\mathrm{Im}\,\chi^T_{AA}(\omega) \qquad (T=0) \label{eq:8-chiT-chiR} \end{equation} $$

∎ That is, at $T=0$, after computing $\chi^T$ with Feynman diagrams, we obtain $\chi^R$ by flipping by hand the sign of the imaginary part on the negative-frequency side. This equation also shows that the imaginary part of $\chi^T$ is an even function of $\omega$, whereas that of $\chi^R$ is an odd function.

This trick does not work at finite temperature

In deriving Eq. \eqref{eq:8-chiT-chiR} we used the fact that at $T=0$ “only the first term contributes for $\omega>0$ and only the second term for $\omega<0$.” For $T>0$, as warned in Section 8.1.4, $\omega_{nm}$ can be either positive or negative, so the terms cannot be separated by the sign of $\omega$. A simple conversion rule from $\chi^T$ to $\chi^R$ does not exist.

In Chapter 13 we therefore take a completely different route. We build perturbation theory not in real time but in imaginary time $\tau=\ii t$, compute the Matsubara Green's function $\mathcal{G}(\ii\omega_n)$, and obtain $\chi^R$ directly by an analytic continuation $\ii\omega_n\to\omega+\ii\eta$ from the discrete points $\ii\omega_n$ (the Matsubara frequencies) to the real axis. The justification for “being allowed to continue analytically” is precisely the analyticity in the upper half-plane (Theorem 8.1) shown in this chapter.

Theorem 8.1 of this chapter is the technical starting point of Chapter 13.

8.9 Summary and Exercises

8.9.1 Summary

  • Inserting the complete set $\sum_n\ket{n}\bra{n}=1$ into the commutator is all it takes to bring the Kubo formula into a computable form. This is because the matrix element of the Heisenberg operator becomes the simple phase factor $\bra{0}\hat A_H(t)\ket{n}=A_{0n}\ee^{-\ii\omega_{n0}t}$.
  • The result is the Lehmann (spectral) representation \eqref{eq:8-lehmann}. Here $\eta$ is a convergence factor that comes from the adiabatic switching (Chapter 6); it combines with the lower limit of integration $0$ imposed by $\theta(t)$ to give $+\ii\eta$.
  • The pole positions $\pm\omega_{n0}$ give the excitation energies $\hbar\omega_{n0}=E_n-E_0$. The weight $\abs{A_{0n}}^2$ determines “how much of that excitation we can kick out with that operator,” and an excitation on which the weight is concentrated is a collective excitation.
  • Since all the poles lie slightly below the real axis, $\chi^R$ is analytic in the upper half-plane (Theorem 8.1). This is exact regardless of the strength of the interaction, so the Kramers–Kronig relations apply to quantum many-body systems just as they are.
  • Splitting with the Sokhotski–Plemelj formula shows that $\chi''$ is a comb of delta functions \eqref{eq:8-chi-im-AA}. It is a line spectrum for a finite system and a continuous spectrum in the thermodynamic limit. The absorbed power is $P=-\frac12 f_0^2\omega\chi''$, which agrees exactly with Fermi's golden rule.
  • The spectral function $S_{AB}(\omega)$ is a correlation function that is not a commutator; it describes the equilibrium fluctuations in the absence of an external force. Its total integral is $\braket{\hat A\hat B}$. At $T=0$, $S(\omega<0)=0$.
  • Detailed balance $S_{BA}(-\omega)=\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}S_{AB}(\omega)$. It can be measured directly as the Stokes/anti-Stokes intensity ratio in Raman scattering.
  • The fluctuation–dissipation theorem $\chi''_{AB}(\omega)=-\frac{\pi}{\hbar}(1-\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega})S_{AB}(\omega)$. Dissipation (how the response dies away when we kick the system) and fluctuation (how the system jiggles on its own) are determined by the same function. In the classical limit it reduces to Nyquist's $\braket{V^2}=4k_BTR\Delta\nu$ and Einstein's $D=\mu k_BT$.
  • The $f$-sum rule $\int\frac{\dd\omega}{\pi}\omega(-\chi''_{AA})=\frac{1}{\hbar^2}\braket{[[\hat A,\Ham_0],\hat A]}$. The integral over the whole spectrum is fixed by a simple quantity, a double commutator. For the single-particle $\hat x$ it gives the TRK sum rule $\sum_n f_{n0}=1$, and for the density operator it gives $nq^2/m$ (independent of the interaction). It can be used to check numerical calculations and to normalize experimental data absolutely.
  • The difference between $\chi^T$ and $\chi^R$ is only the sign of $\ii\eta$ on the negative-frequency side, but because of it $\chi^T$ loses analyticity in the upper half-plane. At $T=0$ the two can be bridged by \eqref{eq:8-chiT-chiR}, but at finite temperature they cannot. This is the motivation for the Matsubara formalism and analytic continuation in Chapter 13.
  • The sign convention (whether $\Ham_{\text{ext}}=+f\hat B$ or $-f\hat B$) changes the overall sign of $\chi$. This book adopts the former to match Fetter–Walecka, so the dissipative part is $-\chi''$ (positive for $\omega>0$). When comparing with other books, always check the sign of the coupling.

8.9.2 Exercises

Exercise 8.1: Lehmann representation of a two-level system

Consider a system consisting only of the ground state $\ket{0}$ (energy $0$) and an excited state $\ket{1}$ (energy $\hbar\omega_0$). The matrix elements of the operator $\hat A$ are $A_{01}=A_{10}=a$ (a real number) and $A_{00}=A_{11}=0$.

(1) Write down $\chi^R_{AA}(\omega)$ from the Lehmann representation, and combine it into a single fraction by bringing it to a common denominator.

(2) Write down $\chi''_{AA}(\omega)$ and verify that it is an odd function of $\omega$.

(3) Actually integrate the left-hand side of the $f$-sum rule \eqref{eq:8-fsum}, and show that it equals $2a^2\omega_0/\hbar$.

(4) Suppose that $\hat A$ corresponds to the position $\hat x$ (the electric dipole moment is $e\hat x$), with $\hbar\omega_0=2\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $x_{01}=a=1\ \text{Å}$. Find the oscillator strength $f_{10}=\frac{2m\omega_0}{\hbar}a^2$ numerically. Is the value reasonable in light of the TRK sum rule?

Hint: (1) $\chi^R_{AA}(\omega)=\dfrac{a^2}{\hbar}\left[\dfrac{1}{\omega-\omega_0+\ii\eta}-\dfrac{1}{\omega+\omega_0+\ii\eta}\right]=\dfrac{2a^2\omega_0/\hbar}{(\omega+\ii\eta)^2-\omega_0^2}$. (4) $f_{10}=2\times9.11\times10^{-31}\times3.04\times10^{15}\times(10^{-10})^2/(1.055\times10^{-34})\approx0.52$. Since it does not exceed $1$, there is no contradiction.

Exercise 8.2: Deriving the sum rule from the high-frequency expansion

Expand the Lehmann representation \eqref{eq:8-lehmann} for $\abs{\omega}\to\infty$.

(1) Using $\dfrac{1}{\omega\mp\omega_{n0}}=\dfrac{1}{\omega}\left(1\pm\dfrac{\omega_{n0}}{\omega}+\dfrac{\omega_{n0}^2}{\omega^2}+\cdots\right)$, show that for $\hat A=\hat B$ (Hermitian) the $1/\omega$ term of $\chi^R_{AA}(\omega)$ vanishes.

(2) Show that the next term is $\chi^R_{AA}(\omega)\simeq\dfrac{2}{\hbar\omega^2}\sum_n\abs{A_{0n}}^2\omega_{n0}$, and, by connecting it with the double commutator of Section 8.7, derive the result that

$$ \chi^R_{AA}(\omega) \;\xrightarrow{\;\abs{\omega}\to\infty\;}\; \frac{1}{\hbar^2\omega^2}\braket{[[\hat A,\Ham_0],\hat A]} $$

holds.

(3) Verify that for the density operator this becomes $\dfrac{nq^2}{m\omega^2}$. This is the basis for the result $\epsilon(\qq,\omega)\to1-\omega_p^2/\omega^2$ obtained in Chapter 11.

Hint: (1) If $\hat A=\hat B$, then $\sum_n(A_{0n}A_{n0}-A_{0n}A_{n0})=\braket{[\hat A,\hat A]}=0$. The sum rules are precisely the individual orders of the high-frequency expansion.

Exercise 8.3: Both limits of the fluctuation–dissipation theorem

Consider the thermal factor $1-\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:8-fdt}.

(1) Explain that as $T\to0$ ($\beta\to\infty$) the factor equals $1$ for $\omega>0$, while $S_{AB}(\omega)=0$ for $\omega<0$, so that the result agrees with Eq. \eqref{eq:8-fdt-general}.

(2) Show that Eq. \eqref{eq:8-fdt-classical} follows for $\hbar\omega\ll k_BT$.

(3) At room temperature, $300\ \mathrm{K}$, above what frequency (in THz) does the quantum correction become $10\%$ or more? You may use $1-\ee^{-x}\simeq x(1-x/2)$.

(4) When visible light ($\hbar\omega=2\ \mathrm{eV}$) is shone on a material at room temperature, what is the value of $\ee^{-\beta\hbar\omega}$? Discuss whether, from this result, it can be said that in absorption experiments with visible light there is no need to distinguish “fluctuation” from “dissipation.”

Hint: (3) The correction is $10\%$ when $x=\beta\hbar\omega=0.2$. $\hbar\omega=0.2\times25.85\ \mathrm{meV}=5.2\ \mathrm{meV}$, $\nu=1.3\ \mathrm{THz}$. (4) $\ee^{-2/0.02585}=\ee^{-77}\approx3\times10^{-34}$. This is effectively zero, so $\chi''=-\frac{\pi}{\hbar}S$ holds virtually exactly, and the $T=0$ formula suffices.

Exercise 8.4: Checking a numerical calculation with the $f$-sum rule

The optical spectrum of a certain semiconductor was obtained by a first-principles calculation, and the main part of the absorption was concentrated in a single peak near $\hbar\omega=3.0\ \mathrm{eV}$. Let the number of valence electrons per unit cell be $Z=8$ and the unit-cell volume be $V_{\text{cell}}=40\ \text{Å}^3$.

(1) Write the right-hand side (per unit volume) of Eq. \eqref{eq:8-fsum-density} for the charge density operator, and find $n$ numerically.

(2) From the standpoint of the TRK sum rule $\sum_n f_{n0}=Z$, estimate the “effective oscillator strength” under the assumption that this single peak has used up the entire sum rule.

(3) When the calculated spectrum was integrated, it satisfied only $85\%$ of the sum rule. Give two possible causes.

Hint: (1) $n=Z/V_{\text{cell}}=8/(40\times10^{-30}\ \mathrm{m^3})=2.0\times10^{29}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$. (3) Truncation of the integral (transitions on the high-energy side not included), inconsistency of the velocity operator caused by the basis functions or nonlocal pseudopotentials, omission of the contribution of core electrons, and so on. When a sum rule is not satisfied, first suspect the range of integration.

Exercise 8.5: Deriving the Einstein relation from the fluctuation–dissipation theorem

A heavy particle of mass $M$ undergoes Brownian motion in a medium at temperature $T$. Suppose that the terminal velocity under an applied external force $F$ is $v=\mu F$ ($\mu$ is the mobility).

(1) State that the $\omega\to0$ limit of the velocity response function to the external force $F$ ($v(\omega)=\chi_{vF}(\omega)F(\omega)$) equals $\mu$.

(2) Apply the classical fluctuation–dissipation theorem to the position. With the Kubo-style sign, $\tilde S_{xx}(\omega)=\frac{2k_BT}{\omega}\chi''_{xx}(\omega)$. For a free Brownian particle with no confining potential (equation of motion $M\ddot x+\gamma\dot x=F$, $\gamma=1/\mu$), show that $\chi''_{xx}\simeq\mu/\omega$ as $\omega\to0$, and from this derive $\tilde S_{xx}(\omega\to0)=2\mu k_BT/\omega^2$. Furthermore, using $\tilde S_{vv}=\omega^2\tilde S_{xx}$, which follows from $\hat v=\dot{\hat x}$, show that

$$ \tilde S_{vv}(\omega\to0) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dd t\,\braket{v(t)v(0)} = 2\mu k_BT $$

holds.

(3) Using the Green–Kubo expression for the diffusion coefficient, $D=\int_0^{\infty}\dd t\,\braket{v(t)v(0)}$ (in one dimension), derive $D=\mu k_BT$ (the Einstein relation).

Hint: (2) Fourier transforming the equation of motion gives $\chi_{xx}(\omega)=-\left[M\omega^2+\ii\gamma\omega\right]^{-1}$. Its imaginary part is $\gamma\omega/\abs{M\omega^2+\ii\gamma\omega}^2$, which approaches $1/(\gamma\omega)=\mu/\omega$ as $\omega\to0$. (3) Since the correlation function is an even function of $t$, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}=2\int_0^{\infty}$. Diffusion (fluctuation) and mobility (dissipation) are tied together by the same $k_BT$.
