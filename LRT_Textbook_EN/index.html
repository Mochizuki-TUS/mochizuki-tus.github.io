Introduction to Linear Response Theory— From the Damped Oscillator to the Kubo Formula —

Linear response theory is one of the hurdles at which the most people give up in condensed-matter physics. The reason is clear. You open a textbook, and the time-evolution operator in the interaction picture appears out of nowhere, then commutators in the Heisenberg picture, then you are told something about the upper half-plane of the complex plane, and before you know it you no longer understand what you are calculating. You can follow the equations, yet you cannot see what we kick and what reply we listen to.

This book was written to correct that one point. Boiled down, the content of linear response theory is this.

Kick the system gently. Listen to the reply. The ratio of the reply to the way we kick is called the “response function.”
This response function is determined entirely by the properties of the system before we kick it.

The last line is the Kubo formula, and it is the whole of linear response theory. The “response to a force applied from outside” can be written using nothing but the “fluctuations of the equilibrium state in which no force is applied”—this is the fluctuation–dissipation theorem, and it is the reason this theory is called beautiful.

The organization of this book has a clear aim. We do not start from quantum mechanics. In Part I, we deliberately re-solve the damped oscillator $m\ddot{x}+\gamma\dot{x}+kx=0$, which you learn in high school and first-year university courses, using Green's functions. It may look like a detour, but the toolkit that appears here—such as the retarded Green's function, causality, the response function, and poles in the complex plane—can be used exactly as it is when we treat quantum systems in Part III. All that changes is that $x$ becomes an operator and the average becomes an expectation value. If you understand the classical damped oscillator, you can understand the Kubo formula—this is the claim of this book.

Also, students in engineering often have not taken a course in complex analysis. We have therefore devoted the whole of Chapter 5 to complex analysis, explaining the residue theorem, principal-value integrals, and Jordan's lemma in a self-contained way, restricted to the forms actually used in linear response. Together with the Math Notes in Appendix A, this allows you to read the book through without consulting other books.

How to Read This Book
  • Read straight through: in order from Chapter 1. If you skip Part I (classical) and go straight to Part III (quantum), you will almost certainly give up. If you want to hurry, take the long way around.
  • If you are not confident in complex analysis: Read Chapter 5 first. If a complex integral appears partway through Chapter 2 or Chapter 3, you can simply return to Chapter 5 each time.
  • If you only want to learn the Kubo formula: Chapter 3 → Chapter 6 → Chapter 7. Note, however, that you should compare the classical response function of Chapter 3 and the quantum response function of Chapter 7 side by side.
  • If your goal is screening and plasmons in the electron gas: Chapter 9 → Chapter 10 → Chapter 11. The complete derivation of the Lindhard function is in Chapter 10.
  • If you are interested in superconductivity: Chapter 9 → Chapter 10 → Chapter 11. Chapter 11 contains Goldstone's theorem and the Anderson–Higgs mechanism (the Meissner effect, the London penetration depth, and the Higgs mode).
  • If you want to go as far as finite temperature and the Matsubara formalism: Chapter 13. This is the one chapter that is more abstract than the others, so it is best to read it after reviewing Chapter 7 and Chapter 8.
  • If your goal is first-principles calculations (GW and QSGW): Chapter 9 → Chapter 10 → Chapter 13 → Appendix C → Chapter 14. After getting a firm grasp of Feynman diagrams and Hedin's equations in Appendix C, proceed to Chapter 14.
