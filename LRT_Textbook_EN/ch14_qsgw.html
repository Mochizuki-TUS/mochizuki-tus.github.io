Chapter 14The Quasiparticle Self-Consistent GW Method (QSGW) — Determining the Best Independent-Particle Picture Self-Consistently

In Chapters 9–12 we built the dielectric function of the electron gas, $\epsilon(\qq,\omega)=1-v(q)\chi^0(\qq,\omega)$. In Chapter 13 and Appendix C we learned the Dyson equation, which packs the effects of interactions into the self-energy $\Sig$, and the language of Feynman diagrams used to compute $\Sig$. At the end of Appendix C we introduced the GW approximation $\Sig=\ii GW$, which is the standard tool in first-principles calculations of solids. Here $W=v/\epsilon$ is nothing but the linear response of Chapter 9, and the GW approximation is nothing other than computing, by linear response, “the reaction that an electron receives from the polarization cloud it has itself created.”

However, as soon as we try to apply the GW approximation to real materials, we run into a single question right away: “From which one-electron states do we build $G$ and $W$?” The standard prescription (one-shot GW, $G^{\rm LDA}W^{\rm LDA}$) builds them from the Kohn–Sham orbitals of density functional theory (DFT) in the local density approximation (LDA). But for materials in which the LDA orbitals and band gaps are poor—transition-metal oxides, $d$/$f$-electron systems, and InN and Ge, whose gaps almost close—the answer is dragged along by the starting point and is wrong even qualitatively. On the other hand, if we make $G$ “fully self-consistent” through the Dyson equation, the bandwidths now become too wide and the plasmon spectrum is destroyed.

One answer to this difficult problem is the quasiparticle self-consistent GW method (QSGW), proposed in 2004–2007 by Takao Kotani, M. van Schilfgaarde, and S. Faleev. The idea is clear: “determine, self-consistently, the independent-particle Hamiltonian $H^0$ itself—the starting point of perturbation theory—so that the perturbation is minimized.” The guiding principle for this choice is Landau's quasiparticle picture, and as a result the eigenvalues of $H^0$ coincide with the quasiparticle energies within the GW approximation. It is a parameter-free first-principles method that depends on neither LDA nor DFT.

In this chapter we derive the fundamental equation of QSGW in the notation of Fetter–Walecka, and explain “why it is the best” from two viewpoints: minimization of the norm functional and cancellation of the $Z$ factors. We then see how well it agrees for real materials, where systematic errors remain, and which part of linear response theory they come from. The source texts are the four original papers by Kotani et al. (Refs. [3]–[6]). We have written it so that you can grasp “what the method does” from Section 14.1 and Section 14.5 even if you do not follow the equations.

14.1 Motivation — The Answer of the GW Approximation Depends on “What We Build It From”

14.1.1 The one-shot GW procedure

We first review the standard procedure described in Appendix C, Section C.8.3. (i) Solve the LDA Kohn–Sham equation to obtain the orbitals $\psi_i$ and the eigenvalues $\epsilon_i$. (ii) Then construct $G^{\rm LDA}$ and, within the RPA, construct $W^{\rm LDA}=v/\epsilon^{\rm RPA}$. (iii) Compute $\Sig=\ii G^{\rm LDA}W^{\rm LDA}$ just once, and obtain the quasiparticle energies by first-order perturbation as follows:

$$ \begin{equation} E_i=\epsilon_i+Z_i\Big[\bra{\psi_i}\Sig(\epsilon_i)\ket{\psi_i}-\bra{\psi_i}V^{\rm xc}_{\rm LDA}\ket{\psi_i}\Big],\qquad Z_i=\Big[1-\bra{\psi_i}\frac{\partial\Sig}{\partial\omega}\Big|_{\epsilon_i}\ket{\psi_i}\Big]^{-1} \label{eq:14-oneshot} \end{equation} $$

Ever since Hybertsen and Louie (1986) applied this prescription to $sp^3$ semiconductors and showed that the gap of Si, only about $0.5\,\mathrm{eV}$ in the LDA, opens up to a value close to the experimental value of $1.17\,\mathrm{eV}$, “GW calculation” has almost always meant this one-shot GW ($G^{\rm LDA}W^{\rm LDA}$, $G_0W_0$).

14.1.2 The three pathologies

However, when the calculation is carried out carefully with an all-electron implementation that includes the core states instead of using pseudopotentials, it became clear in the early 2000s that one-shot GW has systematic defects (Kotani–van Schilfgaarde 2002 [3], Faleev et al. 2004 [4]).

  1. Systematic underestimation of the gap. As Table 14.1 shows, once the calculations are put on the same all-electron footing, the gap of Si falls short by $0.3\,\mathrm{eV}$. The same tendency is seen in almost all $sp$ semiconductors ([5] Fig. 1, upper panel). The reason is simple: if the LDA gap is small, $\chi^0$ is large, screening is too effective, $W$ becomes small, and the correction to the gap falls short. The apparently good agreement with experiment in the early pseudopotential calculations owed much to cancellation against other errors, such as those in the treatment of the core states.
  2. Starting-point dependence. If one performs the “self-consistent treatment of the eigenvalues only,” in which the LDA eigenvalues alone are updated, the gap of ZnS moves as $3.19\to3.64\,\mathrm{eV}$ and that of ZnSe as $2.32\to2.41\,\mathrm{eV}$. Moreover, merely changing the starting point from the LDA to the GGA moves the ZnSe gap $2.41\to2.69\,\mathrm{eV}$ (Luo et al., introduction of [6]). Here lies one cause of the long-standing trouble with GW calculations, namely that the results for the same quantity disagree from one group to another. If the answer changes with what we use for $G^0$, then it is not a theory but a recipe.
  3. Qualitative breakdown when the LDA orbitals are poor. In the LDA, the gaps of InN and Ge are nearly zero (or negative), and a one-shot GW starting from there cannot follow, by first-order perturbation, the large change “from a metal to an insulator.” For NiO the one-shot GW gap stays at $\sim1\,\mathrm{eV}$ (experiment: $4.3\,\mathrm{eV}$), and for CoO and ErAs it is qualitatively wrong. This is because, when the LDA orbitals get the hybridization of the $d$ and $sp$ electrons wrong, correcting only the eigenvalues does not correct the orbitals.

14.1.3 Why does fully self-consistent GW make things worse?

Naively, one would expect that it suffices to rebuild $\Sig=\ii GW$, $W=v/(1-v\Pol)$ and $\Pol=-\ii GG$ all from the $G$ that satisfies the Dyson equation $G=G^0+G^0\Sig G$, and to iterate until convergence (fully self-consistent GW, full SCGW). This is the “conserving approximation” derived from the Luttinger–Ward functional (Appendix C, Section C.8.4, Reason 1), and it is theoretically well founded. In an actual calculation, however (Holm–von Barth 1998 [10], Ku–Eguiluz 2002 [11]):

  • The occupied bandwidth of the uniform electron gas becomes $15\%$ wider than the free-electron one. Experiment (photoelectron spectroscopy of Na), on the other hand, gives a bandwidth $15\%$ narrower than the free-electron one. One-shot GW makes it $15\%$ narrower, in agreement with experiment.
  • For Si and Ge as well, full SCGW overestimates the valence bandwidth (for $\Gamma_{1v}$ of Ge in Table 14.1: one-shot $-12.9$ to $-13.1$, SCGW (Ku) $-14.8$, experiment $-12.6\,\mathrm{eV}$).
  • The plasmon peak is blurred, and the shape of the loss function $\Im(-1/\epsilon)$ is destroyed.

The cause lies in the $Z$ factor discussed in Appendix C. The interacting $G$ can be written as $G=ZG^{\rm QP}+\bar G$ (quasiparticle part + noninteracting part, $Z\simeq0.7$ to $1$). When we build $\Pol=-\ii GG$, the weight of the particle–hole pair excitations is suppressed by a factor of $Z\times Z$, and the remainder goes to physically unclear “quasiparticle × satellite” intermediate states. The suppressed $\Pol$ yields a $W$ whose screening is too weak, and the Hartree–Fock-like widening of the bandwidth (which occurs in the unscreened limit) appears. Furthermore, $\Pol$ loses its meaning as the density response function $\delta n/\delta V$, and it does not satisfy the $f$-sum rule (Chapter 8) either. Landau's quasiparticle picture demands that what enters $\Pol$ should be quasiparticles of weight 1—it is fully self-consistent GW that violates this (we state this precisely using the Ward identity in Section 14.4.2).

14.1.4 The idea of QSGW — optimizing the “partitioning” of perturbation theory

To summarize, what we want is a method that satisfies the following two conditions at the same time. (a) The answer does not depend on the starting point (the LDA). (b) The $\Pol$ used to build $W$ consists of quasiparticles of weight 1.

The idea of QSGW is as follows. We set up perturbation theory by splitting the many-body Hamiltonian $\Ham$ into an independent-particle Hamiltonian $\Ham^0$ and the remainder $\Ham-\Ham^0$. Here there is a great deal of freedom in the choice of $\Ham^0$—$V^{\rm eff}$ may even be nonlocal as long as it is static and Hermitian. Then let us choose $\Ham^0$ so that the effect of the perturbation $\Ham-\Ham^0$ is minimized. The yardstick for measuring “minimal” is Landau's quasiparticle: we determine $\Ham^0$ so that the eigenstates of $\Ham^0$ (bare quasiparticles) and the quasiparticles of the interacting system calculated in the GW approximation (dressed quasiparticles) agree as closely as possible. Since this condition contains $\Ham^0$ itself, it is solved self-consistently—this is self-consistent perturbation theory.

Physical Note: Hartree–Fock already embodies the same idea

The Hartree–Fock method does the same thing within the bare Coulomb interaction $v$. It determines the orbitals self-consistently so that “the first-order correction vanishes” (Brillouin's theorem). As a result, the HF eigenvalues are one-electron excitation energies in the sense of Koopmans' theorem. QSGW can be viewed as a reassembly of the same structure, with $v$ replaced by the screened $W$ and the static exchange $\Sigma_x$ replaced by the dynamic $\Sig(\omega)$. At the end of Section 14.3, we verify that QSGW returns exactly to HF in the limit $W\to v$.

14.2.2 The independent-particle Hamiltonian and $G^0$

The starting point is the independent-particle Hamiltonian with a static, Hermitian, and (in general) nonlocal potential $\Veff(\rr,\rr')$:

$$ \begin{equation} H^0=-\frac{\nabla^2}{2m}+\Veff(\rr,\rr'),\qquad H^0\psi_i=\epsilon_i\psi_i . \label{eq:14-H0} \end{equation} $$

In Kohn–Sham theory, $\Veff$ is restricted to be local, $\Veff(\rr)\delta(\rr-\rr')$, but in QSGW this restriction is lifted. The Fock operator of Hartree–Fock, $-\sum_{i}^{\occ}\psi_i(\rr)\psi_i^*(\rr')v(\rr,\rr')$, is a typical example. From the eigenpairs $\{\epsilon_i,\psi_i\}$ of $H^0$ we construct the noninteracting Green's function (a generalization of F–W (9.14); Appendix C, Section C.1.2):

$$ \begin{equation} G^0(\rr,\rr',\omega)=\sum_i\frac{\psi_i(\rr)\psi_i^*(\rr')}{\omega-\epsilon_i\pm\ii\delta},\qquad \begin{cases}-\ii\delta&(\text{occupied}),\\ +\ii\delta&(\text{unoccupied}).\end{cases} \label{eq:14-G0} \end{equation} $$

The positions of the poles (occupied states in the upper half-plane, unoccupied states in the lower half-plane) follow the convention of the time-ordered Green's function of F–W. The spectral function $A^0(\omega)=-\frac1\pi\Im\Tr G^0$ of $G^0$ is a collection of delta functions located at $\epsilon_i$, with all weights equal to 1 and infinite lifetimes (Appendix C, Section C.1.2).

14.2.3 The RPA polarization, the screened interaction, and the GW self-energy

From $G^0$ we construct the RPA polarization. Written in terms of the eigenfunctions (including the spin sum), it is

$$ \begin{equation} \Pi^0(\rr,\rr',\omega)=\sum_{i}^{\occ}\sum_{j}^{\unocc} \left[\frac{\psi_i^*(\rr)\psi_j(\rr)\,\psi_j^*(\rr')\psi_i(\rr')}{\omega-(\epsilon_j-\epsilon_i)+\ii\delta} -\frac{\psi_i(\rr)\psi_j^*(\rr)\,\psi_j(\rr')\psi_i^*(\rr')}{\omega+(\epsilon_j-\epsilon_i)-\ii\delta}\right]. \label{eq:14-Pi0} \end{equation} $$

If the $\psi_i$ are plane waves, this coincides (Exercise 14.1) with the Lindhard function $\chi^0(\qq,\omega)=\frac1V\sum_{\kk\sigma}\frac{n_\kk-n_{\kk+\qq}}{\omega+\epsilon_\kk-\epsilon_{\kk+\qq}+\ii\delta}$, which was fully derived in Chapter 10. In other words, Eq. \eqref{eq:14-Pi0} is “the Lindhard function for a general band structure.” The screened interaction is nothing but the self-consistent screening argument of Chapter 9,

$$ \begin{equation} W=v+v\,\Pol\,W=(1-v\Pol)^{-1}v=\epsilon^{-1}v,\qquad \Pol\simeq\Pi^0\ \ (\text{RPA}). \label{eq:14-W} \end{equation} $$

(The product of operators is a convolution via $\int\dd^3r''$. F–W (9.40), Appendix C, Section C.7.) With this we can write the self-energy of the GW approximation (Hedin 1965 [1], Appendix C, Section C.8.1):

$$ \begin{equation} \Sig(\rr,\rr',\omega)=\frac{\ii}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dd\omega'\;G^0(\rr,\rr',\omega-\omega')\,W(\rr,\rr',\omega')\,\ee^{-\ii\delta\omega'} . \label{eq:14-SigmaGW} \end{equation} $$

This is the $\Sig=\ii G^0W$ of Appendix C written in the frequency representation. The convergence factor $\ee^{-\ii\delta\omega'}$ is a manifestation of Feynman rule 6 of F–W (a factor $\ee^{\ii\omega\eta}$ for a line joining the two ends of the same interaction line; Appendix C, Definition C.2), and it correctly defines the frequency integral of the exchange term that comes from the instantaneous part $v$ of $W$.

Derivation 14.1: The $W\to v$ part of Eq. \eqref{eq:14-SigmaGW} becomes the Fock exchange operator

Split $W=v+W_c$ (where $W_c\equiv W-v$ is the correlation part). Since $v$ does not depend on $\omega'$, the $v$ part is

$$ \Sigma_x(\rr,\rr')=\ii\,v(\rr,\rr')\int\frac{\dd\omega'}{2\pi}\,G^0(\rr,\rr',\omega-\omega')\,\ee^{-\ii\delta\omega'} . $$

Setting $\omega''=\omega-\omega'$, we have $\ee^{-\ii\delta\omega'}=\ee^{-\ii\delta\omega}\ee^{+\ii\delta\omega''}$. The factor $\ee^{\ii\delta\omega''}$ decays in the upper half-plane, so the $\omega''$ integral is closed in the upper half-plane (Chapter 5, Jordan's lemma). Let us check the positions of the poles of Eq. \eqref{eq:14-G0}: the denominator for an occupied state is $\omega''-\epsilon_i-\ii\delta$, so its pole is at $\omega''=\epsilon_i+\ii\delta$ (upper half-plane), whereas the poles of the unoccupied states are at $\epsilon_j-\ii\delta$ (lower half-plane). Therefore only the occupied states contribute to the integral closed in the upper half-plane, and by the residue theorem

$$ \int\frac{\dd\omega''}{2\pi}\,G^0(\rr,\rr',\omega'')\,\ee^{\ii\delta\omega''}=\ii\sum_i^{\occ}\psi_i(\rr)\psi_i^*(\rr') \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Sigma_x(\rr,\rr')=-\sum_i^{\occ}\psi_i(\rr)\psi_i^*(\rr')\,v(\rr,\rr') . $$

This is nothing but the exchange (Fock) operator of Hartree–Fock. It does not depend on $\omega$ (it is static) and is Hermitian. It is the extension to general bands of $-\int\frac{\dd^3k_1}{(2\pi)^3}V(\kk-\kk_1)\theta(k_F-k_1)$, which was obtained from the momentum-space rules of F–W in Appendix C, Derivation C.5. The correlation part $\Sigma_c=\frac{\ii}{2\pi}\int\dd\omega'\,G^0(\omega-\omega')W_c(\omega')$ depends on $\omega$ and has a non-Hermitian part (the lifetime).

14.2.4 The “frequency-dependent potential” given by GW, and the quasiparticle equation

When we combine the external potential $\Vext$ due to the nuclei, the Hartree potential $\VH$ built from the density $n(\rr)=-\frac{\ii}{2\pi}\int\dd\omega\,G^0(\rr,\rr,\omega)\ee^{\ii\delta\omega}=\sum_i^{\occ}\abs{\psi_i(\rr)}^2$ of $G^0$, and $\Sig(\omega)$, the GW approximation returns, for $\Veff$, the potential

$$ \begin{equation} \VGW(\omega)=\Vext+\VH+\Sig(\omega),\qquad \Delta V(\omega)\equiv\VGW(\omega)-\Veff \label{eq:14-VGW} \end{equation} $$

which is a frequency-dependent one-body potential. Given $\Veff$, $\VGW(\omega)$ is determined—the GW approximation is a map $\Veff\mapsto\VGW(\omega)$. By the Dyson equation (Chapter 13, Section 13.10; Appendix C, Section C.6.2), the Green's function of the interacting system is

$$ \begin{equation} G(\omega)=\big[\omega-H^0-\Delta V(\omega)\big]^{-1}=\Big[\omega+\frac{\nabla^2}{2m}-\VGW(\omega)\Big]^{-1}. \label{eq:14-Dyson} \end{equation} $$

$\Delta V(\omega)$ is the “perturbation on $H^0$.” One-shot GW is nothing other than inserting $\Veff=V^{\rm KS}_{\rm LDA}$ and evaluating $\Delta V$ by first-order perturbation (Eq. \eqref{eq:14-oneshot}).

Supplement: The rule of thumb that it is better not to multiply by the $Z$ factor

The $Z_i$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:14-oneshot} is the factor that appears when the frequency dependence of $\Sig$ is linearized, and it is the same as the weight $Z$ of the Lehmann representation (Chapter 13; Appendix C, Section C.1.3). From a textbook standpoint it is “correct” to multiply by it, but Kotani et al. showed that setting $Z=1$ gives systematically better results (within the scope of one-shot calculations) (compare one-shot and one-shot-NZ in Tables II–IV of [6]: Si $0.98\to1.13$, GaAs $1.44\to1.75\,\mathrm{eV}$). Mahan, in his analysis of the Fröhlich Hamiltonian, argues that $Z=1$ corresponds to Rayleigh–Schrödinger perturbation theory and multiplying by $Z$ corresponds to Brillouin–Wigner perturbation theory, and that the former is preferable. A deeper reason lies in the cancellation of the $Z$ factors in Section 14.4.2. In QSGW, the question of what to do about $Z$ disappears altogether (Section 14.3.4).

14.3 The Fundamental Equation of QSGW — Making the Bare and Dressed Quasiparticles Coincide

14.3.1 Two kinds of quasiparticles

According to Landau's quasiparticle picture, at least near the Fermi energy $E_F$ there exist elementary excitations of a one-particle nature (quasiparticles). Within the GW approximation, the energy $E_i$ and wave function $\Phi_i(\rr)$ of such a quasiparticle are determined, as the poles of $G$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:14-Dyson}, by the equation

$$ \begin{equation} \Big[-\frac{\nabla^2}{2m}+\Vext+\VH+\Re\big[\Sig(E_i)\big]\Big]\Phi_i=E_i\,\Phi_i \label{eq:14-dressed} \end{equation} $$

Here $\Re[\Sig(E_i)]$ is the Hermitian part. We take only the Hermitian part in order to make $E_i$ real; the anti-Hermitian part (the inverse lifetime, Appendix C, Section C.1.3) vanishes as $E_i\to E_F$, so it causes no problem near $E_F$. Eq. \eqref{eq:14-dressed} is a nonlinear eigenvalue problem in the sense that it “contains $\Sig$ evaluated at its own energy $E_i$,” and its solutions $\{E_i,\Phi_i\}$ are called dressed quasiparticles (dressed QPs).

On the other hand, the eigenpairs $\{\epsilon_i,\psi_i\}$ of $H^0$ are called bare quasiparticles (bare QPs). Bare quasiparticles interact with one another through the bare Coulomb interaction $v$, and when $\Ham-\Ham^0$ is switched on adiabatically they turn into dressed quasiparticles. A dressed quasiparticle consists of “a central bare quasiparticle plus the polarization cloud formed by the other bare quasiparticles,” and this is exactly the picture used when the poles of $G$ are computed from $G^0$ in the RPA. This view is consistent with the Pines–Nozières charged Fermi-liquid theory (the Landau–Silin quasiparticles).

14.3.2 From the requirement of coincidence to the fundamental equation

The QSGW postulate is that the bare quasiparticles and the dressed quasiparticles coincide as closely as possible near $E_F$: $\{\epsilon_i,\psi_i\}\simeq\{E_i,\Phi_i\}$. We want to determine $\Veff$ (more precisely, its exchange–correlation part $\Vxc\equiv\Veff-\Vext-\VH$) so that this holds. Suppose that self-consistency has been achieved. Then in Eq. \eqref{eq:14-dressed} we may set $\Re[\Sig(E_i)]\ket{\Phi_i}\simeq\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_i)]\ket{\psi_i}$. Since $\{\psi_i\}$ are the eigenfunctions of $H^0$, they form a complete set (note that $\{\Phi_i\}$, being solutions of a nonlinear eigenvalue problem, do not in general form a complete set). We therefore expand:

$$ \begin{equation} \Re[\Sig(\epsilon_i)]\ket{\psi_i}=\sum_j\ket{\psi_j}\,\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_i)]_{ji},\qquad \Re[\Sig(\omega)]_{ji}\equiv\bra{\psi_j}\Re[\Sig(\omega)]\ket{\psi_i}. \label{eq:14-expand} \end{equation} $$

We want to replace the right-hand side by “a frequency-independent operator that, acting on $\ket{\psi_i}$, returns the same result.” Such an operator is given by

$$ \begin{equation} R\equiv\sum_{j,i}\ket{\psi_j}\,\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_i)]_{ji}\,\bra{\psi_i},\qquad R\ket{\psi_i}=\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_i)]\ket{\psi_i} \label{eq:14-R} \end{equation} $$

(to each $\ket{\psi_i}$ we assign $\Sig$ evaluated at the energy $\epsilon_i$ of that state itself). The operator $R$ can be used in place of $\Re[\Sig(E_i)]$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:14-dressed}, but $R$ is not Hermitian—$\bra{\psi_j}R\ket{\psi_i}=\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_i)]_{ji}$ and $\bra{\psi_i}R\ket{\psi_j}^*=\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_j)]_{ji}$ differ, because the energy at which $\Sig$ is evaluated is $\epsilon_i$ in one and $\epsilon_j$ in the other. $\Veff$ must be Hermitian (so that the eigenvalues are real and the eigenfunctions form a complete orthonormal set). We therefore take the Hermitian part of $R$:

Derivation 14.2: The Hermitian part of $R$

Since $A(\omega)\equiv\Re[\Sig(\omega)]$ is Hermitian, $A(\omega)_{ji}^*=A(\omega)_{ij}$. Then $R^\dagger=\sum_{j,i}\ket{\psi_i}A(\epsilon_i)_{ji}^*\bra{\psi_j}=\sum_{j,i}\ket{\psi_i}A(\epsilon_i)_{ij}\bra{\psi_j}$. On the other hand, renaming the indices in $R$ as $i\leftrightarrow j$ gives $R=\sum_{i,j}\ket{\psi_i}A(\epsilon_j)_{ij}\bra{\psi_j}$. Hence

$$ \Re R=\frac{R+R^\dagger}{2}=\frac12\sum_{i,j}\ket{\psi_i}\big\{A(\epsilon_i)_{ij}+A(\epsilon_j)_{ij}\big\}\bra{\psi_j}. $$

The diagonal elements ($i=j$) are $A(\epsilon_i)_{ii}=\bra{\psi_i}\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_i)]\ket{\psi_i}$, which is the same as the correction term at $Z=1$ in the one-shot GW formula, Eq. \eqref{eq:14-oneshot}. The off-diagonal elements are the average of $\Sig$ evaluated at the energies of the two states.

The fundamental equation of QSGW (van Schilfgaarde–Kotani–Faleev 2006 [5], Eq. (2); Kotani–van Schilfgaarde–Faleev 2007 [6], Eq. (10); “mode A”)

$$ \begin{equation} \Vxc_{\rm QSGW}=\frac12\sum_{i,j}\ket{\psi_i}\Big\{\Re\big[\Sig(\epsilon_i)\big]_{ij}+\Re\big[\Sig(\epsilon_j)\big]_{ij}\Big\}\bra{\psi_j}, \qquad \Veff=\Vext+\VH+\Vxc_{\rm QSGW}. \label{eq:14-QSGW} \end{equation} $$

The $\Sig$ and $\{\epsilon_i,\psi_i\}$ on the right-hand side are computed from $\Veff$ in the GW approximation, so this is a self-consistent equation for $\Veff$.

Eq. \eqref{eq:14-QSGW} was derived from the postulate $\{\epsilon_i,\psi_i\}\simeq\{E_i,\Phi_i\}$ near $E_F$, but this postulate alone does not determine $\Vxc$ uniquely. There are other choices for the treatment of the off-diagonal elements. An alternative prescription that Kotani et al. tried in their first paper [4] is

$$ \begin{equation} \Vxc_{\rm mode\,B}=\sum_i\ket{\psi_i}\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_i)]_{ii}\bra{\psi_i}+\sum_{i\neq j}\ket{\psi_i}\Re[\Sig(E_F)]_{ij}\bra{\psi_j} \label{eq:14-modeB} \end{equation} $$

(evaluating the off-diagonal elements at $E_F$, “mode B”). The diagonal part is the same as in mode A. The two results are very similar (Si gap: mode A $1.25$, mode B $1.28\,\mathrm{eV}$; GaAs: $1.97$ and $2.03\,\mathrm{eV}$ [6]), and this difference indicates the size of the ambiguity inherent in the QSGW prescription. In practice, mode A agrees with experiment most systematically.

14.3.3 The self-consistency cycle

The cycle is as shown in Figure 14.2. LDA is used as the starting point merely to supply initial values, and the converged solution does not depend on LDA (in [6], it was confirmed that the result does not change when the starting conditions are varied). Once $\Vxc$ has been updated, we run an inner loop that converges the density and the Hartree potential while keeping $\Delta\Vxc\equiv\Vxc-\Vxc_{\rm LDA}$ fixed. Since $\Delta\Vxc$ is less sensitive to changes in the density than $\Vxc$ itself, the number of recomputations of the expensive $\Sig$ can be reduced.

14.3.4 What holds once convergence is reached

For the converged $\Veff$, the following hold by construction.

  1. The diagonal elements agree. $\bra{\psi_i}\Vxc\ket{\psi_i}=\bra{\psi_i}\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_i)]\ket{\psi_i}$. Therefore the Hermitian part of the one-shot first-order correction $\bra{\psi_i}\Sig(\epsilon_i)-\Vxc\ket{\psi_i}$ is identically zero. The question in Eq. \eqref{eq:14-oneshot} of whether or not to multiply by $Z$ disappears, because the thing to be multiplied is zero.
  2. The bands of $H^0$ are the quasiparticle bands. Near $E_F$, $\epsilon_i=E_i$ and $\psi_i=\Phi_i$ (within the Hermitian part). In other words, the QSGW band structure is itself the quasiparticle energy of the GW approximation. There is no need to “add a GW correction” to the LDA band structure. This is what is meant by “giving physical meaning to the eigenvalues of $H^0$,” and it contrasts with the Kohn–Sham eigenvalues, which are not quasiparticle energies (even in exact DFT).
  3. The lifetime comes afterward. Since $H^0$ is static and Hermitian, it has no lifetime. The quasiparticle lifetime $1/\tau_i=-2\Im\bra{\psi_i}\Sig(\epsilon_i)\ket{\psi_i}$ is evaluated just once, at the end, from the converged $G^0$ and $W$. $\Im\Sig$ vanishes at $E_F$; in an insulator it is “exactly zero if the electron energy is at most (conduction-band minimum) $+E_g$” (because the electron cannot decay by exciting a particle–hole pair; Section 14.6.3).
  4. $W$ is built from quasiparticles of weight 1. The $G^0$ in $\Pi^0=-\ii G^0G^0$ is that of $H^0$, so particle–hole pairs enter with weight 1. Postulate (b) of Section 14.1.3 is satisfied.
  5. Density. The density used in the Hartree potential is the density of $G^0$, $n_{G^0}=\sum_i^{\occ}\abs{\psi_i}^2$ (the quasiparticle density). It differs from the true density $n_G$ of the interacting system by an amount proportional to $\Sig-\Vxc$, and this difference is a measure of the size of the internal inconsistency of QSGW ([6], Eq. (23)).

Theorem 14.1 (QSGW coincides with Hartree–Fock in the limit $W\to v$)

If we switch off the screening and set $W=v$, then by Derivation 14.1 $\Sig=\Sigma_x$ becomes a Hermitian operator that does not depend on $\omega$. In this case $\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_i)]_{ij}=\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_j)]_{ij}=(\Sigma_x)_{ij}$, so Eq. \eqref{eq:14-QSGW} gives, without any approximation, $\Vxc=\sum_{ij}\ket{\psi_i}(\Sigma_x)_{ij}\bra{\psi_j}=\Sigma_x$. The cycle then coincides with the self-consistent solution of $\Veff=\Vext+\VH+\Sigma_x[\{\psi\}]$, that is, with the Hartree–Fock SCF equations. More generally, whenever $\Sig$ is static and Hermitian, QSGW reproduces it exactly, whatever $\Sig$ is. The “approximation” of QSGW is concentrated in one point: the frequency dependence of $\Sig$ is represented by the average of its values at $\epsilon_i$ and $\epsilon_j$.

Supplement: The difference from COHSEX

The classic approximation of a static, Hermitian self-energy is Hedin's COHSEX (Coulomb hole + screened exchange): $\Sigma^{\rm SEX}(\rr,\rr')=-\sum_i^{\occ}\psi_i(\rr)\psi_i^*(\rr')\,W(\rr,\rr',\omega=0)$, $\Sigma^{\rm COH}(\rr,\rr')=\tfrac12\delta(\rr-\rr')\big[W(\rr,\rr',0)-v(\rr,\rr')\big]$. This approximation neglects the frequency dependence of $W$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:14-SigmaGW} (static screening); it is computationally light but is known to overestimate the gap by $\sim1\,\mathrm{eV}$. The $\Vxc$ of QSGW is also static and Hermitian, but the essential difference is that it does not discard the frequency dependence of $\Sig(\omega)$, but uses the value evaluated at the energy $\epsilon_i$ of each state. Bruneval–Vast–Reining (2006 [9]) implemented QSGW with the pseudopotential method and compared it systematically with COHSEX and HF.

14.4 Why Is It the Best? — The Norm Functional, the Landau–Silin Picture, and Cancellation of the $Z$ Factor

14.4.1 Minimization of the norm functional

The derivation in Section 14.3.2 was a construction based on the requirement that we “want the two to coincide.” The same equation also follows from a variational form in which one minimizes a norm that measures the size of the perturbation. We define a positive-definite norm functional that measures the size of the perturbation $\Delta V(\omega)=\VGW(\omega)-\Veff$ on the quasiparticles of $H^0$ [6]:

$$ \begin{equation} M[\Veff]\equiv\Tr\Big\{\Re[\Delta V(\omega)]\;\rho\;\Re[\Delta V(\omega)]\Big\},\qquad \rho\equiv\delta(\omega-H^0), \label{eq:14-norm} \end{equation} $$

The trace is taken over space (and spin) and over $\omega$. The weight $\rho=\delta(\omega-H^0)$ means “measure the size of the perturbation at the quasiparticle energies of $H^0$.”

Derivation 14.3: Minimizing the norm yields Eq. \eqref{eq:14-QSGW}

Carry out the $\omega$ integral first. Since $\delta(\omega-H^0)=\sum_j\ket{\psi_j}\delta(\omega-\epsilon_j)\bra{\psi_j}$,

$$ M=\int\!\dd\omega\sum_j\delta(\omega-\epsilon_j)\bra{\psi_j}\Re[\Delta V(\omega)]\,\Re[\Delta V(\omega)]\ket{\psi_j} =\sum_j\bra{\psi_j}\Re[\Delta V(\epsilon_j)]^2\ket{\psi_j} =\sum_{i,j}\Big|\bra{\psi_i}\Re[\VGW(\epsilon_j)]-\Veff\ket{\psi_j}\Big|^2 . $$

In the last equality we inserted the complete set $\sum_i\ket{\psi_i}\bra{\psi_i}$ and used the facts that $\Re[\Delta V]$ is Hermitian and that, because $\Veff$ is Hermitian, $\Re[\VGW-\Veff]=\Re\VGW-\Veff$. Since $\Vext+\VH$ is common to both terms, writing $A(\omega)\equiv\Re[\Sig(\omega)]$ and $X\equiv\Vxc$ (Hermitian, $X_{ji}=X_{ij}^*$), we have

$$ M=\sum_{i,j}\abs{A(\epsilon_j)_{ij}-X_{ij}}^2 . $$

Combine the terms for the index pairs $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$. From $X_{ji}=X_{ij}^*$ and $A(\epsilon_i)_{ji}=A(\epsilon_i)_{ij}^*$ we get $\abs{A(\epsilon_i)_{ji}-X_{ji}}^2=\abs{A(\epsilon_i)_{ij}-X_{ij}}^2$, so for each pair with $i\neq j$ the quantity to be minimized is

$$ \abs{a-x}^2+\abs{b-x}^2,\qquad a\equiv A(\epsilon_j)_{ij},\ b\equiv A(\epsilon_i)_{ij},\ x\equiv X_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}. $$

Differentiating with respect to $x$ (by taking $\partial/\partial x^*$) gives $-(a-x)-(b-x)=0$, that is, $x=(a+b)/2$. For the diagonal terms $i=j$, minimizing $\abs{A(\epsilon_i)_{ii}-X_{ii}}^2$ gives $X_{ii}=A(\epsilon_i)_{ii}$. To summarize,

$$ X_{ij}=\frac12\Big\{\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_i)]_{ij}+\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_j)]_{ij}\Big\}, $$

which is nothing other than Eq. \eqref{eq:14-QSGW}. Note, however, that $\rho$ (that is, $\{\epsilon_j,\psi_j\}$) and $\VGW$ change through $\Veff$, so what we have done here is “the minimization with respect to $X$ with $\rho$ and $\Sig$ held fixed.” In that sense the QSGW solution is a self-consistent stationary point of $M$; an exact minimization is beyond our reach, but Eq. \eqref{eq:14-QSGW} is obtained as an approximate solution, and this is the position taken in [5,6].

This formulation teaches us two things. First, QSGW can be defined as a functional that determines $\Veff$ for a given $\Vext$ (in the same sense that DFT determines $\Vext\mapsto V^{\rm KS}$). Second, the perturbation $\Ham-\Ham^0$ contains not only the Coulomb interaction but also the one-body counterterm $\Vext-\Veff$, and QSGW chooses $\Veff$ so that this counterterm cancels the change of the quasiparticles caused by the Coulomb interaction. The physical content is that $H^0$ has been determined so that “the motion of the electron at the center of a dressed quasiparticle is not changed by $\Ham-\Ham^0$.”

14.4.2 The Landau–Silin picture and cancellation of the $Z$ factor — why expand perturbatively in $G^0$ rather than $G$?

In Reason 2 of Appendix C, Section C.8.4, we stated that the $Z$ factor and the vertex function $\Gamma$ cancel. Because this is the core of the justification of QSGW, we state it once more, a step more precisely, following Appendix A of [6]. Below we use symbolic notation (omitting integrals and indices).

The exact relation is $\Sig=\ii GW\Gamma$ (Hedin's first equation, Appendix C, Section C.8.1). We split $G$ into a quasiparticle part and a noninteracting part:

$$ \begin{equation} G=Z\,G^{0}+\bar G . \label{eq:14-ZG} \end{equation} $$

Here $G^0$ is the quasiparticle Green's function with weight 1 (near $E_F$, the one given by the QSGW $H^0$), $Z$ is the renormalization factor, and $\bar G$ is the noninteracting part, such as plasmon satellites.

(a) Cancellation in $\Sig$. What dominates the integral of $\ii GW\Gamma$ is the long-range, static part of $W$ ($q\to0$, $\omega\to0$). In this limit, in an insulator, the Ward identity gives

$$ \begin{equation} \Gamma\;\longrightarrow\;1-\frac{\partial\Sig}{\partial\omega}=\frac1Z . \label{eq:14-Ward} \end{equation} $$

Therefore $\ii GW\Gamma\simeq\ii(ZG^0)W(1/Z)+\ii\bar GW\Gamma=\ii G^0W+(\bar G\text{ terms})$. If $\bar G$ is poor in structure, $\ii\bar GW$ gives only a nearly constant, state-independent shift (of the order of a change in the chemical potential). In the end, $\Sig\simeq\ii G^0W$: the quasiparticle $G^0$ with weight 1 together with the bare vertex is the correct combination, and $\Sig=\ii GW$ (the dressed $G$ with the bare vertex) includes one extra factor of $Z$.

(b) Cancellation in $\Pol$. The exact proper polarization can be written, using the two-particle-irreducible kernel $I$, as $\bar\Pi=G_2+G_2IG_2+G_2IG_2IG_2+\cdots$ ($G_2=-\ii GG$) (an electron–hole ladder). The intermediate particle–hole pairs in $G_2$ enter with weight $Z\times Z$, but the factors $1/(1-G_2I)$ appearing at the two ends of the ladder play the role of the vertex function and supply $1/Z$ each at $q\to0$, $\omega\to0$. The net weight is $\tfrac1Z\times Z^2\times\tfrac1Z=1$—the particle–hole pairs enter $\Pol$ with weight 1. This means that $\Pol\simeq\Pi^0=-\ii G^0G^0$ ($G^0$ being the quasiparticle with weight 1) is a good approximation. Bechstedt et al. have confirmed this cancellation numerically at the lowest order [12].

Fully self-consistent GW dresses $G$ while keeping $\Gamma=1$, so in both (a) and (b) the $Z$ factors remain uncanceled. The Luttinger–Ward form $\Sig=\delta E_{xc}[G]/\delta G$ is exact, but an expansion in $G$ involves large cancellations among its terms, needed to cancel out $Z$, and is extremely inefficient. On the other hand, an expansion in $G^0$ ($G_0W_0$ or QSGW) includes the quasiparticle with the correct weight 1—this is what the Landau–Silin quasiparticle picture expects from the outset. The question is “how do we extract $G^0$ from $G$?”, and QSGW gives a (nearly) optimal prescription for it: $G\to G^0\to\Sig\to G\to G^0\to\cdots$.

In a metal, the right-hand side of Eq. \eqref{eq:14-Ward} acquires an additional term owing to the presence of the Fermi surface, and $\Gamma>1/Z$, but the backbone of the argument is unchanged. Note also that, for the same reason, the Bethe–Salpeter equation (the sum of ladders) should likewise be built with $G^0$ rather than $G$.

The nonlocality of $\Vxc$ and the material dependence of the screening through $\Sig$ do this automatically.

14.5.3 The origin of the systematic error — the RPA $W$ lacks the electron–hole interaction

The errors of QSGW are not only small but consistent in direction. The gap is overestimated, $\epsilon_\infty$ is underestimated, and the effective mass is overestimated. The three are connected in a single chain:

The RPA $\Pol\simeq\Pi^0$ does not include the attraction between the excited electron and hole (ladder diagrams, Appendix C, Figure C.19 right, and Section C.8.4, Reason 3) → the excitation energies of the particle–hole pairs are too high, and $\epsilon_\infty$ is $15$–$25\%$ too small (Table 14.6) → the screening in $W=v/\epsilon$ is too weak → the exchange term $\ii G^0W$ opens the gap too much → by $\kk\cdot\pp$ perturbation theory $m_c\propto E_g$, so the effective mass is also overestimated in proportion (GaAs: the ratio of $E_g$ is $1.16$ and that of $m_c$ is $1.18$ [5]).

  • Extensions of the implementation. Kotani extended FP-LMTO to the PMT method, which uses APW and MTO together, and released QSGW as the code ecalj ([7]). In VASP, a similar self-consistent quasiparticle GW by Shishkin–Marsman–Kresse (scQPGW, QP$GW$ in Appendix C, Table C.3) is implemented [16].
  • QSGW80. To correct the systematic error of Section 14.5.3 in a practical way, a hybrid that mixes $\Vxc=0.8\,\Vxc_{\rm QSGW}+0.2\,\Vxc_{\rm LDA}$ was proposed (Deguchi et al. 2016 [13]). It is an empirical rule that puts the gaps of many semiconductors on the experimental values, but its theoretical basis is the compensation of the under-screening of $W$.
  • QSG$\hat W$. It was shown that “QSG$\hat W$”, which runs QSGW with the $\hat W$ obtained by putting Bethe–Salpeter ladders into $\Pol$, removes the systematic errors of both $\epsilon_\infty$ and the gap at the same time (Cunningham et al. 2018, 2023 [14,15]). This is a demonstration that the diagnosis of Section 14.5.3 was correct.
  • Toward strongly correlated systems. Progress is being made on QSGW+DMFT, which uses QSGW as the starting point of dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT), and on extensions that incorporate spin fluctuations. All of them continue along the line of the design philosophy of QSGW: “first determine the best independent-particle picture, and then add the rest as a perturbation.”

14.6 Key Points of the Implementation — All-Electron, Mixed Product Basis, $\omega$ Integration, and $q\to0$

The QSGW equation, Eq. \eqref{eq:14-QSGW}, is concise, but computing even the off-diagonal elements of $\Sig$ accurately in an all-electron framework is not easy. We describe the main points of the implementation of Kotani et al. [3,6,7], keeping in mind how it corresponds to the tools of this book (Chapters 4 and 5).

14.6.1 Why all-electron, and what is the mixed product basis?

Core electrons make a non-negligible contribution to $\Sig$. If the core is eliminated with a pseudopotential, the exchange term between valence and core electrons is lost, and the treatment of semicore states such as Ga $3d$ also becomes inaccurate. Building on the all-electron full-potential LMTO (FP-LMTO) method, [3] implemented for the first time a GW that treats core and valence electrons on the same footing, and showed that “the apparent agreement of pseudopotential GW gaps with experiment was due to a cancellation of errors.”

$W$ and $\Pol$ are two-point functions $f(\rr,\rr')$, and expanding them requires a basis that can span the products of eigenfunctions $\psi_i^*(\rr)\psi_j(\rr)$. The mixed product basis $\{M_I^\kk(\rr)\}$ of Kotani et al. consists, inside the muffin-tin (MT) spheres, of Aryasetiawan's product basis (built from the products of radial functions $\varphi_{l}\varphi_{l'}$ with the linear dependencies removed; $100$–$150$ functions per transition-metal atom) and, in the interstitial region, of plane waves from which the MT-sphere contribution has been projected out (IPW). Products of localized $d$ and $f$ electrons and of core states are also spanned efficiently. The Coulomb interaction is expanded with the dual basis $\tilde M$ as $v=\sum_{\kk IJ}\ket{\tilde M_I^\kk}v_{IJ}(\kk)\bra{\tilde M_J^\kk}$, and likewise for $W$ and $\Pol$.

14.6.2 Matrix elements of the self-energy

In the mixed product basis, the exchange term of Derivation 14.1 is given by (with $i=(\qq,n)$, and $\sum_\kk^{\rm BZ}$ the sum over the Brillouin zone)

$$ \begin{equation} \bra{\psi_{\qq n}}\Sigma_x\ket{\psi_{\qq m}}=-\sum_{\kk}^{\rm BZ}\sum_{n'}^{\occ}\sum_{IJ} \braket{\psi_{\qq n}|\psi_{\qq-\kk\,n'}\tilde M_I^\kk}\,v_{IJ}(\kk)\,\braket{\tilde M_J^\kk\psi_{\qq-\kk\,n'}|\psi_{\qq m}} \label{eq:14-Sigx} \end{equation} $$

(Eq. (31) of [6].) The polarization, Eq. \eqref{eq:14-Pi0}, is (assuming time-reversal symmetry)

$$ \begin{equation} \Pi^0_{IJ}(\qq,\omega)=\sum_{\kk}^{\rm BZ}\sum_{n}^{\occ}\sum_{n'}^{\unocc} \braket{\tilde M_I^\qq\psi_{\kk n}|\psi_{\qq+\kk\,n'}}\braket{\psi_{\qq+\kk\,n'}|\psi_{\kk n}\tilde M_J^\qq} \left[\frac{1}{\omega-\epsilon_{\qq+\kk n'}+\epsilon_{\kk n}+\ii\delta}-\frac{1}{\omega+\epsilon_{\qq+\kk n'}-\epsilon_{\kk n}-\ii\delta}\right], \label{eq:14-PiIJ} \end{equation} $$

and the correlation part, written with $W^c\equiv W-v$, is

$$ \begin{equation} \bra{\psi_{\qq n}}\Sigma_c(\omega)\ket{\psi_{\qq m}}=\sum_{\kk}^{\rm BZ}\sum_{n'}^{\rm all}\sum_{IJ} \braket{\psi_{\qq n}|\psi_{\qq-\kk\,n'}\tilde M_I^\kk}\braket{\tilde M_J^\kk\psi_{\qq-\kk\,n'}|\psi_{\qq m}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\ii\,\dd\omega'}{2\pi}\,\frac{W^c_{IJ}(\kk,\omega')}{\omega-\omega'-\epsilon_{\qq-\kk\,n'}\pm\ii\delta} \label{eq:14-Sigc} \end{equation} $$

($-\ii\delta$ for occupied states and $+\ii\delta$ for unoccupied states; Eq. (34) of [6]). The only inputs required are the matrix elements of the products $\braket{\psi|\psi\tilde M}$, the eigenvalues $\epsilon_{\kk n}$, the Coulomb matrix $v_{IJ}(\kk)$, and the overlap matrix. Since QSGW needs even the off-diagonal elements with $n\neq m$, it takes $3$–$10$ times more effort than standard GW.

14.6.3 The $\omega'$ integration — the contour deformation of Chapter 5 is used as it is

The $\omega'$ integral in Eq. \eqref{eq:14-Sigc} is hard to handle on the real axis, because $W^c(\omega')$ has sharp structures corresponding to plasmons and particle–hole pairs. We therefore rotate the contour onto the imaginary axis (the deformation of the contour in Chapter 5, Section 5.4). The arrangement of the poles of the integrand is shown in Figure 14.4.

The result splits into “an integral on the imaginary axis” plus “the contributions of poles in a finite interval on the real axis” ([6], Eqs. (56)–(58)). Writing $\omega_\epsilon\equiv\omega-\epsilon_{\qq-\kk n'}$ and suppressing the indices, we have

$$ \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} X&\equiv\int\frac{\ii\,\dd\omega'}{2\pi}\frac{W^c(\omega')}{\omega_\epsilon-\omega'\pm\ii\delta}=X_{\rm img}+X_{\rm real},\\ X_{\rm img}&=-\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\dd\omega'\,W^S(\omega')\frac{\omega_\epsilon}{\omega'^2+\omega_\epsilon^2} +\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\dd\omega'\,W^A(\omega')\frac{\omega'}{\omega'^2+\omega_\epsilon^2},\\ X_{\rm real}&=\begin{cases}\ \ W^c(\omega_\epsilon)&(\omega-E_F>\omega_\epsilon>0),\\ -W^c(\omega_\epsilon)&(0>\omega_\epsilon>\omega-E_F),\\ \ \ 0&(\text{otherwise}),\end{cases} \end{aligned} \label{eq:14-contour} \end{equation} $$

Here $W^S(\omega')=[W^c(\ii\omega')+W^c(-\ii\omega')]/2$ and $W^A(\omega')=[W^c(\ii\omega')-W^c(-\ii\omega')]/2\ii$ ($W^A=0$ if time-reversal symmetry holds). $W^c(\ii\omega')$ on the imaginary axis is a smooth real function and is easy to integrate numerically. What matters physically is $X_{\rm real}$, and the lifetime of a quasiparticle (the anti-Hermitian part of $\Sig$) comes entirely from the imaginary part of $X_{\rm real}$. At $\omega=E_F$ the interval on the real axis vanishes, so the lifetime is infinite. In an insulator, $\Im W^c$ makes no contribution until the electron energy exceeds (conduction-band minimum) $+E_g$, so the lifetime is exactly infinite—the threshold for decay by creating particle–hole pairs (the threshold for impact ionization) can be read off directly from the figure of the integration contour.

Another tool from Chapter 4 is also used. The Brillouin-zone integral of the polarization, Eq. \eqref{eq:14-PiIJ}, is carried out by the tetrahedron method, but Kotani et al. first compute $\Im\Pi^0$ as a histogram for each energy window, and obtain $\Re\Pi^0$ from the Kramers–Kronig relations (Hilbert transform). Since $\Im\Pi^0$ can be found analytically as the cross-sectional area of $\delta(\omega-(\epsilon_{n'}-\epsilon_n))$ within a tetrahedron, the computation time is greatly reduced in QSGW, where $\Pi^0$ is needed over a wide range of $\omega$. This is an example in which causality becomes a computational tool.

14.6.4 The $q\to0$ divergence and other techniques

  • The offset-$\Gamma$ method. $v_{IJ}(\kk)$ diverges as $1/\abs{\kk}^2$ for $\kk\to0$ (an integrable singularity). If the Brillouin-zone sum is done on a discrete mesh, the $\Gamma$ point cannot be handled. Kotani et al. devised the offset-$\Gamma$ method, in which the evaluation is done at points slightly displaced from the $\Gamma$ point and the integral of the singular part is treated analytically [3]. It is numerically equivalent to the Gygi–Baldereschi method and is now also used by other groups.
  • Interpolation of $\Vxc$. $\Vxc$ is computed on the irreducible $k$ mesh, but band plots and effective masses require $H^0$ at arbitrary $\kk$. If we stay in the QSGW basis (the eigenfunctions of $H^0$), the interpolation near band crossings is ambiguous, so we transform to an MTO basis localized in real space and then interpolate to arbitrary $\kk$ by a Bloch sum. For high-energy states above $E_F+2$–$3.5\,\mathrm{Ry}$, a diagonal approximation linear in the LDA eigenvalues, $\Delta\Vxc\simeq a\epsilon^{\rm LDA}+b$, is used (its effect on the results is negligible).
  • Classification of core states. Deep core states (more than $\sim2\,\mathrm{Ry}$ below $E_F$) are treated with exchange only (no screening), and shallow semicore states are put, as local orbitals, on the same footing as the valence electrons (VAL). Because the MT sphere may be unable to contain a shallow core state completely, we check that the results do not change when the classification is changed.
  • Convergence parameters. These are the IPW cutoffs ($\abs{\qq+\bm G}^\Psi_{\max}$ for the wave functions and $\abs{\qq+\bm G}^W_{\max}$ for $W$), the upper limit of the angular momentum of the product basis $l_p^{\max}\simeq2\times$ (the largest occupied $l$; 4 for transition metals), and the $k$ mesh. For GaAs, an accuracy of the order of $0.1\,\mathrm{eV}$ is obtained even when $\abs{\qq+\bm G}^W_{\max}$ is taken considerably smaller than $2\abs{\qq+\bm G}^\Psi_{\max}$ ([6], Table I). Convergence with respect to the $k$ points is slower than in LDA: $0.01\,\mathrm{eV}$ with $8\times8\times8$.

The physical meaning of the correlation part of the self-energy, $\Sigma_c=\ii G^0W^c$, can be stated in the language of Chapter 9 as follows. When one electron is added, the surrounding electron gas responds linearly to its charge ($\chi^0$, $\epsilon$), and a polarization cloud forms. The potential $W^c=W-v$ created by the polarization cloud acts back on the original electron itself. The energy of this reaction is $\Sigma_c$. If we approximate it statically, this becomes the COHSEX “Coulomb hole” $\tfrac12[W(\rr,\rr,0)-v]$ mentioned in Appendix C—the interaction with the hole that the electron has dug around itself. Because this is a reaction computed by linear response, GW breaks down in the strongly correlated limit, where the response ceases to be linear; but for many semiconductors, metals, and oxides it is surprisingly good, as we saw in Section 14.5.

Moreover, the systematic errors of QSGW can be diagnosed precisely in the language of linear response theory. The RPA $\chi^0$ is the response of independent particle–hole pairs, and it does not include the attraction between the electron and the hole within a pair (in terms of the spectral representation of Chapter 8, the effect by which the excitation energy $E_n-E_0$ is lowered below the independent-particle difference $\epsilon_j-\epsilon_i$). As a result, $\epsilon_\infty$ is too small, $W$ is too strong, and the gap opens too wide. The $f$-sum rule (Chapter 8) is preserved even in the RPA, but, strictly speaking, it is violated for a $\Pi^0$ built from a nonlocal $\Veff$ (the same situation as for the “scissors operator”)—this too is a point to be careful about when evaluating the $\epsilon(\omega)$ of QSGW.

QSGW is a single self-consistent loop: build $W$ by linear response theory (Chapters 9–12), build $\Sig$ by the Dyson equation (Chapter 13 and Appendix C), and return to $H^0$ by Landau's quasiparticle picture. The tools we have learned in this book appear at every stage of this loop.

14.8 Summary and Exercises

Summary of This Chapter

  1. One-shot GW ($G^{\rm LDA}W^{\rm LDA}$) systematically underestimates the gap in all-electron calculations, depends on the starting point, and is qualitatively wrong for systems in which the LDA orbitals are poor. Because $\Pol$ is suppressed by $Z\times Z$, fully self-consistent GW under-screens $W$ and widens the bandwidth too much.
  2. QSGW determines the independent-particle Hamiltonian $H^0=-\nabla^2/2m+\Veff$ (static, Hermitian, nonlocal) self-consistently so that the bare quasiparticles $\{\epsilon_i,\psi_i\}$ and the dressed quasiparticles $\{E_i,\Phi_i\}$ coincide. The fundamental equation is Eq. \eqref{eq:14-QSGW}: $\Vxc=\tfrac12\sum_{ij}\ket{\psi_i}\{\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_i)]_{ij}+\Re[\Sig(\epsilon_j)]_{ij}\}\bra{\psi_j}$.
  3. Upon convergence, the bands of $H^0$ become the GW quasiparticle bands as they are, the first-order correction vanishes, the $Z$ problem disappears, and $W$ is built from quasiparticles of weight 1. In the limit $W\to v$, the theory returns exactly to Hartree–Fock.
  4. There are two justifications: the minimization of the norm of the perturbation, $M[\Veff]$ (Eq. \eqref{eq:14-norm}, Derivation 14.3), and the cancellation of the $Z$ factors by the Ward identity $\Gamma\to1/Z$ ($\Sig\simeq\ii G^0W$ and $\Pol\simeq-\ii G^0G^0$ are the correct combination).
  5. Results: from $sp$ semiconductors to MnO and NiO, the gaps, $d$ levels, bandwidths, and magnetic moments are systematically improved. The remaining errors are $+10$–$20\%$ for the gap and $-20\%$ for $\epsilon_\infty$, which point in the same direction. The cause is that the RPA $W$ has no electron–hole interaction. The problem is resolved by QSG$\hat W$, which puts ladder diagrams into $W$.
  6. In implementation, the key ingredients are the expansion of two-point functions in the mixed product basis, the contour deformation of the $\omega'$ integral onto the imaginary axis (Chapter 5), $\Re\Pi^0$ by Kramers–Kronig (Chapter 4), and the offset-$\Gamma$ method.
