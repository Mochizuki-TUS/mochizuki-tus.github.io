X-Ray Diffraction — From Bragg's Condition to Peak Positions Set by the Bravais Lattice

IM 01

In ①, select crystal planes in CsCl, SrTiO₃, or NaCl and see waves from adjacent planes reinforce at 2dsinθ=λ, while the atomic layers within one period determine peak intensity. In ②, draw the reciprocal lattice and Ewald sphere to confirm that the Laue condition says the same thing as Bragg's condition. In ③, draw the structure factor as a sum of arrows in the complex plane and follow the systematic absences, in which reflections cancel and vanish, as in face-centered cubic lattices. In ④, keep the lattice parameters fixed and change the contents of the unit cell to verify that the Bravais lattice alone sets the possible peak positions in 2θ. In ⑤, calculate powder XRD patterns for 27 real crystal structures. In ⑥, estimate crystallite sizes from peak widths using the Scherrer equation.

Sintering — How Powder Particles Stick Together and Become Dense

IM 02

In ①, see calcination, the step before sintering, in which two powders in contact mix by atomic diffusion to form an ordered compound or a solid solution. From ② onward, turn to sintering itself and confirm that its driving force is the reduction of surface energy. In ③, compare neck growth in a two-sphere model on log–log plots: the exponent n in (x/a)n∝t is 7 for surface diffusion, 6 for grain-boundary diffusion, 5 for volume diffusion, and 3 for evaporation–condensation. In ④, see that whether matter comes from the particle surface or the grain boundary determines whether shrinkage occurs. In ⑤–⑥, plot densification and see it stop when growing grains leave pores behind. In ⑦, compare how effective each of four approaches is: pressure, a liquid phase, powder design, and additives.

Binary Phase Diagrams — How Does the Microstructure Change as You Move Composition and Temperature?

IM 03

Choose an isomorphous, eutectic, or peritectic system. In ①, drag across the phase diagram to read the phases, microstructure, and phase fractions calculated by the lever rule in two-phase regions. In ②, use Gibbs free-energy curves and their common tangent to trace the equilibrium compositions and construct the phase diagram. In ③, cool a melt and compare equilibrium cooling with Scheil-type solidification without solid-state diffusion to see microsegregation and nonequilibrium eutectics in schematic microstructure diagrams. In ④, compare glass formation on rapid quenching with crystallization on slow cooling. In ⑤, use schematic atomic arrangements to see how a glass or solid solution that is homogeneous at high temperature separates into two phases by spinodal decomposition on cooling.

Strength of Materials and Fracture Mechanics — Why Do Ceramics Break Suddenly?

IM 04

Ceramics can be hard and strong yet fracture suddenly with little deformation. In ①, compare the elongation and failure of metal and ceramic specimens in tensile tests. In ②, explore stress concentration near holes and cracks, and the strength σf=2Eγs/(πa) predicted by Griffith's energy balance. In ③, use a schematic of the atoms at the crack tip to see that the tip blunts in metals, where dislocations can move, but bonds break and the crack runs in ceramics, where they cannot. Use fracture toughness KIC to calculate the tolerable crack size for each material. In ④, break specimens one by one to see strength scatter follow a Weibull distribution. In ⑤, see how strength differs with specimen volume and test method. In ⑥, press a diamond indenter into a sample and measure the residual indentation diagonals yourself to calculate Vickers hardness, then check against the automatic measurement. In ⑦, inspect temperature and stress distributions during thermal shock.

Thermoelectric Conversion — Why Is There an Optimal Carrier Concentration?

IM 05

In ①, see the origin of the Seebeck effect, in which a temperature difference produces a voltage: charged particles on the hot side diffuse toward the cold side, leaving a charge imbalance. In ②, use a single parabolic band model to calculate the Seebeck coefficient S, electrical conductivity σ, and electronic thermal conductivity κe versus carrier concentration. Their competing effects create a peak in the figure of merit ZT=S2σT/κ, typically at 10¹⁹–10²¹ cm⁻³. In ③, see that the quality factor B alone determines the peak height, while the effective mass shifts its position. Explore reducing lattice thermal conductivity (PGEC) in ④, conversion efficiency and the cooling coefficient of performance (COP) in ⑤, and applications of real materials in ⑥.

Comparing Synthesis Methods — Solid-State, Solution, and Vapor Routes (CVD and PVD)

IM 06

For solid-state synthesis, add ethanol to powders, grind, dry, and calcine. For solution synthesis (coprecipitation), add solutions dropwise, precipitate, and collect the product. In CVD, adjust substrate temperature, pressure, and precursor gases; in PVD, switch between vacuum evaporation and sputtering. Compare product layers growing at particle contacts, mixing and precipitation of ions in solution, chemical reactions and gaseous by-products at the substrate surface, and the attachment of ejected atoms and film growth. Linked apparatus diagrams, schematics, and graphs show how the state of the starting materials, transport paths, and locations of reaction or deposition differ between methods and determine the resulting powders or films.

YBCO Synthesis and Superconductor Levitation — How Firing and Oxygen Content Determine Levitation

IM 07

Weigh Y₂O₃, BaCO₃, and CuO, mix with ethanol, then follow one sample through calcination, regrinding, pressing, sintering, oxygenation, polishing, bulk-density measurement, and four-probe resistance measurement. Track phase changes, densification, and oxygen deficiency δ. Cool your sample or a prepared sample (melt-grown YBCO bulk, GdBCO bulk, or sintered Bi-2223) with liquid nitrogen and place it over a magnet. Using flux diagrams and force plots, compare zero-field cooling with field cooling, and see how pressing the sample toward the magnet, moving it sideways, and warming it change the behavior. Being below Tc does not guarantee identical levitation: oxygen content, microstructure, current-loop size, and cooling history all matter.

Barium Ferrite — From a Coprecipitated Precursor to a Magnetic Material

IM 08

Pour iron chloride and barium chloride solutions, with a chosen starting Fe/Ba ratio, into sodium hydroxide and sodium carbonate solution. Wash the precipitate with warm water, vacuum-filter, dry, calcine in an electric furnace, then press, sinter, and magnetize the powder. Track pH, precipitation yield, components lost to the supernatant and filtrate, changes in phase fractions and mass during calcination, and apparent remanent magnetization measured with a balance. Schematics show ions, particles, grains, pores, and magnetization directions. The precipitate is a precursor made of hydrous iron oxide and BaCO₃; BaFe₁₂O₁₉ forms only during calcination. Explore how an off-stoichiometric feed, dissolution during washing, or insufficient calcination temperature affects the product and magnet strength.

Making Glass — Mix Oxides, Melt, and Cool

IM 09

Choose the SiO₂–Al₂O₃–MgO–B₂O₃–Na₂O–K₂O composition in mol% or mass%. Weigh the actual raw materials (silica sand, carbonates, and boric acid), mix in a mortar, and melt in an electric furnace. Then choose water quenching, pressing between metal plates, furnace cooling, or another cooling method. Follow CO₂ bubbles from carbonate decomposition, bubble removal and homogenization, the roles of Al and B in the network, and cooling curves overlaid on CCT and TTT diagrams. Glass formation depends on both cooling rate and composition; not every mixture forms a glass.

Gypsum and Cement — Why Do They Harden When Water Is Added?

IM 10

Choose a powder, water-to-powder ratio, and temperature, then add the powder, mix, and advance time. With calcined gypsum (plaster of Paris), the solution becomes supersaturated with respect to gypsum dihydrate and needle-shaped crystals interlock. With Portland cement, C–S–H, calcium hydroxide, and ettringite grow from particle surfaces and connect them. Follow reaction progress, heat release, and porosity. Setting occurs because water-containing hydrates precipitate and connect, not because the water evaporates. More mixing water leaves more pores.
